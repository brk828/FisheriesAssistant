# Interactions Between Spikedace, Loach Minnow, and Introduced Red Shiner in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 final report to the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGF), prepared by Arizona State University researchers (Marsh, Abarca, Douglas, and Minckley), examines whether the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) contributes to the decline of two native cyprinids of the [[Gila River Basin]]: [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The study combined two years of field surveys in seven reaches of five streams with an experimental artificial stream system. Field data showed that spikedace occupied significantly faster currents when syntopic with red shiner than when alone. Experiments showed that aggressive, spawning red shiners displaced spikedace into slower water. The authors conclude that species interaction contributes to displacement, but they state that population-level effects and the mechanism of decline remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

**Project scope.** The contract covered investigation of ecological interactions between native and exotic fishes in Arizona and New Mexico. The report is organized into eight sections, including habitat relations, food habits, and artificial-system experiments. Food-habit analyses of benthic and drifting macroinvertebrates were reported separately in a thesis by P. J. Abarca.

**Spikedace.** A slender, compressed cyprinid with reduced scales, two spinous dorsal rays, seven dorsal rays, and small pharyngeal teeth (1,4-4,1 formula). Historically widespread across the Gila basin, it has been reduced to scattered populations, with the Aravaipa Creek population the best studied. It is federally listed as threatened.

**Loach minnow.** A secretive, bottom-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the Gila basin of Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora. Once common in suitable habitat, it is now restricted to scattered localities. It is federally listed as threatened and protected by state rules in New Mexico and Arizona.

**Red shiner.** A moderately small, tolerant cyprinid native to central North America, introduced to the Colorado River basin in the early 1950s. It spread widely via natural colonization and intentional or accidental bait-bucket introductions, and is now among the most abundant fishes in the Gila basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat alteration.** Dewatering, impoundment, channelization, groundwater pumping, grazing, mining, and agriculture have degraded habitat. Sedimentation that replaces clean gravels with silt and sand is especially harmful to loach minnow. Native desert fishes are adapted to natural flooding, which exotic species appear to withstand less well.

**Exotic fishes.** Channel catfish, centrarchids (largemouth bass, green sunfish), salmonids, and red shiner are associated with native declines. Among these, the authors identify red shiner as particularly important because it occupies the same habitats where it is sympatric with natives.

**Co-occurrence.** Loach minnow and red shiner co-occurred in only one sample set, and their habitat use differed substantially. Spikedace and red shiner co-occurred at Gila-Redrock and Verde River.

**Study reaches.** Seven reaches of five streams were sampled from May to September 1987: east and west Aravaipa Creek, Blue River, Gila River at Gila and at Redrock (New Mexico), Sycamore Creek, and Verde River (Arizona).

**Methods.** Fish were collected with a 2.0 x 1.2 m, 0.32 cm mesh straight seine (10 m² per haul) or bank electroshocking. Physical variables were depth, current velocity, and weighted substrate size. Analyses used ANOVA on log-transformed values.

## Key Findings

**Habitat use: spikedace**
- Spikedace in syntopy with red shiner (Gila-Redrock and Verde) occupied water with significantly faster current (36.9-40.4 cm/s) than spikedace alone (Aravaipa Creek and Gila-Gila; F = 4.94, p < 0.03, n = 141), and had smaller weighted substrate and shallower depths in some comparisons.
- Available habitat did not differ significantly in mean current velocity among the four main reaches, so the shift reflects selection rather than availability.

**Habitat use: red shiner**
- Red shiner occupied significantly slower current when present with spikedace at Sycamore Creek and Gila-Redrock (for example, 14.3 vs. 24.1 cm/s at Sycamore, F = 6.97, p < 0.009, n = 100) and deeper water.

**Habitat use: loach minnow**
- Loach minnow occupied shallow, swift riffles. Occupied averages were approximately 14.1-20.8 cm depth, 43.8-70.3 cm/s velocity, and 27.3-37.3 weighted substrate across sampled reaches (Table 7).
- Loach minnow habitat was distinct from red shiner habitat, so the authors judged that interaction was unlikely to shift loach minnow habitat use.

**Food habits**
- Spikedace consumed a diverse array of prey, mainly immature and adult trichopterans and ephemeropterans, supplemented by other benthic insect larvae.
- Red shiner foods included amorphous organic matter, ephemeropteran nymphs, trichopteran larvae, and chironomids, with a broad opportunistic diet.
- Loach minnow fed primarily on immature riffle-dwelling benthic invertebrates, mostly ephemeropterans, trichopterans, and dipterans, with low dietary diversity.
- Dietary overlap indices showed no significant spikedace-red shiner overlap (≥0.60) where the species co-occurred at Verde River. The authors therefore found no strong evidence of food competition.

**Artificial stream experiments**
- An artificial system of nine replicate pool-run units was used to test spikedace and red shiner alone and together. Observations were recorded over six months.
- Red shiner, especially during August spawning activity, aggressively chased spikedace. Spikedace aggregations shifted from pool headwaters into mid-run areas (subunit C2).
- The displacement was observed only in August; in the artificial system spikedace moved to slower water, opposite to the faster-water shift seen in nature.
- Authors note that habitat-shift results from both field and experimental studies are consistent: spikedace in syntopy with red shiner occupied habitats they would not normally use.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Some level of species interaction likely displaces spikedace, but direct causation has not been demonstrated.
- The authors recommend long-term life-cycle studies and controlled artificial-system studies of spawning behavior, reproduction, and early life stages, including predation by red shiner on young spikedace.
- Population-level impacts remain unknown.

**Cross-references**
- Comparisons with [[Propst Et Al 1987]] and [[Rinne And Kroeger]] suggest broadly similar spikedace habitat use, with minor differences attributed to protocols and ontogenetic or seasonal shifts.
