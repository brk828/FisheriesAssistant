# Environmental Regulation of Domestic Mining: The Final Nail in the Coffin?

#environmental-law #mining-regulation #water-quality #air-quality

## Executive Summary

This legal article, written by David P. Kimball, III of Evans, Kitchel & Jenckes, P.C., argues that federal and state environmental regulation threatens to put the [[Domestic Mining]] industry out of existence. It traces regulation from the 1970s focus on air and water pollution to 1980s emphasis on waste management and toxic cleanup, and contends that foreign competitors face far lighter rules. The paper examines the laws it considers most threatening: the [[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act]] (RCRA), the [[Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act]] (CERCLA), Arizona's [[Aquifer Protection Permit]] program under the [[Arizona Environmental Quality Act]], and recent developments under the [[Clean Water Act]] and [[Clean Air Act]].

## Background & Description

The article is a legal analysis rather than a field study. It reviews regulatory history, agency rulemaking, court decisions and statutory text up to about 1986.

**RCRA (1976).** RCRA defines solid waste to include mining waste. Hazardous waste is governed by [[Subtitle C]], and solid waste by [[Subtitle D]], which relies on state plans approved by the EPA.

**The Bevill Amendment (1980).** This amendment temporarily exempted mining "special wastes" from Subtitle C. The article describes a long dispute over whether the exemption covered "processing" wastes such as smelting and refining residues. Key events include:

- EPA's 1980 interim interpretation, which broadened "processing" to include smelting and refining.
- Concerned Citizens of Adamstown v. EPA (D.D.C. 1985), which ordered EPA to complete its mining waste study.
- EPA's September 1985 proposed reinterpretation, which was withdrawn on September 30, 1986.
- EPA's June 30, 1986 determination that [[Extraction Waste]] and [[Beneficiation Waste]] do not warrant Subtitle C regulation.

**CERCLA (1980).** CERCLA requires notification of releases of hazardous substances and allows government removal and remedial actions. Owners and operators may be liable for response costs and [[Natural Resource Damages]]. The article cites a maximum natural resource damage figure of $50,000,000 under CERCLA § 107.

**State water and air programs.** Groundwater regulation is largely a state responsibility. Surface water is governed by the federal [[National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System]] (NPDES) program. Air quality is governed by the [[National Ambient Air Quality Standards]] and related programs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article does not present ecological measurements. Its environmental content concerns the regulatory framing of contamination and resource protection. It notes several points relevant to aquatic and terrestrial systems:

- **Waters of the United States.** EPA and the courts have read this term broadly to include non-navigable tributaries, intermittent streambeds and arroyos, so mining operations in desert regions can require NPDES permits.
- **Desert arroyos.** Arizona's aquatic and wildlife copper standard of 0.5 mg/l applies even where arroyos have no aquatic life, and the article states that copper mining discharges can almost never meet it. The state drinking water standard for copper is 1.0 mg/l.
- **Groundwater.** All Arizona aquifers are presumed to be drinking water aquifers, and federal Safe Drinking Water Act primary standards are adopted as aquifer water quality standards. Examples include arsenic at 0.05 mg/l, cadmium at 0.010 mg/l, and nitrate at 10 mg/l.
- **Naturally occurring contaminants.** The article notes that thirteen naturally occurring inorganics (including arsenic, cadmium, copper, lead, mercury and zinc) are CERCLA hazardous substances, and that virtually all mining wastes contain some detectable amount of them.
- **Air pollution.** Non-ferrous smelters emit large amounts of [[Sulfur Dioxide]], and the article discusses how smelter controls already remove about 90 percent of sulfur dioxide emissions.
- **Public health and asthma.** The article discusses the health evidence for short-term sulfur dioxide exposure, arguing that effects are measurable mainly in some hypersensitive asthmatics, are temporary, and are usually reversible.

## Key Findings

### 1. RCRA regulatory status of mining waste
- Mining waste is expressly regulated under Subtitle D.
- After the June 30, 1986 determination, "extraction" and "beneficiation" wastes are regulated under Subtitle D and are not subject to Subtitle C.
- "Processing" wastes remain temporarily exempt from Subtitle C under the [[Bevill Amendment]], but the article warns that EPA may later find they merit Subtitle C regulation.

### 2. The chain of liability in the article's argument
The article argues that a Subtitle C designation of processing wastes could reach the whole integrated mining facility through three linked provisions:
- The [[Derived From Rule]], which makes residues of hazardous wastes hazardous.
- The [[Mixture Rule]], which makes any mixture containing a listed hazardous waste hazardous.
- The [[Continuing Releases Provision]] (RCRA § 3004(u)), which applies corrective action to all solid waste management units at a treatment, storage or disposal facility.

EPA's broad readings of "solid waste management unit" and "facility" would extend this reach beyond the processing unit itself.

### 3. The acid plant blowdown example
- [[Acid Plant Blowdown]] from primary copper smelters has been listed or proposed for listing as hazardous waste on at least two occasions (45 Fed. Reg. 33124, May 19, 1980; 50 Fed. Reg. 40282, Oct. 2, 1985).
- The article says the only way for a smelter to avoid TSD facility status would be to ship untreated blowdown off-site every ninety days, at very high cost.
- Recycling, neutralization, land disposal and surface impoundments each face obstacles, including the July 8, 1987 land disposal ban for low pH materials and "minimum technology" retrofitting requirements.

### 4. CERCLA and the Eagle-Picher decision
- In [[Eagle-Picher Industries v. EPA]], 759 F.2d 905 (D.C. Cir. 1985), the court held that the parenthetical exclusion in subparagraph C of the "hazardous substance" definition applies only to subparagraph C.
- The article argues the decision exposes the mining industry to broad liability, since mining wastes commonly contain listed naturally occurring inorganics.
- The court treated the [[National Priorities List]] as an "informational tool" or "first step." The article argues this is naive, because remedial action can be triggered once a site is listed, or even proposed for listing.
- The [[Hazard Ranking System]] (HRS) threshold for listing is a score of 28.50 or more.

### 5. Congressional response through SARA
- SARA requires EPA to revise the HRS so that it "accurately assesses the relative degree of risk," with an effective date no later than 24 months after SARA's enactment on October 17, 1986.
- SARA requires special consideration of "special study wastes" at mining facilities added to the NPL after October 18, 1986 (pending HRS revision).
- SARA's protection for naturally occurring substances (CERCLA § 104(a)(3)(A)) covers only unaltered or naturally altered deposits, not man-disturbed ones.

### 6. Arizona's aquifer protection permit program
- The [[Arizona Environmental Quality Act]] (1986) requires an individual or general [[Aquifer Protection Permit]] for any discharge that has a reasonable probability of reaching an aquifer.
- Presumed discharging facilities include surface impoundments, solid waste facilities, injection wells, [[Mine Tailings]] piles and ponds, mine leaching operations, and septic systems over 2,000 gallons per day.
- Exempt facilities include mining overburden returned to the excavation site and facilities closed before January 1, 1986.
- Applicants must demonstrate [[Best Available Demonstrated Control Technology]] (BADCT), which may include technology that prohibits all discharge where practicable.
- For hazardous substances, the point of compliance is generally at the edge of the waste management area.

### 7. Clean Water Act and NPDES
- Under broad readings of "waters of the United States," an NPDES permit can be required for overburden dumped into a desert gully or arroyo, and for erosion from spoil piles (citing [[Sierra Club v. Abston Construction Co.]], 620 F.2d 41 (5th Cir. 1980)).
- The article argues that water quality standards applied to intermittent desert tributaries "make no sense" in many cases.

### 8. Clean Air Act developments
- **Sulfur dioxide NAAQS.** Current standards are a primary annual average of 0.03 ppm, a primary 24-hour average of 0.14 ppm, and a secondary 3-hour average of 0.5 ppm. EPA has considered a one-hour primary standard. OAQPS staff recommended a range of 0.2 to 0.5 ppm in its September 1986 draft addendum, and the article argues this could require very costly retrofits.
- **Smelter consent decrees.** Phelps Dodge's Douglas Reduction Works agreed to a six-minute concentration limit of 2.0 ppm, and the Douglas facility has since closed. A proposed Arizona order for Magma's San Manuel smelter would require a six-minute average of 1.75 ppm.
- **U.S.-Mexico Border Agreement.** Signed January 29, 1986, it affects copper smelters within sixty miles of the border. The article describes its impact as largely one of "moral suasion."
- **PM10 proposal.** EPA proposed a size-specific particulate standard on March 20, 1984, covering particles of 10 micrometers or less. The article notes that retaining the TSP secondary standard would require two monitor types and two compliance plans.
- **Prevention of significant deterioration.** PSD increments are hard for surface mines to meet, and several mining companies are litigating over whether fugitive dust should count.
- **Stack height.** EPA's final stack height regulations (July 8, 1985) limit credit for tall stacks, which may reduce emission limits for affected sources.
- **Inorganic arsenic NESHAP.** Promulgated August 4, 1986. Some smelters may need secondary hoods because of higher arsenic in copper matte.
- **Toxic air pollutants.** EPA has promulgated only seven NESHAPs, and states are developing their own toxic air programs. The article criticizes states that adopt occupational guidelines (from more than 800 guidelines) with reductions of 100- to 1000-fold.

### 9. Overall conclusion
The author concludes that the domestic mining industry is at a competitive disadvantage against foreign producers and must be relieved of "the unfair burden" of many state and federal environmental laws. Other laws the article lists as affecting mining include NEPA, the National Historic Preservation Act, the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act, the Endangered Species Conservation Act, the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act, the Wilderness Act, the Safe Drinking Water Act and the Toxic Substances Control Act.
