## Executive Summary

This document records the minutes of a binational (US–Mexico) planning meeting convened by [[Conservation International]] and [[American Rivers]] (November 2–3, 1994) to scope a scientific research initiative on the [[Lower Colorado River]], the [[Colorado River Delta]], and the [[Upper Gulf of California]] ([[Sea of Cortez]]). Rather than presenting original data, the meeting synthesizes expert consensus among ichthyologists, hydrologists, botanists, and social scientists on the ecological state of the system following decades of upstream damming and flow diversion. Key scientific concerns raised include the near-total replacement of native freshwater fish fauna by exotic species, uncertainty over nutrient/sediment collapse in the Upper Gulf, the resilience of the [[Cienega de Santa Clara]] wetland refuge, and the absence of systematic historical baselines needed to define realistic restoration goals. The group proposed a phased research program (rapid inventory/synthesis, followed by long-term ecosystem modeling) intended to produce a scientifically credible "white paper" to inform binational restoration and management policy.

## Background & Description

- **Setting**: The meeting addressed the entire hydrological continuum from Hoover Dam/Glen Canyon Dam through the [[Lower Colorado River]] mainstem, into the [[Colorado River Delta]], and terminating in the [[Upper Gulf of California Biosphere Reserve]].
- **Institutional context**: Convened jointly by [[Conservation International]] and [[American Rivers]], with scientific input from [[W.L. Minckley]], [[Paul Marsh]], [[Richard Felger]], [[Ed Glenn]], [[Duncan Patten]], [[Julie Stromberg]], [[Bob Ohmart]], [[Lloyd Findley]], [[Don Thomson]], [[Bob MacNish]], and Mexican collaborators including [[Jose Vincio Macias-Zamora]] and [[Cuauhtemoc Leon]].
- **Historical framing**: Participants stressed that the system has been "heavily altered" but is not biologically dead — high productivity persists in the Upper Gulf despite flow curtailment. There is a recognized "window of opportunity" to intervene before further, potentially irreversible, losses occur.
- **Taxonomic scope discussed**:
  - **Upper Gulf marine fauna**: [[Don Thomson]] reported 61 marine species within the Upper Gulf Biosphere Reserve, with an additional 35 species documented elsewhere in Mexican waters. No commercial fishery is permitted in the reserve (only sport fishing); illegal fishing was noted as an ongoing problem.
  - **Freshwater fish fauna**: [[W.L. Minckley]] stated that the [[Colorado River]] is unique among major rivers in that its **entire native freshwater fish fauna has been replaced by non-indigenous species**, characterizing exotics as "biological pollutants that cannot be eliminated."
  - **Cienega de Santa Clara refuge fauna**: [[Ed Glenn]] presented satellite/remote-sensing data showing the cienega (~12,000 acres of cattail habitat within the Biosphere Reserve) is highly resilient. When Bureau of Reclamation test manipulations reduced bypass-canal flows and dried the wetland, subsequent flow restoration allowed the system to "green up" completely, with **desert pupfish**, grass shrimp, and other species persisting through the disturbance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime**: Historic river flow to the delta is now essentially limited to occasional large flood events; regulated flows remain channelized and do not spread across the floodplain as required to benefit fish and wetland vegetation.
- **Salinity**: The Cienega de Santa Clara currently exhibits maximum recorded salinity near 6 ppt, with inflows ranging from ~108,000 to 140,000 acre-feet annually. Along the riparian corridor (Blythe to Yuma), soil/water salinity above an EC of 3 was flagged as biologically excessive for riparian vegetation.
- **Sediment starvation**: Multiple participants ([[Bob MacNish]], [[Ed Glenn]]) noted that essentially all major deltas — not just the Colorado — are experiencing sediment starvation from upstream damming, a factor likely compounding fish and invertebrate habitat loss in the delta.
- **Vegetation–hydrology feedback affecting fish/wildlife habitat**: [[Bob Ohmart]] and [[Julie Stromberg]] described how long-term, non-flooding dam releases favor salt cedar (Tamarix) over native cottonwood-willow, indirectly degrading riparian corridor quality for neotropical migratory birds (e.g., willow flycatcher) and altering the terrestrial-aquatic interface relevant to fish nursery/riparian habitat.
- **Endemism and Gulf productivity**: [[W.L. Minckley]] hypothesized that the Upper Gulf delta shows **low endemism** because its extreme geologic-scale environmental variability historically prevented speciation — potentially a stabilizing factor for the Gulf ecosystem, though he separately warned a future **nutrient collapse** in the Upper Gulf remains plausible due to cutoff of Colorado River-derived nutrients and sediment.
- **Nutrient dynamics debate**: [[Don Thomson]] noted continued mixing of deltaic sediments currently sustains nutrient levels in the Upper Gulf, and evidence of impacts on some (but not all) species; no dedicated studies exist to predict a collapse timeline ([[Lloyd Findley]]).
- **Species loss estimate**: [[Richard Felger]] estimated that the historic delta supported 200–400 species (cross-taxa, not fish-specific), most now extirpated, with no formal historical species list — recreating this baseline was flagged as a priority research task.
- **Fisheries perception vs. data**: [[Tom McGuire]] reported that Upper Gulf fishing communities perceive the decline of fisheries as directly caused by reduced freshwater inflow from the Colorado River; the group noted this is "part historic recollection, part perception," underscoring the need for empirical flow–fishery correlation studies (e.g., shrimp population vs. flow correlation, proposed by [[Jose Macias-Zamora]]).

## Key Findings

- **No systematic historical fish/faunal baseline exists** for the delta or Upper Gulf; participants agreed an inventory (using missionary records, aerial/photo archives, sediment cores, and ethnographic sources) is a prerequisite before restoration targets can be set.
- **Entire native freshwater fish assemblage of the Colorado River has been extirpated and replaced by exotics** — a globally unusual case cited by [[W.L. Minckley]] as defining the severity of ecosystem alteration.
- **The Upper Gulf supports at least 61 endemic/resident marine species** within the Biosphere Reserve (plus 35 more regionally), sustained currently by sport fishing management only; commercial fishing is considered likely to cause collapse.
- **The Cienega de Santa Clara functions as a resilient refugium**, having withstood an experimental flow cutoff without total loss of pupfish and invertebrate populations, suggesting engineered flow management (rather than simple "flow dumping") could most efficiently expand marine/wetland habitat.
- **A proposed correlational research question** — whether shrimp population abundance in the Upper Gulf can be statistically linked to Colorado River flow volume — was identified as a priority testable hypothesis for Phase I research.
- **Nutrient/sediment collapse risk in the Upper Gulf remains an open, high-priority scientific question**, with competing hypotheses (buffered by deltaic sediment mixing vs. progressive starvation).
