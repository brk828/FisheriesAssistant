# Hybridization as a Factor in the Speciation of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hybridization

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Robert R. Miller, argues that [[Hybridization]] between fish species is common in temperate freshwaters, especially in the [[Nearctic Region]], and that human-driven environmental change and species introductions have accelerated it. The author classifies hybrids into three types with different evolutionary consequences and presents two case studies: a [[Gobiomorus]] population in [[Gatun Lake]], Panama, created by the [[Panama Canal]], and a population of [[Cyprinodon]] pupfishes in [[Coahuila]], Mexico. The paper concludes that hybridization has so far played only a minor role in vertebrate evolution, but that occasional backcrossing may supply genetic material for new adaptive peaks, especially after catastrophic environmental change.

## Background & Description

Hybridization between fish species is described as rather common in temperate fresh waters. Human modification of continental environments and the introduction of species into waters where they were not native have increased its frequency, which the author says is rising at a geometric rate.

The paper distinguishes three categories of interspecific hybrids, which are hard to demonstrate without breeding experiments:

- **[[F1 Hybrids]]**: usually sterile in at least one sex, intermediate between the parental species, and relatively low in genetic variability.
- **[[F2 Hybrids]] and later generations**: greatly increased variability; may resemble one parent more than the other and show traits absent from both parents.
- **[[Introgressive Hybridization]]**: the gradual infiltration of one species' genes into another through repeated [[Backcrossing]].

The author considers F1 hybrids the commonest or at least the easiest to detect. Natural interbreeding of F2 and later generations is rare, because most wild populations of full species do not produce interfertile hybrids. Introgression is the most subtle and hardest to detect, so the author suggests it may be less uncommon than is generally held. Outside fishes, introgression has been reported in frogs and toads (Mecham, 1960), and it has been proposed (A. H. Miller, 1955) that a woodpecker species may have arisen this way.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gatun Lake, Panama.** The [[Panama Canal]], completed in 1913, mixed Atlantic and Pacific waters. Two allopatric gobies, the Atlantic [[Gobiomorus Dormitor]] and the Pacific [[Gobiomorus Maculatus]], came into contact in the newly created lake. By 1926, samples showed a population with characters of both species. The hybrids more closely resemble *dormitor* overall. All 70 Gatun Lake specimens available to the author had originally been identified as *G. dormitor* by competent ichthyologists. The author describes this case as a recently detected, unpublished example of introgressive hybridization.

**Coahuila, Mexico.** Two oviparous *Cyprinodon* species hybridize there, producing all three hybrid types. The parental species differ strongly in morphology, ecology, life colors and behavior, although they cross easily and their hybrids appear fertile. The author notes the parents do not seem closely related. Both belong to a distinctive endemic fauna of 10 of the 15 species known from a small desert valley. Key features of the setting include:

- Average annual rainfall of roughly 6 to 12 inches on the valley floor.
- A limestone floor honeycombed with subsurface waters, with many large springs, creeks, sinkholes and marshes.
- Eight irrigation canals, with much of the valley's water now diverted out of the basin.
- The valley was previously not connected to adjacent drainage systems.

The two species are ecologically segregated and normally do not occur together, even where habitats are closely adjacent. They are thought to mix occasionally when violent summer storms turn the desert surface into temporary sheets of water. All hybrids were found in the canals, which the author calls the "[[Hybridized Habitat]]" (a term from [[Edgar Anderson]]).

## Key Findings

- Interspecific hybridization is common and increasing in North American freshwater fishes. As of 1960, 175 interspecific combinations (80 intergeneric) were known across 17 families. Nearly half were detected after 1950.
- Over the same period, only 11 combinations were recorded across 9 marine families, which the paper presents as a contrast with freshwater fishes.
- Introgressive hybridization is the least obvious and hardest to detect of the three hybrid types, so it is probably underreported.
- The Gatun Lake gobies show that canal-created contact between formerly allopatric species can produce hybrid populations. Specimens that look like one species can still be hybrids, because the hybrids resemble *G. dormitor* more than *G. maculatus*.
- In Coahuila, the two *Cyprinodon* species produce F1, F2 and introgressed hybrids. A total of 1,517 specimens of hybrid or hybrid origin were collected from 7 stations.
- Hybrids were concentrated in the canals. One station yielded a small number of non-hybrid specimens (2 of one type, 1 hybrid; this line is partly garbled in the scan). Another yielded 1 bicolor specimen and 61 hybrids.
- The author concludes that hybridization has so far played only a minor role in the evolution of sexually reproducing vertebrates. Fertility barriers or reduced fecundity would doom hybrids, but occasional backcrossing may add genes to the [[Gene Pool]] more effectively than mutation in supplying material for [[Adaptive Peaks]].
- Catastrophic environmental change, often caused by humans, may favor hybrids by giving them a selective advantage over both parental forms.
