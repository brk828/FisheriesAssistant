# Movements of Channel Catfish in the Savannah River and Upper Three Runs (1953–1954)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 report by Robert L. Humphries, prepared at the [[Institute of Radiation Ecology]] of the [[University of Georgia]], presents a mark-recapture study of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*) in the [[Savannah River]] and its tributary [[Upper Three Runs]] within the [[Savannah River Plant]], an [[Atomic Energy Commission]] (AEC) reservation. The work was conducted in 1953 and 1954 and was motivated by the possibility that fish could enter contaminated tributaries, be exposed to radioactive effluents, and return to the river where they could be caught and eaten by people. The study found three types of movement: local activity within a limited river area, a spring upstream movement into Upper Three Runs followed by a June–July downstream exit, and long-distance, apparently random movement along the river. Angler tag returns were unusually high (12.2 percent), which the author attributed to low population levels, high exploitation, or both.

## Background & Description

The study was a follow-up to earlier work on the Savannah River, including a U.S. Public Health Service survey (1951–1952), a fish survey by Harry W. Freeman, and a biological survey by Ruth Patrick. It was supervised by Dr. Donald C. Scott and was submitted as a Master of Science thesis in 1955. The Foreword, written by Eugene P. Odum, notes that the study "clearly demonstrated a potential route" by which [[Radionuclide Contamination]] could reach people, and that waste disposal at the plant was designed to keep radioactive releases to streams as low as possible.

Channel catfish were chosen as the focal species because they are abundant, are easily caught alive in traps, and are economically the most important fish in this section of the river. Other species, including [[Flat Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus platycephalus*), eel, redhorse suckers, bluegill, largemouth bass and carp, were caught only incidentally.

**Study area:**
- **Savannah River:** a typical large, mature Coastal Plain river with a well-developed floodplain. Width was 300–500 feet, average channel depth 12.1 feet and greatest depth 23.8 feet. The bottom is shifting sand and silt. Flow during the study ranged from 5,260 to 22,300 cubic feet per second (cfs), with an average monthly mean of 6,775 cfs.
- **Upper Three Runs:** a black-water creek crossing a swampy floodplain 0.5–1.0 mile wide. It is unusually clear, has a sandy bottom, and had flows of 130–300 cfs. It was the only tributary in the area that still retained more or less natural conditions, and it was the largest and most accessible stream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Fish were captured in welded-wire traps baited with cottonseed meal cake, a method similar to that of commercial catfishermen on the river. Seven stations were used: Savannah River stations 1, 2 and 3 (mile 172.2, 170.8 and 171.6), Upper Three Runs stations 4, 1-2, 1-3 and 2-3. Each captured fish was measured, weighed, marked and released at its capture point.

**Marking methods.**
- **Fin-clipping (1953):** Paired fins were clipped in a combination that identified the station of marking. Ten combinations were possible, and seven were used.
- **Tattooing (1953):** Tried on a small number of fish, but abandoned because the marks became illegible and yielded few returns.
- **Petersen-type tags (1954):** Serially numbered pairs of red plastic discs (0.5 inch) joined by a nickel pin. The tags carried a return legend for the Department of Biology, University of Georgia, and a press release asked local fishermen to return them.

**Environmental drivers.** The author related trap catches to Savannah River level. Catches rose with fluctuating water levels but fell when the river overflowed its banks, probably because fish moved onto the inundated floodplain beyond the traps. Catches also fell during long periods of stable levels. The 1954 drought produced low, stable flows, which likely reduced the number of river fish entering Upper Three Runs. Water temperature was also considered as a factor in the October decline in 1953.

## Key Findings

**Trapping and marking totals**

| Season | Channel catfish handled | Recaptures | Individuals marked/tagged | Flat bullheads handled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1953 (June 5/6 – Dec. 12) | 1,921 | 446 | 1,475 (fin-clipped) | 127 (109 marked, 18 recaptures) |
| 1954 (Apr. 3 – Sept. 18) | 413 | 44 | 369 (tagged) | 80 (70 tagged, 10 recaptures) |

Across both seasons, 2,176 fish were handled in 1953 (464 recaptures). Trapping success differed sharply between years. The author attributed this mainly to river-level fluctuations, not to trapping duration.

**Site fidelity in the Savannah River (1953)**
- Channel catfish marked at river stations mostly remained near the marking site. At most 5 percent of fish marked at Station 1 were recaptured elsewhere, 2 percent at Stations 2 and 3, and 1 percent at Station 4. Station 1 retained 22 percent of its own marked fish.
- Flat bullheads also showed limited movement. Of 18 recaptures, all but two were at the marking station.

**Movement in Upper Three Runs**
- Most movement from Upper Three Runs stations was downstream, except for one fish. Movement percentages from these stations were greater than the percentages recaptured at the station of marking.
- Channel catfish disappeared from the upper Upper Three Runs stations after August 5 in 1953 and after July 31 in 1954. Gill nets and additional traps set in the same period caught none.
- Greatest recorded distance in 1953: 9.6 miles, by two fish moving from Station 2-3 to Station 1 in at least 17 days.
- In 1954, fish marked in the river at Station 4 showed upstream movement in Upper Three Runs, mostly before July 5. Only one fish showed definite spring downstream movement.

**Tagged fish (1954)**
- Two fish tagged at Station 1 on May 11 were later recaptured in Upper Three Runs (on July 1 at Station 4 and on July 5 at Station 1-3).
- Seven fish tagged at Station 4 moved upstream in Upper Three Runs, and nine moved downstream to the river.

**Angler returns (1954–1955)**
- At least 45 of 369 tagged channel catfish (12.2 percent) were caught by fishermen by July 1, 1955. Part of this high return was due to one commercial fisherman trapping just upstream of the study area at mile 173.3.
- Returns from the river showed downstream movement from May to late July (0.6 to 67.9 miles), followed by mostly upstream movement from late July through October (up to 17.3 miles).
- Greatest distances: 67.9 miles downstream and 32.1 miles upstream for river-tagged fish; 32.1 miles upstream and 30.3 miles downstream for Upper Three Runs-tagged fish.
- Three tags returned in 1955 after 225, 332 and 352 days at large showed little or no movement.

**Movement classification (multiple recaptures)**
1. Little or no movement at river stations: 14 fish.
2. Exchange between river stations or movement to other river sections: 10 fish plus 2 others.
3. Spring movement into Upper Three Runs (mid-April to mid-July 1954): 25 fish.
4. Summer movement out of Upper Three Runs: 4 fish.
5. Movement into and out of Upper Three Runs as far as the influence of river level: 26 fish.

**Conclusions**
- Channel catfish showed three types of movement: (1) local activity within roughly 0.5 to 5 miles of river, possibly including the lower tributary during high water; (2) a spring upstream movement into Upper Three Runs in May–June, followed by a June–July downstream exit, possibly related to spawning (not confirmed); and (3) apparently random, long-distance movement in the river, with no identified environmental cause.
- Some evidence suggested schooling: fish tagged on the same day at one station sometimes appeared together at another.
- The high tag return rate indicates low population levels, high exploitation, or both.

**Comparison with other studies.** Earlier work on [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Rock Bass]] and centrarchids generally found sedentary fish with home ranges of a few hundred feet to a few miles. Tagging studies of channel catfish in Ohio, the Upper Mississippi, and the Des Moines River (Iowa) reported return rates of 2.0–2.1 percent and, in some cases, long downstream movements. The author noted that the Des Moines River pools restricted movement, while the continuous deep channels of the Savannah and Mississippi did not. No comparable study had documented fish moving into and out of a tributary in spring and summer.
