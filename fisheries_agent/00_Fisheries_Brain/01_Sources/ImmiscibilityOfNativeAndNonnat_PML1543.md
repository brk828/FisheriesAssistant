# Immiscibility of Native and Non-native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This paper argues that the decline of native fishes in the lower [[Colorado River]] is driven mainly by the presence of [[Non-native Fishes]], not by physical habitat alteration. Native and non-native species overlap broadly in habitat and resource use, but non-natives include more piscivores and nest-building, parental-care species that can displace natives. The authors predict that natives would thrive in altered habitats if non-natives were absent, and case studies from ponds and reservoirs support this. The paper recommends segregated management of native and non-native fishes. It was prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau Of Reclamation]] under the [[Biological Opinion]] on lower Colorado River operations.

## Background & Description

**Historical system.** The pre-development Colorado River was an unregulated, free-flowing system of extremes. Discharge at Yuma, Arizona, ranged from near 0 during summer droughts to more than 7,000 m³/s during floods. Annual sediment transport averaged 108 million metric tons from 1925 to 1935. Water temperatures ranged from freezing in headwater streams to more than 30 °C in low-desert channels.

**Native fauna.** The native fish fauna comprised 36 species, most living in smaller habitats such as streams, springs, and cienegas. The large rivers supported a relatively depauperate fauna of "big-river fishes," including:
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

**Present-day system.** The river is now dammed, diverted, channelized, and desiccated in places. Watershed impacts include mining, timber harvest, grazing, agriculture, and urbanization. Few free-flowing reaches remain, and none are in historical condition. (See [[Habitat Alteration]].)

**Non-native fauna.** About 70 of the more than 100 fish species now present are introduced. Many come from Africa, Asia, marine environments, and other parts of the United States, and they belong mainly to Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae, Salmonidae, Catostomidae, and Ictaluridae. Prominent non-natives include:
- Large predatory sport fishes: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], and [[Walleye]]
- Small but damaging predators: [[Green Sunfish]], which feeds on the young of native fishes

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native–non-native overlap:** Non-native fishes dominate native fishes in altered habitats, even though such habitats should support self-sustaining native populations. The native community of the lower river has been reduced to only a few species in isolated reaches, and the upper basin shows a similar pattern.
- **Competition versus predation:** Habitat overlap opens the door to competition. The authors acknowledge competition's role but argue that the high number of piscivores among non-natives reduces its relative importance.
- **Case-study habitats:** The sites where natives succeed are mostly small, earthen depressions filled with water. They often have poor or variable water quality, dissolved oxygen depletion, and extreme temperature or pH. Bonytail and razorback sucker still complete their life cycles there, which is notable because bonytail is considered one of the most habitat-specific native fishes.
- **Management framing:** The paper frames its work within the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] [[Biological Opinion]] on lower Colorado River operations, specifically Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 4.

## Key Findings

**Methods and scale.** A computer-based literature search generated almost 100,000 citations, from which about 8,500 were judged relevant. About 400 papers were selected for evaluation. Data were organized into a series of 21 matrices by species (native vs. non-native), life stage (larva, juvenile, adult), environmental variable, and functional/behavioral attributes such as food habits, feeding mode, piscivory, cannibalism, territoriality, spawning, and reproductive mode. The illustrative matrix covers 40 lower Colorado River fish species by roughly 200 life-history and habitat features.

**Resource-use comparison.**
1. Native and non-native fishes overlap broadly in physical habitat, resource use, and life functions.
2. Non-natives include a greater number and proportion of **piscivores**. Most natives are **functional generalists**.
3. Natives are, with few exceptions, **broadcast spawners that provide no parental care**. Non-natives are predominantly **nest-builders** that give at least some parental care to eggs and young.
4. The authors conclude that non-natives' feeding and reproductive attributes allow them to displace, replace, or exclude natives. They found no evidence that habitat per se is responsible for the degraded native community.

**Predation.** Non-native predation on early life stages is described as the single most important factor in the decline of native Colorado River fishes. Evidence comes from:
- The razorback sucker recovery plan (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1998), which lists direct predation by channel catfish, common carp, green sunfish, flathead catfish, and largemouth bass.
- Studies of bonytail and razorback sucker at [[Lake Mohave]] involving largemouth bass and other non-natives.
- Documented green sunfish effects on larval-to-juvenile recruitment of razorback sucker.
- Evidence that non-natives consume natives in the laboratory, in artificial habitats, and in natural habitats.

**Case studies (Table 3).** About 12 sites were reviewed where native fishes were raised alone or with non-natives. Sites include the ASU Golf Course, ASU Research Park, BC Golf Course and Wetland Ponds, [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]], Cibola High Levee Pond, CRIT Native Fish Ponds, Davis Cove, Dexter National Fish Hatchery, Emerald Canyon Golf Course, Mohave Backwaters, and Page Golf Course. Where non-natives were absent, natives showed growth, maturation, and reproduction. Survival was impaired where non-natives such as sunfish, largemouth bass, channel catfish, or green sunfish were present.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Natives would likely survive and complete their life functions in most altered and managed habitats if non-natives were absent.
- The evidence supports **segregated management** of native and non-native fishes.
- Continued decline of imperiled native fishes requires prompt action by responsible parties to plan and implement appropriate strategies (see [[Native Fish Management]]).
