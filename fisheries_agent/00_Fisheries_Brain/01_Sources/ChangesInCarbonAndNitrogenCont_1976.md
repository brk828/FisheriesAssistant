# Changes in Carbon and Nitrogen Content During Decomposition of Three Macrophytes in Freshwater and Marine Environments

## Executive Summary

This study by Hunter (1976) is **not a fish-focused study**, but rather an investigation into the biogeochemical fate of three aquatic macrophytes — [[Chara Contraria]] (a submerged charophyte), [[Lemna Minor]] (a floating angiosperm/duckweed), and [[Fucus Vesiculosus]] (a marine brown alga/rockweed) — during in situ decomposition in freshwater and marine settings. Using the "mesh bag" (litterbag) technique, the author tracked ash, organic carbon, and nitrogen content over 63–70 days to assess how decomposition alters the nutritional quality (C:N ratio) of detritus, a critical food source for aquatic invertebrates at the base of the [[Detritus Food Chain]]. The relevance to ichthyology/aquatic ecology lies in understanding the **nutritive quality of primary production entering the detrital pool**, which underpins secondary (invertebrate) and higher trophic level (fish) productivity in ponds, swamps, salt marshes, and rocky shores.

## Background & Description

- **Study organisms:**
  - *Chara contraria* — a medium-sized, non-vascular, submerged charophyte with unusually high inorganic (ash) content; collected from a shallow, hard-water freshwater pond with clay-ooze bottom and low biological productivity.
  - *Lemna minor* — a small floating-leaved angiosperm (duckweed); collected from a shallow, densely-vegetated freshwater swamp with reducing black ooze substrate.
  - *Fucus vesiculosus* — a marine brown alga (rockweed); collected from a rocky intertidal shore (Woods Hole, MA) and comparatively transplanted into a salt marsh tidal channel (Little Sippewissett Marsh, Falmouth, MA).
- **Methodology:** Plant material was dried, weighed, and placed into fiberglass mesh bags (1 mm² mesh) submerged in situ. Bags were periodically retrieved and analyzed for ash content (muffle furnace, 500°C), organic carbon ("wet oxidation" dichromate method of Russell-Hunter et al. 1968), and total nitrogen (micro-Dumas method via Coleman Model 29 Nitrogen Analyzer). Supplementary laboratory leaching experiments quantified rapid physical loss of solutes independent of microbial decomposition.
- **Experimental duration:** 70 days (freshwater: Chara, Lemna); 63 days (marine: Fucus).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitats studied:**
  - Freshwater pond (Chara site): hard water, low productivity, fauna dominated by amphipods, insect larvae, gastropods (*Helisoma anceps*, *Amnicola* sp.).
  - Freshwater swamp (Lemna site): reducing black ooze, dense vegetation, fauna including amphipods, insect larvae, tubificid worms, pulmonate snails (*Physa gyrina*, *Lymnaea palustris*).
  - Rocky marine shore (Fucus site 1): oxygenated, high wave action, near [[Marine Biological Laboratory]] water intake, Woods Hole, MA.
  - Salt marsh tidal channel (Fucus site 2): low-oxygen, reducing detrital/mud substrate dominated by decomposing *Spartina*, with surface colonization by purple sulfur bacteria; occasional *Codium* and *Ulva* present.
- **Physico-chemical variation:** Salt marsh site showed strong tidal temperature fluctuation (10–15°C) and very low oxygen (0.16 ml O₂/l) compared to the rocky shore (3.2 ml O₂/l), highlighting a redox/oxygen gradient hypothesized to shape decomposer community composition.
- **Trophic context:** The study is framed within the broader ecological literature on [[Detritus Food Chain]] dynamics (Odum & de la Cruz 1967; Kaushik & Hynes 1971), emphasizing that many freshwater and marine invertebrates preferentially graze on **Aufwuchs** (epiphytic biofilm) or detritus rather than living macrophyte tissue — a preference argued to reflect superior nitrogen/protein content of decomposing versus living plant material.

## Key Findings

- **Initial nutritional differences between plants:**
  - Undecomposed *Lemna*: 437.3 µg C/mg and 65.0 µg N/mg ash-free dry weight (C:N = 6.7:1) — relatively low ash (24.6% of dry weight).
  - Undecomposed *Chara*: 400.6 µg C/mg and 20.8 µg N/mg ash-free dry weight (C:N = 19.3:1) — very high ash (70.3% of dry weight).
  - Undecomposed *Fucus*: 473.1 µg C/mg and 16.8 µg N/mg ash-free dry weight (C:N ≈ 28.2:1).

- **Freshwater decomposition trends (10 weeks):**
  - *Chara*: carbon **decreased** (400.6 → ~294–332 µg C/mg), nitrogen **increased sharply** (20.8 → 35.3–40.4 µg N/mg); C:N ratio dropped from 19.3:1 to ~8.3:1 — indicating strong microbial nitrogen enrichment.
  - *Lemna*: carbon **increased slightly** (437.3 → 468.9–482.8 µg C/mg), nitrogen **decreased** (65.0 → 47.3–54.8 µg N/mg); C:N ratio rose modestly from 6.7:1 to 8.6:1.
  - Convergence: Despite opposite initial trajectories, the **final C:N ratios of decomposing Chara and Lemna converged to nearly identical values (~8.3–8.6:1)**, suggesting decomposition erases initial nutritional differences between disparate macrophyte types.
  - Dry weight loss: *Lemna* retained ~75% of initial mass after 10 weeks; *Chara* declined steadily to ~22% by week 4.
  - Ash content increased during decomposition in both species (Chara: 70.3%→91.4%; Lemna: 24.6%→44.4%).

- **Marine decomposition trends (Fucus, 63 days):**
  - **Salt marsh site:** No significant change in carbon content (473.2→486.2 µg C/mg, n.s.); nitrogen increased significantly (16.8→45.5 µg N/mg, P<0.002). Only ~34% of dry weight and ~30% of carbon remained at 63 days.
  - **Rocky shore site:** Carbon decreased significantly (473.1→421.7 µg C/mg, P<0.05); nitrogen increased significantly (16.8→33.7 µg N/mg, P<0.001). Only ~1% of dry weight and ~0.5% of carbon remained — much more rapid mass loss than salt marsh.
  - C:N ratio declined rapidly at both sites within ~17 days (salt marsh: 28.2:1→10.1:1; rocky shore: →11.0:1), stabilizing by the end of the experiment at 9.8:1 (salt marsh) vs. 12.5:1 (rocky shore) — a **consistent, statistically significant inter-site difference**.
  - Hypothesis: the salt marsh site's persistent carbon levels may reflect colonization by **autotrophic microbes (e.g., purple sulfur bacteria)** that fix carbon, offsetting heterotrophic carbon losses, whereas the rocky shore's exclusively heterotrophic decomposer community drove net carbon loss.

- **Leaching experiments (physical, non-biological losses):**
  - Rapid initial mass loss occurred within the first 20 minutes of submersion in sterile seawater (highest loss rate: 6.1% dry weight/min at 1 minute, dropping to 0.09%/min by 40 minutes).
  - In the first 2 hours, ~17.6% of initial carbon and ~11.4% of initial nitrogen were leached, with a leachate C:N ratio of ~40:1 — indicating that early losses are disproportionately carbohydrate-rich (soluble sugars, mannitol) rather than nitrogen-rich compounds.
  - Ash proportion appeared to increase upon re-drying due to seawater salt imbibition replacing leached organics — a purely physical, non-biological artifact.

- **Broader ecological implication:** Across all three macrophytes and four habitats, decomposition consistently drove **nitrogen enrichment and C:N ratio convergence**, supporting the hypothesis that the ultimate nutritional quality of detritus is governed more by the **nature of the decomposer community** (bacteria, fungi, and their microbial biomass) than by the original chemical composition of the source plant. This finding has downstream implications for invertebrate [[Aufwuchs]] grazers and the broader detrital energy pathway supporting higher trophic levels, including fish, in both freshwater and marine ecosystems.
