# Application of ERTS Data to Arid Regions: 1972 Progress Report

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #remote-sensing

## Executive Summary

This August 1972 progress report, prepared by the [[University of Nevada System]] for the U.S. Geological Survey's [[EROS Program]], evaluates how [[Earth Resources Technology Satellite]] (ERTS) data and small-scale aircraft multiband photography could support land, water and resource management in arid regions. Ten investigators from geology, hydrology, biology, limnology, forestry, range and wildlife management, urbanology and meteorology worked interdisciplinarily on two study sites. Because no actual ERTS imagery was yet available, conclusions rest on aircraft imagery degraded or extrapolated to ERTS scale and resolution.

The document is not a fish study. Its aquatic content concerns lake and reservoir monitoring (algal productivity, turbidity, shoreline change, delta dynamics), phreatophyte vegetation, and water quality indicators. Fish appear only briefly, in relation to delta condition and spawning access.

## Background & Description

**Project scope.** The study covered the arid regions of Nevada and adjacent states, with intensive review limited to two sites:

- **Reno/Lake Tahoe/Pyramid Lake site:** about 4,000 square miles of National Forest, Bureau of Land Management and private land along the [[Truckee River]], which links [[Lake Tahoe]] (a high mountain lake) to [[Pyramid Lake]] (a desert lake).
- **Las Vegas/Spring Mountains/Lake Mead site:** about 1,200 square miles in Clark County, Nevada, and Mohave County, Arizona, including the [[Las Vegas Wash]] outflow to [[Lake Mead]] on the [[Colorado River]].

**Satellite parameters (as described for ERTS-A).** The sun-synchronous orbit (about 500 nautical miles) gives repeat coverage every 18 days. Each frame covers roughly 100 nautical miles on a side at about 1:1,000,000, with estimated ground resolution of 200 to 300 feet. Sensors included three return-beam vidicon cameras (red, green, blue) and a multispectral scanner recording those bands plus near-infrared. Data-collection platforms could relay telemetered ground data every 12 hours, with availability to users within 24 hours.

**Imagery evaluated.** Aircraft data came from the NASA RB57F flights of July 1970 and October 1971, at scales from 1:432,000 (70 mm) to 1:113,000 (9-inch) and ranging to 1:50,000 in some supplementary flights. A 1969 frame at 1:250,000 was optically degraded to 1:800,000 to simulate ERTS quality.

**Interdisciplinary method.** Four steps were used: individual imagery review, a cross-discipline usefulness exchange, joint overlay analysis of selected frames, and a series of planning meetings. These produced an [[Interdisciplinary Operational System]] built around an [[Interdisciplinary Review Panel]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Tahoe.** The lake lies at 6,227 feet in a fault basin, measures 23 by 12 miles, covers 195 square miles and reaches over 1,600 feet deep. The basin contains 44 lakes and reservoirs and 44 perennial streams. Heavy recreation and land development are driving urbanization and pollution concerns. The report identifies sediment plumes from the Upper Truckee River, algal buildup in an "extremely clear" lake, and spring pollen windrows as monitoring targets.

**Pyramid Lake.** This terminal sink of the Truckee River is a remnant of prehistoric [[Lake Lahontan]], about 30 miles long and 7 to 8 miles wide, at roughly 3,800 feet. Its level has dropped about 80 feet since 1910 because of upstream diversions. The delta is rapidly expanding as the river downcuts to track the falling base level. The lake lies entirely within the Paiute Indian reservation. Annual precipitation at the lake is only 4 to 5 inches.

**Las Vegas Wash and Lake Mead.** One small perennial stream carries treated sewage effluent, irrigation runoff, groundwater seepage and industrial effluent to Lake Mead. This degrades recreational use and is a management concern. The report notes that nutrient inputs (phosphates and nitrates) appear to alter vegetation growth along the wash.

**Phreatophytes and groundwater.** Phreatophytes such as [[Cottonwood and Willow]], [[Saltcedar]], mesquite, cattail, alfalfa fields and [[Pickleweed]] serve as indicators of depth to water, water quality and discharge. The report uses phreatophyte distribution to delineate three groundwater availability units.

**Climate.** Pacific air masses dominate. Precipitation falls mainly from October to June, with snowpack from 20 to 50 inches of water content in higher elevations. Basin fog, inversions and summer monsoon thunderstorms are recurring issues.

## Key Findings

**1. Interdisciplinary value.** The report concludes that an interdisciplinary approach revealed resource management problems not apparent when scientists studied the photography individually. It also warns that interdisciplinary work tends to become merely multidisciplinary without constant administrative effort, and that the problem grows with the number of disciplines.

**2. Imagery scale and quality.** Small-scale color, color-infrared and multispectral imagery (1:432,000) was judged suitable for hazard and resource evaluation. Optically degraded 1:800,000 frames were generally inadequate because of poor quality, though they suggested ERTS-scale data could support general hazard assessment. Investigators assumed ERTS quality would be considerably better.

**3. Film and band preferences.** Relative usefulness differed by discipline:

- Color film was most valuable for geology, hydrology and limnology.
- Color-infrared was preferred for renewable natural resource and vegetation work, and for phreatophytes and groundwater recharge.
- Black and white film sufficed for cloud-type analysis in meteorology.
- Of the multiband records, the red band was most useful, the near-infrared band somewhat less so, and the blue band least useful.

**4. Limnology and water quality.** For algae, color film was optimal. Chlorophyll A correlated significantly with color chips on color photographs, and less strongly with color-infrared. The report notes that phytoplankton pigment signatures could indicate nutrient enrichment, and that density currents in Lake Mead could be tracked from imagery. Sequential imagery could also help assess how changes in input-water management affect phytoplankton distribution.

**5. Fish-related observation.** At Pyramid Lake, the report states that a large, broad delta suggests fish cannot get up the stream to spawn and may indicate heavy upstream erosion. Areas of consistently high planktonic algal concentration were flagged as potentially important for fishery management and angler decisions. Neither point is developed as a fish study.

**6. Repetition interval.** The 18-day repeat was judged adequate for vegetation, snowpack and long-term geology, but too coarse for daily weather forecasting, air pollution and rapid flood events. Meteorologists need more frequent imagery, while geologists need less. Biologists may need closer intervals during critical seasons or during storms that obscure cloud-covered terrain.

**7. Hazard and land-use planning.** The report recommends that hazard evaluation (debris flows, flash floods, landslides, fog, inversions) precede land-use decisions. It identified the proposed 48,000-person city in [[Warm Springs Valley]], which was later rejected by the Washoe County Commission, as a test case for using ERTS imagery to screen valleys for development.

**8. User identification.** Seminars in Reno (24 attendees) and Las Vegas (41 attendees) identified at least 32 potential user groups across federal and state agencies, universities and private industry. Most expressed immediate interest in available data and in future ERTS imagery.

**9. Operational system.** A recommended workflow includes:

- Assignment of a major discipline and project coordinator.
- Preliminary evaluation of existing imagery.
- Image acquisition, kept to a minimum.
- Ground data collection coordinated with overflights.
- Cost and time estimates for panel approval.
- Image analysis, publication and feedback into a ground-truth archive.

**10. Ground truth needs.** The report stresses that simultaneous ground data are essential. Thin cirrus clouds can seriously attenuate both color and infrared imagery, and meteorological ground truth is needed for all disciplines. Biology requires ground measurements of phenological stages and surface soil moisture during overflights.

**11. Statistical test.** One geology test compared the photographic expression of caliche with measured caliche thickness across six fan segments rated by five investigators. The Spearman rank correlation was not significant, so no relationship was established between photo expression and caliche thickness.
