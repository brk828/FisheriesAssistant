# Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District and the Gila River Channelization Dispute

#riparian-ecology #freshwater-ecology #water-management

*Note: The source is a journalistic feature on water policy and riparian ecology. It does not address fish, so the ichthyology and marine-biology tags are omitted and no fish species are reported.*

## Executive Summary

The article examines the [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District]] in southwest Arizona, which has received more than $500 million in federal and state subsidies since the late 1940s to address drainage, salinity and irrigation problems. The district now seeks funding for a [[Channelization Project]] on the [[Gila River]], with estimated costs of $20 million to $60 million. Federal agencies and the state Game and Fish Department favor the project, while the [[Environmental Protection Agency]] objects that it lacks adequate alternatives analysis and requires an [[Environmental Impact Statement]]. The author argues that the project would destroy a newly emerged riparian ecosystem created by the [[1993 Gila River Flood]], and that buying out farmland in the flood plain would be cheaper and more effective.

## Background & Description

The Wellton-Mohawk district lies on a narrow strip of riverfront land in the lower Gila River basin, described as one of the most productive farming areas in the world, with two or three crops a year. The district was created after World War II to deliver [[Colorado River]] water to farmland for returning veterans.

Key historical costs and facilities include:

- **Wellton-Mohawk canal system:** $56 million, completed in 1952.
- **Water allocation:** 300,000 acre-feet per year awarded free; the government later bought some rights back at $1,000 per acre-foot.
- **Drainage canal:** $45 million, built to carry salty groundwater back to the Colorado River.
- **[[Coachella Canal]] lining:** a $200 million-plus effort in California that reduced seepage, freeing water to meet the 1944 treaty obligation to Mexico. The article notes this solution was temporary.
- **[[Desalination Plant]]:** built from 1973 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to treat drainage water. Its total cost reached $256 million. Electricity for pumping was subsidized at $12.50 per acre-foot. Removing salt at full capacity was reported at $262 per acre-foot. The plant closed after only months of operation, and the district's drainage was later dumped into the Cienega de Santa Clara in Mexico.

The article also notes that the 1993 Gila River flood had been preceded by an $8 million channel and levee project, begun in 1986 and nearly complete when the flood struck.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

In January 1993, severe winter storms dropped record rain and snow on a 50,000-square-mile watershed. Flows into [[Painted Rock Dam]] reached a record 204,000 cubic feet per second on January 10, 1993. Peak flow in early March 1993 was 26,000 cubic feet per second, more than the district's levees and channels, which were designed to handle less than half that amount.

The flood inundated up to 16,000 acres of farmland for months, destroyed some [[Cottonwood and Willow Stands]] nurtured by the Game and Fish Department, and created new habitat. At [[Growler Ponds]], the river shifted course and carved deep ponds. A cattail marsh developed around them, attracting egrets, herons and osprey. Groundwater inflow keeps the area wetland even though river flows have dropped to a trickle.

Other observations:

- Unplanted fields on the south bank, about 45 miles east of Yuma, sprouted about a hundred cottonwood saplings, with nearby fields showing abundant willows.
- Wildlife manager John Kennedy of the Arizona Game and Fish Department described the potential for regeneration of a desert river ecosystem as unseen in more than 30 years on the lower Gila.
- The Game and Fish Department had spent millions over the previous decade on planting programs, which had "largely been failures."
- [[Salt Cedar]] thickets remain widespread in the understory.
- [[Robin Silver]] of the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity estimated that about 95 percent of natural river habitat has been lost.

The channelization project would affect more than 2,000 acres of streamside habitat. The article cites the EPA's view that a project affecting this much riparian habitat in a desert ecosystem requires thorough examination.

## Key Findings

- **Subsidy scale:** The district has received more than $500 million in government support, and critics argue much of it has been "largely squandered."
- **Flood-control proposal:** The district seeks approval for channelization at a cost of $20 million to $60 million. A later passage gives a range of $18 million to $54 million, depending on design. The article also says at least $20 million more would be added to the taxpayer bill.
- **Design flaw:** The article states the channel would fail if a flood less than half the size of the 1993 torrent came down the Gila.
- **Regulatory conflict:** The EPA, citing the Clinton administration's post-Mississippi flood recommendations, favors land acquisition over expensive levees and demands an Environmental Impact Statement. The Corps of Engineers had aimed to issue a permit by the end of October, but EPA objections have slowed it. Federal wildlife officials doubt the Corps will proceed over EPA objections.
- **Funding structure:** The federal government would pay 75 percent, the state 15 percent, and the district the balance. Funds are available only if environmental requirements are met.
- **Litigation threat:** A Phoenix-based environmental group has said it will sue if a permit is issued without an Environmental Impact Statement.
- **Mitigation claims:** The Game and Fish Department and the district say the project includes backwater wildlife areas and purchase of 1,900 acres of former farmland, including some newly forested areas.
- **Alternative proposed:** Former Colorado water commissioner Getches suggested buying out portions of the Wellton-Mohawk district to reduce salinity, which the author argues would also reduce or eliminate the need for channelization.
- **Disputed damage claims:** The author argues claims of tens of millions of dollars in crop losses are a myth. Most flooded fields suffered minor damage and recovered quickly. The district permanently lost about 1,900 acres to severe flood damage. The Arizona Farm Bureau's forecast of a $250 million loss was described as grossly overstated.
- **Conflict of interest:** [[Herb Guenther]], a Game and Fish Commission member, is also an administrative assistant for the district and strongly supports the project. Commissioners said they saw no conflict, and Guenther dismissed the concern.
- **Ecological opportunity:** The article concludes that the 1993 flood created a rare regeneration opportunity, and that a project that "wipes out" the new riparian habitat would undo it.
