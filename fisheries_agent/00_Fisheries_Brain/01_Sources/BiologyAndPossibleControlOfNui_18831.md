# Biology and Possible Control of Nuisance Caddisflies of the Upper Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydropsychidae

## Executive Summary

This Iowa Agricultural and Home Economics Experiment Station Research Bulletin (No. 483, September 1960) by Calvin R. Fremling examines the biology of hydropsychid caddisflies that create nuisance and health problems in [[Keokuk, Iowa]] on the [[Upper Mississippi River]]. Three species, [[Hydropsyche orris]], [[Cheumatopsyche campyla]] and [[Potamyia flava]], account for the problem. The study covers adult behavior, oviposition, larval habitat and net construction, generations per year, and possible control methods. The author concludes that localized control is feasible, with [[Malathion]] and [[Blacklight Traps]] as the most promising options, and that local larval reduction in the tailwaters would have little effect on fish.

## Background & Description

- **Nuisance impact:** Caddisflies swarm around city lights, blanket lighted store windows, enter restaurants and stores, and fly into faces and clothing. Some residents became hypersensitive and developed hay fever symptoms. Painting riverside houses during the season is impractical, and spider webs become laden with insects.
- **Comparable problems:** Similar nuisances have been reported from New Zealand, Africa, and along the Niagara River at [[Fort Erie, Ontario]], where a control program began in 1949.
- **Identification:** The three species are about 1/2 inch long and superficially resemble small moths. [[Hydropsyche orris]] and [[Cheumatopsyche campyla]] have diagonal black streaks on each antennal segment. *C. campyla* adults measure 8–8.5 mm and *H. orris* adults 9.5–10 mm. *P. flava* adults are uniformly straw-colored with antennae resembling bamboo canes.
- **Larval structures:** Hydropsychid larvae build silken tubes and catching nets on solid structures in swift currents. *H. orris* builds rigid nets, *C. campyla* and *P. flava* build loose, voluminous nets. Dense *H. orris* mats have obstructed cooling-water gratings at a hydroelectric plant.
- **Methods:** Adults were sampled with a nightly collecting pan at a lighted restaurant window, an underwater light trap, emergence traps, and a tandem float apparatus. Larvae were sampled from navigation buoys, rubble, turbine structures and a lotic aquarium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Current dependence:** Larvae are sedentary and depend on silt-free solid substrates and constant current to deliver food to their nets. *H. orris* occurs in the fastest water, while *C. campyla* dominates slower tailwater areas. *P. flava* is most common on sandy, silt-free stretches of the main channel.
- **Velocity and net size:** Net area is inversely related to water velocity. Faster-water *H. orris* needs less net area to filter the same volume of water.
- **Buoy habitat:** Over 100 navigation buoys and chains from Louisiana, Mo. to Burlington, Iowa, were examined in 1957–1958. *H. orris* was the principal trichopteran on them throughout this reach, with the limiting factor being a lack of silt-free attachment surfaces.
- **Dam structures:** Scroll chambers, turbine grills, leaking gates, and a drained lock pit supported large larval populations, with local velocity patterns evident from species distribution.
- **Fish interactions:** [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*) consumed caddisfly larvae extensively, with *P. flava* forming 68 percent of food volume in the examined stomachs. Per the cited work, *C. campyla* and *H. orris* are used little by fish.
- **Winter:** Larval net construction is curtailed in winter. Nets set in winter stayed free of larvae, whereas nets set in late spring or early fall were covered within about a week.

## Key Findings

**Species and abundance**
- The principal nuisance species are *H. orris*, *C. campyla* and *P. flava*. *C. campyla* was the most abundant caddisfly downtown, with *P. flava* and *H. orris* second and third. *H. orris* is the most troublesome species in the residential district, swarming along the river bluff.
- Window catches peaked at 1,963 insects on July 21, 1957, and at 316 on June 4, 1958.

**Life cycle and generations**
- All three species are bivoltine, with peaks in early and late summer. *H. orris* has at least five larval instars.
- Laboratory *C. campyla* completed egg-to-adult development in about 51 days. The pupal stage took about 6 days at 75–85 °F.

**Adult behavior**
- *H. orris* males form dusk swarms of up to a dense mass along the river bluff. Females are attacked by males in flight, and mating pairs descend to the ground or objects.
- Adults rest during the day in shaded shrubbery and on sheltered structures. Adult lifespan in captivity was up to 10 days for females and 13 days for males, with 15 days as the probable upper limit.

**Oviposition**
- Ovipositing females were collected underwater to 20 ft (*P. flava*) and 12 ft (*H. orris*, *C. campyla*). An experiment with a vertical dowel showed oviposition most concentrated at 3–5 ft depth.
- Emergence traps caught far more females than males. Light traps at the dam collected 15,690 female and 48 male *C. campyla* from July 21 to Aug. 8, 1958, indicating upstream migration of gravid females.

**Larval development and hatching**
- *P. flava* eggs hatched from Aug. 24 to Aug. 30, 1958. A *H. orris* batch hatched over 8 to 11 days, apparently without fertilization.
- First instar larvae can swim and drift. Drift and repopulation by larvae are considered likely.

**Control options**
- A [[Blacklight Traps]] design (20-watt blacklight over a 50-gallon drum with fuel oil) killed an estimated 625,000 to 800,000 caddisflies per trap per night. Materials cost less than $10.
- The blacklight (3,500 Å) was the best attractant tested, capturing more individuals than the germicidal and cool-white lights combined.
- [[Malathion]] was the fastest knock-down insecticide tested against male *H. orris*: LT50 5 minutes, complete kill 15 minutes, with 1-minute screen exposure killing all insects in 14 minutes. Parathion, Dibrom 8 and Diazinon followed.
- DDT granules in the tailwaters could be trapped by larval nets, but the author recommends pilot testing. Hydropsychid nets concentrate wettable powders by filtration.
- Replacing blue and green neon with red or yellow lighting was recommended to reduce attraction.
- Plastic-base antifouling paint on buoys kept larvae off treated surfaces.

**Ecological considerations**
- The author argues that losing tailwater caddisfly larvae would affect fish very little, because fish scarcely use hydropsychid larvae there.
- Repopulation would be rapid because of upstream migration and drifting larvae. *Cyrnellus marginalis* may act as a temporary pioneer species.
- Applications should be experimental and small-scale, and may need about one insecticide treatment per year.
