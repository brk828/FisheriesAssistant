# Physiological Genetics of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, a review by [[Myron Gordon]] from a 1957 academic volume on fishes, surveys the physiological processes in fishes for which genetic influences are known, though often incompletely understood. Its central focus is the poeciliid fishes of the genus [[Xiphophorus]] (platyfish and swordtails), with supporting evidence from guppies, medakas, killifishes, carp, trout, and cave fishes. Topics include sex determination mechanisms, pigment cell genes, atypical pigment growth and melanomas, eye characteristics, meristic traits, transplantation and disease resistance, thyroid tumors, and isolating mechanisms. The author concludes that fish genetics offers unique insights into vertebrate biology and argues for the use of inbred stocks in physiological research.

## Background & Description

The review opens by framing physiological genetics, a term adopted from [[Richard Goldschmidt]] (1938), as the study of how genes act in controlling adult development, distinct from classical gene transmission. Earlier fish genetics was dominated by claims that Mendelian methods had failed in fishes; the author rebuts this, citing early demonstrations of sex-linked inheritance in the [[Guppy]] (Schmidt 1920; Winge 1922) and the [[Japanese Medaka]] (Aida 1921).

Key taxa and model organisms include:
- **[[Platyfish]]** (*Xiphophorus maculatus*): exhibits both sex determination systems within one species.
- **[[Swordtail]]** (*X. helleri*) and **[[Montezuma Swordtail]]** (*X. montezumae*): used in hybridization and tumor studies.
- **[[Guppy]]** (*Lebistes reticulatus*): classic sex-linked color model.
- **Other fishes** discussed include killifish (*Fundulus heteroclitus*), brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), goldfish (*Carassius auratus*), sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), and cave characins (*Astyanax mexicanus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several sections address how environment interacts with genotype:
- **Temperature** affects vertebral and fin ray counts in killifish, guppies and sticklebacks, and influences spawning in trout.
- **Salinity and temperature** jointly modify fin ray counts in two [[Belgian Sticklebacks]] populations (freshwater population A and marine population B).
- **Iodine concentration** of aquarium water induces thyroid tumors in susceptible strains of swordtails and platyfish.
- **Natural distribution:** of more than 7,000 platyfish and swordtail specimens collected in sympatry in Mexico, British Honduras and Guatemala, no natural hybrids were found. In 10 localities swordtails occurred alone, in 5 platyfish alone, and in 10 together; swordtails dominated headwaters and platyfish lower reaches.
- **Cave environments** produce blindness in Mexican cave characins, with associated endocrine and body-form changes in eyed fish reared in darkness.
- **Population isolation:** Thompson's work on 17 Illinois populations of the Johnny darter (*Boleosoma nigrum*) and Heuts' work on sticklebacks illustrate genetic divergence under isolation.

## Key Findings

**Sex determination**
- Fishes show both XX-female/XY-male (guppy, medaka, Mexican platyfish) and WY-female/YY-male (British Honduras platyfish) systems; the dual system within a single species is described as unique among vertebrates.
- Sex-linked color genes (e.g., Sp, Sd, Sr, Ma) demonstrate "criss-cross" inheritance and crossing-over of sex chromosomes.
- Winge's proposal that autosomes can take over sex determination is rejected: XX males mated to XX females yielded a total of 1,229 males to 147 matings' females (ratio given as 1:8.3 total in source table, reported as dominated by females across years).
- In one Rio Jamapa mating, 183 offspring were all male; histology of 40 immature F1 fish confirmed male gonads, supporting genetic rather than environmental sex determination.
- Sex-determining mechanisms are judged stable in natural populations, while XX males and XY females are considered unstable.
- In platyfish-swordtail hybrids, Y-chromosome incompatibility is linked to sterility; F1 hybrid sex ratios were roughly 90% female and 10% male in one cross.

**Pigment cell genes and deleterious effects**
- Recessive golden and albino genes in swordtails cause partial lethality during late embryonic development; F2 embryo ratios approximated 9 gray : 3 yellow : 4 albino.
- Dominant gene Fu (fuligonosus) in platyfish is lethal in homozygotes (FuFu fish do not survive beyond 35 days, per Gordon and Baker 1955).
- The scaleless gene N in carp is lethal when homozygous (NN).
- Comet-wagtail pattern results from interaction of a platyfish gene (Co) with a swordtail modifier (E).

**Melanomas and genic imbalance**
- Melanomas occur in ten orders of elasmobranchs and teleosts in nature.
- Melanomas in platyfish-swordtail hybrids require macromelanophores (sex-linked dominant gene from platyfish) combined with swordtail growth-modifying genes; in backcrosses about half of the offspring developed melanomas, despite identical prenatal and postnatal environments.
- Spotted platyfish from roughly 20% of natural populations carry these macromelanophore genes.
- Intraspecific interracial hybrids (Rio Jamapa x Rio Coatzacoalcos) showed melanosis, paralleling interspecific hybrids.
- Penetrance of the Sd gene was 100% in nine experiments with Rio Jamapa platyfish, but fell to 91.3% in Jamapa x Coatzacoalcos hybrids and to about 1% in Jamapa x Belize River hybrids.
- Erythromelanomas arose from linked Rt and Sp genes on the same sex chromosome.

**Eye characteristics**
- A cataract was traced over five generations in platyfish, with the cataractal individual almost blind; distribution was erratic (Table IV: 4 cataracts among 844 normal-eyed third-generation fish from 10 matings; Table V: 20 cataracts among 115 fish from one brother-sister mating).
- Ocular melanomas were tentatively attributed to a recessive gene (oc) interacting with albino (i).
- Flicker response differences between platyfish and swordtail are attributed to genetic factors affecting rods and cones.

**Meristic traits**
- Vertebral numbers in killifish (*Fundulus*) are reduced at 24.5 °C and increased at 18.8 and 13.5 °C, while high parental counts associate with high offspring counts.
- In guppies, selection for dorsal fin rays shifted the mean from 7.05 to 6.465 over five generations (Svärdson 1945).
- Sticklebacks show inverse temperature responses in fresh water but not in saline water for population A.

**Transplantation, resistance and temperature tolerance**
- Autografts and homografts of fins succeeded in one platyfish strain inbred 19 generations (Kallman and Gordon 1957).
- Selective breeding of brook trout reduced infection mortality from 98% to 30.8% in three generations (Embody and Hayford 1926).
- In Wolf's trials, 9 of 11 brook trout strains lost 74 to 100% of fish to disease; the most resistant strain lost 5%.

**Thyroid tumors**
- Thyroid tumors arise under low iodine in water. Montezuma swordtail strain 38 developed tumors about 5 months after birth, strain 43 at the end of a year, and wild swordtails less rapidly.
- Platyfish strain "30" (inbred 20 generations) showed no tumors, while subline "163" (inbred from 7 generations) developed kidney tumors.

**Isolating mechanisms**
- Platyfish and swordtails rarely hybridize in nature; psychological and courtship differences, plus ecological separation, appear to form a combined barrier.
- Median time to first copulation was about 5 minutes in platyfish and 1 minute in swordtails; insemination success was 86.0% for platyfish and 39.4% for swordtails.

**Conclusion and recommendation**
- Genetic factors influence many physiological processes, so the author recommends that physiologists use genetically uniform (inbred) fish for critical experiments. Inbred stocks are noted in the platyfish (20 generations), swordtail (14 generations) and guppy (over 25 years of mass culture).
