# Bonytail Propagation and Handling Protocols for Permit Application

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a draft permit-application framework, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Southwest Regional Director, governing the collection, handling, tagging, sampling, hatchery propagation, transport, health management and disposal of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a federally endangered fish. A consortium of agencies and institutions, including the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Department of Game and Fish]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Arizona State University]], would operate under a shared lead-station structure. The protocols are intended to reduce stress-related losses, which the authors note have occurred in the past, and to ensure that all permit holders follow consistent, peer-accepted techniques.

The document is primarily a procedural framework with many blank fields (marked "_" or "Y/N" in the scanned form); quantitative hatchery parameters are largely unfilled.

## Background & Description

The document comprises an administrative checklist for the lead station, followed by experimental design requirements, biological procedures, fish culture guidelines, broodstock genetic and health management, fish disposal rules, fish health protocols, and four field and laboratory checklists (field collection, tagging and sampling, genetic sampling, and hauling truck).

Bonytail are described as "extremely sensitive to handling." The protocols therefore prohibit non-essential activities such as photography of fish held out of water and "show and tell" displays. All personnel handling bonytail must be trained, and individuals who do not meet minimum qualifications are classed as trainees who work only under the direct supervision of a qualified biologist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The protocols specify water-quality targets for holding, transport and culture:

- **Salinity and anesthesia:** 0.5% NaCl (18.9 g per gallon) in holding and transport water; MS-222 at 25–100 mg/L (95–378 mg per gallon) for anesthesia; Chloramine-T at 0.8 mg/L; Stress Coat at 0.26 mL/L.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** maintained above 6.0 mg/L in holding and culture; saturation required before loading and transport.
- **Hatchery culture:** pH 6.5–7.5; hardness 150–300 mg/L; gas saturation below 102–103%; ammonia and nitrite kept below toxic levels.
- **Temperature:** water temperature during handling kept at or slightly below receiving-water temperature (below 55 °F stated for out-of-water handling).
- **Stocking density:** holding containers at least 30 gallons; fish density not to exceed 100 g/L in collection holding; 30 g/L for bags; 20 g/L for coolers; broodstock not to exceed 10 kg/L (as written).

Water-source and disease-risk considerations include disinfection of parasitic water supplies by UV irradiation or ozone, and the recommendation that aeration rely on spring or dechlorinated tap water supplemented with agitators and oxygen.

## Key Findings

**Administrative prerequisites.** Before a permit is recommended, the lead station requires planning protocols, investigator contact lists, lines of communication for collections, fish transport and hatchery operations, schedules, and a record of permits and previous work. Many checklist items remain unmarked.

**Experimental justification.** Proposals must address research rationale and priority, whether the action is sanctioned by a recovery plan, project history, literature review, anticipated problems, corrective actions and risk assessment.

**Collection methods.** All bonytail are to be collected non-lethally. Seines and trammel nets must be checked at least every four hours, with bonytail removed first and cut from nets. Electrofishing must use settings not exceeding 1.0 A average, based on local water quality. Angling and toxicants are prohibited.

**Tagging, marking and sampling.** Only anesthetized fish may be tagged or marked. PIT tags are placed in the abdomen, and fin clips are cauterized and treated with Betadine. Fish out of water must not exceed 30 seconds. Tissue samples (blood, fin clips, muscle plugs, gametes) are preserved for genetic and microchemical analysis; no tissue is taken from live fish smaller than 150 mm TL. Carcasses of deceased fish are to be frozen, shipped to ASU, and tissues (gonads, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, muscle, otoliths) collected.

**Transport.** Three transport methods are described: truck tanks (density up to 30 g/L), plastic bag shipping in boxes (up to 30 g/L, fish up to 300 mm TL), and insulated ice coolers (20 g/L). Food is withheld 24 hours before transport for fish under 10 g and 48 hours for larger fingerlings and broodfish. Twelve hours of back-up aeration or oxygen must be available.

**Hatchery and broodstock.** Bonytail are reared separately from other species. Broodstock are held in earthen ponds or cement raceways, fed a Biodiet supplemented with spirulina flakes and rainbow trout young-of-year twice annually, and inventoried monthly. Spawning uses LHRHa injections, with milt stored at 4 °C and fertilization in porcelain bowls. Gaps in the form (e.g., egg incubation densities, flow rates, feeding rates for fry) are left blank for the permit applicant to complete.

**Surplus fish and disposal.** Surplus bonytail are not to be released into historical habitats unless taken from the wild and released following capture, or produced for approved stocking projects. Surplus fish may be provided to requestors who bear the cost of preservation or maintenance, or euthanized with MS-222 at 500 mg/L. Planned production aims to minimize surplus; surplus fish are used for public education. Voucher specimens and incidental take go to the ASU ichthyological museum.

**Fish health.** The Regional Fish Health Center must review protocols and conduct risk assessments for any introduction of bonytail to a hatchery or reintroduction to the wild. Quarantine is required until a risk assessment is completed. Prophylactic treatments may include nitrofurazone and praziquantel, and the Regional Director has indicated that exemptions may be given on a case-by-case basis.

**Recovery context.** Once bonytail are downlisted from endangered to threatened, excess fish may be stocked in suitable habitats. Disposal must follow federal and state law and modified [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] guidelines.
