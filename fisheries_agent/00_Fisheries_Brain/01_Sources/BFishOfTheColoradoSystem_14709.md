# Fish of the Colorado System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by J. A. Stanford and J. V. Ward describes the indigenous fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] system, how that fauna arose through [[Quaternary Climate Change]] and desertification, and how [[Flow Regulation]] and [[Exotic Species]] have reduced it. The system holds 32 native fish species in seven families, or 46 native taxa including subspecies before regulation. Of these, 85% are endemic. The authors argue that the big-river fauna was structurally simple and depended on [[Backwaters]] and marshes, that most big-river natives now face extinction, and that more than 50 exotic fish are established in the basin. The chapter closes by identifying the regulated system as a natural testbed for ecological theory.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with archaeological evidence. Abundant remains of large minnows in caves near desert reaches suggest that riverine fish were an important food source for prehistoric people. The authors contrast this with the present, when fish supply to people has changed sharply because of regulation and introductions.

The native fauna comprises 32 species in seven families. Several have diversified into isolated local populations, recognised as subspecies or forms:

- [[Colorado River Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): four forms, in the Gila, Virgin and Pluvial White rivers.
- Spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*): two subspecies, in the Virgin River and Meadow Valley Wash.
- Desert sucker (*Catostomus clarki*): four subspecies in Lower Basin tributaries.
- [[White River Springfish]] (*Crenichthys baileyi*): five subspecies in desert springs.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): the most widespread native species, with four subspecies in the Lower Basin and two in the Upper Basin.
- [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki pleuriticus*): native to headwaters of virtually all Upper Basin tributaries.

The big-river mainstem fauna comprised six endemic minnows and suckers: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) was limited to the Lower Gila and Virgin rivers, and the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularis*) was found mainly in backwaters and marshes. Several euryhaline marine species, including tenpounder (*Elops affinis*), spotted sleeper (*Elotris picta*) and striped mullet (*Mugil cephalus*), used the estuary and delta. Only striped mullet was abundant in the main channel.

The chapter also notes that squawfish may reach 1.5–2.0 m and 35–45 kg, making it one of the world's largest minnows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography and endemism.** Endemism is high (85%, or 38 of 46 taxa, by present taxonomy; 66% at the species level). The authors attribute this to [[Pluvial and Interpluvial Cycles]] in the intermountain West, which isolated populations in desert basins. Many genera were present in the Pliocene, radiated in Pleistocene drainages, and were then fragmented by desertification. Using [[Island Biogeography]] theory, [[Smith 1978]] reported that 51 of 81 intermountain native fish occurred in only one sub-basin. The authors conclude that the current fauna reflects local extinction from a Pleistocene fauna more than recent colonisation. Thirty-five percent of the Colorado fauna occurs only in the Virgin–Pluvial White system, and another 20% is endemic to the Gila drainage. Of the 38 endemics, only nine occur upstream of the Little Colorado River.

**Upper versus Lower Basin.** The Lower Basin fauna is diverse, while the Upper Basin fauna is depauperate. Of 14 Upper Basin taxa, eight occur in adjacent drainages such as the Snake River. The authors attribute the contrast to the Pleistocene [[Lake Hualapai]] near the mouth of the Grand Canyon, and to the greater isolating effect of the xeric Lower Basin. A latitude–altitude gradient also runs through the system. Northern headwater fish are more stenothermic than southern ones. *Salmo gilae* tolerates late-summer temperatures to 27°C in the Gila drainage, while *S. clarki pleuriticus* is found in waters well below 20°C.

**Big-river trophic ecology.** The big-river food web was probably simple. Sandy, unstable substrates held little zoobenthos, so fish foraged in stable areas such as debris dams, rubble riffles and canyon rapids. Backwaters served as refugia, nurseries and feeding areas for all big-river species, and they were reworked and refilled during spates. Radio-tagged squawfish and razorback suckers moved between mainstream and backwater. The authors argue that the river alone could not support large populations of big fish, so backwaters likely subsidised the river's food base through detritus and plankton production.

**Desert adaptations.** Desert fish tolerate temperatures of 35–45°C, salinities up to about 90 g l⁻¹ and drought. [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) persists beneath moist algal mats and can repopulate a stream within weeks. Fish in ephemeral streams exploit food pulses after re-watering, while fish in perennial desert springs depend on a limited but reliable food supply. Extreme flooding or prolonged drought can still eliminate populations, as happened to an introduced [[Gila Topminnow]] population.

**Impacts of regulation.** More than one-quarter of the river has been converted to lentic habitat by impoundment (Bishop & Porcella 1980). Regulated flows have armoured tailwater beds, and most backwaters and marshes no longer receive water. Thermal regimes and trophic structure have changed, and groundwater pumping has dried many desert streams and springs. The authors note that past climate change was comparable in severity, but native fish never before faced many highly competitive exotic species, some carrying diseases.

**Exotic species and hybridisation.** Exotic fish prey on native eggs and juveniles and compete for space and spawning sites. [[Red Shiner]] replaced the Gila spinedace, [[Mosquitofish]] replaced the Gila topminnow and desert pupfish, and largemouth bass and sailfin mollies replaced Moapa dace and White River springfish. Hybridisation has swamped some native gene pools, such as rainbow × cutthroat trout, and native hybridisation, such as razorback × flannelmouth sucker, adds to range restriction.

**Reservoirs and tailwaters.** Exotics have been especially successful in reservoirs and closely regulated reaches. Lower Basin reservoir fisheries depend on planktivorous [[Threadfin Shad]], which feeds [[Largemouth Bass]] and striped bass. The [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] predicts that natives should re-establish some distance below dams, but the authors doubt that enough free-flowing river remains for this.

## Key Findings

- **Native diversity is high but declining.** Of 32 native freshwater species, one is extinct and 15 persist only in sparse populations. Fewer than 8,000 *Salmo gilae* remain in their native habitat. Only a few isolated canyon or unregulated tributary habitats (e.g., Yampa Canyon, White River) still hold a pristine-type assemblage.
- **Big-river natives face extinction.** All native big-river fish except speckled dace and flannelmouth sucker face extinction. Humpback chub, speckled dace, bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker and perhaps razorback sucker remain in Grand Canyon. Razorback suckers are established in Lake Mohave, but bonytail chub there are apparently not reproducing.
- **Exotic dominance.** More than 50 exotic fish are established, led by carp, threadfin shad, rainbow trout and largemouth bass. Table 2 lists the reproducing introductions by family, habitat and distribution. The authors expect further introductions and widely fluctuating populations as competition reshapes the community.
- **Regulation reshapes fisheries.** Some tailwater fisheries are productive. Trout growth below some dams exceeds 38–40 cm annually. At the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] tailwater, a fishery collapsed under 4°C releases after the reservoir filled (maximum depth 134 m). Multi-level release gates were installed in 1978 to hold summer tailwater near 13°C, and trout growth then increased three- to four-fold.
- **Lee Ferry fishery.** Clear, nutrient-rich hypolimnetic water from [[Lake Powell]] supports the green alga *Cladophora glomerata*, which sustains introduced amphipods (*Gammarus lacustris*) that feed fast-growing trout. This is a very different trophic system from the pre-impoundment river.
- **Research agenda.** The authors call for studies of reservoir and tailwater processes, the life histories of forage organisms, and the controls on primary productivity. They also propose using the regulated Colorado system as a macrocosm for testing organism- and ecosystem-level theory.
- **Conservation caution.** For some forms, survival may depend on artificial propagation. Because the system is so altered, questions about the ecology of the unregulated river are described as "largely academic."
