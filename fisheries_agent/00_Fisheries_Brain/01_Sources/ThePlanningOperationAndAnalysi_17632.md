# The Planning, Operation, and Analysis of the Green River Fish Control Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae #rotenone-treatment

## Executive Summary

This joint Utah–Wyoming report documents the planning, execution and post-treatment analysis of a 1962 chemical rehabilitation of roughly 450 miles of the [[Green River]] and its tributaries above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The project aimed to remove non-desirable fish, chiefly [[Carp]], suckers and chubs, before impoundment so that a [[Sport Fishery]] could develop in the new reservoir. Treatment used [[Rotenone]] (Chem-Fish Regular, 21,495 gallons, cost $76,307.25). A [[Detoxification Station]] at Brown's Park Bridge, using [[Potassium Permanganate]], was built to protect [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Fish kills occurred within the monument despite this, and the report concludes that native species were not lost, while invertebrate fauna were almost completely destroyed.

## Background & Description

- **Project scope:** Joint effort of the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]], [[Utah Department of Fish and Game]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife), with Colorado Game and Fish participation. Funded under Section 8 of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] Act at a total cost of $106,000.
- **Trigger:** Closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (November 1962, 495 ft high, 3,930,000 acre-feet, about 42,000 surface acres at full pool) and the planned [[Fontenelle Reservoir]] (8,000 surface acres) were expected to cause population explosions of carp and suckers, as seen at [[Boysen Reservoir]] and [[Pathfinder Reservoir]].
- **Precedent:** Earlier rotenone programs on the [[North Platte River]] (Glendo and Pathfinder) were cited as encouraging.
- **Fishing demand:** Projected use of 120,000–150,000 fisherman-days initially, rising to 185,000–228,000 by 1975. The 1959 estimate of $643,200 in annual expenditure was later revised to at least $991,600 by 1975.
- **Opposition:** Some groups argued treatment threatened native swift-water species: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]]. Surveys verified their presence outside the treatment area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physical setting:** Lower river runs through [[Bridger Formation]] and [[Wasatch Formation]] sediments with highly erosive soils. Precipitation is 13 inches annually at the headwaters and 8 inches on the lower section. Flows range from about 12,000 cfs in spring runoff to about 450 cfs in average low water near Flaming Gorge.
- **Temperature:** Air from −50°F to 100°F; water 32°F to 75°F. Pre-treatment water chemistry (Big Sandy River): pH 8.1–8.2, total alkalinity 170–240 ppm.
- **Habitat:** Good trout habitat extended from the upper project area to Green River town; below it, siltation and temperature limited game fish. Non-game species (suckers, chubs, carp) dominated the fish community.
- **Rotenone decay:** Laboratory data (Post, 1956) gave 1 ppm breakdown in 42 hours at 78.5°F, 3 days at 64°F and 20 days at 44.5°F. The field-derived working rate was about 1 ppm per 16 river miles.
- **Public water supplies:** Fontenelle, Green River (serving Rock Springs, about 15,000 people), and Dutch John drew from the treated reach. [[U.S. Public Health Service]] monitored taste, odor and toxicity, and used [[Activated Carbon]] where needed.

## Key Findings

**Treatment design and operation**
- Stations were set at about 10-mile intervals (38 station sites on the New Fork, East Fork, Green, Big Sandy, Blacks Fork and Henry's Fork rivers). Each was run for 7 hours (6 on tributaries), with downstream stations starting at 3-hour intervals and a stream velocity assumed at 1 mph.
- The target was 5 ppm formulation. The pilot test (August 1961, about 700 cfs) reached only about 3.5 ppm, prompting a refined one-inch hose system with gate and gas valves.
- Airboats treated backwaters; a [[Helicopter]] treated isolated sloughs and the Blacks Fork, where flows fell from over 1000 cfs to under 10 cfs.
- Treatment began 4 September 1962 and finished on 7 September. A re-treatment of the upper 10 miles of the New Fork followed on 9 September.
- Public relations included landowner letters, lectures and a lifted creel limit with salvage of trout by dry ice.

**Flow and dosing errors**
- Final gauge-based flow estimates were higher than actual flows. Flows on 6 September were 19–24% below original calculations, which increased rotenone concentrations and complicated detoxification.
- Helicopter release control was inadequate on the Blacks Fork, causing excessive concentrations.

**Rotenone concentration and movement**
- Field bio-assay at three-quarters of a mile below Green River Station 1 (4 September) gave concentrations ranging from 3.9 to 7.3 ppm across eight samples, with a reading of 5.5 ppm noted on the east bank.
- Rotenone above Station 9 arrived close to schedule. Below Station 9 it ran up to 3¾ hours early. On the New Fork it moved more slowly, possibly leaving an untreated zone.
- Live-cage survival times were 1 to 4 hours after initial contact.

**Fish kill and species composition**
- The tentative checklist (Table 4) recorded 18 species, with flannelmouth sucker and mountain whitefish the most abundant. Only five squawfish were taken, and no humpback chub. Bonytail chub was numerous.
- Trout were extremely infrequent above the dam and absent below it.

**Water chemistry**
- No effect of treatment on water chemistry was found. pH ranged 7.8–8.6, methyl orange alkalinity rose from 91–100 to 135–142 ppm (CaCO₃) over three weeks, and temperatures ranged 52–64°F during treatment.

**Post-treatment fauna**
- Pre-treatment invertebrate groups (including Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera and Diptera) were almost completely destroyed. Most groups reappeared in collections, but none had regained their former distribution by 1 March 1963.
- Seismic work (275 underwater detonations, September–November 1962) observed no fish.

**Detoxification at Brown's Park**
- The station was sited 31 river miles below the dam, about 16 miles above Dinosaur National Monument, with [[Potassium Permanganate]] chosen over calcium hypochlorite on cost, safety and handling grounds. The ratio used was 2.3 parts permanganate per part rotenone.
- The supply was 13,860 pounds ordered, but a further 3,200 pounds were delivered, totaling 17,160 pounds expended. Operation lasted over 83 hours.
- Crystal quality problems (about 20% passing a 30-mesh screen) and gravity-flow dispensers caused stoppages. Ratios had to be reduced to 1:1.4–1:1.5 to conserve supply, which let rotenone escape briefly and killed trout and sunfish at live-cage sites C and D.
- Official assessment: rotenone was eliminated for over 95% of the operation, with reduced amounts passed for the remainder.

**Decay rates**
- Natural decay was non-constant: drip station #10 decayed at 7.2 miles per ppm over the first 37 miles but 28 miles per ppm after 163.5 miles. The minimum average rate was roughly 24–28 miles per ppm.

**Dinosaur National Monument**
- Fish in distress were reported 13–15 September at Echo Park and Split Mountain Campground, mainly channel catfish. Travel-time analysis suggested the causal toxicant passed the detoxification site 12–36 hours after operations ended, so detoxification failure was considered unlikely.
- Post-disturbance surveys (September 1962 and April 1963, Table 10) found native species present. Table 11 shows that bonytail chub and possibly bluehead sucker may have been reduced.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The report recommended better and faster rotenone-detection methods, since bio-assay and colorimetric tests took 1.5–2 hours.
- Flow figures must be recalculated daily during treatment when detoxification is planned.
- Helicopter release controls should be refined.
- The authors concluded that large-scale detoxification is feasible and repeatable.
