# Holy Joe Ranch Feasibility Study and Aravaipa Creek Conservation Partnership

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #native-fishery

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1987 preliminary feasibility study by Don and Bev Geldmacher with a January 1988 [[The Nature Conservancy]] memo. Together they assess whether [[Holy Joe Ranch]] on the west end of [[Aravaipa Creek]] should become public parkland. The ranch has significant water rights and sits next to the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]]. Its main ecological value is protecting the creek's native fishery, which includes two endangered species. On December 31, 1987, the owners gave 41 acres and half of their water right to The Nature Conservancy, which agreed to facilitate a multi-agency partnership. The memo also notes that the water right is important because it protects instream flow for the creek's native fish.

## Background & Description

**Property and history.** The ranch has been under surface-water irrigation since Patrick Cashion settled it after the 1871 [[Aravaipa Massacre]]. Cashion filed the earliest water claim on the west end on April 8, 1887. Ownership passed to Harmon Higgins (1905), Joseph Baker (1920), and the Geldmachers (1963). The authors describe the ranch as the last remaining property of sufficient size and accessibility on the west end that could be considered for parkland.

**Land division (as of the January 1988 memo).** The total holding was 197 acres:
- **41 acres** of streamside bottomland were gifted to The Nature Conservancy, along with stock ponds.
- **79 acres** were proposed for sale to agencies, bringing the total for sale to 120 acres south of Aravaipa Road.
- **80+ acres** were retained north of the road, including the home and a **2,000-acre state lease**.

**Valuation.** A 1985 appraisal valued the land at $940,000. A 1985 estimate valued the 3,560 acre-feet water right at between $1,600,000 and $3,200,000. The owner's 1988 estimate for the whole ranch was $2.1 million. The itemized breakdown lists the gift as "13 acres," which conflicts with the 41 acres stated elsewhere, and the listed gift components do not sum to the stated $856,000 total. These discrepancies should be checked against the original records.

**Improvements.** The gifted acreage includes an unoccupied bunkhouse, sheds, barns, and a modern system of berms, riprap, ditches, and PVC spreading structures that irrigate crops and nut trees. The remaining 79 acres include a roping arena, irrigation headworks, and pasture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishery.** [[Aravaipa Creek]] is a perennial stream and the largest tributary of the [[San Pedro River]]. It supports what the memo calls the healthiest native fishery in Arizona: seven native species, including two endangered species, [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spike Dace]]. The ranch lies about five miles below the wilderness boundary and nine miles above the San Pedro confluence. The creek was described as having an internationally recognized zoological reputation, and the area supports about 300 vertebrate species. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) considers the creek extremely important native-fish habitat.

**Threats.** The 1978 and 1983 floods caused heavy losses and stream scour throughout the watershed. Exotic fish are a major concern. In February 1986 the owner applied to stock fathead minnow, largemouth bass, channel catfish, and tilapia in the ponds. AGFD denied the permit, two appeals were denied, and AGFD obtained a temporary injunction to stop stocking. The study identifies three ponds on the deeded land as sources of exotic species, and it proposes possible barriers to prevent upstream migration of exotics from the San Pedro River.

**Wildlife.** The area supports a successful, expanding herd of [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]], which uses Brandenburg Mountain as a favorite pasture. Great blue and little blue herons nest at the corrals. A 55,000-acre [[Bureau of Land Management]] buffer zone adjoins the state lease lands.

**Water.** The owners hold a claim of about 1,800 acre-feet, with an appurtenant right of 200 "Miner's Inches," or 3,560 acre-feet, per year. This equals 4.9 cfs, roughly the average annual flow of the creek. The claim is the earliest filed on the creek (1887) and is the lowest of 12 diversions serving 78 irrigated acres. Its senior priority gives it significant leverage over upstream use. The right is still under study in the San Pedro adjudication by the [[Department of Water Resources]].

## Key Findings

**1. Proposed potentials by agency.**

- **Pinal County:** provide recreation for county residents (especially elderly and disabled people), encourage tourism, offer education, maintain natural beauty, retain the 3,560 acre-foot water right, and provide sport fishing. The county has heavy equipment and labor for construction but lacks parks staff.
- **State universities:** study archaeology, anthropology, botany, zoology, paleontology, geology, sociology, and history; provide land-planning expertise.
- **State Parks:** develop state lease lands for recreation while protecting resources, and develop the [[Aravaipa Massacre]] site, including protection of the gravesite of El Capitan Chiquito.
- **AGFD:** protect the native fishery by removing exotic species from the ponds, restock natives for sport fishing, provide hunter access, and support non-game viewing.
- **BLM:** guarantee 4.9 cfs of instream flow through the wilderness (about one-third of the flow requested in the wilderness management plan), provide jeep, equestrian, and hiker access to buffer lands, and create a backup location for visitors without wilderness permits. The wilderness limit is 30 people per day from the west end.

**2. Alternatives.** The study considered three models: a county-run park, a state-run park, or a joint county-state park. It concluded that local use would dominate creekside lands, so the county would be best placed to manage them, while the state agencies have planning and resource-management staff. A joint county-state park was judged best to balance recreation and resource protection.

**3. Funding sources identified.** These include the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]], the President's Outdoor Recreation Fund, the Arizona Lakes and Parks Improvement Fund, the Arizona Parklands Foundation, private conservation groups (The Nature Conservancy, Audubon Society, Sierra Club), possible remaining funds from the post-1983 flood transportation bill for the Aravaipa Canyon road, and county in-kind contributions. The Conservancy's memo says water rights converted to instream flow still hold market value for LWCF matching, since they can be converted back to consumptive use.

**4. The Nature Conservancy partnership.** TNC accepted the gift on a pass-through basis after receiving positive expressions from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, AGFD, and Pinal County. TNC made no warranties that agreements could be reached. It committed to facilitate discussion and reserved the right to dispose of the property if no satisfactory agreement is reached by December 1989. Options for the gifted water right include conversion to minimum instream flow, dedication to a native-fish refugium in the ponds, or conversion to municipal rights under joint recreational use with Pima County.

**5. Agency positions.**
- [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]: approved the concept as a potential refugium for endangered species; the water right alone warrants further exploration.
- AGFD (Director Temple Reynolds, letter of December 17, 1987): strongly favors maintaining native-fish habitat. First steps would be chemical treatment of the ponds and increased instream flow. AGFD offered to work with all parties.
- [[Pinal County]]: three commissioners agreed to study the proposal at their December 21 meeting. The county was encouraged to seek a combined $15,000 for a comprehensive park plan.

**6. Concerns.**
- The owner needs to sell the 79-acre parcel quickly. He pays $2,604 per month on a construction loan used for flood repairs and considers TNC's two-year timeline unacceptable.
- TNC cannot provide interim management, so the memo proposes interim management agreements with cooperating agencies.
- Neighbors have raised concerns about public involvement, visitor impact, traffic, law enforcement, county costs, and flooding.

**7. Timeline.** Response booklets were due to Pinal County by December 1, 1987. The Conservancy memo was circulated on January 11, 1988. The partnership deadline is December 1989.
