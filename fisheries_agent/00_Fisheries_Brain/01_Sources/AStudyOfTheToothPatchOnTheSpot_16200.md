# A Study of the Tooth Patch on the Spotted Black Bass (Micropterus punctulatus)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Centrarchidae #taxonomy #morphology

## Executive Summary

This 1959 thesis by Judith A. Loring examines the reliability of the tooth patch on the tongue as a diagnostic character for identifying [[Micropterus punctulatus]] (spotted black bass). The study analyzed specimens from Kentucky and surrounding states, comparing tooth patch presence across subspecies of M. punctulatus and other [[Micropterus]] species. The research found that while the tooth patch is present in M. punctulatus subspecies, it is not a reliable distinguishing character where ranges overlap with [[Micropterus coosae]] and [[Micropterus notius]], as these species also exhibit tooth patches in some or all specimens.

## Background & Description

[[Micropterus punctulatus]] was originally described by Rafinesque in 1819 in the Ichthyologia Ohiensis as *Callurus Punctulatus* (meaning "Dotted Painted Tail"). Hubbs and Bailey (1940) assumed the spelling was a printing error for "punctulatus." The species was later redescribed by Hubbs (1927) as *Micropterus pseudaplites* with the common name "Kentucky bass." By 1931, the distribution was known to extend beyond Kentucky, and Hubbs suggested changing the common name to "spotted bass." In 1948, the American Fisheries Society adopted "spotted black bass" as the official common name.

The tooth patch on the tongue was mentioned by Hubbs (1927) and Hubbs and Viosca (1931), but prior to this study, identification relied primarily on color pattern, relative height of dorsal spines, and pyloric caeca. This research was undertaken to determine whether the tooth patch could serve as a more reliable diagnostic character, particularly in Kentucky where distinguishing M. punctulatus from other black basses had proven difficult.

Four subspecies of M. punctulatus are recognized within the range from the [[Ohio River]] system south to the Gulf states and northward through Texas to Kansas and Oklahoma.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Specimens were collected from diverse freshwater habitats across Kentucky and adjacent states, including:

- **Rivers**: [[Ohio River]], [[Cumberland River]], [[Kentucky River]], [[Green River]]
- **Creeks**: Cane Run, Wilson Creek, Harrods Creek, Floyd's Fork, Drake's Creek, Jenning's Creek, Martin's Fork, Blood River, Yellow Creek, Lagan Run, Crocus Creek, Goose Creek, Rolling Fork River, Salt Lick Creek, Lusk Creek, Massac Creek
- **Lakes**: Dewey Lake, [[Kentucky Lake]], [[Lake Cumberland]], [[Cumberland Lake]]

Additional specimens examined from Illinois (Pope County, Hardin County, Massac Creek) and Indiana (Crawford County, Harrison County, Dearborn County, Ohio County).

The study compared M. punctulatus with four other recognized [[Micropterus]] species:
- **[[Micropterus coosae]]** (Redeye bass): Restricted to southeastern streams from Alabama to Savannah, generally in uplands
- **[[Micropterus dolomieui]]** (Smallmouth bass): West to eastern Kansas and Oklahoma, south to northern Alabama, north to Minnesota and Quebec
- **[[Micropterus notius]]** (Suwannee bass): Ichthucknee Springs in northern Florida
- **[[Micropterus salmoides]]** (Largemouth bass): North Carolina to southeastern Canada throughout Great Lakes region, southward through Mississippi Valley to Mexico and Florida

## Key Findings

### Tooth Patch Analysis

**Presence/Absence by Species:**
- **M. punctulatus**: Tooth patch present in examined subspecies
- **M. coosae**: Tooth patch present in approximately 50% of specimens, absent in the other 50%
- **M. notius**: Tooth patch present in all examined specimens
- **M. salmoides**: Tooth patch entirely absent
- **M. dolomieui**: Tooth patch absent except in one specimen

**Size Analysis**: No apparent divergence shown by the size of the tooth patch when comparing species (Figure 1). Tooth patch measurements were taken with dividers and expressed as proportion of standard length.

### Morphometric Comparisons

**Scale Counts:**
- **Cheek scale rows**: Highest mean in M. dolomieui, lowest in M. salmoides
- **Scales above lateral line**: Highest in M. dolomieui, lowest in M. salmoides
- **Scales below lateral line**: Highest in M. dolomieui, lowest in M. salmoides
- **Lateral line count**: M. dolomieui differs significantly from other black basses

**Fin Ray Counts:**
- **Dorsal soft rays**: Significant differences except in M. dolomieui
- **Anal soft rays**: No significant differences among species

**Measurements:**
- **Upper jaw length**: Similar proportions across all species
- **Dorsal spine ratio** (longest/shortest): M. salmoides shows significant difference; M. coosae differs significantly from M. dolomieui, M. p. punctulatus, and M. p. treculi

### Subspecies Variation

Few differences between subspecies of M. punctulatus were apparent from the data. The tooth patch was present in all three examined subspecies (M. p. punctulatus and two others mentioned).

### Taxonomic Conclusions

The tooth patch is **not a reliable character** for distinguishing M. punctulatus where ranges overlap with M. coosae and M. notius, as these species also exhibit tooth patches in some or all individuals. Traditional diagnostic characters (color pattern, dorsal spine proportions, pyloric caeca) remain more reliable for species identification.

Methods followed Hubbs and Bailey (1940) and Hubbs and Lagler (1958) for counting and measuring. Most measurements are given as proportion of standard length.
