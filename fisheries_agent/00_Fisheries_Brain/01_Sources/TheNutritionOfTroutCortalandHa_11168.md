# The Nutrition of Trout - Cortland Hatchery Report Number 23 for the Year 1954

#ichthyology #salmonidae #brook-trout #brown-trout #fish-nutrition #hatchery-science

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive 1954 report from the [[Cortland Hatchery]] presents multiple experimental studies on [[Brook Trout]] (Salvelinus fontinalis) and [[Brown Trout]] (Salmo trutta) nutrition and physiology. The research, conducted cooperatively between the [[New York Conservation Department]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Cornell University]], addresses calcium absorption from water, cobalt metabolism, metabolic rate reduction techniques, vitamin requirements, and dietary components. Key findings demonstrate that non-feeding brook trout absorb significant calcium directly from water (1.58% of total body calcium in 96 hours), with absorption rates dependent on water chemistry. The studies establish optimal conditions for high-density fish transport (up to 62.4 pounds per cubic foot), identify essential vitamins for brown trout, and evaluate phospholipid effects on growth. All experiments were conducted at controlled temperatures (36-51°F) using rigorous factorial experimental designs.

## Background & Description

The [[Cortland Hatchery]] operates under a cooperative agreement to advance salmonid nutrition and management science. This report encompasses five major research areas conducted during 1954:

**Calcium Absorption Studies**: Small non-feeding brook trout (average 21.6 grams) were exposed to radioactive calcium (Ca⁴⁵) in eight different water compositions over geometrically progressive time intervals (0.75 to 96 hours). Waters ranged from 5-100 ppm calcium in distilled water, and mixtures of 0-100% spring water maintaining 50 ppm calcium. Fish were conditioned for 64 hours before exposure to eliminate feeding effects and establish maintenance metabolism. Dissection separated gills, stomach, intestines, and remainder for radioactivity measurement.

**Cobalt Absorption**: Yearling brook trout (average 84.2 grams) were exposed to radioactive cobalt chloride (Co⁶⁰, 700 microcuries in 8,000 ml) for 12, 24, 48, or 96 hours following 60-hour conditioning periods. Tissues analyzed included gills, gastro-intestinal tract with pyloric caeca, head, liver, and remainder.

**Metabolic Rate Reduction**: Three size classes of brook trout (8.9, 35.5, and 53.4 grams average weight) were tested under factorial combinations of starvation periods (15, 39, 63, 87 hours), water temperatures (36°F and 48°F), and sodium amytal concentrations (0.0, 0.5, 1.0 grain per gallon). Fish were stocked at 3,400 grams per 7 liters (30.3 pounds per cubic foot) in stainless steel aquaria. Ammonia accumulation (direct nesslerization) and oxygen depletion (Winkler method) were measured over 12-hour periods.

**Vitamin Requirements**: Brown trout were fed Wolf's synthetic diet with systematic vitamin omissions (niacin, folic acid, riboflavin, pyridoxine) over 20 weeks at 47°F. Brook trout experiments tested vitamins A and D substitution for cod liver oil.

**Phospholipid Studies**: Brook trout (starting weight 1.32 grams) received diets with various combinations of cod liver oil concentrate, cephalin, lecithin, and fatty acids over 20 weeks at 47°F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research addresses fundamental questions about mineral acquisition in freshwater fish and its implications for hatchery and stream management. Natural and hatchery waters vary widely in calcium and mineral content, potentially affecting fish survival and growth. The calcium absorption studies reveal that water chemistry profoundly influences fish physiology:

**Water Chemistry Effects**: Fish absorbed 9.72% of available labeled calcium from 100% spring water (50 ppm Ca) in 96 hours. Distribution of absorbed calcium within the body was independent of external water composition but dependent on calcium content of body parts. The gills contained 10.5% of total absorbed calcium despite representing only 4.7% of body calcium (distribution ratio 3.1), confirming their role as primary absorption sites.

**Osmoregulation**: Calcium ions exerted osmoregulatory effects preventing cytolysis in concentrations as low as 5 ppm in distilled water. Fish survived 160 hours (64-hour conditioning plus 96-hour experimental exposure) in this minimal calcium environment. Metabolic rate increased significantly in dilute solutions (<50 ppm calcium), evidenced by faster calcium distribution to stomach and intestines (probable egress sites). The distribution ratio for the remainder (skeletal tissues) approached unity (0.96), indicating proportional deposition.

**Metabolic Activity Indicators**: Rate of calcium distribution served as a valid index of metabolic activity. In 5 ppm versus 100 ppm calcium solutions, absorbed calcium per gram of gill tissue at 0.75 hours was nearly proportional to concentration (2.55 vs 35.25 micrograms/gram). However, by 96 hours, increased metabolism in dilute solutions compensated, with only 13.36% more calcium in fish from concentrated solutions versus 80.46% more at 6 hours.

**Cobalt Metabolism**: Non-feeding brook trout absorbed radioactive cobalt through gills, with larger accumulation in digestive tract (38.3% at 96 hours) than liver (7.14% at 96 hours), contrasting with terrestrial animals where liver shows greatest accumulation. This suggests fundamental physiological differences between aquatic and terrestrial vertebrates, possibly related to a complex cobalt compound isolated from fish pyloric caeca.

**Hatchery Management Implications**: Poor survival of hatchery fish in some natural waters may result from low calcium concentrations requiring excessive energy expenditure for osmoregulation, even with abundant food. The "space factor" in salmon culture was indirectly confirmed by size-dependent responses to crowding.

## Key Findings

**Calcium Absorption and Distribution**:
- Small non-feeding brook trout absorbed 1.5077 mg labeled calcium over 96 hours (1.58% of total body calcium, 95.22 mg)
- Absorption rate varied with water composition: 1.099-2.168% of body calcium depending on mineral concentration
- Distribution to body parts was independent of water composition but proportional to calcium content of tissues
- Gills served as primary absorption site (distribution ratio 3.1-3.6 for gills, stomach, intestines)
- Remainder (skeletal tissues) showed distribution ratio of 0.96, indicating proportional deposition
- Hourly absorption rate remained constant after 3 hours for whole fish, after 12 hours for gills
- Deposition in gills began after 12 hours; in remainder after 3 hours

**Water Chemistry Effects on Calcium Metabolism**:
- Calcium concentration <50 ppm in distilled water significantly increased distribution rate
- Increased mineral concentration (beyond calcium) enhanced absorption and metabolism
- Fish survived 160 hours in 5 ppm calcium (minimum tested)
- At 5 ppm calcium: 2.55 μg/g gill tissue at 0.75 hours, 107.65 μg/g at 96 hours
- At 100 ppm calcium: 35.25 μg/g gill tissue at 0.75 hours, 194.34 μg/g at 96 hours
- Metabolic compensation in dilute solutions reduced difference from 80.46% (6 hours) to 13.36% (48 hours)

**Cobalt Absorption**:
- Radioactive cobalt absorbed through gills from water
- Distribution at 96 hours: gills 39.9%, digestive tract 38.3%, head 11.2%, liver 7.14%, remainder 3.44%
- Gill distribution decreased from 67.5% (12 hours) to 39.9% (96 hours)
- Digestive tract distribution increased six-fold: 6.48% (12 hours) to 38.3% (96 hours)
- Liver accumulation increased from 0.70 to 1.47 milli-microcuries between 12-96 hours
- Pattern differs from terrestrial animals (greatest liver accumulation)

**Metabolic Rate Reduction**:
- Starvation period of 63-87 hours most effective for reducing ammonia accumulation
- Water temperature of 36°F drastically reduced ammonia versus 48°F
- Sodium amytal (0.5-1.0 grain/gallon) showed delayed action (2-8 hours) before effectiveness
- Fish size significantly affected metabolic response: medium fish (35.5 g) responded after 39 hours starvation; small (8.9 g) and large (53.4 g) required 63-87 hours
- Mortality at 48°F varied by size: small fish highest (108 deaths at 15-hour starvation, standard), medium intermediate (11 deaths), large lowest (3 deaths)
- At 36°F, mortality eliminated except small fish at 15-hour starvation (1 death)
- Optimal combination: 63-hour starvation + 36°F + 0.5 grain sodium amytal eliminated all mortality

**Carrying Capacity**:
- Successfully carried 30.3 pounds/cubic foot (3,400 g/7 L) with zero mortality under optimal conditions
- Achieved 44.9 pounds/cubic foot (5,000 g/7 L) with minimal mortality using sodium amytal at 36°F
- Maximum tested: 62.4 pounds/cubic foot (5,000 g/5 L) succeeded only with large fish + sodium amytal
- At 30.3 lb/ft³, 36°F: ammonia 5.6-9.9 ppm (12 hours), oxygen 4.5-8.8 ppm
- At 44.9 lb/ft³, 36°F with 1.0 grain amytal: ammonia 6.1-13.6 ppm, oxygen 5.7-9.0 ppm

**Vitamin Requirements - Brown Trout**:
- Riboflavin essential: omission reduced growth to 197-239% gain versus 325-388% control (20 weeks)
- Pyridoxine essential: omission caused 100% mortality by 10 weeks, only 33% weight gain
- Folic acid not required: 303-324% gain versus 325-388% control
- Niacin not required: 300-325% gain versus control
- Mortality with riboflavin omission: 4.5-5.0% per two-week period versus 0.8-1.0% control
- Synthetic diet produced slightly reduced growth and 3× higher mortality versus hatchery diet

**Vitamin Requirements - Brook Trout**:
- No demonstrated need for vitamin A or D
- Cod liver oil superior to synthetic vitamins A+D in corn oil: 790-885% gain versus 679-715% gain (20 weeks)
- Vitamins A (1,500 units/g) and D (600 units/g) in corn oil failed to replace cod liver oil
- Conversion ratios: cod liver oil 3.0-3.2, synthetic vitamins 3.6-3.7

**Phospholipid Effects**:
- Cephalin demonstrated growth stimulation: 1,400-1,485% gain versus 1,159-1,258% control
- Lecithin alone ineffective: 1,175-1,385% gain
- Fatty acids + concentrate + cephalin most effective: 1,630% gain
- Fatty acids + concentrate + lecithin: 1,720-1,755% gain (exceeded cod liver oil at 1,430-1,520%)
- Cod liver oil concentrate + both phospholipids: 1,515-1,645% gain
- Interaction effects suggest multiple growth stimulators in cod liver oil beyond cephalin
- Conversion improved with cephalin: 3.0-3.1 versus 3.2-3.3 control

**Dried Liver Substitution**:
- Commercial dried liver product failed as fresh meat substitute
- Growth rate: 508-551% versus 1,025-1,060% for liver-spleen diet (20 weeks)
- Fish lost appetite by 14 weeks
- Anemia developed: 660,000-720,000 red cells/mm³ versus 950,000-970,000 in controls
- Conversion poor: 4.2-4.3 versus 2.9-3.0 for liver-spleen
- Mortality elevated: 1.8-2.4% per two weeks versus 0.3-0.5% control

**Methodological Considerations**:
- Ammonia accumulation served as valid metabolism index when mortality was low
- No correlation between mortality and oxygen content alone
- Sodium amytal's effectiveness possibly due to reduced activity and/or reduced toxic metabolite production beyond ammonia
- Factorial experimental design (Fisher 1937) enabled interaction analysis
- Randomized selection balanced seasonal variation across experiments
- 64-hour conditioning period established maintenance metabolism (constant blood sugar)
