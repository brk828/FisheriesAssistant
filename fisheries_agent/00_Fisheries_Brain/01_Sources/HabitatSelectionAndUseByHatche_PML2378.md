# Habitat Use and Predator Overlap of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Sucker and Flathead Catfish in the Imperial Division, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department contract report (Contract Report No. 05, September 27, 2002) presents Phase IV of a multi-phase study of [[Razorback Sucker]] habitat use in the [[Imperial Division]] of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The work was funded under a cooperative agreement with the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]. It has two parts: (1) habitat selection and use by hatchery-reared adult razorback suckers, including a predictive model for backwater habitats and the response of the fish to off-channel habitat restoration; and (2) a comparison of habitat use and spatial overlap between razorback suckers and the non-native predator [[Flathead Catfish]].

Razorback suckers strongly preferred off-channel [[Backwater Habitat]]. A [[Logistic Regression]] model was useful for identifying backwaters that are unlikely to be used, which can help prioritize restoration. Restored habitats were used, and dredging did not cause significant displacement. Flathead catfish and razorback suckers showed moderate overlap, but they rarely shared the same physical habitat except while moving between preferred areas.

## Background & Description

**Species.** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae) is a native southwestern fish that has declined severely and is federally listed as endangered. Recovery efforts have shifted from stocking larval and juvenile fish, which had low survival, to stocking larger hatchery-reared adults, which are less vulnerable to predation by resident sportfish. The flathead catfish (*Pylodictis olivaris*, Ictaluridae) is a non-native piscivore introduced to the Colorado River system in the 1960s for angling. It has established reproducing populations in the lower river and is considered a potential threat to razorback recovery.

**Study history.** The project was organized in phases:

- **Phase I:** A literature review of fish distribution, plus [[Habitat Assessment Index]] (HAI) scoring. The HAI was based largely on upper-basin and reservoir research, so its applicability to the Imperial Division was questionable.
- **Phase II:** In-situ telemetry showed that razorback suckers preferred backwaters over side and main channels.
- **Phase III:** Predictive habitat models were developed to replace the HAI for backwaters.
- **Phase IV:** Monitoring continued during restoration. Flathead catfish were added for comparison.

**Restoration.** Between December 1999 and February 2001, [[Habitat Restoration]] reconnected isolated off-channel areas, dredged the impoundment near [[Imperial Dam]], and excavated a wetland. The goals were to improve water quality and wetland productivity, restore aquatic and riparian habitat, and increase recreation.

**Study site.** The study area was the lower 9.6 km of the river, from Imperial Dam to just south of Martinez Lake. It was divided into 36 sampling sites in four habitat types: 25 backwaters, nine side channels, a main channel of about 133 ha, and an impoundment of about 13 ha. The total surface area was about 303 ha. Non-native fishes, including flathead catfish, channel catfish, common carp and [[Largemouth Bass]], were abundant throughout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish source and tagging.** Study razorback suckers were hatchery-reared from [[Lake Mohave]] broodstock, raised at Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery, and implanted with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] transmitters (69–83 kHz, about 5 g). Lengths were 413–570 mm TL. Fish were located by boat with a directional hydrophone, usually every 2–3 weeks during daylight.

**Habitat dynamics.** Natural flood pulses no longer maintain off-channel access under current flow regimes. Sedimentation, dense vegetation and bank stabilization have reduced connectivity, so access now depends on mechanical dredging and excavation. This makes site prioritization cost-effective.

**Water quality.** After restoration, backwater temperature and conductivity decreased and pH increased. The authors attributed these changes to greater flushing from the mainstem. Side-channel velocities increased. Dynamic water-quality variables were weakly correlated with fish use.

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker habitat use (Section II)**

- Use of the four habitat types differed highly significantly from availability (P < 0.01), with backwaters strongly favored.
- Observed use versus expected use based on area was:

| Habitat | Area proportion | Observed use |
|---|---|---|
| Backwater | 44% | 76% (311 obs.) |
| Impoundment | 5% | 4% (17 obs.) |
| Main channel | 45% | 15% (62 obs.) |
| Side channel | 6% | 5% (20 obs.) |

- Of 75 released fish, 67 were contacted at least once. Contacts totaled 410 over 25 reconnaissance surveys (December 6, 1999 to July 12, 2001) at 31 sites. The mean was 5.6 contacts per fish, with a maximum of 21. Eight fish were never contacted, and six were found outside the study area.
- Inferred habitat exposure was broad: 57% of fish were exposed to more than one habitat type and five fish to all types.
- Diel surveys (five surveys, 16 fish, 95 observations, January–June 2000) showed no significant difference in habitat use across the 24-hour period (P = 0.879). Movement occurred in 57 of 95 observations, mostly within the same habitat type.
- Of the 42 fish released into restoration areas, 23 remained largely in or among them. Six left and later returned. Overall, 96% (72 of 75) of released fish had moved from their release sites.

**Predictive model**

- Model-A (all static and dynamic variables) was the working model. Variables with significant coefficients included backwater area, mean depth, distance to main channel, conductivity, pH and temperature.
- The model predicted non-use of backwaters reasonably well but predicted use poorly. Performance declined when applied to post-restoration data, because physical and water-quality conditions had changed.
- Models for the main and side channels performed poorly and were not useful.
- Backwater depth and area were more influential than water-quality variables. Razorback suckers were associated with irregularly shaped backwaters with mean depths greater than 1.5 m.
- The model estimated that three restored backwaters (AB12.8, AB12.4 and AB12.3) were most suitable, covering about 10.74 ha, or roughly 56% of the 18.94 ha of restored backwater.

**Flathead catfish overlap (Section III)**

- Fifty-two flathead catfish were tagged and released (May 2000, September 2000, May 2001, October 2001). Mean TL was 874 mm in 2000 and 884 mm in 2001. Adult razorback suckers were tagged in October 2000 and 2001 (n = 37 each; mean TL 439 mm and 446 mm). Anglers captured seven flathead catfish (13% of tagged fish).
- Percent use by habitat:

| Habitat | Flathead catfish | Razorback sucker |
|---|---|---|
| Main channel | 74.7% | 21.3% |
| Side channel | 6.2% | 9.6% |
| Backwater | 14.6% | 61.8% |
| Impoundment | 4.5% | 7.3% |

- Chesson's alpha (random = 0.25): flathead catfish selected the main channel (0.41) and avoided backwaters (0.09). Razorback suckers avoided the main channel (0.09) and showed selection for side channels (0.30) and the impoundment (0.35), with moderate selection for backwaters (0.26).
- Percent resource overlap (Schoener's index) was 46, indicating moderate overlap, concentrated in side channels.
- Co-occurrence within the same site was recorded 80 times (8% of contacts). Most occurred in the main channel during winter and fall, and very little during spring and summer.
- Razorback suckers moved more often per individual than flathead catfish did and were more often located in backwaters. Flathead catfish moved more frequently and left the study area more often.

**Conclusions and management implications (Section IV)**

- The authors judged the overall potential for flathead catfish predation on adult hatchery-reared razorback suckers to be moderate. Co-occurrence is rare and brief, but predation risk rises during movements between habitat types.
- Other backwater predators, such as largemouth bass, green sunfish and carp, may pose equal or greater threats because they overlap more in time. The authors recommend a shift toward a backwater community approach.
- Stocking larger adults appears to have improved survival and allowed fish to establish in preferred habitat.
- Reconnecting backwaters increases habitat for razorback suckers while reducing habitat for flathead catfish. The authors recommend expanding studies of the whole native-fish community in these habitats.
- The model can be used to rank candidate sites for restoration by identifying those least likely to be used. It should be applied with caution in habitats that differ substantially from the Imperial Division. The authors also note that structural cover was not measured and that the researchers' presence sometimes influenced fish behavior.
