# Giant Seahorse (Hippocampus ingens) Returns to Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #syngnathidae

## Executive Summary

This article, reprinted from *Pacific Discovery* (1963), reports the live capture of a large adult female [[Giant Seahorse]] (*Hippocampus ingens*) off Coronado, California, on November 20, 1962. The authors, [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Sam D. Hinton]], trace the species' occurrence in California through published literature and museum collections, and argue that southern California lies on the fringe of its range. The historical record suggests that shore waters were warmer about a century ago, when the species was more common and may have reached as far north as San Francisco.

## Background & Description

The specimen was caught by fisherman David Ghio, who found it hooked by its prehensile, finless tail to a halibut net set at 75 feet, about 3¾ miles south of [[Point Loma]]. Ghio donated it to the [[Thomas Wayland Vaughan Aquarium-Museum]] of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]] at La Jolla. A second, somewhat smaller adult was later obtained by a Scripps expedition in [[Magdalena Bay]], Baja California, within the species' usual range.

The authors describe *Hippocampus ingens* as the only seahorse in the eastern Pacific. It is fairly common, especially in bays, from central Baja California to northern Peru. The article notes that it is one of the largest members of its genus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Husbandry and temperature tolerance.** The two specimens were kept in a well-lit small aquarium at about 74°F, often alongside a local [[Pipefish]]. During a heater malfunction, they tolerated an overnight drop to 58°F and a rapid return to 76°F without apparent harm, suggesting hardiness toward short cold spells and abrupt temperature changes. Their care was credited to aquarist Monte Kirven, who fed them frequently on live [[Brine Shrimp]] (*Artemia*) and trained them to take thawed frozen brine shrimp. Water was kept clean by circulation with compressed air through a subsand filter.

**Geographic range.** Before the 1962 capture, the northernmost recent records were numerous specimens from Black Warrior Lagoon, on the Baja California coast about 300 miles below San Diego, and from Sebastian Vizcaino Bay, where waters are warmer than on either side. The authors state that the warm-temperate waters of southern California lie at the very fringe of the species' range.

**Historical warm period.** The authors link the species' past presence in California to a warmer climate. Tropical fishes collected by the [[Pacific Railroad Survey]] at San Diego in the 1850s suggest that air and water temperatures were warmer around the mid-19th century. The authors argue that five specimens of this tropical species could hardly have been gathered in such a cursory survey unless the animal was then much more common and the water warmer.

**Ocean currents.** The authors propose that the [[Davidson Current]], a seasonal warm northward surface flow inside the southward-flowing [[California Current]], may have carried warm-temperate and tropical fishes north beyond their usual range. They suggest it was stronger during the warm regime of the mid-1850s.

**Possible recent return.** The 1962 animal may have arrived during the unusually warm years of 1957–1959, when many southern marine forms spread northward, and then remained near San Diego through the cooler years of 1960–1962. The authors describe it as "presumably a waif from more hospitably warm waters to the south."

## Key Findings

**Historical California records (chronological, from the article):**

- **1857:** Five specimens taken at San Diego, probably in the bay, by Andrew Cassidy, operator of the initial tide-gauge station established by the Pacific Railroad Survey. This was the first organized exploration of the Pacific Coast fauna, and the species was discovered in California at this time.
- **1872:** One specimen from San Diego collected by S. D. Whaley, now held at the [[Museum of Comparative Zoology]] (MCZ), Harvard.
- **1860:** A small specimen (MCZ 35292, 65 mm along the curved axis) recorded as collected at San Francisco by [[Alexander Agassiz]]. The authors confirmed its identification as a typical *Hippocampus ingens*, closely matching a young Galapagos specimen. The label's attribution to "Prof. Agassiz" is taken to mean Alexander, not Louis Agassiz. The authors consider the locality fairly reliable, with some residual doubt.
- **1880:** [[David Starr Jordan]] and [[Charles Henry Gilbert]] listed "One large specimen seen" from San Diego in their survey for the United States Fish Commission. The specimen was not in their collections, and its date and location were unrecorded.
- **1892:** [[Carl E. Eigenmann]] did not see the species but reported it "said to occur about the Coronado Islands."
- **1902–1905:** The survey by Edwin Chapin Starks and Earl Leonard Morris found no seahorses. Neither did the senior author's San Diego collections in 1913–1916, 1922–1923, and 1940–1963.
- **1938 or 1939:** John E. Fitch reported a specimen collected in [[Mission Bay]], San Diego, by a fellow student. Fitch then spent several summers trying daily for two or three weeks each season to catch one in the same bay without success.

**Inferences drawn by the authors:**

- Several decades of failed collecting in Mission Bay and elsewhere, including by Scripps Aquarium staff, indicate that the species was very rare in southern California, if it persisted there at all.
- The M.C.Z. specimens support the idea that about a century ago the California coast was considerably warmer, and that the giant seahorse occurred in some numbers in southern California and possibly as far north as San Francisco.
- The 1962 capture is most likely an occasional northward wanderer from warmer southern waters rather than evidence of an established population.
