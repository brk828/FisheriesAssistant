# Nevada Native Fish Program: Protected, Game, and Unprotected Fishes of Nevada (1984)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1984 publication from the [[Nevada Department Of Wildlife]], prepared by Chief of Fisheries Patrick D. Coffin, presents the status framework of the [[Nevada Native Fish Program]]. It sorts Nevada's fishes into federal and state endangered, threatened, and sensitive categories; native and introduced game fishes; and native and introduced unprotected species. The document stresses that Nevada's isolated desert waters hold many endemic fishes, that several taxa are extinct or suspected extinct, that introduced species have often been stocked without regard to their impacts, and that the status of many native populations is unknown. ## Background & Description

The Nevada Department of Wildlife is required by statute and Commission policy to protect, manage, and restore wildlife within the state. Its mandate covers the ecological, recreational, and economic values of wildlife, and it extends to maintaining the status of species that are not game fish.

Nevada has many aquatic systems and closed basins, and these isolated waters have produced a high number of endemic fish species and subspecies. The document notes that fishes representing several genera are considered extinct in Nevada. The text states that the Department's taxonomic names follow the American Fisheries Society's common and scientific names list for North American fishes (Special Publication No. 12, fourth edition).

The publication's categories are:

- **State extinct species:** fishes known or suspected to be extinct in Nevada, several of which survive only outside the state within their historic range.
- **Federal and state endangered species:** taxa in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of their range. Listed taxa include [[Cui-ui]], [[Moapa Dace]], [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], [[Ash Meadows Pupfish]], [[Pahrump Poolfish]] and [[Speckled Dace]] (Moapa-area and Nevada subspecies).
- **Federal threatened species:** taxa likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future. [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] is listed here.
- **State protected and state sensitive species:** taxa for which information is being gathered to decide whether protection is needed. The text also records that 20 species and subspecies are classified as state sensitive.
- **Native game fishes (7):** Bull (Dolly Varden) Trout, Lahontan Cutthroat Trout, Lewis's cutthroat trout, Bonneville Cutthroat Trout, Redband Trout, Rainbow Trout, and Mountain Whitefish.
- **Introduced game fishes (27 approximate):** salmonids such as [[Kokanee Salmon]], Coho Salmon, [[Lake Trout]], Brook Trout, Kamloops and Southcoast Rainbow, Golden Trout and Brown Trout; warmwater species such as Northern Pike, [[Channel Catfish]], bullheads, Yellow Perch, Walleye, White and Striped Bass, Sacramento Perch, [[Largemouth Bass]], Spotted, Florida, and Smallmouth Bass, Bluegill, Redear and Green Sunfish, and [[Black Crappie]] and White Crappie.
- **Native unprotected species (about 17):** suckers (Mountain, Big-lip, Ridgelip, Utah, Flannelmouth, Tahoe), Squawfish, Chiselmouth, Utah Chub, Lahontan Tui Chub, Redside Shiners, Speckle Dace subspecies, Mottled Sculpin, and Paiute Sculpin.
- **Introduced unprotected species (about 19):** including Threadfin Shad, Golden Shiner, Common Carp, Goldfish, Fathead Minnow, Mosquitofish, Guppy, Moonfish (Platy), Grass Carp, cichlids, and several tilapias. Their distributions range from statewide to specific reservoirs or the southern warm springs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies several threats to Nevada's native fishes:

- **Introduced species:** Non-native fishes have frequently been stocked "with little regard to the potential impacts" on native fauna, and the text notes that competition and predation from them have caused population declines.
- **Habitat alteration and loss:** Native fish populations have been influenced by habitat changes, and the publication links declines to decreased available water habitat.
- **Limited knowledge:** The present status of many species and subspecies is unknown, and the text states that more information on the species' biology and habitat conditions is needed to ensure survival.
- **Introduced invertebrates:** Crustaceans and mollusks have also been introduced into state waters, often without concern for the protection of native species.

The Department states it is obligated to maintain these species through management and scientific study. Protected populations are mapped in a location key (Map I), and listed taxa are tied to particular spring systems and drainages, such as the Lahontan and Columbia River drainages and the southern warm springs.

## Key Findings

- Nevada's native fish fauna is highly endemic and fragmented across closed basins and springs.
- Several taxa, including [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Ash Meadows Killifish]], are listed as extinct or suspected extinct in the state, and some are extinct only within Nevada.
- Multiple taxa are protected at the federal or state level, including several spring-dwelling pupfishes, the Cui-ui, and Moapa Dace.
- Introduced game and forage fishes outnumber native game fishes, and many introduced species compete with or prey on natives.
- The status of many native fishes is unknown, so the Department identifies research and monitoring as necessary for protection decisions.
- The Department's stated goals are to protect, manage, and restore native fish, maintain the species it is obligated to conserve, and use scientific studies to guide decisions about state sensitive and protected listings.
