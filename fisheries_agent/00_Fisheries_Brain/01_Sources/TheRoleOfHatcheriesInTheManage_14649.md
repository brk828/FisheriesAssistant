# Hatcheries in the Recovery of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, a contribution to the 1985 symposium *The Role of Fish Culture in Fisheries Management* (Lake Ozark, Missouri), reviews how artificial propagation in [[Hatchery Facilities]] supports the recovery of imperiled freshwater fishes, mainly in the American Southwest. It centres on [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, the first multispecies endangered-fishes facility in the United States, and on mono-species programs for the [[Cui-Ui]] at Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and the [[Apache Trout]] in Arizona. The authors conclude that hatchery propagation is politically and operationally feasible and can play a positive role, but that it cannot substitute for habitat protection, which they identify as the primary recovery action.

## Background & Description

**Legal and policy context.** Federal involvement began with the Endangered Species Preservation Act (1966), followed by the Endangered Species Conservation Act (1969). The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (ESA) protected habitats, barred federal actions that jeopardize listed species, prohibited taking, and provided for recovery. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) implemented it. The 1982 amendments created an "experimental" population category, though the authors note that regulations finalized in 1984 were so cumbersome that removing federal protections became harder than listing the species.

**Dexter NFH.** Built by the FWS in the mid-1930s as a warm-water hatchery for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], Dexter served New Mexico for about 40 years before the federal warm-water program was centralized in Texas. After the state declined it, the facility was converted in 1974 into a refugium, research centre, and propagation site for imperiled fishes. Its objectives were to maintain a protected gene pool, develop culture techniques, study biology, supply fish to cooperating agencies, publish findings, and run public outreach. The first six native southwestern species arrived in November 1974.

**Facility.** Dexter lies in the [[Pecos River Valley]] at 3,500 ft, with about 12 inches of annual rainfall and a 180 to 200 day growing season. Three shallow wells supply water that is slightly alkaline (pH 7.2 to 7.5), held at a constant 64 °F, with total hardness averaging 2,100 ppm and total dissolved solids near 3,500 ppm. The station has a holding house with sixteen 350-gal tanks, a hatching battery, and a laboratory, plus 48 earthen ponds totalling 20.8 acres. Waste water is impounded on site and isolated from other surface waters.

**Species held.** Table 1 lists the species held at Dexter as of July 1, 1982. The list includes the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], several *Gila* chubs (including [[Chihuahua Chub]] and [[Yaqui Chub]]), [[Woundfin]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], three pupfishes, [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Big Bend Gambusia]]. Federal statuses ranged from endangered and threatened to proposed or candidate. Several *Gila* chubs were not yet recognised by the Committee on Names of Fishes.

**Apache trout.** Recognised in the early 1960s as a unique, declining salmonid, the Apache trout was moved to [[Sterling Springs Hatchery]] in 1963 from 82 fish collected at Ord Creek. It was listed under the ESA and later down-listed to threatened in 1975, when 118 fish were collected from Soldier Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Causes of decline.** The authors attribute the decline of southwestern fishes mainly to habitat alteration: dam construction, ground-water pumping, range grazing, soil erosion, and introduced non-native fishes that prey on or compete with natives. Habitat loss is identified as the principal factor.
- **Scale of the problem.** About 81% of native Arizona fish are classed as threatened or endangered or proposed as such, and 42% of New Mexico's species. The FWS estimated that more than 35 southwestern species may need artificial propagation.
- **Pyramid Lake.** This endorheic lake is a Pleistocene remnant, 25 miles long and 4 to 10 miles wide, with maximum and mean depths of 338 and 164 ft. The [[Truckee River]] is its main water source. Diversions beginning in 1905 for the Newland Reclamation Project lowered the lake by 75 ft, creating a delta that blocked spawning runs. The Pyramid Lake cutthroat trout was considered extinct by 1938.
- **Colorado River system.** Dams such as [[Davis Dam]], completed in the early 1950s, impounded the river and created [[Lake Mohave]], where bonytail survived but did not reproduce. Razorback suckers show no documented recruitment there since the mid-1950s.
- **Genetic risk.** Closely related *Gila* species can hybridise, so separate ponds and strict equipment disinfection are needed. Hybridisation between bonytail and humpback and roundtail chubs is also occurring in the upper Colorado Basin.

## Key Findings

**Dexter NFH and the shift in hatchery philosophy**
- Hatchery operation changed from rearing game fish to rearing non-game fish for species survival rather than sport-fishery production.
- By 1985, Dexter had held more imperiled species from more native habitats at one site than ever before.
- Pupfish and chub populations peak in August and September and decline in winter. Live-bearers such as Big Bend gambusia and Gila topminnow can lose about 90% of their numbers over winter, so some individuals are overwintered in aquaria.
- The [[Chihuahua Chub]] was thought extinct for nearly 40 years. It was rediscovered in 1975, and after a 1978 flood only 10 fish were captured. Reintroduction of hatchery-reared fish was planned for 1987.

**Reintroduction milestones**
- Yaqui chub: 35,000 stocked on private land in 1980, the first breakthrough against reintroduction apprehension.
- Razorback sucker: a 1981 Memorandum of Understanding with Arizona Game and Fish (AG&F) led to more than 6 million fry and 296,000 fingerlings stocked in Arizona by 1985. The FWS agreed not to propose listing until success could be judged, estimated at 10 years.
- Gila topminnow: more than 70 isolated habitats were stocked in June 1982. The authors state more than 100 separate reintroductions have since occurred, which they describe as possibly the most intensive recovery effort for an endangered species.
- Bonytail: 45,000 stocked into Lake Mohave in November 1981 and about 40,000 more in summer 1982, to protect the gene pool rather than to create a self-sustaining population.
- Dexter distributed 6,108,965 razorback sucker fry and 296,376 fingerlings, and 116,638 Colorado squawfish fingerlings, between 1981 and 1984.

**Reproductive biology and culture data**
- Razorback suckers reared at Willow Beach NFH reached sexual maturity in six years at 14 to 16 inches. Dexter two-year-old males were mature in 1983, and three-year-old females spawned in 1984. Maturity appears to depend more on size than on age.
- Female razorback suckers spawn near 55 °F. Optimum egg incubation and fry development occur near 70 °F. Eggs hatch in 96 to 144 hours.
- In 1984, 70 domestic three-year-old females (mean 2.8 lb) gave a fecundity of 63,674 and egg viability of 61.1%. Twenty-five wild females (mean 7.76 lb) gave a fecundity of 123,110 and viability of 78.8%.
- Colorado squawfish reached maturity in their sixth year. Nine domestic females in 1984 produced 20,656 eggs per pound of body weight. Their growth is slow: nine-year-old hatchery fish averaged 20 inches and three pounds.
- Bonytail: 24 females spawned in May 1983, with mean fecundity of 4,677 (range 1,015 to 10,384) and mean egg viability of 67.5%. Lake Mohave specimens aged from otoliths were 40 to 42 years old.
- Woundfin: in 1984, 12 of 20 injected females (60%) spawned. Total fecundity was 2,067 and mean fecundity 172, yielding 1,163 swim-up fry.

**Cui-ui recovery at Pyramid Lake**
- The cui-ui is probably the only surviving member of its genus. A closed-system tribal hatchery was operating by 1979, with biological filtration, ultraviolet sterilisation, and a diesel backup generator.
- Hatchery water averaged 54.7 °F (±1.1 °F) with an 8.3 °F diel fluctuation during 1982 incubation. Cold water helps inhibit *Saprolegnia* fungus.
- In 1982, 17.7 million eggs were taken and 10.9 million fry reached swim-up (61.6% survival).
- Gill-net comparisons show a decline: 1954 to 1958 gave 2.31 cui-ui per net (4.40% of catch); 1975 to 1977 gave 1.11 per net (1.28% of catch). Catch per unit effort fell by about half.
- The authors argue the hatchery program may be the reason the species survives. Future work should cover rearing to fingerling size, post-release survival, and age-composition monitoring.

**Apache trout culture problems**
- Over 18 years, an average of 69.7% of eggs failed to hatch, and only 38.7% of hatched eggs survived to brood stock or management use. Overall, total output averaged 11.8% of the yearly egg take.
- Mean egg production for 2- to 5-year-old females was about 274.
- Fry resist artificial diets and are highly territorial, so intensive rearing produces high mortality. Fry grew to about 2 inches after 150 days at 50.9 °F.
- Limited brood stock from one headwater population and only two raceways create inbreeding risk.
- Hybridisation with introduced rainbow trout is often blamed for decline. The authors suggest that low fecundity, slow growth, angler harvest, and competition from larger non-native salmonids are also important.
- FWS took over refinement of Apache trout rearing at Williams Creek NFH.

**Overall conclusions**
- The authors contrast easy-to-propagate species (razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish, Gila topminnow, cui-ui) with difficult ones (woundfin, Apache trout).
- Hatcheries are essential for species that survive only in single disjunct populations, such as the cui-ui, bonytail, and Chihuahua chub. Other species, such as the Gila trout, are better restored by transfer from wild to wild.
- Genetic variation, mating systems, and gamete output are underconsidered in propagation. The authors recommend [[Genetics and Conservation]] (Schonewald-Cox et al. 1983) as required reading.
- Hatchery success means little without suitable wild habitat. The authors conclude that propagation must proceed slowly and systematically, with careful documentation of each step.
