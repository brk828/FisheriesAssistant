# Fish and Wildlife Service Comments on the Draft Policy on Genetics in Endangered Species Activities

#conservation-genetics #endangered-species #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

This March 11, 2005 memorandum from the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] Mountain-Prairie Region, signed by [[Ralph O. Morgenweck]] and addressed to the Regional Directors of Regions 2 and 6, comments on a draft [[Genetic Policy]] for [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) activities. The Regional Director supports the goal of giving guidance on genetics but argues that a broad application of the draft policy would conflict with the purposes of the ESA and with the mandate to use the [[Best Available Science]]. The letter gives four numbered objections, centred on preserving genetic lineages, using new genetic information after listing, requiring genetic criteria in [[Recovery Criteria]], and preventing loss of genetic diversity in small populations.

## Background & Description

The memorandum responds to the draft policy's directions on genetics in recovery and listing decisions. As quoted in the letter, the draft policy:

- states that genetic discussions during recovery planning "should be limited to reducing or minimizing threats to the species" while the protection of the Act is still needed;
- suggests that recognizing genetic divergence in a recovery plan is appropriate only if that divergence was identified at the time of listing;
- holds that preserving unique genetic lineages is inappropriate to require for [[Delisting]] or [[Downlisting]].

The author states that he can identify situations in which preserving unique genetic combinations throughout a species' range is needed to reduce extinction risk, and concludes that a broad application of the policy would conflict with the ESA. The letter quotes the ESA's stated purpose of providing "a means whereby the ecosystems upon which endangered species and threatened species depend may be conserved."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter's ecological points rest on three ideas:

- **Local adaptations:** Conservation biology literature, as cited by the author, holds that long-term survival depends on preserving a variety of [[Local Adaptations]] across the range. Genetic variation throughout the range can help design [[Recovery Units]] that improve a species' ability to survive future environmental change.
- **Connectivity and isolation:** Genetic data can show which populations are isolated or where [[Gene Flow]] is restricted. Isolated populations can act as [[Refugia]] against disease, wildfire and other foreseeable catastrophes.
- **Small-population genetics:** Endangered and threatened species, by definition, have small or declining populations. These suffer [[Inbreeding]] and loss of [[Genetic Diversity]], which slowly increase extinction risk.

The letter's main example is the endangered Santa Catalina Island fox. Its population was saved from extinction after a narrow isthmus prevented the spread of [[Canine Distemper]] to the other side of the island. The author recommends that fox populations on both sides of the island be maintained and that recovery criteria for both be met before delisting. The letter notes that barriers or [[Bottleneck]] effects to gene flow are not always visible to humans, and that genetic information can reveal them.

## Key Findings

The memorandum does not report new data. Its main points are objections and recommendations:

1. **Preserving genetic lineages:** The draft policy undervalues preserving distinct genetic lineages within a listed entity. Genetic variation across the range is considered crucial for designing recovery units and criteria.
2. **Use of post-listing information:** Restricting genetic information discovered after listing limits the Service's ability to design the best recovery plan. It also treats genetic information differently from other biological information, which the author considers inconsistent. Genetic information should be treated like any other type of biological information.
3. **Recovery units and criteria:** Recovery plans should be able to require that criteria be met in several separate locations (recovery units) across the range, since conservation literature stresses population-wide persistence.
4. **Genetic standards for delisting:** The draft's statement that it would be "inappropriate to require genetic standards for delisting when so little remains in the gene pool" is rejected. A major objective of listed-species recovery should be to minimise inbreeding and loss of genetic diversity, so genetic standards in recovery criteria can be appropriate.

**Overall conclusion:** The author concludes that a broad application of the draft policy would conflict with the purposes of the ESA and recommends revising it so that genetic information can inform recovery criteria, delisting decisions and threat reduction.
