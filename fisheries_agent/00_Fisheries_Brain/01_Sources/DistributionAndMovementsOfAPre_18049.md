# Predator-Prey Distribution and Movements of Rainbow Trout and Redside Shiner in Paul Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by E. J. Crossman (University of British Columbia), extracted from a Ph.D. thesis, examines why [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*, Kamloops form) prey heavily on [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*) in [[Paul Lake]], British Columbia. Predator and prey are brought together in midsummer through a seasonal movement pattern of the shiners that is governed largely by shoal water temperature, combined with diurnal offshore movements after dark. Shiners made up over 90% of the diet of trout longer than 14 inches in August, but were a negligible winter food item.

## Background & Description

Until about 1945, Paul Lake contained only rainbow trout. Redside shiners then reached the lake from upstream lakes in the chain, where they had been established following an accidental introduction. The shiner influx was followed by an explosive increase in shiner numbers, a reduction in trout growth rate, and a decline in catch per unit effort, attributed to competition for food and space. By about 1950, trout over 10 inches had shifted from plankton to fish prey. By 1956, shiners made up over 90% of the summer diet of trout over 14 inches.

The study was supported by the [[British Columbia Game Branch]] and the [[Institute of Fisheries]] at the University of British Columbia. Earlier work on the lake includes [[Larkin et al. 1950]] and [[Larkin and Smith 1954]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trout distribution and movement (1952):** Anglers returned 641 trout caught May to September, and 1,155 trout were tagged and released between May 16 and September 23. Catches concentrated in sections DE and FG. Catch share rose in BC (14% to 22%) and DE (28% to 37%) from May–June to July–September, indicating movement into these sections. Large trout (13–18 inches) appear to have left section A. Of 1,155 tagged trout, 201 were recaptured across 1952–1954. Recaptures were widely distributed, and the authors inferred that no discrete trout populations existed at any one place or time.

**Shiner distribution around the perimeter:** Shiners first appeared in the lake proper on May 28, 1955. Schools formed along the north shore by mid-June and moved out to the shoal edge, a maximum of 150 feet from shore. Shiner fry appeared in early July. Perimeter catches peaked in June and September, and in September section BC held over 50% of the catch. Shiners disappeared from shores by late autumn, with the largest individuals leaving first.

**Vertical distribution:** Shiners showed a consistent size stratification, with smaller fish near the surface and larger fish deeper. Average nightly overnight gill-net catches were 100–1,000 at surface to 8 feet, 100–300 at 8–16 feet, 10–20 at 17–24 feet, and 0 at 25–150 feet. No shiners were caught in daytime sets at lake centre.

**Diurnal movement:** Shiners were absent from deep water by day and moved offshore at dusk, reaching maximum catches in lake-centre gill nets by about 0400 hours. Daytime movement was limited to roughly 15–25 feet beyond the drop-off.

**Horizontal movement across the shoal (1956):** Catches moved from shoreline toward the shoal edge as surface temperatures peaked, then back toward mid-shoal as shoal temperatures fell. Large shiners (2.5–5 inches) moved more strongly than small shiners (1–2.5 inches).

**Vertical movement at shoal edge (1956):** Catches shifted from surface traps toward deeper traps by the time of maximum water temperature. Large shiners remained at mid-depth and bottom.

## Key Findings

- Rainbow trout were not confined to any part of the lake. In summer they concentrated in areas where shiners were most abundant.
- A 1955 purse-seine sample of 14,300 marked shiners, followed by overnight gill-net recapture, showed that 11 of 1,192 shiners caught were marked. Equal numbers were recaptured near the opposite shore, suggesting that some shiners cross the lake.
- In 1952, 9,168 shiners were caught; 5,343 were marked and 203 were recaptured. Recaptures indicated localized populations. Marked shiners from the west end were recaptured only in the west end, and east-end marks showed a slight downstream drift.
- Trout over 14 inches were the main predators of shiners. At this size, shiners made up over 90% of summer diet.
- Shiners were a negligible winter diet item, though trout readily took shiners as bait through the ice.
- Summary conclusions:
  1. No discrete trout populations were found.
  2. Trout moved into shiner concentration areas in summer.
  3. Shiners were distributed by shoal development and formed isolated schools of thousands.
  4. Shiners moved randomly over the shoal by day, moved offshore at night, and shifted seasonally with shoal temperature.
  5. Predator and prey converged at the shoal edge in midsummer.
  6. When shoal temperatures restricted trout feeding, shiners at the shoal edge were still accessible through short feeding excursions.
  7. In fall and winter, shiners moved to about 30 feet and were largely isolated from trout.
