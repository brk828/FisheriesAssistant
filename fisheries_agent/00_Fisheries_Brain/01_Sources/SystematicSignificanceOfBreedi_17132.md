# Systematic Significance of Breeding Tubercles in Fishes of the Family Percidae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 paper by [[Bruce B. Collette]] (Ichthyological Laboratory, Bureau of Commercial Fisheries, U.S. National Museum) surveys the family [[Percidae]] for [[Breeding Tubercles]], small epidermal structures that aid contact between males and females during spawning, and compares their distribution with the existing classification of the family. Specimens were examined from the collections of more than a dozen museums, covering approximately 118 percid species. Breeding tubercles were found in 48 species in five genera, a substantial increase over the 16 species in 3 genera previously reported. The author argues that tubercle patterns are useful for defining subgenera and species groups within [[Etheostoma]] and [[Percina]], and that they support or challenge several tribal and subgeneric arrangements.

## Background & Description

Breeding tubercles are epidermal projections found on fishes of at least 13 families in three orders. They are used in nest defense, in protecting body and fin surfaces during nest building, and in facilitating contact during spawning. In most families they had been ignored or mentioned only briefly. Before this study, tubercles had been reported in only 13 papers covering 16 species in 3 genera of Percidae. The first record for the family was by Jordan (1877) on [[Percina Evides]].

Methods were direct: males in breeding condition were examined under a binocular microscope, and compressed air was used to dry scales and fin rays so tubercles became discernible. The author notes that tubercles are ephemeral, so absence in a given record does not prove absence in the species. Gonad development was recorded to judge spawning condition.

Taxonomic framework follows [[Collette 1963]], which divided Percidae into two subfamilies, [[Luciopercinae]] and [[Percinae]], each with two tribes: [[Luciopercini]] and [[Romanichthyini]] in the first, and [[Percini]] and [[Etheostomatini]] in the second. The darter tribe [[Etheostomatini]] is the most speciose group, with 102 valid described species plus about a dozen undescribed ones, reduced by Bailey to three genera: Percina (22 species in 8 subgenera), [[Ammocrypta]] (5 species in 2 subgenera), and [[Etheostoma]] (76 species in 13 subgenera).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Tubercle distribution is linked to spawning behavior. In the Etheostomatini, tubercles are concentrated on ventral and ventrolateral surfaces (undersides of pelvic and anal fins, lower caudal fin, belly scales, and sides up to the lateral line). Males mount the female's back or sides during spawning, and tubercles on these surfaces contact the female. In the Romanichthyini, tubercles lie on dorsal and dorsolateral surfaces, the top of the head, the dorsal and pectoral fins, and body scales mainly above the lateral line. The author suggests this may reflect different spawning modes, though no spawning information is available for Romanichthyini.

Many tuberculate species show geographic variation. Tubercles tend to be better developed and present for longer periods in southern populations, as in [[Percina Caprodes]], [[Etheostoma Fusiforme]] (subspecies barratti), and [[Etheostoma Spectabile]], although [[Etheostoma Stigmaeum]] shows the reverse pattern in parts of its range. Tubercle development is also seasonal, peaking in the spring breeding season, and may vary with body size.

The most tuberculate darters often spawn over sand, gravel, or under rocks, and several species show distinctive spawning positions. The [[Etheostoma Nigrum]] group spawns inverted under rocks, while the [[Etheostoma Stigmaeum]] group and related species spawn with the male mounted on the female's back.

## Key Findings

**Overall distribution**
- Breeding tubercles are now known for **48 species in five genera** of Percidae.
- Tubercles are mainly lateral in distribution and function primarily in male-female contact during spawning. In some species on the underside of the pelvic fins, they may also stimulate the female.
- Tubercles are of value in classification at two levels: tribal and subgeneric/species-group.

**Tribal relationships**
- Tubercles occur in the Etheostomatini (subfamily Percinae) and the Romanichthyini (subfamily Luciopercinae), a pattern that might suggest grouping these tribes. However, osteological evidence places Etheostomatini with Percini and Romanichthyini with Luciopercini; the tubercle distributions support this view.
- Similar features in the two tribes (reduced air bladder, small body size, lotic habitat) are likely independent adaptations to a bottom-dwelling stream life.

**Genus and tribe-level results**
- [[Stizostedion]] (five species): no breeding tubercles; little sexual dimorphism.
- [[Zingel]] (three species): *Z. streber* and *Z. zingel* are tuberculate; *Z. asper* was examined in only one male and one female, both lacking tubercles, and is expected to resemble *Z. streber*. In *Z. streber*, tubercles form continuous ridges along the body, a condition unique in the genus.
- [[Romanichthys]] (one species, *R. valsanicola*): four tuberculate males and two tuberculate females examined. It has tubercles on both dorsal fins, the only percid known with this trait, and it is exceeded in tubercle development only by *P. evides* and *P. uranidea*.
- [[Perca]] (three species): no tubercles found in *P. flavescens*, *P. fluviatilis*, or *P. schrenki*.
- [[Gymnocephalus]]: no tubercles on nine males of *G. cernua* or nine males of *G. schraetser*, nor on egg-filled females examined.
- [[Percarina]] (*P. demidoffi*): three females, none tuberculate.

**Darter subgenera (Percina)**
- Nontuberculate: Hypohomus, Alvordius, Hadropterus, Swainia, and Cottogaster. Several show unusual alternative structures instead, such as ctenoid midventral keels or fleshy ridges.
- Tuberculate: subgenus Percina (*P. caprodes* and *P. rex*, mainly on ventral scale rows), subgenus Imostoma (*P. shumardi* and *P. uranidea*), and the heterogeneous subgenus Ericosma (*P. evides*). The author suggests *P. evides* may not be as closely related to the nontuberculate *P. crassa* and *P. palmaris* as previously thought.
- *Percina uranidea* has its maximum development from February 8 to 16 in Pearl River collections, when spawning likely peaks. [[Percina Shumardi]] shows similar, though slightly less extensive, development.

**Ammocrypta**
- All four species of Ammocrypta and the one species of Crystallaria are believed to be tuberculate. Tubercle patterns support synonymizing Crystallaria with Ammocrypta at the generic level. The author has not yet seen anal fin tubercles in *A. pellucida*.

**Darter subgenera (Etheostoma)**
- Tuberculate: [[Etheostoma Stigmaeum]] and *E. jessiae* (and *E. chlorosomum*, with tubercles on pelvic and anal rays only) in Boleosoma; the variatum group (five species, where both sexes are tuberculate); the inscriptum group (males only); and the spectabile and nianguae groups within Oligocephalus.
- Nontuberculate: the nigrum group within Boleosoma (five species), subgenus Joa (*E. vitreum*), subgenera Ulocentra, Allohistium, Nothonotus, Villora, Austroperca, Psychromaster, and Catonotus.
- Hololepis (six species) and Microperca (three species) are tuberculate on the anal rays and undersides of pelvic fin rays. The presence of these tubercles in both subgenera supports their close relationship, distinct from Villora.

**Taxonomic implications**
- Within Etheostoma, tubercle patterns support the variatum group's close relations, suggest removing *E. blennius* from that group, and indicate that *E. sellare* probably does not belong there.
- The author suggests that Joa may be better placed within Boleosoma, and that *E. stigmaeum*, *E. jessiae*, and *E. chlorosomum* might be removed from Boleosoma and placed in Vaillantia. He notes that the subgenus Boleosoma is not a natural group.
- Patterns of tubercle development are useful for phylogeny within Etheostomatini but have no value at the generic level if Percina, Ammocrypta, and Etheostoma are recognized as large genera.
- The author cautions against making nomenclatural changes based primarily on tubercle patterns, and instead discusses groups where reallocation seems desirable.

**Closing conclusion**
- Patterns of breeding tubercles confirm close relationships within many subgenera and species groups and indicate that several other groups may need rearrangement.
