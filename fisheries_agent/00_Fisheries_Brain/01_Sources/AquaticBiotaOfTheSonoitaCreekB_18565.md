# Aquatic Biota of the Sonoita Creek Basin, Santa Cruz County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-biota

## Executive Summary

This report by W. L. Minckley (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, 1968) synthesizes periodic studies of the aquatic biota of the [[Sonoita Creek Basin]] since autumn 1963, with an intensive survey in 1967. The basin is small and largely dry, but its surviving perennial flows, two major springs ([[Monkey Spring]] and [[Cottonwood Spring]]) and earthen tanks shelter remnants of an indigenous fauna that has been depleted across southern Arizona. Fourteen fish species have been recorded, seven of them native. The report reconstructs historical conditions, documents the fishes, plants and invertebrates, describes water quality and habitat permanence, and warns that introduced species, impoundment, groundwater pumping and population growth threaten the remaining native biota.

## Background & Description

- **Scope and support:** Studies were supported by Arizona State University, the Sport Fishing Institute, the National Science Foundation (NSF Grant GB-154) and [[The Nature Conservancy]], which funded the Sonoita survey.
- **Historical setting:** Before 1890 the creek likely flowed through a broad, marshy floodplain with multiple channels and seepage. The 1890s onset of [[Arroyo Cutting]] transformed it into an incised, sandy-bottomed channel with woody floodplain vegetation. Erosion was recorded regionally over 1875–95, and severe overgrazing followed by drought in 1891–93 is linked to major habitat change.
- **Collection history:** Early records came from the U.S. and Mexican Boundary Surveys (1851–54), [[Edgar A. Mearns]] (1892–94), and F. W. Chamberlain (1904), followed by collections by C. E. Burt, C. L. Hubbs and R. R. Miller (1928–59), providing a chronology of faunal change.
- **Hydrology:** About 10 linear miles of perennial flow remain, including disjunct tributary segments, supplemented by two major springs and several tanks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types and biota**
- **Headsprings:** Distinctive fauna of planarian flatworms, talitrid amphipods, *Hyalella azteca*, a minute hydrobiid snail, mayflies (*Baetis* sp.), belostomatid and naucorid bugs. Rich vegetation including [[Lilaeopsis Recurva]].
- **Permanent ponds:** Bottom fauna dominated by insect larvae, oligochaetes, leeches and ostracods; vegetated ponds are most diverse.
- **Streams:** Mid-channel shifting sands are poor in invertebrates; margins and vegetation clumps are richer. Notonectids and corixids become abundant at low flow.
- **Artificially enriched marshes below Patagonia:** Hold species absent elsewhere, including tubificid oligochaetes, daphnids and snails (*Physa virgata*, *Planorbella tenuis*), linked to organic pollution.

**Water quality**
- Total hardness generally above 200 mg/l, up to 750 mg/l; waters unusual for Arizona in high sulfate and low chloride.
- Monkey Spring source: 81.5°F average, pH 6.8–7.0, dissolved oxygen 2.3–2.6 mg/l, no detectable turbidity.
- Hill Pond: pH 7.2–8.1, Ca²⁺ averaging about 198 mg/l and CO₃²⁻ about 117 mg/l, with carbonate precipitation along canals.
- Flowing streams: near 100% oxygen saturation; mainstream pH 7.5–8.4 (11 samples).
- Thermal regime is complex, shaped by shading and substrate; a thermal shock after a August 1966 spate may have caused fish mortality.
- [[Temporal Creek]] receives seepage from abandoned mines with inorganic pollutants.

**Threats and permanence**
- Pumping from the valley alluvium and impoundment of tributary canyons would reduce flow.
- Increasing fluctuation in water levels is expected, as cienega soils are gone.
- Introduced species, enrichment from Patagonia wastewater, and a proposed major lake on lower Sonoita Creek are key concerns.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish diversity:** Fourteen species recorded in the basin, seven native; the report covers six families: Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae and Centrarchidae.
2. **Gila chub (*Gila robusta intermedia*):** Never taken in Sonoita Creek but thrives at Monkey Spring; once widespread, now abundant at fewer than 10 places. Recommended for consideration as endangered. Males reach under 100 mm versus over 200 mm for females in the Monkey Spring population.
3. **Speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus osculus*):** Considered extinct in Sonoita Creek in 1959; two individuals were caught in 1963, and reproduction and frequent occurrence followed favorable rains in 1966–67.
4. **Longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*):** Dominant fish of Sonoita Creek and most desert watercourses; spawning observed in April 1967 at Patagonia, with 20–30 fish on a sand bar and 2 of 33 spawnings involving two males.
5. **Desert pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*):** Restricted to two localities in Arizona (Quitovaquito Spring and Monkey Spring pond); breeding March–September at Monkey Spring. Recommended for establishment in other ponds.
6. **Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Once ranged across the Gila basin; now totally restricted to the Sonoita Creek drainage (healthy populations at Monkey and Cottonwood springs). Stocks held at Arizona State University as insurance.
7. **Gila mountain-sucker (*Pantosteus clarki*):** Declined by 1959; 2 and 3 specimens caught in 1963–64, but second in abundance to Agosia in 1966–67.
8. **Introduced species:** Goldfish (one caught 1966), carp (not yet recorded in Santa Cruz County), Pacific channel catfish (last taken in the 1950s, apparently failed), black bullhead, mosquitofish, largemouth bass, bluegill and green sunfish. Recommendations include excluding goldfish and carp, drying the Cottonwood tank to destroy mosquitofish, and avoiding spread of green sunfish.
9. **Gila coarse-scaled sucker (*Catostomus insignis*):** Taken once from Sonoita Creek (Chamberlain, 1904); now only in the Babacomari River and very rarely the upper Santa Cruz River.
10. **Plants:** Table 1 lists the aquatic and semi-aquatic taxa, including characean algae, Azolla, several Baccharis species, Potamogeton, and Lemna. Lilaeopsis recurva may now be restricted in the U.S. to Monkey Spring; Ludwigia natans was a first record for Arizona.
11. **Invertebrates:** Only some groups studied, many unidentified. Headsprings, permanent ponds and vegetated margins are the richest habitats.
12. **Conservation conclusion:** Minckley recommends basin-wide planning, a "living museum" approach to protected areas, and an up-to-date file on land and water developments, given that development is likely to make natural aquatic areas hard to maintain.
