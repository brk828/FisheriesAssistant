# Discharge Variability and Predictive Models of Stream Fish Assemblage Structure in Northeastern Australia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether the predictive power of models linking stream fish assemblage structure to habitat varies with [[Discharge Variability]]. Four Queensland rivers were sampled between June 1994 and July 1997: two northern "low-variability" rivers (the [[Mulgrave River]] and [[Johnstone River]]) and two southeastern "variable" rivers (the [[Mary River]] and [[Albert River]]). Using [[Classification and Multiple Discriminant Analysis]] (MDA) models and [[Mantel Test]] comparisons, the authors found that density-based models predicted assemblages better in the low-variability northern rivers. Presence/absence models did not follow the same pattern and were instead shaped largely by differences in [[Beta Diversity]]. Catchment-scale variables explained nearly as much assemblage variation as in-stream habitat variables, and a substantial share of variation remained unexplained. The authors suggest that fish assemblages are better modelled species by species than as discrete units.

## Background & Description

The paper is a research article published in *Ecology of Freshwater Fish* (2000, vol. 9, pp. 30–50) by B. J. Pusey, M. J. Kennard and A. H. Arthington of the [[Centre for Catchment and In-Stream Research]] at Griffith University. It forms part of a larger program on the ecology and [[Environmental Flows]] requirements of freshwater fishes in rivers with different flow variability in Queensland.

The authors frame habitat use by stream fishes as a driver of assemblage structure, with species sorting along gradients of depth, velocity and substrate. They note that these relationships are often weak or obscured. Possible reasons include life-history plasticity, disturbance, resource limitation, predation and other biotic interactions. Regional processes, such as the species pool and landscape "filters" (for example waterfalls, river capture and [[Stream Order]] effects), are also considered important. Flow variability is described as a landscape-scale template that influences species richness, trophic diversity and habitat specialization. Flow regulation tends to make flows more constant, which may alter these relationships. The authors also note that management tools such as the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) rely on assumed flow–habitat–fish relationships that may not transfer between systems with different variability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** Queensland drains about 40% of the continent's annual streamflow from 22.5% of its landmass.

- **Mulgrave and Johnstone rivers** (Wet Tropics, northern Queensland) have steep mountains (maximum altitude 1684 m), dense rainforest, a narrow coastal plain and rainfall of 2000–3500 mm/yr, the highest in Australia. A basalt aquifer sustains dry-season flows. Annual flow CV was 28% (Mulgrave) and 34% (Johnstone). Monthly flows were highly predictable ([[Colwell's Index]] P = 0.67 for both rivers) and tended towards constancy (C = 73–74%). The rivers were classed as low variability.
- **Mary and Albert rivers** (southeastern Queensland, subtropical to temperate climate) have a summer rainfall peak and a minor June–July peak. Annual flow CV was 87.5% (Mary) and 100.5% (Albert). Predictability was low (Mary monthly P = 0.44; Albert minimum monthly P = 0.44). The rivers were classed as variable.

**Sampling design.** Sites were about 40 m long, covered 200–400 m², and comprised a discrete pool, riffle or run. Fish were collected by repeated-pass backpack electrofishing.

**Habitat variables.** Three groups were measured:
- *Catchment variables:* elevation, distance from source and from sea, stream order, riparian cover and catchment area.
- *Physical variables:* site gradient, mean depth, mean water velocity and substrate composition (bedrock through mud).
- *Microhabitat variables:* ten cover types, including macrophytes, woody debris, rootmasses and undercut banks.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and species filtering**
- Sample numbers: 58 (Mulgrave), 144 (Johnstone), 293 (Mary) and 147 (Albert), totalling 642 samples.
- Rare species were excluded. Only species that together made up 99% of each river's catch were retained. In the Mulgrave River, eight species made up less than 1% of individuals.

**Internal validation (Table 1)**

| River | Density success | Presence/absence success |
|---|---|---|
| Mulgrave | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Johnstone | 96.5% | 100.0% |
| Mary | 67.5% | 68.8% |
| Albert | 81.2% | 88.0% |

Habitat–assemblage concordance was therefore higher in the two low-variability northern rivers.

**External validation: density models (Bray-Curtis scores, Table 2)**
- Mulgrave: reference 0.441, test 0.442, random 0.852. Test sites were predicted about as well as possible.
- Johnstone: reference 0.391, test 0.434, random 0.835. Test sites were predicted almost as well as possible.
- Mary: reference 0.539, test 0.589, random 0.858. Predictions were only moderately good.
- Albert: reference 0.461, test 0.565, random 0.786. The model predicted poorly, with substantial overlap between test and random distributions.
- Overall, density models performed better in the two low-variability rivers.

**External validation: presence/absence models**
- Mulgrave: reference 0.316, test 0.345, random 0.658. The model was powerful, and reference and test scores did not differ significantly.
- Albert: reference 0.217, test 0.284, random 0.662.
- Mary: reference 0.275, test 0.323, random 0.663. Overlap with random distributions was high (about 62–65% of scores fell between 0.20 and 0.50), so the model did only marginally better than chance.
- The authors found no clear effect of flow variability on presence/absence predictability. Instead, predictability tracked [[Beta Diversity]] differences between rivers.

**Beta diversity (Table 3)**

| River | Sites | Mean proportion of sites per species | Whittaker's beta | Routledge's beta |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mulgrave | 49 | 0.32 | 2.13 | 0.81 |
| Johnstone | 115 | 0.31 | 2.26 | 0.95 |
| Mary | 234 | 0.47 | 1.14 | 0.57 |
| Albert | 117 | 0.33 | 2.05 | 0.75 |

Most species in the Mary River were widespread, so beta diversity was low and random models performed nearly as well as habitat-based ones.

**Biotic–abiotic association (Mantel tests)**
- All Mantel comparisons were significantly different from random (P < 0.001).
- For density data, the lowest Mantel distance was 0.16 in the Mulgrave River. The other three rivers had minimum distances of at least 0.24, so habitat explained more of the biotic variation in the Mulgrave River.
- For presence/absence data, the lowest distances were 0.14 (Albert), 0.15 (Mulgrave), 0.15 (Mary) and 0.18 (Johnstone).
- Presence/absence data were generally more strongly associated with habitat than density data.
- Catchment variables explained almost as much variation as physical variables. Adding physical variables to catchment variables gave the greatest explanatory power in most cases.
- Microhabitat variables added little beyond catchment and physical variables, except in the Mary River.
- A substantial amount of variation remained unexplained in every river. The authors interpret this as evidence that species vary along individual environmental gradients rather than forming discrete assemblages.

**Conclusions and implications**
- Fish assemblage structure is mainly set by regional and catchment factors, which determine which species occur at a site. Local habitat then influences species abundance. The authors argue that regional differences in flow variability are expressed at this local level.
- Predictive models of fish assemblages are more transferable in low-variability systems, and their applicability depends on the variability of the system in which they are applied.
- The authors recommend modelling the distribution and abundance of individual species rather than whole assemblages. This is relevant to [[Environmental Flows]] assessments, which depend on flow–habitat–fish relationships.
