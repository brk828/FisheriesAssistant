# Comments on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Cui-ui Recovery Plan, Second Revision

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This August 1991 letter, prepared by J. Thomas Barnes of [[LMR Fisheries Research]] (San Diego, California), comments on the second revision of the [[Cui-ui Recovery Plan]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The author argues that the plan's background facts, population model and cost tables are flawed. He asks that cost figures be corrected or removed, that survival and spawning-access parameters in the [[Cui-ui Population Index Subroutine]] (CPIS) be revised, and that the recovery strategy be reevaluated before recovery objectives or implementation schedules are adopted. The central argument is that recent juvenile abundance suggests the [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) is not presently in danger, and that the plan underestimates survival and reproductive potential.

## Background & Description

The comments address sections C (Life History and Habitat), D (Reasons for Listing), E (Recent Conservation Measures), F (Future Conservation Measures) and G (Recovery Strategy) of the plan, plus Appendices A to C. The [[Cui-ui]] is a long-lived catostomid endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]] that spawns in the lower [[Truckee River]]. Its reproduction depends on several structures and management tools:

- **Access routes:** Adults reach spawning grounds either over the [[Delta Passage]] at the lake-river interface or through the [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]] and the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]] (MBFF) below [[Marble Bluff Dam]]. The fishway was designed for [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], not cui-ui.
- **Hatchery program:** Since the 1970s, the [[Hatchery Operations]] at the [[Pyramid Lake Indian Tribal Enterprises]] facility have produced and released larvae.
- **Tagging:** [[Carlin Tags]] were applied to hatchery broodstock from 1971 to 1984.

The author also references [[Lahontan Redside Shiner]] predation on early life stages and the [[Tahoe Sucker]] hybridization issue of 1989.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and lake level.** Spawning access depends on [[Pyramid Lake]] elevation and May-June river flow. Delta passage is possible at or above 3,812 feet MSL (1,161.9 m). The author documents access at elevations as low as 3,792 feet during high-flow years, and some access when May-June inflow exceeds about 50 thousand acre-feet per month.

**Spawning habitat.** About 0.5 km of spawning gravel lies below MBFF. Larvae were produced there in 1984, but egg incubation was not observed there in 1982 or 1983. Spawning run timing may depend on stream temperature, a point the author links to [[Geen et al. 1966]] findings for other catostomids.

**Egg survival and temperature.** Egg survival declines as incubation temperature rises. Cited values are 23.8 to 32.8 percent at 20.6 to 14.4°C. Laboratory and field data for 1985 (10.5 percent) and 1986 (16.0 percent) form the basis of the temperature tie-point. Chatto (1979) found that eggs cannot survive in undiluted lake water.

**Survival rates.** The author proposes these annual values: larvae 0.002, yearlings 0.79, juveniles 0.95 and adults 0.92. The plan's adult value of 0.85 implies that nearly all spawners die each year, which the author considers implausible.

## Key Findings

**1. Juvenile abundance indicates recovery potential.** Sampling captured 3,000 to 4,000 cui-ui in 1988, 90 to 95 percent of them juveniles under seven years old. The author infers a juvenile population of several million and calls this "the first truly positive and quantifiable development" in two decades. Population estimates should include a best point estimate with confidence limits, supported by appendices.

**2. Year-class dominance.** The 1969 year-class made up 97 percent of the 1983 run, 73.2 percent in 1986 and 55.6 percent in 1987. Only three year-classes (1942, 1946, 1950) were recorded before 1966.

**3. Hatchery contribution.** Larval releases averaged 9,314,000 per year in 1982 to 1986, and 10,000,000 were reported for 1987. Hatchery releases since 1972 total over 70 million larvae. Appendix C argues that a 75 percent downward revision of 1979 to 1986 production is unjustified. A recalculation from about 1,334 females, 30,000 eggs per female and 90 percent survival yields 36.0 million larvae for 1980 to 1984, against 34.5 million originally reported. The author recommends continuing the program at 10 million larvae annually, with survival equal to that of natural larvae.

**4. Tag-return bias.** The plan's claim of poor return from hatchery-origin adults is disputed. The author notes that Carlin tags differ from Floy tags in shedding rate and fish impact, and that 39 percent of 1980 Carlin tag returns were previously hatchery-used fish.

**5. Model sensitivity and errors.** The author describes the model as "not robust," citing a wide range of outputs from larval survival sensitivity alone. Requested corrections include:
- Expanding the larval survival sensitivity range to 0.001 to 0.0054.
- Reducing the spillway fallback loss factor from 12.5 to 3.5 percent for years with delta access, since fallback fish can spawn below the dam.
- Adding a third run-size curve for lake levels between 3,792 and 3,812 feet, where delta access occurs under high flow.
- Correcting the egg-survival tie-point from 13.25 percent to 27.3 percent at 66°F. This revision follows the adjustment for May-June flow imbalance and a typographical error that had mislabeled the maximum temperature as 59°F instead of 66°F.

**6. Spawning-run curves.** The maximum fraction of mature females in a run is 23.5 percent. The author argues that the 1983 fishway run should be doubled and the 1982 run increased by about 1.5 to account for delta access.

**7. Delta dredging and artificial channels.** Lowering delta passage to 3,800 feet MSL may equal 25,000 to 40,000 acre-feet per year of supplemental Pyramid Lake inflow, per Figure D-7 of the plan. The author recommends this option receive top implementation priority. Artificial spawning channels are feasible: USFWS data require about 2 square feet of gravel per spawner, so 20,000 spawners need roughly one acre.

**8. Translocation.** The author argues that establishing a second, genetically pure population should be investigated. The 1989 hybridization crisis was later undermined by electrophoretic evidence showing no hybridization, but it illustrated the value of a backup population. Translocation is permitted for 26 of 39 U.S. fish species with approved recovery plans.

**9. Cost tables are incomplete.** Many costs on pages 44 to 48 are listed as "unknown" or tied to unlisted legislation. The author requests deletion of the page iv totals and subtotals unless complete estimates are provided, and says the cost table should not be truncated at 1996.

**10. Recommendations.** The author asks that recovery objectives, action plans and implementation schedules be deferred until these comments are addressed. He recommends further research on what caused recent reproductive success, on predator reduction (especially Lahontan redside shiners), and on the causes of spawning cycles and temperature cues.
