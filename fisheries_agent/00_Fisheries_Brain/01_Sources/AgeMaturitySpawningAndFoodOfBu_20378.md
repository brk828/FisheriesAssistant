# Age, Maturity, Spawning and Food of Burbot in Lake Winnipeg

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lota-lota

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study by L. C. Hewson of the [[Central Fisheries Research Station]] examines the [[Burbot]] (*Lota lota*) in [[Lake Winnipeg]], Manitoba. The study draws on 886 burbot sampled with [[Trap-Net]], [[Otter Trawl]] and [[Gill-Net]] gear. Ages were determined from otoliths. The analysis covers length-weight relationships, growth, sexual maturity, spawning timing and diet. Burbot are regarded as a nuisance by commercial fishermen, since they foul gill nets and waste time, and they are seldom marketed.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Personnel of the Central Fisheries Research Station had studied the summer commercial fishery in the northern part of Lake Winnipeg since 1948. The main focus was [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*), but burbot were also recorded. Burbot usually ranked second by weight in the catches, about 20 per cent of the total weight of the fish studied. Summer catches of all species combined totalled 300,000 to 900,000 pounds annually, from a special-study fraction of about 15 per cent of the fishery. Attention later extended to the fall and winter fisheries in the southern lake, where burbot also held a prominent place.

**Status.** Burbot are considered a game fish in parts of the United States. In Canada, where they occur in most lakes, they are regarded as a nuisance. In Lake Winnipeg they are abundant enough to hinder the gill-net fishery, and some productive grounds are avoided because of them.

**Methods.**
- **Gear:** Trap-nets (large, fully submerged Lake Erie type with baffles), an otter trawl (a flounder drag type), and experimental and commercial gill nets with a range of mesh sizes.
- **Biological data:** Burbot were weighed individually to the nearest tenth of a pound. Stomachs were examined immediately. Sex and maturity were judged from the gonads. Total length was recorded only for the Mukutawa River sample of 1951 and the Winnipeg Beach sample of 1954.
- **Ageing:** Otoliths were removed through a median skull incision, stored, and examined under a low-power microscope. Dark (winter) rings were treated as annual marks, so recorded ages are completed years. Opaque otoliths were filed or ground to improve light penetration.
- **Localities:** The main samples came from trap-nets off [[Mukutawa River]] (1950, 1951, 1953) and off [[Winnipeg Beach]] (1954). Gill-net samples came from Hecla Island, Catfish Creek, Black Bear Island and Kinwow Bay. Otter-trawl samples came from Bull Head.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Of the 886 burbot aged, 719 were from trap-nets and 30 from otter-trawls. The author judged trap-net and otter-trawl catches more representative than gill-net catches. Gill nets were selective: the commercial gill nets at Mukutawa River mainly took larger fish, while the small-mesh nets at Winnipeg Beach took smaller fish. The author suggested the Winnipeg Beach burbot may be virtually unexploited.

**Water conditions at spawning.** Limnological effects on spawning are unknown. Spawning coincided roughly with the period of minimum water temperature, about 1 °C at the bottom and 0 °C under the ice. Dissolved oxygen was fairly constant in January and February at about 8 and 10 cc/litre for bottom and surface samples. Dissolved CO₂ ranged from 10 to 18 p.p.m.

**Prey and associated species.** The main prey were [[Cisco]] (*Leucichthys* spp.), [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*), trout perch, sauger, yellow walleye, yellow perch, sculpins, darters, sticklebacks and freshwater drum. Burbot in trap-nets fed heavily on other fish confined with them, which may bias estimates of natural diet.

## Key Findings

**1. Length-weight relationship differs between localities.**
- Length and weight were recorded for 183 burbot at Mukutawa River and 303 at Winnipeg Beach, all from trap-nets.
- Analysis of covariance gave F = 3.995 for mean length at comparable weight (not significant).
- The rate of change of length with weight gave F = 16.306, which is highly significant.
- For burbot over 4 pounds, Mukutawa River fish are shorter per unit weight than Winnipeg Beach fish. The reverse holds for fish under 4 pounds.
- Fitted power equations were given for each locality. The OCR of the exponents is unreliable, so only the coefficients are reported here: 0.00017 for Mukutawa River and 0.00085 for Winnipeg Beach (weight in pounds, length in inches).

**2. Growth differs between localities.**
- Average weight at age 6 was 3.4 lb at Mukutawa River and 2.6 lb at Winnipeg Beach.
- Average length at age 6 was 22.3 in at Mukutawa River and 20.9 in at Winnipeg Beach.
- Mukutawa River burbot grew faster, despite the cooler water. The author attributed this to heavier exploitation by larger-mesh gill nets, which reduced competition among older fish.
- The Winnipeg Beach sample may be unrepresentative for ages 4 and 5. The growth curve for the gill-net sample was judged a poor estimate, owing to net selection.
- The trap-net was assumed to be fully efficient only for burbot of 8 lb or more, so fish older than about 6 or 7 were fully vulnerable.

**3. Maturity.**
- Some burbot at Mukutawa River probably matured by age 2 and at less than 1 lb.
- Immature females averaged 1.2 lb and immature males 1.9 lb, suggesting females mature at lower weight, possibly at a younger age.
- The largest immature female weighed 1.5 lb and the largest immature male 2.2 lb.
- Immaturity was more closely linked to weight than to age.
- Some mature fish do not spawn every year.

**4. Spawning period.**
- Males were near-ripe by about January 20 in each of four winters beginning in 1950.
- Spawning probably began about January 31 and lasted about three weeks.
- Many fish were spent by February 10, and ripe unspawned fish of both sexes occurred as late as February 20.
- About 5 per cent of mature fish on the grounds were unready to spawn each year.
- Catches under the ice suggested spawning occurs near the surface. This agrees with [[Cahn]] (1936), who observed spawning in a Minnesota lake.
- Spawning fish were common inshore and offshore, though more numerous in inshore nets.

**5. Food habits.**
- More than 500 stomachs were examined. About 69 per cent of the more than 400 trap-net stomachs from Mukutawa River contained food.
- In about one-third of stomachs with food, fish was the only item. Another third held crayfish only, and the rest held both.
- Among fish-containing stomachs, cisco was the main fish item in 84 per cent. Sauger, yellow walleye and yellow perch together occurred in 7 per cent. Coregonines occurred in 2 per cent. In 57 per cent, the fish were too digested to identify.
- Of 30 otter-trawl burbot examined, 27 stomachs were analysed and 16 contained fish, which were mostly under 5 inches long.
- Younger burbot ate mainly small bottom organisms, mostly Ephemeridae (mayfly) larvae. Older burbot took fish and crayfish.

**6. Gear suitability.** The otter trawl was judged the best gear for diet studies. Its catches were fresh and the crayfish could be dated. The author recommended a longer trawl operation to build a larger sample.

**7. Summary of conclusions.**
- Burbot from two localities differ significantly in length-weight relationship.
- Exploitation probably accelerated growth in one locality.
- Trap-net and otter-trawl samples are more representative than gill-net samples.
- Diet depends on size: insect larvae for small fish, fish and crayfish for large fish.
