# Fish, Fish Management, and Fishing in West Virginia (1948)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1948 angler's guide, produced by the [[Division of Fish Management]] of the [[Conservation Commission of West Virginia]] and written by fishery biologist E. A. Seaman, explains the state's freshwater fish resources, how they are managed, and where anglers can fish. It argues that fishing quality has declined because of [[Pollution]], silt, deforestation, soil erosion, dry streams and heavy fishing pressure. It promotes [[Scientific Fish Management]] based on inventories, field research and creel censuses rather than opinion. The booklet also describes [[Hatcheries]], [[Farm Fish Ponds]], and a list of major fishing waters grouped by drainage.

## Background & Description

The booklet opens with a historical comparison: early anglers on the Shaver's Fork of the [[Cheat River]] around 1918 reportedly caught hundreds of trout a day, while the modern fishing public is far larger and the resource much smaller. The author frames the response as [[Conservation]]: to "conserve, reserve, restore, replenish, and manage" what remains.

The state's fishing waters are organized into drainage systems:

- **Monongahela system:** Cheat, Buckhannon, Tygart and West Fork rivers.
- **Direct Ohio River tributaries:** Middle Island Creek, Wheeling Creek, Little Kanawha River and others.
- **Great Kanawha system:** New River (from North Carolina through Virginia), Greenbrier and Gauley rivers, and the main Kanawha.
- **Southern drainages:** Guyandotte and Big Sandy rivers.
- **Potomac and James systems:** headwaters in West Virginia, plus Potts Creek in Monroe County.

West Virginia has no natural lakes. Its main standing waters are artificial:

- [[Tygart Reservoir]] (Taylor and Barbour counties), about one-half mile wide and twelve miles long.
- [[Cheat Lake]] (Lake Lynn, near Morgantown), privately owned, averaging one-quarter mile or more in width and about eight miles long, but open to public fishing.
- [[Bluestone Reservoir]], a flood-control project due for completion in 1948, impounding at least eight miles of the New River near Hinton. Expected to offer [[Yellow Pikeperch]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]] and crappie fishing.
- [[Bear Rock Lakes]], three small lakes under construction on Tedd Run about fifteen miles from Wheeling, designed as intensive production fisheries.

The state fishing license covers more than 20 kinds of fish, or more than 22 when species within some families are counted. Licensed kinds include trout, black bass, sunfish, crappie, perch, drum, catfish, bullhead, suckers, redhorse, pickerel and [[Muskellunge]]. The booklet notes that more than 140 species occur in the state's waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The booklet identifies several threats to fishing waters:

- **Silt loads** from soil erosion, which are harmful to fish life.
- **Industrial and domestic wastes** that have polluted miles of streams.
- **Overcutting of forests**, which causes some streams to run dry in sections.
- **Heavy fishing pressure** on a shrinking resource.

Fishing waters are not uniform. Trout streams include:

- Williams River
- Seneca Creek
- Back Fork of Elk River
- North Fork of Cherry River
- North Fork of South Branch of Potomac River
- Cranberry River
- East Fork of Greenbrier River

About 150 minor trout streams are also noted. Major bass streams include the South Branch of the Potomac, New River, Greenbrier, Cacapon, Elk, Middle Island Creek, Little Kanawha, Hughes, Wheeling Creek, Coal River, Pocataligo, and parts of the West Fork. The author notes that many good bass waters lie in the Ohio River drainage, not only the Potomac.

Other notable waters:

- **Muskellunge:** Middle Island Creek, Little Kanawha, Pocataligo, Coal (in part), Elk and smaller tributaries of the Ohio and Great Kanawha.
- **Yellow pikeperch:** abundant in the New River, also in the Elk and Greenbrier.
- **Catfish:** New, Little Kanawha, Gauley, Coal, Elk, Middle Island, Greenbrier, West Fork (in part) and lower Great Kanawha.

[[Farm Fish Ponds]] are spreading across the state through cooperation with Soil Conservation Districts. Equipment was loaned to eight districts, and the ponds relieve fishing pressure on streams.

## Key Findings

**Management principles**

- Fish management is treated as a scientific discipline requiring inventories, investigations and research. Stocking hatchery fish without management has largely failed to improve fishing.
- Each stream needs a survey to determine its carrying capacity, fish population and food base.
- Every impoundment should be placed under management, and more impoundments are recommended to replace lost stream fishing.
- Soil conservation, forest management and continued [[Pollution Control]] are considered essential. The state [[Water Commission]] is credited with guiding industries and municipalities toward better waste treatment.

**Ongoing field studies (as of 1947–1948)**

- **Depth distribution:** Studies at Tygart and Cheat reservoirs found that game and rough fish move vertically, sometimes from the surface to the greatest depths in warm periods. Angling techniques must adapt accordingly, and year-round fishing on Tygart was judged beneficial.
- **[[Creel Census]]:** State-wide creel censuses are underway, especially on trout streams, to measure catch relative to stocking. The aim is to replace opinion with facts.
- **[[Tagging and Migration]]:** Several thousand channel catfish were tagged in 1947. They migrated more than two hundred miles downstream after stocking, with some averaging seven miles per day. Trout were fin-clipped to track migration, stocking effectiveness and winter survival.
- **Fish distribution collections:** Extensive collections across streams and impoundments use fyke nets for large species such as muskellunge, flathead catfish, sturgeon and paddlefish. Seining documents forage minnow abundance.
- **[[Age and Growth Studies]]:** Fish ages are determined from scale readings, similar to tree rings. The goal is to learn how long bass take to reach spawning or legal size.
- **[[Stream Surveys]]:** Surveys of minor trout streams examine dry-season flow, water temperature, acidity, food supply (insects and minnows), winter ice conditions and angler numbers.

**Hatcheries**

- Hatchery work requires constant attention: daily feeding, disease control, grading, and water-supply management. Trout are especially sensitive to water shortages, and cold snaps during bass spawning can cause losses that cannot be recovered.
- Hatchery fish may restock denuded waters or heavily fished trout streams, but only follow-up studies can show whether they reach anglers. The booklet notes that many hatchery fish never reach a creel, and that feeding costs limit expansion.
- Stocked trout studies on Seneca Creek and the North Fork of Cherry River are described as valuable. The effectiveness of minnow stocking was unproven but considered beneficial in larger streams.

**Public guidance**

- The author argues that anglers' local knowledge is vital and that cooperation with creel censuses is necessary.
- The booklet closes with conservation actions for individuals: prevent forest fires, practice soil conservation, fish sportingly rather than as a "meat hog," report violations, challenge polluters, and support research.
- A reading list is provided, including Hubbs and Lagler's *Fishes of the Great Lakes Region* and Swingle and Smith's *Management of Farm Fish Ponds*. A tentative list of West Virginia fishes was compiled by Dr. E. C. Raney.
