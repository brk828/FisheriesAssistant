# Post-Hatching Development of Red Shiner, Spawning Tubercles in Minnows, and Spotted Seatrout Subpopulations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled source (partly OCR-degraded) contains three linked items. The first is a discussion of breeding tubercles in North American minnows (*Notropis*), arguing that head tubercle arrangement tracks spawning behavior. The second is a laboratory study of the [[Post-Hatching Development]] of the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), covering prolarval through juvenile stages, obtained by hormone-induced spawning. The third is a fisheries study of [[Spotted Seatrout]] (*Cynoscion nebulosus*) from Florida, testing whether the population is split into subpopulations using growth and tagging data. A literature list of roughly two dozen references closes the document.

## Background & Description

**Breeding tubercles and spawning behavior.** Nuptial tubercles on the head, body and fins of minnows are used during the spawning act. Species differ in how they spawn. Frenzied spawners with pronounced fighting, such as [[Notropis Cornutus]] (common shiner), have well-developed head tubercle lines and many scattered organs. Sedate spawners have only the lines. Species spawning in rapidly flowing water or in large congregations (for example, *N. nubila*) have numerous small organs to ensure close contact, and less developed head lines. The source cautions that head rows are probably primitive within a group, so they must be interpreted alongside life history and behavior.

**Red shiner larval study.** The study aimed to link age with gross morphology in the postlarval stages of the red shiner, because field biologists struggle to identify young-of-year fishes and most early fish stages are undescribed. Gravid fish were collected on 15 July 1960 from a small creek in Norman, Oklahoma, and held at the Oklahoma University Fisheries Research Center.

**Spotted seatrout fisheries.** The species is a major commercial and recreational fish in Florida. Commercial landings in 1959 had an ex-vessel value reported in the source (figure partly illegible in OCR), and anglers reported poorer catches. Concerns about overexploitation and habitat alteration (dredging, filling, and freshwater diversion) prompted state-funded studies at several sites from 1951 to 1960.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning conditions.** Red shiner eggs were demersal and adhesive. Spawning occurred only at night, and only in the aquarium with a male-to-female ratio of 5:6. Intense courtship occurred in all other aquaria, but no spawning was observed there. The source notes that the species probably spawns naturally in groups, though the data were too limited to prove this. Water temperature on the spawning day was about 77°F. Eggs hatched at 77–82°F.

**Rearing environment.** Larvae were kept in 10-gallon and 16-gallon aquaria with aerated water. Food came from a pond with an algal bloom, which was supplemented with commercial fertilizer when the bloom declined. Acriflavin was added occasionally as a disease preventive.

**Egg size comparisons.** Egg diameter in *N. lutrensis* was 1.2 mm. Comparison values from the literature were 1.5 mm for *N. rubellus* and *N. bifrenatus*, 0.8–0.9 mm for *N. chalybaeus*, and 1.25–1.4 mm for [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*).

**Seatrout stock structure.** Growth and tagging data came from several Florida sites, with studies at Indian River, Flamingo, St. Marks–Apalachicola, Cedar Key, and Fort Myers. Females grow faster than males, and the sex ratio of catches varied by area.

## Key Findings

### Red Shiner Development

- **Hatching:** Eggs hatched 57–72 hours after spawning. The mean egg diameter was 1.2 mm.
- **Prolarva (3 days):** Total length 4.7 mm. Yolk sac elongated and compact. Pigmented eyes, fin folds, and a single row of melanophores along the body. Myomeres numbered 19 before the vent and 14 behind.
- **Postlarva (7 days):** Yolk sac fully absorbed. Mouth open and gut content showed that feeding had begun.
- **Postlarva (9 days):** Caudal fin separating from the median fin fold.
- **Postlarva (15 days):** Four pectoral fin rays. Upper and lower jaws developed.
- **Postlarva (20 days):** Forked caudal fin with 19 rays. Gas bladder divided into two unequal chambers.
- **Postlarva (25 days):** Caudal rays segmented. Dorsal fin with 9 rays, anal fin with 8.
- **Postlarva (30 days):** Pelvic fins each with six rays.
- **Juvenile (34 days):** Fin development nearly complete, but rays not yet branched. Scales present. Myomeres no longer evident.
- **Stage durations:** Four days from prolarva to postlarva. About 30 days from postlarva to juvenile. The abstract's "80 days" appears to be an OCR error, since the conclusions give 30 days.

### Conclusions from the Larval Study

1. The study is a preliminary account of the larval stages of the red shiner, from hatching to the juvenile stage.
2. Spawning was induced by injecting gravid females intraperitoneally with a water extract of acetone-dried carp pituitary (0.1 cc).
3. Development follows the general pattern typical of teleosts. Eggs are demersal and adhesive.
4. Hatching occurred 57–72 hours after spawning.
5. Time from hatching to juvenile was 34 days for *N. lutrensis*, compared with about six weeks for *N. bifrenatus*, 72–91 days for *N. chalybaeus*, and nine weeks for *Carassius auratus*.

### Breeding Tubercle Conclusions

Tubercle arrangement can be correlated with observed spawning behavior, but only when interpreted with life history knowledge. Frenzied, fighting spawners have well-developed lines and scattered organs. Spawners in fast water or large groups have many small organs and less developed head lines.

### Spotted Seatrout Subpopulation Findings

- **Growth rates:** Growth rates differed among study areas, suggesting separate subpopulations.
- **Recapture data:** About 95 percent of recaptured seatrout were taken near where they were tagged. This points to a close association between the fish and their local environment.
- **Sex ratio:** The sex ratio of catches varied by area and by year, with some samples showing more females.
- **Overall aim:** The study sought to determine whether Florida's spotted seatrout form separate subpopulations. The growth and tagging evidence supports this, and the authors planned to add meristic and morphometric comparisons to the analysis.
