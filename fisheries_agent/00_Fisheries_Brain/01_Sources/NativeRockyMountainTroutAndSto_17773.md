# Native Rocky Mountain Trout and Stocked Trout: Can We Have Both?

#ichthyology #salmonidae #cutthroat-trout #conservation-biology #species-interactions

## Executive Summary

This paper examines the status and distribution of native trout species in the [[Rocky Mountains]] and evaluates the impacts of introduced non-native trout species. Native [[Cutthroat Trout]] subspecies and [[Bull Trout]] have experienced dramatic range contractions, with most populations now restricted to isolated headwater streams representing small fractions of historic ranges. The primary threats include genetic introgression through hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]], competition and displacement by [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]], predation, and differential angling vulnerability. Several subspecies are under consideration for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The paper draws heavily on U.S. Forest Service conservation assessments and Behnke's (1992) monograph on native western trout, documenting both immediate and long-term impacts of non-native salmonid introductions across stream ecosystems throughout the region.

## Background & Description

The family [[Salmonidae]] evolved approximately 50 million years ago (Eocene Epoch) into three major subgroups: graylings, whitefishes, and trout/salmon. During the Oligocene (30-40 million years ago), the trout-salmon subgroup divided into chars (genus *Salvelinus*) and a branch containing *Salmo* and *Oncorhynchus*. About 15 million years ago, further differentiation produced an Atlantic Ocean group (*Salmo*) and Pacific Ocean group (*Oncorhynchus*).

**Native Species Covered:**

- **[[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]]** (*O. c. bouvieri*): Isolated in the [[Snake River]] above 65-m-high [[Shoshone Falls]] in southcentral Idaho, subsequently dispersing into the [[Green River]] of the [[Colorado River]] basin
- **[[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]]** (*O. c. lewisi*): Extended into [[Columbia River]] basin, impacted by catastrophic floods from ice-dammed [[Lake Missoula]], crossed Continental Divide to Saskatchewan and Upper Missouri drainages
- **[[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]** (*O. c. pleuriticus*): Native to upper tributaries of Colorado River in Colorado, Wyoming, New Mexico, and Utah
- **[[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]]** (*O. c. stomias*): Established in South Platte and Arkansas headwaters of Colorado and Wyoming
- **[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]** (*O. c. virginalis*): Native to Rio Grande basin of Colorado and New Mexico
- **[[Bull Trout]]** (*Salvelinus confluentus*): Formally recognized as distinct species in 1978, represents more recent immigration from Asia, native only to western North America from 41°N to 60°N latitude
- **[[Montana Arctic Grayling]]** (*Thymallus arcticus montanus*): Once abundant in upper Missouri River drainage above Great Falls

**Introduced Species:**

- **[[Brook Trout]]** (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): Native to eastern North America, introduced to Rocky Mountains 120 years ago, stocked in all western states by 1930s
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): First introduced outside native range in 1874, widely introduced throughout western states in 1880s-1890s
- **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*): Native to Europe, northern Africa, and western Asia, officially introduced to North America in 1883

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native cutthroat trout subspecies evolved in relative ecological vacuums with depauperate fish faunas. [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] in [[Yellowstone Lake]] coexisted only with longnose dace (*Rhinichthys cataractae*). Most subspecies evolved with species of *Cottus* (sculpins), *Catostomus* (suckers), and *Rhinichthys* (dace), but not with other competitive salmonids like rainbow trout.

[[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]] experienced more substantial interactions, evolving with [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*) and [[Bull Trout]], showing substantial segregation for food and space with these more benthic species. They were naturally sympatric with chinook salmon and rainbow trout/steelhead in the [[Salmon River]] and [[Clearwater River]] in Idaho, though surviving sympatric fluvial populations were not found, with cutthroat occupying upper stream portions.

[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] evolved with only minnow and sucker species at middle to upper elevations (2,000-2,600 m), including Rio Grande sucker (*Catostomus plebius*) and Rio Grande chub (*Gila pandora*).

[[Bull Trout]] evolved with and continue to coexist with cutthroat trout, rainbow trout, chinook salmon, sockeye salmon, mountain whitefish, and several sculpin and minnow species. Being the only apex predator likely provided an energetic advantage.

**Life History Disadvantages:**

Interior cutthroat trout stocks exhibit several characteristics indicating innate inability to compete with introduced salmonids:
- Lack of morphological specialization
- Smaller body size (juvenile coastal cutthroat averaged 10% less weight than equal-length steelhead)
- Later emergence from gravel, creating persistent size disadvantage
- Innately less aggressive than equal-sized rainbow trout or steelhead
- Highly susceptible to angling, more readily caught than brook or brown trout

**Environmental Factors:**

Condition-specific competition is strongly influenced by abiotic factors:
- **Gradient**: Brook trout not expected to replace cutthroat if gradient exceeds ~7% (sub-optimal for brook trout)
- **Temperature**: Bull, brook, and coastal cutthroat have similar cold temperature preferences; where annual heat budgets <1,600 degree-days, brook trout replaced native *Oncorhynchus mykiss*, but not in warmer downstream reaches
- Laboratory studies show brook trout more aggressive and consumed more food at 20°C, but species nearly equal competitors at 10°C

## Key Findings

**Current Distribution and Status:**

**[[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Currently most abundant interior cutthroat subspecies
- Populations of substantial purity occur in ~2,400 km of streams (approximately 10% of historic stream habitat)
- ~38,500 ha of lacustrine habitat (about 85% of historic level)
- 91% of present range lies within [[Yellowstone National Park]]
- In Montana, only 8% (~320 km of original 4,200 km) of fluvial range apparently inhabited by genetically unaltered populations
- Considered taxon of special concern by Idaho, Montana, and Wyoming

**[[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Distribution and abundance both in decline
- Persist in only 27% of historic range in Montana
- Pure populations in only 2.5% of native Montana range
- Strong populations (≥50% of historic potential) remain in only 11% of stream reaches in Idaho historic range
- Less than 4% of Idaho historic range supports strong populations not threatened by hybridization
- Considered sensitive by U.S. Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management

**[[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Restricted to headwater streams and lakes
- Occupies less than 1% of historic range
- By 1977 in Wyoming, restricted to 40 streams and 2 lakes; only 8 populations regarded as genetically pure
- Most stream populations in isolated, small, high-gradient headwater streams above 2,290 m
- Of 37 populations sampled 1978-1987 in northwestern Colorado, 12 declined in genetic purity and 4 disappeared
- 1992 study of 50 streams on [[White River National Forest]]: disappeared from 8 streams, not expected to remain long in 11 others
- Considered sensitive by U.S. Forest Service, Category 2 by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service

**[[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Only 5 natural pure populations survive, all in isolated headwaters or alpine lakes in Colorado
- Originally listed as endangered, listed as threatened since 1978
- Recovery plan goal: preserve and re-establish 20 stable populations throughout historic range

**[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]:**
- Restricted primarily to first-order headwater tributaries
- 62 populations of substantial purity known in New Mexico (all but 7 on national forests)
- Exists in 34 Rio Grande tributaries and 5 lakes to the north, most within national forests
- Regarded as sensitive by U.S. Forest Service, taxon of special concern by American Fisheries Society

**[[Bull Trout]]:**
- Formally recognized as distinct species in 1978
- Native from northern California to Yukon River headwaters, coastal rivers east to Saskatchewan, Peace, and Liard rivers
- Populations declined and distribution distinctly fragmented
- Recognized as taxon of special concern by some state agencies and American Fisheries Society
- Categorized as indicator species by U.S. Forest Service
- Petitioned for emergency endangered listing in 1992; May 1993 ruling found enough evidence of decline to formally consider listing

**[[Montana Arctic Grayling]]:**
- Once abundant in all larger streams in upper Missouri River drainage above Great Falls
- Now relegated to remnant populations in less than 8% of native range
- Stream-resident populations found only in upper [[Big Hole River]] and tributaries in Montana
- Identified as taxon of special concern by American Fisheries Society

**Immediate Impacts of Stocking:**

Seven potential immediate effects identified from stocked individuals (typically first few months):
1. Predation by stocked trout
2. Increased mortality from increased fishing effort
3. Increased harvest as wild fish feed more actively
4. Hatchery trout supplanting wild fish from quality habitat
5. Hatchery fish mass emigration stimulating wild fish to follow ("pied piper" effect documented in [[Wenatchee River]])
6. Stress in wild fish induced by hatchery fish presence
7. Disease spread by hatchery fish

Studies show mixed results: [[St. Joe River]] (Idaho) stocking showed overt aggression but cutthroat abundance remained stable except at highest densities. Alberta stream study found wild westslope cutthroat not supplanted and did not experience increased mortality. Oregon streams showed increased harvest of wild coastal cutthroat and bull trout when stocked with rainbow trout, with catch varying directly with angler effort.

**Long-term Impacts of Naturalized Populations:**

**1. Hybridization and Genetic Introgression:**
- Interior cutthroat trout (except some westslope populations) evolved apart from rainbow/redband trout and lack innate isolating mechanisms
- When rainbow trout established, mass hybridization with cutthroat almost invariably followed
- Most important cause for decline of westslope cutthroat in Montana
- Primary threat to Yellowstone cutthroat throughout historic range
- Initially, more Colorado River cutthroat populations lost to hybridization than any other factor
- Bull trout hybridization with brook trout common; little temporal or spatial spawning segregation
- Hybrids likely sterile with developmental abnormalities

**2. Predation (Piscivority):**
- Brook and rainbow trout predation generally not a concern
- Brown trout implicated in reduction of Rio Grande cutthroat, westslope cutthroat, bull trout, and Montana grayling (but not necessarily Yellowstone cutthroat)
- Brown trout affect species composition and abundance where predaceous fishes previously lacking
- Brown trout switch to fish diet at ~30 cm size threshold
- Brown trout typically absent from headwater streams, altitudinal distribution appears temperature-limited
- Where brown trout gain access to cutthroat populations with favorable habitat, rapid replacement expected

**3. Differential Angling Vulnerability:**
- Cutthroat trout highly susceptible to angling
- More readily caught than brook trout and especially brown trout
- Selective harvest depletes native species accessible to anglers
- [[South Fork Snake River]]: special regulations protecting only cutthroat resulted in 79% density increase for cutthroat but no change in brown trout density

**4. Greater Reproductive Potential:**
- Brook trout typically mature earlier than bull trout; combined with hybridization could eliminate resident bull trout
- Migratory bull trout larger with higher fecundity than brook trout
- Brook trout may gain reproductive advantage over slower-maturing cutthroat
- Brown trout longevity often substantially exceeds interior cutthroat, carrying significant advantage

**5. Displacement and Competition:**
- Interspecific competition involves securing territories for space, food, or cover
- Continued interaction produces niche shift, extinction of one species, or fluctuating coexistence
- "Ecological crunch" hypothesis: periods of severe resource shortages interspersed with periods when numbers depressed by floods/droughts
- Competition most intense in systems where extrinsic events least frequent
- Lakes: trout populations less regulated by abiotic events, ecological crunches more frequent than streams

**Competitive Disadvantages of Cutthroat Trout:**
- Lack of morphological specialization
- Smaller body size (10% less weight than equal-length competitors)
- Later fry emergence creates persistent size disadvantage
- Less aggressive than equal-sized competitors
- Displacement occurs very early in life: rainbow/steelhead excluded first 2-3 age classes of coastal cutthroat and bull trout in mid-Columbia tributaries
- Colorado stream study: no adult competition evidence between greenback cutthroat and brook trout, but juveniles shifted positions after brook trout removal; low flow years forced cutthroat to compete with age-0 brook trout sooner

**Documented Replacement Events:**

Numerous accounts document native trout demise:
- Nearly all greenback and Colorado River cutthroat stocks extirpated by competition, hybridization, or habitat destruction
- [[Yellowstone National Park]]: brook trout introduction almost always resulted in cutthroat disappearance
- Replacement typically occurs in ~10 years (roughly 3 fish generations)
- [[Gunnison River]] (Colorado): rainbow trout stocked 1888, by 1897 rainbow fishery flourished and native cutthroat virtually disappeared
- [[Henrys Fork]] of Snake River (Idaho): Yellowstone cutthroat virtually disappeared within decade following 1930s rainbow trout introduction
- Colorado River tributary: brook trout replaced Colorado River cutthroat between 1970-1975
- [[Madison River]]/[[Firehole River]]/[[Gibbon River]] (Yellowstone): rainbow stocked 1889-90, brown trout in upper Firehole, both introduced to Madison 1929-30; by 1930s grayling and cutthroat nearly gone
- [[Priest Lake]] (Idaho) and [[Flathead Lake]] (Montana): lake trout may have severely depressed migratory bull trout through predation, competitive displacement, or other processes

**Persistence Patterns:**

Native trout not extirpated where:
- Bull trout distribution in mid-Columbia tributaries terminated in reaches not blocked by barriers, suggesting requirement for larger streams than brook or cutthroat trout
- Except some Yellowstone stocks, cutthroat populations survive only as fragmented headwater stocks in highly variable environments
- Cutthroat population <2,000 individuals may not persist over 100 years; many populations fail to meet this criterion

**Management Implications:**

- Native fish disappearing faster than knowledge accumulates
- [[Endangered Species Act]] increasingly affecting management decisions on federal lands
- Recovery plan proceeding for greenback cutthroat trout
- Calls for federal regulation of salmonid hatchery programs jeopardizing declining wild stocks
- Prudent management: define uniqueness of stocks, races, and populations; prioritize most significant units relative to extinction risks and "irreplaceability"
- Extent of current conservation efforts varies considerably among states
- Genetic variability better understood; increased realization that manipulation negatively impacts performance traits
- Local populations often unique, best managed as distinct stocks
- Relatively little learned about exact nature of interactions between introduced and native trout
