# Lower Colorado River Basin Management Plan for "Big-River" Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This revised draft, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) Parker Fishery Resource Office and submitted to the Region 2 Regional Director in November 1996, proposes a conservation management program for four endangered "big-river" fishes of the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The plan argues that no comprehensive recovery framework exists for the lower basin, that dedicated funding has been lacking, and that recovery requires ecosystem-level action focused on recruitment. Its main strategy is to dedicate isolated, non-native-free habitats (chiefly on National Wildlife Refuges), maintain brood stocks at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], manage genetic diversity, and coordinate all efforts through a lower-basin Recovery Team and Coordinator. The plan is designed to complement, not replace, existing recovery plans.

## Background & Description

**Basin context.** The Colorado River basin drains a very large area of western North America, including parts of seven U.S. states and two Mexican states. Elevations range from above 4000 m in the Rocky Mountain headwaters to below sea level in the Salton Sea/Laguna Salada basins. For administration, the basin is split at Lees Ferry, near [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The lower basin comprises the Colorado mainstem and the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila subbasins. Its waters are described as more heavily used than any comparable drainage in the USA and perhaps the world. (The scanned area figure is illegible and is not reproduced here.)

**Native fauna.** The basin originally held 34 native fish taxa (species and subspecies), a small number for a basin of its size, attributed to long-term regional aridification. Isolation and environmental severity promoted endemism: 21 of the 34 taxa (62%) are endemic, the highest proportion of any western North American watershed. Two taxa have gone extinct in the past 100 years. The lower basin fauna includes one extinct species and several threatened or endangered species; the scanned count for the latter is not reliably legible. The four "big-river" fishes once made up more than half the fish fauna of the Colorado mainstem below Lees Ferry.

**Species profiles (summarized from the text):**
- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*): still infrequently caught in Lakes Mohave and Havasu.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): largest known population is in the Grand Canyon area, mostly in the lowermost [[Little Colorado River]].
- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): extirpated from the lower basin; reintroduced as an "experimental, nonessential" population in the Salt and Verde rivers, Arizona.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): largest remaining population is in [[Lake Mohave]]; wild individuals are occasionally captured in Lake Mead, Lake Havasu, and downstream.

All four share traits suited to a harsh, variable river: powerful musculature, hydrodynamic bodies, falcate fins, leathery skins, and high temperature tolerance. Their decline is attributed to habitat loss and modification plus interactions with [[Non-Native Fishes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic conditions.** Before impoundment, the mainstem alternated between narrow canyons with large, violent rapids and broad valley reaches with slow flow. Snowmelt drove high, cold spring flows and low summer and winter flows. Heavy alluvial sediment produced high flood turbidity, and desert streams reached high summer temperatures. Native fishes evolved under short floods and drought alternating with longer periods of variable, less extreme discharge.

**Drivers of decline.**
- *Habitat loss:* long dry reaches of the middle and lower Gila River, caused by upstream impoundments and groundwater mining.
- *Flow regulation:* dams block movement corridors, release water too cold for spawning, and suppress natural flow variance through power-generation operations.
- *Predation and competition:* predation on eggs, fry, and juveniles by non-native fishes is identified as a major biological factor. Reservoirs, livestock tanks, and sport- and forage-fish stocking sustain these alien species. Striped bass and ictalurid catfishes also thrive in the basin.

The plan notes two interrelated effects: physical stabilization of the river, and a biological shift favouring lake-adapted non-native species. Efforts that force native fish into highly controlled environments may have similar harmful effects.

**Why a new plan is needed.** No comprehensive plan exists for lower-basin listed fishes. Upper-basin plans do not transfer well because the upper basin has more free-flowing river and substantial dedicated research and management funding. The lower basin has little free-flowing habitat, and no dedicated recovery funding has existed. Existing actions are also fragmented, including recovery plans, [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] requirements, a [[Jeopardy Biological Opinion]] on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, and a [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] Environmental Assessment.

## Key Findings

**Program rationale**
- Recovery scenarios are chosen for likelihood of survival, reestablishment, and recovery, given that the lower mainstem is highly and irreversibly modified.
- Off-channel habitats are identified as having the highest probability of success with minimal conflict with water use.
- The program stresses **recruitment** as the bottleneck, supported by reproductive biology data, wild larvae from Lake Mohave, and hatchery stocks.
- Long generation times mean results may take decades, so planning must match the time scales of water development projects rather than annual budgets.

**Recovery levels and objectives.** The program defines six levels: (I) prevent extinction; (II) acquire maximum existing genetic variability; (III) stabilize population size, structure, and genetics; (IV) reintroduce and reestablish stocks; (V) promote self-sustaining status; and (VI) downlist or delist. Status by species:
- Bonytail is at Level I (extinction risk) and needs immediate action.
- Humpback Chub is less endangered, with genetic information available and stabilization and expansion underway.
- Razorback Sucker faces an apparent collapse of the Lake Mohave stock, but genetic data exist and stabilization is well underway.
- Colorado Squawfish has no wild fish remaining in the lower basin, so it requires a distinct approach.

**Shared regional approach.** All four species became rare at about the same time (early 1950s), apparently through similar recruitment failure. Experimental data show that all four survive to maturity when protected from non-native fishes during early life stages. The plan therefore treats them as a coevolved, collectively managed unit.

**Implementation actions**
1. **Preventing extinction:** Build or identify isolated habitats from which non-native fish are excluded, starting on National Wildlife Refuges (Bill Williams River, Cibola, Havasu, Imperial). Stock bonytail once they reach about 300 mm total length, for augmentation, translocation, or as a catastrophe buffer. Humpback Chub habitat will be within or associated with Grand Canyon National Park.
2. **Hatchery facilities:** Dexter National Fish Hatchery is pivotal, holding brood stocks of all species except humpback chub. A humpback chub stock should be acquired from the Grand Canyon/Little Colorado River. Willow Beach NFH and AGFD's Bubbling Ponds Hatchery may also contribute.
3. **Fish health:** Monitor and assure health of hatchery, native, and stocked fish annually and before site-to-site transfers.
4. **Genetic concerns:** Maximize founder numbers (wild larvae collection for razorbacks, which show positive phototaxis, is ongoing at Lake Mohave), use pedigreed hatchery propagation where wild larvae or embayments are unavailable, and minimize inbreeding in brood stocks.
5. **Population stabilization and expansion:** Create or renovate large (about 80 ha or larger), isolated, semi-natural habitats managed within an ecosystem framework. Expand to non-federal lands, especially tributaries, within a decade. Use reservoir drawdowns and refilling as opportunities to stock adults and larvae when predators are absent.
6. **Habitat maintenance:** Periodically remove non-native fish, evaluate native–non-native interactions (especially large predators), and recreate lost off-channel features such as oxbows and backwaters by dredging or excavation.

**Program coordination**
- A lower-basin Coordinator would oversee the program, consult with upper-basin representatives, and advise the Regional Director.
- A single Recovery Team would address all four species, with no more than half its members drawn from agencies that affect the taxa. Team members are chosen for species knowledge, basin ecosystem expertise, and hydrology.
- The Coordinator would meet USFWS administration at least twice a year and convene annual working sessions to set brood stock, husbandry, and population stabilization decisions by consensus.

**Conclusion.** The plan proposes that lower-basin big-river fish recovery be built on integrated, long-term, recruitment-focused ecosystem management, with dedicated funding and coordination, complementing existing upper-basin and species-specific recovery plans.
