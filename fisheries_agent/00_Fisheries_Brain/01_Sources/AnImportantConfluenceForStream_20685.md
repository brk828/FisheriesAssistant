# Integrating Scales and Perspectives in Stream Ecology: A Symposium Overview

#stream-ecology #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This TREE (Vol. 11, No. 7, July 1996) feature by [[David D. Hart]] and [[Dina M. Fonseca]] reviews the symposium *New Concepts in Stream Ecology: An Integrative Approach*, organized by J. Vaun McArthur and Peter Koetsier and held at the [[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]] in New Ellenton, SC, USA, the previous October. The organizers judged that new ideas in stream ecology had stagnated, so they deliberately paired distant fields (behavioral ecology with fluvial geomorphology, population genetics with ecosystem health) to create an "intellectual hybrid zone." The review frames the symposium as showing a discipline shifting toward new techniques (notably genetic tools), explicit attention to scale, views of populations as open systems shaped by dispersal, and a greater role for evolutionary perspectives. Several speakers pointed to large gaps: physical-biological coupling, long-term population dynamics, and catchment-scale management. The review closes by arguing that a "general field theory" of ecology depends on fusing studies *in* streams with studies *of* streams.

The document also begins with a short reply by [[Nancy Knowlton]] (Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute) on conspecific self/non-self recognition in corals and sea anemones. Its reference list is included below only in summary.

## Background & Description

The review is a conference overview rather than a primary study, so it reports no original sample sizes or statistics. Its stated aims were:

- To counter perceived stagnation in stream ecology by juxtaposing disparate disciplines, including [[Behavioral Ecology]], [[Fluvial Geomorphology]], [[Ecosystem Health]], [[Population Genetics]], [[Invertebrate Physiology]], and [[Food Web Theory]].
- To track three shifts in the field: adoption of new techniques (e.g., genetic tools), greater attention to scale, and the view of populations as open systems where dispersal can be paramount.
- To move from mechanistic, short-term, local-scale studies toward work at larger temporal and spatial scales.

The authors organize their account in that order: from mechanistic predator-prey work to landscape and management questions.

**Preceding item (Knowlton reply):** The opening section addresses why conspecific non-self recognition in anemones and corals produces strong reactions in some groups and weak ones in others, which the author calls "a polyp puzzle for the future." Cited work includes [[Ayre and Grosberg]] (1995) on aggression and clonal coexistence in *Anthopleura elegantissima*, [[Francis]] (1973, 1976) on clone-specific segregation, [[Lang]] (1971, 1973) on interspecific aggression in scleractinian corals, and [[Neigel and Avise]] (1983) on clonal diversity in *Acropora cervicornis*. The reference list is not reproduced in full here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator-prey interactions and adaptive behavior.** [[Bobbi Peckarsky]] (Cornell University) studied grazing mayflies, predatory stoneflies, and insectivorous trout, emphasizing the difficulty of scaling short-term sublethal predator effects to field-level impacts on prey distribution and performance. [[Scott Cooper]] (UC Santa Barbara), working with [[Roger Nisbet]], tested how prey dispersal rates modify predator impacts across spatial scales, using three-trophic-level models timed to match field experiments. [[Peter Abrams]] (University of Maryland) showed that adaptive prey behavior and temporal variation in prey abundance can change predator-prey dynamics both quantitatively and qualitatively. He argued that current ecological theory is better used to identify uncertainties than to make strict predictions.

**Community assembly and dispersal.** [[LeRoy Poff]] (University of Maryland) proposed a framework for quantifying species traits (e.g., dispersal ability, physiological tolerance) and environmental filters that determine which regional species can join local communities. Dispersal ability emerged as a key trait generating spatially hierarchical community patterns. [[Stuart Bunn]] (Griffith University) used a nested sampling design across and within catchments and found strong genetic differences at a broad range of scales, implying low dispersal and gene flow. Combined with founder effects, this produced highly variable recruitment and marked temporal shifts in community composition.

**Microbial and nutrient processes.** [[J. McArthur]] (Savannah River Ecology Laboratory) argued that the environmental controls on bacterial genetic diversity should be understood to clarify bacterial roles in energy transformation and nutrient cycling. [[Jan Stevenson]] (University of Louisville) modeled nonlinear responses of algae to nutrients and of grazers to algae, aiming to predict nutrient thresholds for shifts in assemblage composition (e.g., "nuisance algae"). [[Antoine Morin]] (University of Ottawa) defended empirical multiple-regression models for secondary productivity against mechanistic models, acknowledging lower precision but citing the higher cost and narrower generalization of mechanistic approaches.

**Physical template and river management.** [[Jack Schmidt]] (Utah State University) described how channels and bed forms evolving over many space and time scales form the physical setting for ecological processes. Focusing on the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], he discussed geomorphic and hydraulic effects of impoundments. The review highlights a high-profile test: federal permission to substantially increase water released from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] that spring, intended to test whether major floods rebuild sandbars and to evaluate effects on several species. [[Stuart Fisher]] (Arizona State University) proposed [[Organismal Functional Morphology]] as an ecosystem metaphor, generating hypotheses on how ecosystem processes depend on channel form and tributary branching and how processes such as retentiveness may feed back on geomorphology.

**Management and policy.** [[Judy Meyer]] (University of Georgia) championed "ecosystem health" as a metaphor to raise public concern and advocated large-scale, long-term management treatments as experiments to reduce decision uncertainty. Her talk sparked debate on the roles of basic versus applied ecologists in public environmental policy.

## Key Findings

- **Symposium aim:** Deliberately combining distant disciplines was meant to revive stream ecology, which the organizers saw as stagnating.
- **Trends identified:** Adoption of new techniques (genetic tools), explicit attention to scale, populations treated as open systems with dispersal central, and an underdeveloped but welcome emphasis on evolutionary perspectives.
- **Dispersal is central:** Bunn's genetic data imply low dispersal and gene flow across scales; Poff identifies dispersal ability as a primary trait structuring communities; Cooper shows prey dispersal modulates predator impacts.
- **Scaling is hard:** Peckarsky and Abrams both highlight difficulty in moving from short-term or abstract models to field predictions. Abrams urges using theory mainly to locate uncertainty.
- **Model choice trade-offs:** Stevenson favors mechanistic nutrient-threshold models; Morin favors empirical regression models, accepting lower precision for lower cost and broader use.
- **Physical-biological coupling is underexplored:** Schmidt and Fisher show flow and geomorphology drive ecology, yet the review states that progress in rigorous physical-biological coupling has been slow.
- **Long-term and catchment-scale gaps:** Little is known about ecological consequences of interannual hydrological variability, few long-term animal population time series exist, spatially explicit stream population models are rare, and catchment-scale studies are needed for management.
- **Policy role:** Meyer's "ecosystem health" framing and large-scale experimental management raised the question of how ecologists should engage with public policy.
- **Closing conclusion:** The authors argue that ecology's "general field theory" depends on combining (a) using streams to reveal general ecological patterns and (b) studying what makes streams distinctive.
