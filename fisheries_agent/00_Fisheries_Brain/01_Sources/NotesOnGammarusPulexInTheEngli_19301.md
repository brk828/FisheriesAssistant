# Notes on *Gammarus pulex* in the English Lake District

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae

## Executive Summary

Macan and Mackereth (Freshwater Biological Association) report field observations on the freshwater amphipod [[Gammarus Pulex]] from two small stony streams, Smooth Beck and Ford Wood Beck, in the English Lake District, collected over several years (1949–1952). The note covers the breeding season, egg production per female, seasonal and spatial abundance, absolute density, distribution across habitat types, and predation by stoneflies. The authors conclude that breeding is near-continuous from January to October, that population totals stay relatively stable through the year (probably regulated by density-dependent jostling), that densities are roughly 200 individuals per square metre, and that the species is abundant in small stony streams but scarce in rivers, lakes and tarns.

## Background & Description

*G. pulex* is the only member of the genus recorded in the Lake District; *G. lacustris* has not been found there. The study builds on the distribution and life-history work of [[Hynes Reproductive Studies|Hynes]] (1954, 1955a,b).

Two study streams were used, both called becks locally:

- **Ford Wood Beck:** About 2000 m of stream studied. Nowhere more than about 2 m broad and mostly ankle-deep, knee-deep after heavy rain. Fed by a main stream from an artificial underground channel, a tributary from a peat moss, and a tributary crossing the plateau. Almost all the drainage area is pasture. Valley-bottom gradient about 1 in 56; plateau-edge slope about 1 in 12.5. Maximum recorded temperature 19°C in a tributary, generally not above 16°C in fine weather.
- **Smooth Beck:** Steeper (fall 1 in 11.5), originates in a series of tarns, and flows mostly through a wooded gorge.

Sampling methods: Smooth Beck was sampled roughly weekly from April 1949 to March 1952 (pipette for the first seven months, then hand net); Ford Wood Beck was sampled with a hand net.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Breeding.** Between 50 and 100% of females bore eggs through spring and summer, except in July 1949 and on 23 March 1950, when the proportion was lower. Egg-bearing ceased from October, with a resting period to the end of the year. The authors contrast this with [[Berg River Susaa Study|Berg (1948)]], who found a lower proportion of ovigerous females in summer and autumn.

**Egg production.** Egg counts per female ranged from 1 to 35. Mean eggs per female fell steadily through the season, because more females carried small clutches and fewer carried large ones. Production was lower throughout 1949, an exceptionally warm, dry summer. Authors compare averages with [[Heinze Fortpflanzung Study|Heinze (1932)]] and Berg, and attribute the seasonal decline partly to the death of large females in June–July.

**Abundance and density.** Weekly five-minute catches at Smooth Beck were lowest in winter, particularly February, suggesting a small winter drop, though not statistically significant. At Ford Wood Beck, ten-minute catches at five stations varied little through the year, indicating a density-dependent control. The authors hypothesise that crowding under stones pushes animals into the current, where they are swept downstream, and that density depends on cover and food. Mean density was 10.7 animals per 1/20 m² (roughly 200 per m², with a standard deviation of 9); Berg reported up to 2504 per m².

**Spatial variation.** Within Ford Wood Beck, the most torrential station (St. 3, near the base of the steep reach) had the lowest and steadiest population. St. 4, above the tributary confluence, had a steadier flow, more soil-bedded stones and cooler temperatures. A tributary (Ss) that dried up in June 1950 and 1951 was recolonised within a month of both events, despite the loss of the population.

**Occurrence elsewhere.** In Smooth Beck, *Gammarus* was the most common individual species in 13 of 14 full collections. In Ford Wood Beck it was often outnumbered by the mayflies *Rhithrogena semicolorata* and *Baetis rhodani*. Three higher-altitude becks had scarce populations at lower stations, with Whelpside Ghyll yielding 1–29 animals per 10-minute catch at 600 m but abundant specimens at its source (850 m). Fourteen collections in wider stony waters (10–30 m) yielded only three specimens. Lakes: widespread but never numerous; recorded to 3 m depth. Tarns: scarce, but locally abundant where stony shores have interstitial spaces.

**Calcium.** [[Schumann Calcium Hardening|Schumann (1928)]] suggested about 5 mg/l Ca is needed for moulting; [[Wundsch Calcium Threshold|Wundsch (1922)]] found none below about 8 mg/l, a figure the authors consider coincidental. *Gammarus* occurs in tarns with calcium ranging from 1.8 to 5.0 mg/l, so the threshold appears lower than earlier workers believed. Dittmar (1953) proposed that magnesium toxicity may limit it.

**Predators.** Of 205 stoneflies (*Perla carlukiana*) from Ford Wood Beck, 148 had identifiable gut contents, containing 303 *Baetis*, 58 *Rhithrogena*, 70 *Simulium*, 35 other animals and only 6 *Gammarus*. Similar results were seen for *Perlodes mortoni* and *Isoperla grammatica*. Carnivorous caddisfly larvae were harder to assess. The authors suspect fish predation (e.g. [[Bullhead Cottus|Cottus]]) keeps numbers low in larger waters, since Cottus is common in the River Brathay but absent from Ford Wood Beck.

## Key Findings

1. **Breeding:** Near-continuous from January to October; ceases almost completely from October to year-end.
2. **Fecundity:** 1–35 eggs per female; mean declined steadily through the season and was lowest throughout the warm, dry 1949 summer.
3. **Population stability:** Totals vary little across the year. Density-dependent displacement into the current is proposed as the regulating mechanism.
4. **Spatial pattern:** In Ford Wood Beck, lowest numbers occurred in the swiftest stretch and highest in the slowest; the five stations did not differ greatly overall.
5. **Recovery:** A tributary that dried up was rapidly recolonised.
6. **Density:** Approximately 200 specimens per square metre (mean 10.7 per 1/20 m² sample).
7. **Habitat preference:** Abundant in small stony streams; scarce in stony rivers, lakes and ponds, though it can be numerous in stony-bottomed ponds.
8. **Predation:** Large carnivorous stoneflies consume a small proportion of *Gammarus* relative to its abundance in the stream.
9. **Cover:** Stones and some plant cover appear important; the authors suggest tarn scarcity may reflect substratum and behaviour rather than water chemistry alone.
