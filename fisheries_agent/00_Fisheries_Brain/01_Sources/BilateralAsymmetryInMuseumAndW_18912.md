# Bilateral Asymmetry in Museum and Wild-Caught Specimens of the Endangered Gila Topminnow

## Executive Summary
This study investigates bilateral asymmetry—a proxy for developmental stability and fitness—in the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), comparing museum specimens (collected 1964 and 1979) with wild-caught specimens (collected 1993) from two southeastern Arizona populations: [[Monkey Spring]] and [[Sharp Spring]]. The researchers measured fluctuating asymmetry in lateral line scale counts, pectoral fin rays, and pelvic fin rays. Results indicate uniformly low levels of bilateral asymmetry across all populations and time periods, with no significant differences between the two springs or between wild-caught and museum samples. These findings contrast with an earlier study ([[Quattro and Vrijenhoek 1989]]) that found significantly higher asymmetry and population differences in laboratory-reared fish, likely attributable to elevated stress/mortality (~50%) in that study's laboratory conditions versus low mortality (<10%) in the comparison studies. The data support the conclusion that Gila Topminnows maintain robust developmental stability despite documented low genetic variation, and show no temporal trend toward increasing asymmetry in wild populations.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*, a small, live-bearing (poeciliid) fish.
- **Traits Measured for Bilateral Asymmetry**:
  - **Lateral line scale counts**: Counted from top corner of operculum to peduncle along the lateral line (per [[Hubbs and Lagler 1964]] methodology).
  - **Pectoral fin ray counts**: Right vs. left comparison via cleared-and-stained specimens.
  - **Pelvic fin ray counts**: Right vs. left comparison via cleared-and-stained specimens.
- **Specimen Preparation**: Fish fixed in 10% formalin, stored in 70% ethanol; subset cleared and stained (per Talent, unpublished manuscript) for fin ray visualization.
- **Asymmetry Index**: Fluctuating asymmetry calculated as mean squared right-left (R-L) trait differences across N individuals, chosen for statistical power in small or subtly differing samples ([[Palmer 1994]]).
- **Symmetry Rate**: 68% of all sampled topminnows were fully symmetrical across all three traits (69% Monkey Spring; 67% Sharp Spring).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historic Range**: Once abundant throughout the lower [[Gila River]] drainage ([[Hubbs and Miller 1941]]); now restricted to isolated relict populations in the southwestern United States, primarily springs and stream headwaters.
- **Monkey Spring**: A thermally and chemically stable tributary to [[Sonoita Creek]], isolated by a 10-meter-high travertine barrier — likely contributing to genetic and environmental stability of this population.
- **Sharp Spring**: A thermally fluctuating, spring-fed tributary to the upper [[Santa Cruz River]]; historically subject to environmental variability.
- **Heron Spring**: A spring-fed arroyo 0.5 miles south of Sharp Spring; population descended from Sharp Spring fish transplanted in 1981 ([[Meffe et al. 1982]]); used as proxy wild-caught "Sharp Spring stock" in this study.
- **Threats**: By 1993, the lower reach of Sharp Spring was heavily infested with the invasive [[Gambusia affinis]] (western mosquitofish), a known ecological competitor/threat to topminnow populations.
- **Conservation Status**: Federally endangered; populations are geographically isolated remnants of a once widespread Sonoran desert fish lineage exhibiting low genetic diversity (allozyme and mtDNA data).

## Key Findings
- **No significant differences** in bilateral asymmetry were found between Monkey Spring and Sharp Spring populations for any of the three traits (lateral line scales, pectoral fin rays, pelvic fin rays), tested via two-tailed Mann-Whitney tests.
- **Pectoral and pelvic fin ray asymmetry** was not significantly different from zero in either population, indicating high developmental stability for these meristic traits.
- **Lateral line scale asymmetry** approached significance from zero (~0.05 level) across all samples but did not differ between populations.
- **No temporal trend**: No increase in bilateral asymmetry was detected from earlier museum samples (1964, 1979) to more recent wild-caught samples (1993), suggesting stable developmental conditions over time.
- **Sharp Spring vs. Monkey Spring**: In three of the comparisons, Sharp Spring fish showed marginally (but not statistically significantly) higher asymmetry than Monkey Spring fish.
- **Comparison with Laboratory Studies**:
  - Results align closely with [[Sheffer et al. 1996]], who examined laboratory-raised offspring of wild-caught fish (1994) from four locations and found similarly low asymmetry and low mortality (<10% over 12 weeks).
  - Results sharply contrast with [[Quattro and Vrijenhoek 1989]], who found significantly higher bilateral asymmetry (especially in Monkey Spring fish) and much higher mortality (~50% over 12 weeks) in laboratory-reared fish, suggesting stressful/unnatural laboratory rearing conditions rather than inherent population-level developmental instability.
- **Developmental Buffering Hypothesis**: Despite low genetic variation (per [[Vrijenhoek et al. 1985]] and [[Quattro et al. 1996]]), Gila Topminnows appear capable of buffering development against natural and typical laboratory stressors, consistent with findings by [[Angus and Schultz 1983]] on meristic trait stability across genetic and temperature treatments.
- **Trait Robustness**: Discrete meristic traits (e.g., fin rays) may require a greater developmental "insult" threshold to manifest asymmetry compared to continuous traits, potentially explaining their resistance to perturbation under natural conditions.
- **Selection Bias Consideration**: Since only adult fish were sampled, the possibility exists that less symmetrical (less fit) juveniles were selectively eliminated from wild populations prior to sampling, potentially underestimating "true" baseline asymmetry.
- **Overall Conclusion**: Both museum and wild-caught Gila Topminnow specimens from Monkey and Sharp Springs exhibit low, stable bilateral asymmetry, with no evidence of a temporal trend toward increased developmental instability in these endangered populations.
