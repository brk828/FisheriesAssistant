# Pecos River Fish Barrier and Trap No. 1 Job Completion Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This State of New Mexico job completion report (Project F-10-R-1, Job C-1) evaluates the design, operation and results of the [[Pecos River Fish Barrier and Trap No. 1]]. The structure was built in summer 1954 by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] to reduce "trash fish" that were thought to occupy much of the stream's carrying capacity for sport fish. Operation ran from August 1954 and through the 1955 season, using a two-trap barrier, dipnet removal, and later [[Fish Basket Trap]] units. The report concludes that it is not yet possible to determine whether game fish carrying capacity has increased, and that the 1956 season should decide the future of the project.

## Background & Description

The Pecos River, from the head of [[McMillan Reservoir]] south to the New Mexico–Texas state line, was described as the most important sport fishing water for Eddy and Lea Counties. The report identifies several rough species that dominated the catch: [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[River Carpsucker]], Gray Redhorse Sucker, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] and [[Longnose Gar]]. Before the barrier, [[Earl Troglin]] had removed rough fish for years, mainly by [[Gill Netting]], and this had altered the population only slightly.

The structure sits about two miles below McMillan Dam and was designed by Fred A. Thompson, Assistant Director of the Department. Its layout includes:

- **Barrier:** A line of removable 2-ft screens mounted on a 1-ft concrete barrier dam, set at a 20° angle to direct fish across the stream into the proper trap.
- **Upstream trap (east bank):** Faces downstream to catch upstream migrants, with a four-inch V-grate entrance.
- **Downstream trap (west bank):** Opens upstream to catch downstream migrants.
- **Walls and slabs:** 3-ft concrete walls at each bank prevent flow from cutting around the ends, with concrete trap floors and removable 1½-inch mesh side screens.

Water rights belong to the [[Carlsbad Irrigation District]], which holds rights from Alamogordo Reservoir to Carlsbad Springs. When McMillan flood gates close, the river runs dry from the dam to about 50 yards below the trap, so trap operation coincided with irrigation seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Trap operation depended on water release schedules. The 1954 trial ran five days, and the 1955 season had intermittent releases from April 1 to September 20. A severe flood on October 7, 1954, washed out earth fill behind the walls and damaged horizontal walking grates, but the trap and barrier screens were not damaged. Repairs were completed in December 1954.

Rough fish carcasses were returned to the river below the trap to recycle nutrients. The report notes that after two months of this practice, fishermen reported consistently good [[Channel Catfish]] fishing below the trap. Spot checks at two-mile intervals to Lake Avalon found no unusual numbers of dead fish on the banks.

## Key Findings

**1954 operation (August 16–20, five days, Table 1)**
- Total catch: 10,075 lb and 28,391 fish.
- Upstream trap: 9,405 lb and 27,997 fish. [[Common Carp]] made up 8,413.83 lb (90.15% of the population), with 25,242 fish.
- Downstream trap: 670 lb and 394 fish, with carp at 59.40% of the catch.
- Rough fish totaled 10,032.33 lb. Game fish totaled 42.67 lb.
- A later six-day run by Troglin had an estimated catch of 22,903 lb.

**Tagging studies**
- In spring 1955, about 400 fish were caught by gill net, tagged and released 5, 6 and 10 miles below the trap. None were recaptured at the trap.
- In 1955, 80 game fish were tagged or marked by clipping the left pectoral fin. Only one marked fish was retaken.
- The report found no conclusive information from tagging in either year.

**1955 season (April–September, 120 emptyings in 85 days)**
- Upstream trap: 27,858.0 lb, 59% of the 1955 catch, averaging 328 lb per day.
- Downstream trap: 3,846.5 lb over 80 days, 8% of the catch.
- [[Fish Basket Trap]] units: 23 days of operation starting August 8, totaling 11,850 lb (25%). The three-day catch of August 27, 29 and 30 was 8,231.0 lb (17%).
- Downstream migrants were taken at about 515 lb per day in the baskets, versus 8 lb per day in the downstream trap.
- Fish picked up off the walking grates totaled 3,669 lb, nearly 7% of the catch.
- Game fish totaled 67.5 lb, or 1.43% of the total. (The annual game total in Table 2-B reads 674.5 lb, which the report does not reconcile.)

**Trap modifications**
- Boards placed in the downstream trap did not increase catch. One board under each downstream screen stopped current-related mortality of game fish.
- A 2" × 12" board across the gap in the barrier dam, which created a drop for migrating fish, is reported to have greatly increased downstream catch.
- The experimental [[Fish Basket Trap]] (½-inch hardware cloth) took 99 carp (6 lb), 6 gizzard shad and 5 sunfish in its first 16½ hours. The downstream trap took 2 carp and 2 sunfish in the same period.
- Four permanent baskets were built with angle-iron frames and ½-inch hardware cloth.
- A basket placed in the upstream trap opening, with fish swept in by seine, cut emptying time by about 50%. The report describes basket removal as "100% more effective" than dipnet removal. It could not be used in the downstream trap.

**Disposal and cost**
- Undesirable species were first hauled to a pasture and dumped. The 1955 method of returning rough fish to the river below the trap was judged more convenient and economical.
- Labor ($745 for two laborers) and pickup use ($392, about 5,600 miles) totaled $1,137, or about 2½¢ per pound of rough fish removed. This excludes barrier construction, maintenance and the project leader's salary.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The barrier was built to reduce trash fish and increase game fish carrying capacity between Avalon and McMillan Dams. Whether this has happened cannot yet be determined.
- The 1956 trapping season will complete the experimental control operation as the project is currently written. Any rewriting of the project should depend on the significance of the 1956 results.
