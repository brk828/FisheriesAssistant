# Four Years of Landlocked Salmon Study in New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This New York State Conservation Department progress report (Bulletin No. 2, 1948) summarizes four years of experimental work on [[Landlocked Salmon]] management. The project began in 1944 with a $7,500 legislative appropriation and was continued as a regular Division of Fish and Game activity. Its goal was to determine whether landlocked salmon, a freshwater form of the [[Atlantic Salmon]], could be built up into a reliable angling resource in New York's many deep, cold lakes. The authors report encouraging progress, especially in the [[Schroon Lake]] area, but stress that the project is far from complete. Most experimental plantings are too young to support fishing, and about four years are needed to assess survival.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The landlocked salmon has been regarded either as a subspecies of the Atlantic salmon or as indistinguishable from it. The report defines a landlocked salmon as any permanently freshwater Atlantic salmon, whether or not its ancestors have spent many generations in fresh water. Fish grown under similar hatchery conditions were found to be indistinguishable from landlocked stock.

**History.** [[Lake Ontario]] and [[Lake Champlain]] were originally inhabited by salmon. Earlier attempts to establish landlocked populations in inland lakes had many failures, and the surviving breeding populations were small. These efforts were not to be confused with stocking of "salmon trout" (lake trout).

**Project structure.** Several agencies took part. The Bureau of Fish and Wildlife Investigations ran field surveys and management planning. The Bureau of Fish Culture propagated fish and collected eggs. The Supervisor of Fish Management directed weir construction. The Bureau of Inland Fisheries netted salmon and controlled undesirable fish. The Bureau of Law Enforcement helped with information gathering and stocking. The [[Vermont Department of Conservation and Development]] supplied landlocked yearlings in 1944 and 1945, and the Dominion of Canada Department of Fisheries provided Atlantic salmon eggs on an exchange basis.

**Study area.** Experimental work covered 39,582 acres in total:

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Young salmon are stream fish. They usually move to lakes only at about 5 inches, in the silvery [[Smolt Stage]]. Parr growth is slow but highly variable, lasting one to three years. Schroon Lake fish usually migrate as two-year parr when under 7 inches long. Spawning occurs in October and November. Fry emerge in May and June.

**Natural reproduction.** Spawning grounds on the [[Schroon River]] are well suited to fry production, but breeder numbers have been low in most years. Observations from 1940 to 1948 show that fry production tracked adult abundance. Good fry production followed years of many spawners, such as 1941, 1943 and 1947. Rock bass, smallmouth bass and sunfish were thought to reduce survival of small salmon. In 1947 rock bass were apparently absent and smallmouth bass were scarce, while [[Brook Trout]] increased in 1947 and 1948.

**Migration.** Young salmon have a restricted range. Lake salmon cruise widely, and at the smolt stage small salmon migrate long distances. A marked fish planted in Clear Pond was caught many miles downstream in the Schroon River. Some salmon are lost over the Starbuckville dam at the outlet of Schroon Lake, but observations suggest this loss is only partial. The dam has a flume that salmon can apparently jump. A spring spawning run up the Schroon River provides the best fly fishing, usually in April or May.

**Growth.** Scale analysis showed high individual variation and significant differences between waters. Schroon Lake salmon grow faster than the average for other lakes. Three-year-olds average about 18 inches and four-year-olds about 22 inches. Fish weigh over 3 pounds at 20 inches, and the maximum weight exceeds 8 pounds. Schroon Lake has produced fish up to 7 years old. Lake George shows faster growth, with fish over 12 pounds taken. Upper Saranac Lake, Clear Pond and Eagle Lake appear to grow more slowly than Schroon Lake.

**Water temperature.** Salmon need higher feeding temperatures than brook trout. [[Warrensburg Hatchery]] has a spring supply several degrees warmer in winter and gave excellent hatching results. The [[Crown Point Hatchery]] mixes creek water with spring water and raised yearlings well.

## Key Findings

**Management methods**

- [[Fin-Clipping]] was the most useful marking method for checking results. Numbered metal tags were used less often.
- Two [[Weir Barrier]]s were built, on the Schroon River and on [[Mill Brook]] (a tributary of Upper Saranac Lake). They kept undesirable fish out of salmon areas and trapped adults for spawn and examination.
- An electric shocker removed undesirable fish from Mill Brook at the [[Saranac Hatchery]], improving survival of stocked salmon.
- Small trap nets controlled suckers and other competitors.

**Natural reproduction**

- Egg deposition in 1948 exceeded every year from 1941 to 1947 on the basis of October–November spawning counts.
- A weir and fish removal in 1945 kept many rock bass out of the spawning area. Yearling salmon from natural reproduction were then seen in encouraging numbers in 1946.
- Changes in the Schroon River fish community make it hard to evaluate the effect on survival.

**Stocking survival**

- Stocking alone was judged inadequate after many planting failures. Plantings were adjusted to local conditions: lakes received advanced smolt-size fish when food supply looked favorable, and stream plantings were sparse, placed in rapid sections, and timed with favorable temperatures.
- The report lists 16 numbered plantings. Among the most successful, Schroon River spring yearlings from Vermont stock (1945) gave excellent survival, producing legal-sized fish in 1947 and 3½- to 4-pound fish in 1948. Marked fall plantings in [[Clear Pond]] (1945) produced females yielding eggs at the end of the fourth and fifth seasons. Atlantic salmon planted in the Schroon River and The Branch in 1947 established well, and some males returned to spawn at three years old in 1948.
- Several 1947–48 plantings had no follow-up data yet. The authors note that survival checks need about four years.

**Schroon Lake population**

- The 1938 natural year class survived well and supported fishing before 1943.
- Populations were sparse in 1944–46, then rose sharply in 1947, mainly from marked planted fish of the 1944 hatch.
- Before experimental management, the angling supply dropped suddenly when particular size groups failed to survive. The authors hope that a stable annual crop of young salmon can be maintained through stocking or natural reproduction.

**Egg production and hatchery work**

- Wild-trapped eggs totalled 21,930 in 1947. Over 60,000 were expected from wild fish in 1948.
- Hatchery-breeder eggs at [[Crown Point Hatchery]] totalled 16,170 in 1947. These were judged inferior to wild eggs because sac-fry mortality was high. The 1948 experiment involved 64,250 eggs from 57 females.
- Total 1947 eggs reached 38,100, a threefold increase over 1945 or 1946. The 1948 collection was reported at over 125,000 when the report was written, and the authors expected it to exceed 150,000. The yield of over 35 quarts (over 150,000 eggs) is cited in the Foreword.
- Atlantic salmon eggs from Canada helped test hatchery suitability and increased the number of fish available for stocking.

**Angling and regulations**

- A 20-inch size limit and a daily bag limit of two salmon took effect in 1945. The report credits these rules with material benefit, and many undersized fish of 15 to 19 inches were released unharmed in 1946.
- A closed season was rejected because angling reports were needed to check experiments.
- Anglers were warned to distinguish small salmon from trout, and an identification leaflet was distributed.
- Schroon Lake had a large increase in anglers in 1947 and heavy angling in 1948. Catches were good early in April, then dropped after very cold weather.

**Economic outlook**

- Deep, cold lakes are numerous in New York and are valuable recreational areas. Landlocked salmon offer high-quality fishing, strike artificial flies readily, and provide early-season sport in spring.
- The report concludes that the species is a promising economic asset, provided propagation and management capacity are expanded gradually.

**Overall conclusion:** Results so far are encouraging, and the project is considered justified for expansion. However, most salmon now in the study waters are too young to support angling, Atlantic and landlocked fish appear equally suitable, and further checks are needed before extensive management is feasible.
