# Guidelines for the Promotion of Environmental Management of Coastal Aquaculture Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture-policy

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Technical Paper (No. 328, 1992, 122 p.), written by consultant U.C. Barg for the Inland Water Resources and Aquaculture Service, provides guidelines for improving the environmental management of [[Coastal Aquaculture]]. It is aimed at aquaculture specialists, coastal planners and government officials. The document reviews published experience to address the potential adverse environmental effects of coastal aquaculture, sets out methods for assessing and monitoring hazards and impacts, and describes management options at policy and farm levels. It states that most aquaculture has had little adverse ecosystem effect to date, although problems have occurred with [[Intensive Cage Culture]] in Europe and [[Shrimp Farming]] in Southeast Asia and Latin America.

## Background & Description

**Definitions and scope.** Aquaculture is defined following FAO (1990) as the farming of aquatic organisms involving intervention in rearing (stocking, feeding, predator protection) and individual or corporate ownership of stock. "Coastal" covers shoreland influenced by the sea, the water column and seabed out to the edge of the continental shelf, so "coastal aquaculture" includes land-based and water-based brackish and marine systems.

**Scale.** Aquaculture made up about 15.3% of world fishery production in 1990 (14% in 1989). Coastal aquaculture produced approximately 7.5 million metric tons in 1990, valued at US$13,230 million.

**Farming systems.** The document distinguishes extensive (low density, natural food, occasional fertilization), semi-intensive (higher density, fertilization and supplementary feed) and intensive systems (high density, regular complete feeds). Culture types covered include [[Seaweed Culture]], [[Bivalve Culture]], [[Shrimp Farming]] and [[Finfish Culture]].

**Guideline structure.** Section 2 gives general principles, policies and 54 numbered actions. Sections 3 to 6 provide the supporting overview: context, factors, assessment methods and management options. Annexes cover case studies, control strategies, monitoring parameters, socio-economic parameters, EIA sequences, ICAM considerations, a code of practice for inhibitory compounds, and a project checklist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient and organic enrichment.** Metabolic wastes and uneaten feed settle as organic matter on the seabed beneath farms. This can cause oxygen depletion, anoxic sediments, outgassing of [[Hydrogen Sulphide]] and methane, and reduced macrofaunal biomass and diversity. Effects are typically localized, with recovery normally within about 30 m of cage farms (occasionally up to 100 m).

**Wetland degradation.** Mangroves are highly productive nursery grounds, and several tropical countries have lost large areas to pond conversion, often with soil salinization and acidification. [[Acid Sulphate Soils]] released on pond drying can acidify water and mobilize toxic aluminium and iron compounds.

**Chemical use.** Therapeutants, disinfectants, anaesthetics, biocides and hormones are used. Concerns include residues in tissues, toxicity to non-target organisms, and the spread of [[Antibiotic Resistance]].

**Biological interactions.** Introductions of species and breeds can alter biodiversity and genetics, and can carry parasites and disease. Examples include Bonamia ostreae in European flat oysters, Gyrodactylus salaris in Norwegian salmon, and the impact of [[Pacific Oyster]] introductions on North American fauna.

**Socio-economic and health effects.** Mangrove conversion has displaced rural communities that depended on mangrove resources. Large producers can displace small-scale fishers, and use conflicts (for example between rice and shrimp farmers in Thailand) sometimes turn violent. Consumption of raw or partially cooked shellfish from polluted waters poses risks including gastro-enteritis, hepatitis, cholera, typhoid and various [[Shellfish Poisoning]] syndromes.

**Pollution threats to aquaculture.** Sewage, industrial metals, oil, pesticides and toxic [[Harmful Algal Blooms]] can all damage cultured stock or contaminate products. Coliform counts above 1,000/100 ml were recorded in Manila Bay. Cadmium exceeded WHO standards in 76% of Jakarta Bay fish and shellfish samples tested.

## Key Findings

- **Guideline framework.** The guidelines emphasize integrated coastal area management, EIA applied to major proposals, precautionary and adaptive regulation, and farm-level management focused on site selection, stocking density, feed and chemical control, and monitoring.
- **Evidence gap.** The paper states there is a substantial lack of adequate data on adverse effects in developing countries. Temperate evidence cannot be used to quantitatively predict effects in tropical settings, and many concerns remain speculative.
- **Bivalve filtration.** In Japan, culture of 50,000–60,000 oysters reduced seston by 76–95%. Suspended mussel culture in New Zealand removed up to 60% of available food. A Spanish mussel raft removed 35–40% of plankton and detritus.
- **Biodeposition.** In Hiroshima Bay, a raft with 420,000 oysters generated 16 t of faeces and pseudofaeces over a 9-month season. Sediment under Swedish mussel farms accumulated up to about 10 cm per season.
- **Shrimp pond waste.** Pond water exchange of 30–40% per day is typical of semi-intensive and intensive systems. Water requirements are 11,000–21,430 m³/t for semi-intensive and 29,000–43,000 m³/t for intensive culture in Taiwan.
- **Taiwan collapse.** Shrimp output fell from 80,000 t (1987) to under 20,000 t (1988) after MBV disease. The causes listed include overstocking, poor larval rearing, pond aging, unregistered feeds and drugs, and polluted intake water. Annex 1 presents this as a model of ecosystem mismanagement.
- **Finfish waste.** A Danish pond farm producing rainbow trout reported 300 kg BOD/t/yr, 10 kg total P/t/yr and 81 kg total N/t/yr. A Norwegian net-pen farm producing 200 t of salmon annually was estimated to discharge 2 t of P, 18 t of N and 100 t of BOD.
- **Antibiotics.** Only about 20–30% of medicated feed is ingested by fish, so roughly 70–80% reaches the environment. Oxytetracycline in sediments can remain active for up to 12 weeks after treatment.
- **Feed inefficiency.** An estimated 5.3 t of wild fish is needed to produce 1 t of cage-farmed salmon. Asian shrimp farming absorbed 180,000 t of fishmeal in 1988, with about 1.1 million t of shrimp feed possibly needed by 2000.
- **Assessment methods.** The paper recommends mass balance equations for waste estimation, the environmental capacity concept, hazard assessment, benthic indicators (SAB curves, ABC method), GIS and remote sensing, and a baseline before any monitoring.
- **Key policy actions.** The paper calls for prohibiting prophylactic antibiotic use, controlling chemical registration and licensing, restricting mangrove conversion for intensive shrimp farms, adopting EIFAC/ICES codes of practice on introductions, and creating ICAM programmes with long-term funding.
- **Institutional gaps.** The authors identify weak legislation, overlapping authorities, unclear tenure, lack of trained personnel, and insufficient coordination among sectors as major constraints in many countries.
