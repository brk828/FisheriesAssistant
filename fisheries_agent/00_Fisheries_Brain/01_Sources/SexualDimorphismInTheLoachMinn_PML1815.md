# Ichthyological Notes: Loach Minnow Dimorphism, Flyingfish Spawning, and Catfish Eye Melanoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cypriniformes #exocoetidae #callichthyidae

## Executive Summary

This Copeia (1965, No. 3, pp. 380–382) "Ichthyological Notes" section contains three short independent reports. The first describes sexual dimorphism in the [[Loach Minnow]] (*[[Tiaroga Cobitis]]*) from the [[Gila River]], New Mexico. The second documents the first observed spawning of the [[Margined Flyingfish]] (*[[Cypselurus Cyanopterus]]*) in the [[Gulf of Guinea]], off Gabon. The third opens a review of a malignant melanoma of the eye of a catfish (*[[Corydoras Julii]]*), but the text is cut off before the case description is complete.

## Background & Description

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*, Cypriniformes).** The species once occurred in many streams of the Gila River system in Arizona and New Mexico, but had become increasingly rare in Arizona streams by the time of the report. The author, W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]], prepared the note because little specific information existed on the species. The study was based on 46 specimens (17 males, 29 females) collected on 25–26 April 1964 and deposited in the ASU fish collection (ASU 629).

**Margined Flyingfish (*Cypselurus cyanopterus*).** The note by Gerald W. Bailey Jr. of the University of California, Irvine, reports the first observed spawning of this species in the region. The report is based on observations and captures made on 21 August 1961.

**Catfish Eye Melanoma (*Corydoras julii*).** The note places eye melanomas within the context of earlier work on fish tumors. Skin melanomas in fishes can be induced by selective hybridization of *[[Xiphophorus]]* species (Gordon, 1952, 1953), and Schlumberger and Lucke (1948) list spontaneous skin melanomas across several fish families. Only one eye melanoma was reported in that review, in xiphophorine fishes (Levine and Gordon, 1946).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gila River habitat (Loach Minnow).** At the collection site the river was 40 to 75 ft wide and more than 5 ft deep in larger pools. Pool bottoms were sand, and riffles were a mix of sand and gravel. Some swift riffles lacked vegetation, while shallow turbulent areas (under 6 inches deep) held *Zannichellia palustris*, *Potamogeton foliosus*, and *Cladophora* sp. Water temperature ranged from 52 °F in early morning to near 65 °F in the afternoon. *Tiaroga* occurred only in shallow, turbulent riffles with about 50% of the bottom covered by macrophytes. It was absent from unvegetated riffles and rare in riffles choked with plants. Most collections also contained plecopteran nymphs and baetid mayfly nymphs. *[[Agosia]]*-associated species such as *Rhinichthys* were not described in the source; the co-occurring *Peada*-type fishes noted were *Pantosteus clarkii* (more abundant in swift open riffles) and *Meda fulgida* (in eddies).

**Open-ocean surface conditions (Flyingfish).** The spawning school was observed at the sea surface around a drifting vessel just before sunset (5:30 pm local time). Sea surface temperature was 24.4 °C and air temperature was 25.0 °C. No plant material was seen in the area, and no predatory fishes were observed feeding on the flyingfish.

## Key Findings

### Sexual Dimorphism in the Loach Minnow
- Breeding males have pectoral fins that curve upward distally, similar to some *Rhinichthys* species. Breeding colors are also similar, especially reddish pigment on the lips and at fin bases.
- Tubercles are present on the distal two-thirds of the second, third, and fourth pectoral rays, largest and most recurved on the posterior branch of each ray. Tubercles also occur on the opercles, dorsum, cheeks, and snout, and in regular rows on the caudal peduncle scales (one per scale, sharp and recurved). Some females have a few tubercles on top of the head; the rest of the body is smooth.
- Males are much thicker-bodied than females. Females have greater body depth because of mature ova. Pectoral and pelvic fins are larger in males, and the anal fin is larger in females. Both sexes have a thickened, mucus-like pad on the inner fin rays near the origins.
- Colors were recorded in the field and expanded two days later. Males show intense orange on the lower lobes of the caudal fin, and orange-blotched pelvic fins. Females show canary yellow in the axillae and orange blotches on the pelvic and anal fins. The basic color pattern is similar in both sexes.
- Field observation: both sexes released gametes under slight pressure when removed from the seine. Turbulence and plant cover prevented observation of spawning behavior.

### Spawning Behavior of the Margined Flyingfish
- Spawning was noted for the first time off Gabon, with several hundred spawning fish at the surface. Groups contained up to 4 males with 1 female. Up to 9 fish were captured in one sweep of a scoop net.
- Females discharged strings of eggs while males swam alongside with vents in close proximity. The eggs remained suspended near the surface.
- No violent courtship or sexual frenzy was observed. The school avoided the net shortly before dusk and disappeared about dark, despite deck lighting and a night light.
- No flyingfish was seen to leap from the water between 5:30 and 6:45 pm. The author suggests the absence of predators may explain the lack of flight.

### Sex Ratio and Size Data (Flyingfish)
- Sample: 18 females and 61 males. The sex ratio was estimated at 3 to 4 males per female, approximately the same as in smaller spawning groups.
- Female fork length: 255–283 mm (mean 268.2 ± 8.1 mm).
- Male fork length: 225–273 mm (mean 254.9 ± 11.4 mm).
- A t-test showed spawning females were significantly larger than males (t = 5.18, 77 df).
- The author notes these males were shorter than a 330 mm (total length) male reported by Poll (1953). The text gives this figure as both 330 mm and 380 mm across the two copies, so the value is uncertain.
- In Puerto Rican specimens, fork length was 79.1% of total length.
