# Identifying and Estimating Original Lengths of Ingested Prey Fishes Using Diagnostic Bones

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study, by Hansel, Poe, Lott and Gray (US Fish and Wildlife Service, [[National Fishery Research Center]], Columbia River Field Station), develops a method for identifying and sizing partly digested prey fish from the stomachs of piscivorous predators in [[John Day Reservoir]], [[Columbia River]]. The authors examined four bones, the [[Cleithrum]], [[Dentary]], [[Opercle]] and [[Pharyngeal Arch]], which persist in predator guts after the soft tissue has been digested. Bone measurements were regressed against fork length for 14 prey species, producing usable length estimates. The approach increased the dietary information recovered from stomach analysis of three predators, [[Northern Squawfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Walleye]], by 50 to 1,100%. The work supports consumption estimates used in [[Bioenergetics Modeling]] of piscivore predation.

## Background & Description

Diet studies of piscivorous fishes have shifted from purely descriptive work toward integrating food consumption rates into [[Metabolic Energetics]] models. Estimating consumption requires knowing predator size and the identity, number and original size of prey, yet prey information is usually reconstructed from fragmentary remains. Prior work used bones in several ways:

- Archaeological use of fish bones to identify taxa and estimate length and weight (Casteel 1976).
- Vertebral columns used to identify fishes and estimate prey length graphically (Clothier 1950; Crossman and Casselman 1969; Pikhu and Pikhu 1970).
- Pharyngeal arches used in distinguishing [[Catostomidae]] and other fishes (Eastman 1977; Mann and Beaumont 1980).
- Linear regressions of pharyngeal arch or opercle length to estimate prey length (Newsome 1977; Mann and Beaumont 1980; McIntyre and Ward 1986).
- Pharyngeal arches, dentaries and otoliths used by Eurasian biologists (Popova 1967).

The authors' objectives were to (1) describe diagnostic characteristics of selected bones for identifying prey fish from predator stomachs, and (2) estimate original prey length from bone measurements. They applied these procedures to three piscivores in the Columbia River.

### Methods

- **Specimens:** More than 700 fish under 250 mm fork length, representing 24 species, were dissected to select diagnostic bones and to determine bone-to-fork-length relationships. Fish were collected from fish hatcheries in John Day Reservoir during spring and summer 1984–1986 (Table 1).
- **Preparation:** Specimens were iced, measured to ±1.0 mm, frozen, thawed and boiled for 30–60 s to free the skeleton. Bones were preserved in 4% buffered formalin.
- **Bone measurements:** Cleithra were measured diagonally from the anteroventral to posterodorsal tip. Dentaries were measured from the symphysis to the posterior edge of the angular articulation (salmonids to the posterodorsal notch). Opercles were measured from the anterodorsal edge to the anteroventral margin. Pharyngeal arches were measured from dorsal tip to ventral tip. Bones under 15 mm were measured with an ocular micrometer (±0.16 mm) and larger ones with calipers (±0.05 mm).
- **Statistics:** Simple linear regressions of fork length on left-bone measurements were calculated for 14 fishes from nine families with bone sample sizes (N) greater than 30. Slopes were tested with an F-test against zero. The Methods state P < 0.05; the Results give P < 0.01. Confidence limits and percent error were computed using the shortest and longest bones in each sample.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study sits within the [[Bonneville Power Administration]] research program on predator impacts on juvenile and resident fish in the Columbia River (Gray et al. 1985; Poe et al. 1986). Predators in John Day Reservoir feed heavily on fishes, and the authors note that adult walleyes are almost exclusively piscivorous there. Differences in bone recovery among predators likely reflect differences in digestibility of fish versus nonfish prey, the proportion of fish in the diet, digestion rates, or combinations of these.

The method also carries a bias. Larger prey tend to have more digestion-resistant bones, so the approach may over-represent larger prey in food habit data. Results may also depend on preservation, because regressions were derived from preserved bones.

## Key Findings

**Identification of prey**

- Of the 24 species examined, 18 could be readily distinguished from bone characteristics. Identification of some congenerics was inconsistent or unreliable across size ranges. These included [[Oncorhynchus]] (salmon and steelhead), [[Catostomus]] (suckers), [[Lepomis]] (sunfishes) and [[Micropterus]] (black basses).
- The cleithrum was diagnostic for all genera except the salmonids, where steelhead could not be separated from the three salmon species.
- Cleithra were the most persistent and useful bone for identifying young-of-year fishes. Small catostomids under 20 mm were identifiable by cleithrum shape.
- Pharyngeal arches with long comb-like tooth sets were diagnostic for the two [[Catostomus]] species. Cyprinids were separated by arch shape and tooth formula.
- Dentaries were diagnostic for all genera but were rarely used because the pharyngeal arches and cleithra were more often recovered.

**Length estimation**

- Regressions were produced for 14 species with coefficients of determination (r²) ranging from 0.79 to 0.99. Most regressions had r² of 0.90 or higher, and 75% of regressions had r² of 0.97 or higher.
- Mean fork length at the 95% confidence level was estimated with errors of less than 9%, 10%, 6% and 5% for the four bone types.
- Cleithra and opercles gave slightly more accurate length estimates than pharyngeal arches or dentaries.
- Because some congeners could not be separated, the authors used relative abundance and geographic distribution to assign prey. For example, because largescale suckers made up 92% of total sucker consumption (Gray et al. 1985), regressions for this species were applied to all Catostomus spp.

**Predator diet results**

- Stomachs from three predators were analysed for 1983–1986, covering more than 11,000 fish (Table 4).
- Northern squawfish: 5,467 stomachs, 2,696 prey fish, of which about 92% were identifiable from bone fragments.
- Smallmouth bass: 4,940 stomachs, 2,894 prey fish, of which 65% were identifiable from bones.
- Walleye: 1,206 stomachs, 1,095 prey fish, of which 38% were identifiable from bones.
- Overall, 4,786 of the prey fish (about 72%) were identified and sized from bones, adding 50 to 1,100% more dietary information than direct length measurement alone.

**Comparison with other studies**

- The linear bone-length relations differed from the curvilinear opercle-length relations reported by Newsome (1977) for 10 prey species, but agreed with Mann and Beaumont (1980) and McIntyre and Ward (1986) for pharyngeal arches.
- No prior studies had used cleithra and dentaries to estimate lengths of prey in predator stomachs, though Scott (1977) used cleithra for Atlantic cod in a shipwreck, and White (1936, 1953) used bones from kingfisher gizzard pellets.

**Recommendations and limitations**

- Future investigators should preserve bones the same way as the reference specimens before applying these regressions.
- Diagnostic bones may bias food habit data toward larger prey.
- The authors conclude that identifying and measuring cleithra, dentaries, opercles and pharyngeal arches gives an easy and reasonably accurate way to estimate original length of partly digested prey, recovering information that would otherwise be lost.
