## Executive Summary
This document is a 1990 advocacy letter from the [[Desert Fishes Council]], authored by Executive Secretary Edwin P. Pister, addressed to Secretary of the Interior Manuel Lujan. The letter supports a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft jeopardy Biological Opinion opposing the proposed [[Animas-La Plata Project]] in southwestern Colorado, on the grounds that the project's water diversion (154,800 acre-feet annually) from the [[San Juan River]] would jeopardize the survival of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). While primarily a policy and ethics-based argument (drawing heavily on Aldo Leopold's "Land Ethic"), the letter contains embedded biological and historical data on the species' life history, range contraction, and population status that are of scientific and conservation-historical value.

## Background & Description
- **Species:** Colorado squawfish, *Ptychocheilus lucius* Girard (now commonly referred to as the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]])
- **Family:** [[Cyprinidae]] (minnow family) — historically the top predator among North American cyprinids
- **Maximum size (historical):** Weights of 50–80 pounds; lengths up to 6 feet
- **Longevity:** Ages up to 70 years reported historically
- **Coloration:** Described as "silver" — likely contributing to common historical names referencing salmon
- **Common historical names:** "White salmon," "Colorado salmon," or simply "salmon" — attributed to its large size, silver coloration, and long-distance spawning migrations analogous to Pacific salmonids

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic role:** Historically the apex predator of the [[Colorado River]] system for over two million years
- **Habitat:** Large, warm, turbid, free-flowing desert river reaches; requires natural streamflow regimes for physical and biological integrity
- **Migration behavior:** Undertook long spawning migrations, a trait likened to Pacific salmon species
- **Historical range:** Once distributed throughout the Colorado River basin
- **Current range (as of 1990):** Extirpated from nearly 3,000 miles of river in the lower Colorado River basin; restricted to approximately 25% of its original range
- **Remaining wild populations (three identified):**
  1. **[[San Juan River]] Basin** — small population, in serious threat of extirpation
  2. **Mainstem [[Colorado River]] above Lake Powell** — small population, showing signs of decline
  3. **[[Green River]] Basin** — only population considered "viable," though described as "far from secure"
- **Threats to species survival:**
  - Dam construction fragmenting river continuity and blocking migration
  - Introduction of non-native sport fish (competition/predation pressure)
  - Alteration of natural flow regimes via hydropower operations, irrigation diversion, and other water uses
  - Specific threat cited: proposed annual removal of 154,800 acre-feet of water by the Animas-La Plata Project, compounding existing dewatering of the San Juan River

## Key Findings
- The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is officially recognized as an **endangered species**, with the San Juan River supporting "one of the last remaining populations" in the Colorado River basin.
- The species has experienced a dramatic range contraction — extirpated from ~3,000 river miles, now confined to roughly a quarter of its historical distribution.
- Of only three extant wild populations, two (San Juan and mainstem Colorado above Lake Powell) are described as small and declining or threatened; only the Green River Basin population is considered viable, and even that is insecure.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's May 7, 1990 draft jeopardy Biological Opinion concluded that further water withdrawal via the Animas-La Plata Project would reduce the probability of ever achieving the streamflows necessary to maintain the "physical and biological integrity" of the San Juan River — a conclusion the Desert Fishes Council explicitly endorsed.
- Historical/cultural evidence of the species' value is cited: an 1889 Christmas feast menu at Lee's Ferry featured "Colorado salmon" (i.e., Colorado squawfish) alongside turkey and Arizona apples, countering contemporary characterizations of the species as a "trash fish."
- The letter argues the project's Environmental Impact Statement (finalized over a decade prior to 1990) was outdated and inadequate in addressing environmental impacts, and separately challenges the project's economic justification relative to its ~$600 million cost, noting only 36% of project water was allocated to Ute Indian Tribe needs.
- The document reflects a broader conservation ethic rooted in [[Aldo Leopold]]'s "Land Ethic" (from *A Sand County Almanac* and *Round River*), used to frame species preservation as an ethical imperative rather than purely economic calculation — historically influential in shaping the [[Endangered Species Act]], [[National Environmental Policy Act]], [[Clean Water Act]], and [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]].
