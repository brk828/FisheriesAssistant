# Fresh-Water Fishes of Panama East of 80° W

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This classic zoogeographic paper by [[Carl H. Eigenmann]] (Indiana University Studies, Contribution No. 175) analyses the freshwater fishes of eastern and southern Panama, the region east of the 80° W meridian that includes the [[Chagres River]] and [[Panama Canal]] basin, the [[Chepo River]] (Bayano) basin and the [[Tuyra River]] basin. Its central question is how the faunas of the Atlantic and Pacific slopes arose and whether they migrated by land over the continental divide or by sea. Eigenmann concludes that the Tuyra fauna derives largely from the [[Atrato River]] to the south-east of the Atlantic side, that the Chagres fauna derives mainly from South America, and that the ocean was a very minor migration route for freshwater fishes. He urged a full biological survey before the canal merged the two faunas.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The eastern and southern Panama region, including the Chagres (the only large river flowing to the Atlantic in this area), the Chepo (Bayano) and the Tuyra (the largest river studied, draining a territory about 120 miles north to south).
- **Geography:** The continental divide lies close to the Pacific near the Chagres, but within a few miles of the Atlantic between the Chagres and Colombia. Pacific-side rivers are longer and tidal, and the Tuyra is navigable halfway across the continent.
- **Historical surveys:** Earlier work by [[Gunther]] (1864–66), [[Regan]] (Biologia Centrali-Americana), [[Vaillant]] (1897) and Eigenmann himself (1905) was followed by a joint [[Smithsonian Institution]] and [[Field Museum]] survey by [[Meek and Hildebrand]] (1911–1912; published 1916), which the author regards as the definitive account of the pre-canal fauna.
- **Motivation:** The [[Panama Canal]] project raised urgent interest in the two faunas, since the canal would mix them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Atrato–Tuyra divide:** The lowest point is variously reported at 400 to 800 feet (Selfridge gives 400 feet at Cajon and 410 feet at the Cacarica Pass; the Canal Commission map gives 800 feet). The Atrato plain is low and extends close to the divide, whereas the Pacific slope is more broken, with hills of 250–400 feet.
- **Gulf of San Miguel:** A tidal harbour on the Pacific side carrying tidewater halfway across the isthmus, into which the Savana and Tuyra rivers flow.
- **Atrato–San Juan route:** The divide is under 100 feet higher than the Istmina pass, and the gradient is gentle. This route is the strategic point for South American fish distribution, yet no fish records existed from it.
- **Hypothetical sea-level change:** A depression of about 400 feet would convert the Atrato and San Juan valleys and the Canal Zone into a channel, flooding parts of the Tuyra and Mamoni basins.
- **Central American history:** [[Gymnotus]] and [[Symbranchus]] occur in South America to Guiana and Colombia and in Guatemala, but not in Costa Rica or Panama. The Cichlid radiation in Mexico and Central America may predate the emergence of the Panama isthmus.

## Key Findings

**1. Tuyra fauna (Atrato origin)**
- 50 species are recorded from the Tuyra; 104 species are known from the Atrato and Tuyra combined.
- 19 species (18% of the combined total) occur in both rivers; 38% of Tuyra fishes occur in the Atrato.
- Migration was overwhelmingly westward, from the Atrato to the Tuyra. No strictly Pacific-slope forms (for example [[Awaous transmontanus]] and [[Philypnus maculatus]]) reached the Atrato.
- Eleven Tuyra species lack Atrato representatives (for example [[Lasiancistrus planiceps]], [[Hemibrycon dariensis]], [[Sternarchus rostratus]]); most of their genera occur in the Magdalena or San Juan.

**2. Chagres fauna (mainly southern origin)**
- 44 freshwater species were recorded from the Chagres before the canal; 3 are endemic, all members of widespread genera.
- The fauna is distributed across 10 families, including Siluridae, Loricariidae, Characidae, Gymnotidae, Cichlidae, Poeciliidae, Gobiidae and Centropomidae.
- Absent are the Palearctic families (minnows, suckers, sunfishes, perches, salmon and trout, sturgeons), which reach their southern limit north of Guatemala.
- Of 38 genera, 28 also occur in the Atrato or Magdalena.
- Estimated origins: about 70% of the fauna from the south, about 16% from the north, with the remainder marine.
- 18 species (about 40%) occur in the small Pacific streams opposite the Chagres, 43% in the Chepo, 27% in the Tuyra and 18% in the Atrato.

**3. Migration routes**
- The Chagres is the meeting point of three migration streams: a major one from the south, a smaller one from the north, and one from the ocean.
- Most southern immigrants likely followed the route Atrato → Tuyra → Chepo → Chagres, crossing the continental divide twice.
- Eigenmann concludes that the ocean served only to a very small extent as a highway for freshwater fishes. Only the partly marine Eleotridines (Gobiidae) and possibly [[Hyphessobrycon panamensis]] likely came by sea.
- Only [[Astyanax ruberrimus]] is recorded as moving directly between the Tuyra and San Juan without passing through the Atrato.

**4. Marine versus freshwater divergence**
- Of 23 species from strictly freshwater families (Characidae, Siluridae, Loricariidae, Gymnotidae, Cichlidae), 12 crossed the Panama divide.
- Of 15 marine and lowland Gobiidae species, only [[Sicydium salvini]] is identical on both sides, suggesting that marine forms separated long ago and that freshwater intermigration is more recent.

**5. Conservation and research recommendations**
- Eigenmann urged Congress and the Bureau of Fisheries to fund an exhaustive biological survey of the canal zone before the faunas merged.
- He stated that the Atrato–San Juan route would depend on private enterprise.
- Work on that route was later carried out by Eigenmann and Charles Wilson (Indiana University Studies, No. 46).

**6. Central American origins**
- The Cichlid radiation may indicate that the family colonized Central America before the Panama land bridge formed.
- Very few southern Panamanian fishes extend into Costa Rica.
