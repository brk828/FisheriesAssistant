# Seasonal Habitat Use of Striped Bass in a Large Southwestern Reservoir: Influence of Water Quality and Season

#ichthyology #marine-biology #morone-saxatilis

## Executive Summary

This Transactions of the American Fisheries Society paper (Matthews, Hill, Epps & Gelwick, 1989) uses six years of experimental gillnetting (April 1981 to October 1986) to describe seasonal habitat use by [[Striped Bass]] in an uplake study area of [[Lake Texoma]], a large, shallow reservoir on the Red and Washita rivers. Fish were sorted into small, medium and large size classes. Large fish were never caught in the study area when surface water exceeded 22°C, and small fish persisted through summer, declining mainly above 29°C. The authors conclude that the results support the [[Thermal Niche Hypothesis]] and the [[Temperature-Oxygen Hypothesis]], and that summer distribution is shaped by temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) rather than by fish size alone.

The document also contains the truncated opening of a second paper (Maryland Department of Natural Resources) on juvenile striped bass recruitment in the Choptank River, which is summarized briefly below.

## Background & Description

Striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) are native to marine, estuarine and riverine environments, where they show strongly seasonal habitat use. Tagging studies have documented differing annual movement patterns among populations, with some remaining in freshwater rivers and others migrating to and from marine waters.

Since the 1960s, striped bass have been stocked in more than 100 southern U.S. reservoirs. Few studies, however, have examined their seasonal habitat use in reservoirs. Earlier work on [[Keystone Reservoir]] and [[Texoma Reservoir]] (Combs and Peltz 1982; Summers 1982) found large adults concentrated downlake near dams in summer. Those studies had small samples (16 and 20 fish) and included only large fish. Summers (1982) reported that adults (mean weight 6.9 kg) occupied the main basin from June through October or November, then dispersed until the following May. Studies in southeastern reservoirs (Cheek et al. 1985; Siler et al. 1986) provided year-round data, but those reservoirs differ morphometrically and chemically from shallow southwestern reservoirs.

Striped bass were introduced into Lake Texoma between 1965 and 1974 and now sustain a self-sustaining reproductive population (Harper and Namminga 1986). The lake covers about 36,000 ha. Its Red and Washita river arms reach a maximum depth of about 18 m and are strongly influenced by river inflows. The two arms converge into a deep (22-26 m) downlake main basin that stratifies in most years, leaving warm surface water over an anoxic hypolimnion.

### Study Objectives

The authors set three goals:
1. Test for statistically significant seasonality in striped bass abundance in the uplake area.
2. Evaluate habitat use and water quality against [[Coutant]]'s temperature-oxygen hypothesis. This hypothesis proposes that population size is limited by summer loss of suitable adult habitat, defined as water at roughly 18-25°C with DO of about 2-3 mg/L.
3. Combine these patterns with Summers' (1982) data to infer seasonal habitat use by large fish in uplake and downlake areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling.** Gillnetting took place in an uplake area about 40 km from the dam, where the reservoir is 2-3 km wide and about 18 m deep at normal pool. Six gill nets were set at fixed sites: the main channel (14-18 m deep), Buncombe Creek (a tributary bay, 8-10 m deep), and Mayfield Flats (a shallow cove, 3-4 m deep). Each net was 61 m long and 1.83 m deep, with eight 7.62-m panels of monofilament mesh (19-102 mm bar measure). This design followed [[Hubert]] (1983) to reduce size selectivity. Nets were typically set near midday and retrieved the next morning.

**Size classes.** Fish were grouped by weight: small (<1.36 kg), medium (1.36-2.27 kg) and large (>2.27 kg). Most small fish were at least 150 mm total length and at least age-1, so young-of-year were excluded. Terms implying maturity (juvenile, subadult, adult) were deliberately avoided because gonads were not examined.

**Measurements.** Surface temperature was recorded on each collection date and averaged across the study area. In summers 1982 and 1983, a Hydrolab Surveyor II measured vertical profiles of temperature and DO at each site. Downlake profiles were also documented in those years.

**Sampling effort.** From April 1981 to July 1984 (except January 1984), sets were made twice (28 months) or once monthly (13 months). Overall there were 402 net-nights on 67 collection dates in 1981-1984. No collections were made from August 1984 to February 1985. From March 1985 to October 1986, there were 82 net-nights over 20 dates, with three to five nets set in Buncombe Creek and Mayfield Flats; the midchannel site was dropped in 1985-1986.

**Statistics.** Because the data were not normally distributed and could not be normalized by log transformation, the authors used Kruskal-Wallis nonparametric tests. Catch per unit effort (CPUE) was defined as fish caught per net-night.

## Key Findings

### Catch totals and interannual variation
- A total of 3,813 striped bass were collected: 3,428 small, 270 medium and 115 large.
- No significant differences occurred among years in mean CPUE for any size class, so data were pooled.

### Seasonal patterns by size class
- **Large fish:** None were caught in the study area in June-September across all study years (198 net-nights). Large fish were caught in substantial numbers during October-May, but their catch varied widely in those months.
- **Medium fish:** Mean CPUE was significantly lower in June-September than October-May (H = 19.20, df = 1, P < 0.001).
- **Small fish:** Mean CPUE was significantly lower in June-September (H = 9.62, df = 1, P = 0.001), but small fish were caught in every month. Monthly means were 7.2 (June), 3.3 (July), 2.8 (August) and 5.5 (September), versus 9.0 in the other eight months.

### Temperature thresholds
- Large fish were never collected when surface temperature exceeded 22°C.
- Medium fish were collected on 13 of 47 netting dates above 22°C. Mean CPUE was significantly lower above 22°C than below (H = 25.26, df = 1, P < 0.001).
- Small fish were caught in large numbers at most temperatures, with up to 10-20 per net above 22°C. Mean catch was 5.5 per net above 22°C versus 9.2 at or below 22°C (H = 15.42, df = 1, P < 0.001). Many small fish stayed in the area well above 22°C.
- Small-fish CPUE was 11.8 at 28°C, 3.0 at 29°C and 1.9 at 30°C.
- Mean CPUE was 6.5 below 27°C and 3.8 at or above 27°C (H = 3.84, df = 1, P < 0.05).
- Across four temperature intervals (22.0-24.9, 25.0-26.9, 27.0-29.0 and above 29°C), mean CPUE was 7.0, 6.1, 6.5 and 1.9 (H = 7.69, df = 3, P = 0.05). The sharp drop occurred only above 29°C.

### Dissolved oxygen
- Small fish were collected in only 3 of 27 nets set where DO was below 6.0 mg/L (summers 1982-1983).
- Small fish were collected in 37 of 50 nets where DO was at or above 6.0 mg/L.

### Downlake and vertical distribution
- On 16 August 1982, large fish were concentrated downlake just above the anoxic hypolimnion. No downlake water was as cool as 25°C. At about 12 m, water was 27.8°C with 4 mg/L DO.
- Uplake water with more than 3 mg/L DO ranged from 29 to 31.5°C on the same date.
- The authors infer that large fish move downlake to cooler, oxygenated refugia in summer.

### Interpretation
- The authors state that the results support the temperature-oxygen squeeze hypothesis for large fish. Their one departure is that adults in Lake Texoma may tolerate temperatures somewhat above 25°C without high mortality (Matthews 1985). [[Zale]] et al. (1988) reported that adults in [[Keystone Reservoir]] tolerate up to 29°C.
- Small-fish patterns match the [[Thermal Niche Hypothesis]]. [[Cox and Coutant]] (1981) found that laboratory growth of juveniles peaked near 26°C and fell to 50% of maximum near 30°C. The field decline in catch above 29°C corresponds closely to that laboratory threshold.
- The authors conclude that the reduced catch of small fish above 29°C may reflect an underlying physiological cost.
- Managers should account for reservoir-wide physicochemical profiles and size-specific responses when estimating habitat available to striped bass.

### Management implications
- Seasonal habitat use may strongly affect interactions with other species.
- The authors note that absence from collections cannot prove absence from an area, though combined evidence strongly supports summer downlake movement by large fish.

### Second paper (truncated): Choptank River juvenile recruitment
The final pages include only the opening of a Maryland Department of Natural Resources paper on juvenile striped bass in the Choptank River, Chesapeake Bay tributary. Its abstract reports that eggs, larvae and juveniles were sampled from 1980 to 1985 and indicates variable survival, with larval mortality described as critical. Early juvenile mortality was reportedly related to minimum water temperature, and rainfall apparently influenced toxic conditions. The paper also notes that recruitment and landings of striped bass have declined substantially. The sampling methods section begins with weekly sampling at four stations, but the text ends before results, so the full findings cannot be reported.
