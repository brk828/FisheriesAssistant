# Fishes of Aravaipa Creek: Literature Review and History of Research and Monitoring

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This literature review, prepared for the U.S. Bureau of Land Management under a cooperative agreement (March 2005), summarizes the history of fish monitoring, research, and conservation on [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial desert stream in southeastern Arizona that is one of the premier remnants of the native fish fauna of the [[Gila River Basin]]. The report aims to document past work, evaluate the value of monitoring, and recommend future study.

Aravaipa Creek retains seven native fish species, and its fish community is the most extensively studied in the Gila basin. Sampling has occurred since 1943, and the long-term monitoring data set spans 41 years, the longest in the basin. Monitoring shows the native fish community has remained relatively stable despite substantial human disturbance, but nonnative species are increasing. Analyses link native fish responses to natural flow regimes. The authors conclude that monitoring has been central to conservation and management, and they recommend continued biannual monitoring, a centralized data repository, and a mechanism for acting on monitoring results.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The creek rises in the uplands, flows roughly north-northwest from near Dudleyville and Mammoth, and joins the [[San Pedro River]] about 21 miles upstream of its confluence with the Gila River. Perennial flow extends to about 3,300 feet elevation. Stream gradient is generally below 1.0%. The creek is divided into three conventional reaches (lower, middle/canyon, and upper), with site names and sampling stations cross-referenced to earlier studies (Table 1).

**Hydrology.** Aravaipa Creek has a desert monsoonal hydrograph. Mean monthly discharge over a 50-year record ranges from 12 cfs (June) to 64 cfs (February). Base flow derives mainly from subsurface upwelling upstream of the upper canyon. The largest recorded flood, in 1993, peaked at an instantaneous 70,800 cfs according to USGS, though alternative modeling estimates 17,600 to 23,000 cfs. The most recent large flood (1993) was described as a 10+ year event with 13,000 cfs discharge.

**Land ownership.** Ownership is mixed, including [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) lands, [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) preserves, U.S. Forest Service lands, Coronado National Forest, and private holdings. The canyon core is a BLM Wilderness Area.

**Human history.** Native American populations (Hohokam, Mogollon, and later Apache) used the valley. European settlement from the 1800s brought ranching, irrigation, road construction, and later recreation. Major ecological changes included loss of cienega habitat in the upper valley, channel downcutting after the 1960s, and increased gravel substrate.

**Scope and methods.** The authors searched university libraries, abstracting services, and Google Scholar, and drew on unpublished agency records, museum data (the SONFISHES database), and personal collections. Water-rights documents and riparian vegetation and amphibian/reptile literature were excluded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish community.** Seven native species are extant: [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]]. Spikedace and loach minnow are federally listed as threatened. Roundtail chub was petitioned for federal listing but has not been listed. Five further native species are extirpated or probable former residents, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]]. Gila topminnow reintroduction attempts in 1965, 1966, and 1977 failed to establish lasting populations.

**Nonnative species.** Nine nonnative species have been recorded, five of which are established. These include [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], and [[Common Carp]]. [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are known only from ponds or isolated records. A single 1989 smallmouth bass record was judged likely to be misidentified largemouth bass. In 1981, only three nonnative species were in the mainstream; by 2005 four were routinely found, and three more had invaded.

**Comparison with other streams.** Only the upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico) and [[Eagle Creek]] retain more native species in the basin. Aravaipa Creek is distinguished less by species count than by the fact that all its native species are still commonly found, its lack of established nonnatives, its relatively unaltered flow, and its level of protection.

**Contaminants.** An FWS survey in October 1995 found high levels of arsenic and cadmium in parts of the creek. Later studies found low bioaccumulation and mine-tailing sources were considered possible but uncertain.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and monitoring history**
- Sampling began in 1943 with museum collections by James R. Simon. Long-term monitoring by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] at [[Arizona State University]] began in 1963 and has produced a 41-year record (1964 to present). Following Minckley's death in 2001, monitoring continued at the [[University of Arizona]] under Peter Reinthal.
- Monitoring makes up 48% of all fish sampling events. The average is 3.1 monitoring events per year (mode 3, range 0 to 11). Total sampling averages 6.5 events per year (mode 6, range 0 to 17).
- Three heavy-sampling periods occurred: the 1960s (Barber and Minckley), late 1970s and early 1980s (Minckley's students), and 1989 to 1993 (red shiner response). Sampling since 1994 has been below average except for 2002.
- Funding came mainly from the Arizona Department of Water and Game (Arizona Game and Fish Department, AGFD), TNC, and BLM, with much of the long-term work conducted using student labor and university resources.

**Native fish trends**
- Monitoring shows the native fish community has remained remarkably consistent since the 1940s, despite changes in human use.
- [[Velasco]] (1997) found native fish responses were positive to flooding, while nonnative fish responded negatively. He concluded natural flow regime drives much of the ecosystem and recommended protecting flow heterogeneity.
- [[Eby et al. 2003]] analyzed the long-term data and found decreases in species richness in the lower reach, declines in spikedace and loach minnow, and increases in longfin dace and nonnative species. Decreases in lower-reach native diversity were linked to increased nonnative abundance.

**Genetics and taxonomy**
- Aravaipa spikedace and loach minnow populations are genetically distinct from other Gila basin populations, and [[Tibbets]] (1998) argued this supports increased protection. Aravaipa Creek was the most divergent of the upper Gila samples for all three species studied.
- Fish from Aravaipa Creek are considered important genetic reserves for populations elsewhere in the basin, which are declining.

**Conservation and management**
- Land acquisition by TNC and BLM, and the designation of the BLM Aravaipa Wilderness, were key protective steps. Instream flow water rights were obtained by BLM and TNC.
- Federal Endangered Species Act Section 7 consultations involving the creek total 12 entries in Table 12 (five formal and five informal consultations on federal actions affecting spikedace and loach minnow, plus two larger-scope barrier actions).
- Fish barriers were proposed in 1988–1990 after red shiner was detected. Paired barriers on the lower creek were completed in April 2001. The temporary upper barrier was built by TNC in September 1998.
- Nonnative control measures included chemical removal of green sunfish from one stock tank in 1987 and a 1998 closure of the creek to fishing by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission.

**Recommendations**
- Continue long-term biannual (spring and autumn) monitoring using ASU/UofA protocols, which were judged not to cause adverse impacts.
- Consider increased monitoring intensity, such as quarterly sampling or periodic monthly years, and supplemental short-term monitoring for specific issues such as red shiner.
- Consolidate all monitoring data and field notes into one permanent repository, as no single agency held the complete record.
- Limit voucher specimen collection to once every five to ten years, plus novel or incidental mortalities.
- Produce periodic five-year reports, with BLM or TNC suggested as responsible parties.
- Establish a mechanism for management agencies to act promptly on monitoring results.

**Conclusion.** The authors argue that Aravaipa Creek is a unique and irreplaceable native fish resource. Long-term monitoring has been essential for detecting nonnative invasions, guiding conservation, and supporting adaptive management. Its value depends on timely application of monitoring information to management decisions.
