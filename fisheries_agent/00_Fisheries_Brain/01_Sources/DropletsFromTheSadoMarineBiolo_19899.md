# Droplets from the Sado Marine Biological Station, Niigata University-VII: Further Notes on Some Anomalous Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This short report by [[Yoshiharu Honma]] documents six anomalies in six specimens: five bony fishes collected around [[Sado Island]] and one cartilaginous fish (a skate) from off Naoetsu on the western Niigata coast of the [[Sea of Japan]]. The specimens were examined macroscopically and, where useful, by X-ray. The anomalies include traumatic tail loss, a distorted lower jaw, sinistrality (reversed body sides), a bifurcated dorsal spine, a predator-inflicted dorsal injury, and aberrant pectoral fins. Most injuries are attributed to attacks by piscivorous fishes, the jaw deformity appears congenital, and the sinistral flounder is reported as the first record for its species. The skate is the second known batoid record of this kind of deformity in Japanese waters.

## Background & Description

The paper is part of the "Droplets from the Sado Marine Biological Station" series, published in the *Report of the Sado Marine Biological Station, Niigata University*, No. 24 (March 1994), pages 11–21. The author notes that the specimens were often supplied by local fishermen through [[Eikichi Noda]] of the Niigata Prefectural Cultural Fisheries Center on Sado Island, or by [[Rokuroh Mizusawa]].

The six cases are:

1. **Tailless file fish, [[Rudarius ercodes]]** (collected 17 November 1989, Himezu Fishing Port, by hand net from a seaweed bed). The tail, about five vertebrae and the caudal fin, was lost, probably to a single bite. Total length was not measured directly; the author estimated a normal standard length of over 40 mm and total length over 50 mm. Vertebral count was given as 7+8=15.
2. **Young [[Yellowtail]], [[Seriola quinqueradiata]]** (26 March 1990, set net off Tsubaki, Ryotsu Bay). The lower jaw was distorted, leaving the mouth permanently open. Total length 365 mm, standard length 307 mm, fork length 327 mm, body weight 410 g. Upper jaw 32 mm; deformed lower jaw 12 mm.
3. **Sinistral [[Korean Flounder]], [[Glyptocephalus stelleri]]** (4 April 1991, deep-sea gill net for [[Walleye Pollock]], off Suizu). Total length 220 mm, weight 80 g. Externally normal apart from reversed sidedness.
4. **File fish, [[Thamnaconus modestus]], with bifurcated dorsal spine** (21 November 1991, gill net off Aikawa). Total length 230 mm, standard length 220 mm, weight 20 g.
5. **Injured [[Striped Beakperch]], [[Oplegnathus fasciatus]]** (4 June 1992, set net off Toyooka, Ryotsu City). Total length 132 mm, standard length 110 mm.
6. **Skate, [[Raja kwangtungensis]] (Raja sp.), with aberrant pectoral fins** (26 December 1991, motor trawl net, 230 m depth, off Naoetsu). Female; total length 275 mm, disc length 153 mm, tail length 134 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several threads of ecological interpretation run through the paper:

- **Predation-related injuries.** The author attributes the tail loss in *Rudarius ercodes* and the dorsal injury in *Oplegnathus fasciatus* to single or repeated bites from large, sharp-toothed piscivores. The sunrise sculpin, [[Pseudoblennius cottoides]], is suggested as a likely predator of the file fish in seaweed beds. In the open sea, [[Cutlass Fish]] (*Trichiurus lepturus*) is a known candidate for injuring [[Pomfrets]] (*Pampus* spp.).
- **Congenital versus acquired defects.** The jaw deformity in the yellowtail is interpreted as congenital, in contrast to a masu salmon with a missing lower jaw, which was probably caused by a longline hook. The cause of the bifurcated spine and the skate's pectoral anomaly is not established.
- **Human influence on flatfish anomalies.** Earlier authors ([[Koski]], 1974; [[Moore and Posey]], 1974; [[Gartner]], 1986) proposed that anthropogenic environmental change can enhance flatfish anomalies in shallow coastal and estuarine habitats. The author argues this is unlikely for the Korean flounder, which lives in deeper water over 200 m.
- **Genetic and geographic patterns.** Sinistrality in the [[Starry Flounder]] (*Platichthys stellatus*) rises from south to north on the North American west coast (Forrester, 1969), and was reported as 100% in Japanese specimens (Hubbs and Kuronuma, 1942). The genetic basis of sinistrality remains unresolved.

## Key Findings

- **Tailless file fish:** The Sado specimen of *Rudarius ercodes* lost its tail through a predator attack. The wound had partly regenerated but was not fully closed. Similar tailless file fishes were already recorded in the literature, including *Aluterus monoceros* (Honma and Noda, 1987) and *Stephanolepis cirrhifer* (Hori, 1977).
- **Yellowtail jaw deformity:** The lower jaw was reduced and bent, leaving the mouth permanently open. The right dentary reached the left corner of the mouth, and there was no median symphysis. The author found no directly comparable case in fish literature and considers the condition likely congenital, though the cause is unknown.
- **First sinistral Korean flounder:** This is described as the first report of sinistrality in *Glyptocephalus stelleri*. The author notes earlier reversal records in related flatfishes, including *Glyptocephalus zachirus* (Follett et al., 1960), *Poecilopsetta plinthus* (Amaoka, 1964) and *Limanda yokohamae* (Abe and Senna, 1987).
- **Bifurcated dorsal spine in *Thamnaconus modestus*:** The spine split at about 30° and the branches were unequal (15 and 21.6 mm). Each branch was serrated on the inner margin, and the spine was not housed in a groove. This was the second such specimen from Sado Island. A third, 182 mm long, was caught in Ryotsu Bay in late April 1993.
- **Injured striped beakperch:** The dorsal region from nape to caudal peduncle was lost, leaving an X-ray-confirmed loss of most dorsal spine and neural spine elements. The wound healed, but the ridgeline became irregular and the dark cross bands did not meet precisely. Neural spines from the VIth vertebra posteriorly were deformed during healing. The author compares this with the deformed black porgy reported by Kobayashi (1981), which the author suggests was also injured rather than congenital.
- **Skate with aberrant pectoral fins:** The pectoral fins did not fuse with the cranio-thoracic region. The free tips curved inward toward the nostrils, and the angle between each fin and the head was about 40°. Mizusawa (1992) described this as a cranial abnormality, but the author interprets it as a pectoral anomaly. The author relates it to the embryonic extension of the pectoral fins observed by Honma, Iwami and Aoyagi (1993). The only other Japanese batoid record is a stingray, [[Dasyatis akajei]] (Honma and Sugihara, 1971), and the author cites at least nineteen non-Japanese reports of fin abnormalities.
