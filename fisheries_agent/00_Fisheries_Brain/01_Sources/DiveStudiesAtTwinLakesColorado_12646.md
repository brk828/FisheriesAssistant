# Dive Studies at Twin Lakes, Colorado, 1974–75

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-76-15, September 1976) documents qualitative, observational data from SCUBA dives in [[Twin Lakes]], a pair of connected high-altitude glacial lakes in Lake County, Colorado. Three dive series, each about a week long, were made in June and August 1974 and June 1975. The dives supported the [[Twin Lakes Ecological Study]], which was designed to document preproject conditions before the construction of the 200-MW [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. The report also tests the use of [[High Altitude SCUBA Diving]] as a tool in limnological research and checks the validity of altitude-adjusted [[Repetitive Dive Tables]].

## Background & Description

**Study setting.** Twin Lakes lie about 20.9 km (13 mi) south of Leadville, Colorado. A dam built in 1901 on the Lake Creek outlet joined the upper and lower lakes into one water body at 2798 m (9,180 ft). The dam sits at the shallow eastern end, and the deepest areas are in the western and mid-section of the lower lake. The planned forebay will raise the water level to 2804.9 m (9,202 ft).

**Powerplant context.** The Mt. Elbert plant will pump water about 123.5 m (405 ft) uphill to a new forebay during off-peak periods and generate power when demand is highest. The first 100-MW unit was scheduled to begin operation in October 1977. Two 100-MW units will be installed, and the plant could operate up to 23 hours per day. Concerns included mixing of the glacial flour on the lake bottom, which could alter the ecology.

**Diving methods.** Dives were run in pairs with a surface observer timing each dive. Actual lake altitude was 2804.4 m (9,200 ft), but calculations used 3048.7 m (10,000 ft) as a safety factor. The dive team was [[Lower Missouri Region Dive Team]] members who were partly acclimated to high-altitude work. Capillary depth gauges were found to be as much as 20 percent inaccurate below 9.1 m (30 ft), so altitude-adjustable diaphragm gauges were used more extensively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.**
- Maximum depths: 28 m (92 ft) in the upper lake and 27 m (89 ft) in the lower lake.
- Surface areas: about 263 ha (650 acres) for the upper lake and 736.5 ha (1,820 acres) for the lower lake.
- Stratification peaks in early August, with thermoclines between 9 and 15 m (30 and 50 ft). The lakes become nearly isothermal by mid-October.
- Maximum surface temperatures reach 20°C briefly in late July and early August. Winter inverse stratification holds about 4°C near the bottom.
- Ice forms around mid-December, reaches 0.6 to 0.8 m (24 to 30 in), and melts from late April to late May.

**Glacial flour.** Bottom deposits of [[Glacial Flour]] reach up to 7.9 m (26 ft) thick. This very fine flocculent sediment covers most of the bottom, including the plants. It is easily suspended by diver fin kicks and by slight water movement, and it can remain suspended for days. Divers retrieved undisturbed sediment cores in June 1975 using an alcohol and dry-ice sampler, with the aim of determining sedimentation rates and the lake's biological productivity history.

**Fishery and introductions.**
- Lake trout were introduced in the early 1920s. The population is self-sustaining and provides a trophy fishery, with fish reaching 1066 mm (42 in) and 14.4 kg (32 lb).
- Success of the lake trout is attributed to the introduced [[Mysis Shrimp]] (*Mysis relicta*, 12 to 18 mm long). Lake trout up to about 508 mm (20 in) feed on shrimp, and larger fish switch to suckers and rainbow trout.
- Nearly 45 lakes and reservoirs in Colorado, Wyoming, and Utah received shrimp from Twin Lakes in the late 1960s and early 1970s, making it one of the best shrimp sources in the Rocky Mountain region.
- Smelt eggs were introduced in 1972, and about 2,500 [[Bonneville Cisco]] were transplanted from Bear Lake, Utah, in 1971. Transplants of forage fish had not been successful as of the report.

## Key Findings

**Aquatic vegetation**
- Plant abundance and species composition differ strongly between the lakes, and the lower lake is the more productive.
- Plant growth disappears below about 3 m (10 ft) in the upper lake. In the lower lake, the highest densities occur between 3 and 9 m (10 and 30 ft).
- [[Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton praelongus*) is abundant in the lower lake, mostly at the east end at 4.5 to 7.5 m (15 to 25 ft). It grew from 1 to 2 m (3 to 7 ft) tall in June to 2.5 to 4 m (8 to 13 ft) in August. It was never observed in the upper lake.
- [[Stonewort]] (*Chara* sp.) was the most obvious plant to divers in both lakes. It occurred from 1.5 to 14 m (5 to 46 ft) in the lower lake and from 1.5 to 3 m (5 to 10 ft) in the upper lake, and it was blooming in August.
- [[Waterweed]] (*Elodea* sp.) was also prominent in the lower lake. Some plants could not be identified because their roots were buried too deep in the glacial flour.
- The greatest density and variety of plants were seen during the August 1974 dives.

**Invertebrates (Mysis shrimp)**
- Shrimp were observed in both lakes at densities of about 200/m² (19/ft²).
- The highest densities were 500 to 600/m² (46 to 56/ft²), recorded near the pumping plant in August 1975.
- Shrimp stay on the bottom during daylight and migrate upward at night, occasionally rising to within 2 m (6.6 ft) of the surface. Mass migration was never observed. Some shrimp moved toward rocky shore areas.
- Two collection methods were compared. The Colorado Division of Wildlife trawl worked well at all depths. The vacuum cone was less effective because it sank into the glacial flour and recovery was slow.

**Fish**
- Suckers were the most common fish in both lakes and in Lake Creek below the dam. Suckers up to 300 mm (12 in) were seen at night.
- [[Lake Trout Fry]] were observed only once, in June 1974, in the powerplant tailrace area of Juday Bay at 19 m (62 ft). Densities were about 200/m² (19/ft²), roughly 100 mm (4 in) above a bare glacial flour bottom with no cover.
- Lake trout up to 600 mm (24 in) were observed during night dives. Rainbow trout (250 to 350 mm, or 10 to 14 in) were also seen at 4.5 to 6 m (15 to 20 ft).
- A dead smelt was found at about 6 m (20 ft), the first smelt observation recorded.
- Night dives were the most productive for observing fish and shrimp.

**Bottom characteristics**
- Rocks and gravel dominate the shallow eulittoral zone, while glacial flour covers the deep flat bottom.
- Large rocks occur at Rocky Point on the north shore of the lower lake. These rocky areas appear suitable for lake trout spawning.
- A mold-type growth occurred at 6 to 9 m (20 to 30 ft) in the upper lake, most visibly near the outlet in August 1974.
- An unexplained, recently burrowed hole was found at about 4.5 m (15 ft) in the lower lake in August 1974.

**Diving safety and methods**
- The maximum dive reached 23.7 m (78 ft) at 3048 m (10,000 ft) altitude. This is equal to nearly 35 m (115 ft) at sea level.
- Bottom time at that depth was limited to 10 minutes. No decompression stops were required, and no diving sickness was reported.
- Tables A-1 and A-2 by [[E. R. Cross]] were used to build the repetitive dive tables (A-4) for altitudes of 2743 to 3048 m (9,000 to 10,000 ft). The report states these tables "worked well" and supports their validity.

**Conclusions**
1. Vegetation differs greatly between the upper and lower lakes, and the lower lake is more productive.
2. Mysis shrimp are abundant over almost all the bottom.
3. Suckers appear to be the most abundant fish.
4. Rocks and gravel with vegetation dominate shallow areas, while glacial flour covers deep flat areas.
5. Glacial flour is easily disturbed by slight water movement.

The authors conclude that divers complement surface-based quantitative sampling with photography, rapid biological sampling, retrieval of delicate sediment cores, and direct observation. The report also cites other diver-based management work: bass nest counts at Lakes Mead and Havasu, kokanee salmon studies at Banks Lake, and underwater archaeology. Post-operation SCUBA surveys were planned to inform future pumped-storage design.
