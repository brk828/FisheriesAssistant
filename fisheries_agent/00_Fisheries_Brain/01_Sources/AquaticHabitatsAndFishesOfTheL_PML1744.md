# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Imperial Division, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This appendix describes the aquatic habitats and biota of the Imperial Division of the lower [[Colorado River]], a roughly 15 km reach flowing from river mile 642 to 657 (the source text gives "b9"? km", likely about 25 km, which is not confirmed). The division combines a flowing upper channel with heavily vegetated backwaters, marshes and an aggraded section near [[Imperial Dam]]. The survey covers fishes, benthic invertebrates, vascular plants and algae, pigments, and physical-chemical conditions, mainly from 1974 to 1976. The community is dominated by [[Red Shiner]] in the mainstream and by a mixed assemblage of introduced and native species in the backwaters. Benthos is sparse in the channel but abundant in backwaters, and water quality is marked by high pH, supersaturated dissolved oxygen in vegetated areas, and extreme summer and winter temperatures.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The division is 15 km long in part (text figure unclear due to OCR). It consists of a flowing river reach, channels lined by heavy vegetation, backwater areas, and marshes, with an aggraded downstream portion near Imperial Dam, which was closed in the late 1930s and impounds a small reservoir where silt and sand largely fill the channel.
- **Substrate:** Silt and organic material predominate laterally to main currents. Swiftly flowing reaches have clean, moving sand. Dredging is minimal, done locally to maintain access for camping areas and fishing resorts.
- **Depth and structure:** Few places exceed 3 m depth, except where dense reed beds undercut banks. Currents range from absent to locally swift.
- **Water levels:** Fluctuations are smaller and more prolonged than upstream. Near Imperial Dam, daily changes are often under 10 cm and under 0.6 m over the year.
- **Dominant vegetation:** Giant African reed forms dense riparian stands, some over 5 m tall, that extend up to 10 m into the channel and break off as floating mats. Cattail, sedges, cottonwood and willow are also present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishes.** Seine sampling in the mainstream was limited by vegetation along the banks, so many shoreline species were missed. Five species were taken in the mainstream seines: red shiner (about 92% numerically), juvenile largemouth bass, threadfin shad, green sunfish and bluegill. Estimated totals were 291,483 fishes and 9.16 kg/ha. Backwaters held ten species, with an estimated 10,464 individuals and 11.54 kg/ha. Mosquitofish were most numerous there, followed by juvenile bluegill, red shiner, sailfin molly, young largemouth bass and green sunfish. The survey recorded 23 fish species for the division historically, of which 14 were caught in 1975-76. Some sampled gear (hoop nets, gill nets, trammel nets) caught more fish in daylight than at night.

**Benthos.** Mainstream benthic biomass was sparse, ranging from 0.1 to 0.24 kg/ha (excluding Asiatic clams, which made up 0.0 to 99.4% of the weight in some sites). Backwater dredging near Imperial Dam (84 samples) showed a far richer fauna, with mean biomass of 36.8 kg/ha (about 51% Asiatic clam) and mean density of 8,225 organisms/m², dominated by midges.

**Vascular plants and algae.** Spiny naiad was the most common submerged plant, forming dense backwater beds. Cladophora dominated algae in fast water, while benthic films of bluegreens and diatoms covered shallow backwaters. Drift contained more sedges and grasses than upstream areas.

**Pigments.** Chlorophyll a in June-August 1975 averaged 2 to 5 times higher than in upstream divisions. Mainstream and backwater values were similar, which the authors attributed to possible rapid water exchange, though physical-chemical data did not confirm this. Vertical profiles showed little stratification to 4 m.

**Physical-chemical conditions.** Surface water temperatures reached 32.5 °C in some backwaters in summer, and in February 1975 backwater temperatures fell to 7.9 °C, a level considered lethal to species such as threadfin shad, prompting fish to move into the channel (10 °C). Dissolved oxygen stayed near or above 100% saturation in the mainstream. Lentic surface samples exceeded 200% saturation under macrophyte beds, while near-bottom values often fell below 10%. pH ranged 8.0 to 8.4 in most backwaters, with a maximum of 9.3 over spiny naid beds. Turbidity was usually undetected, with a maximum of 35 JTU. Phosphate-phosphorus was present in nearly all samples (typically below 0.10 mg/L), and nitrate-nitrogen was absent in 19 of 68 backwater samples, suggesting uptake by algae.

## Key Findings

- The Imperial Division fish community is numerically dominated by [[Red Shiner]] in the mainstream and by mosquitofish and juvenile centrarchids in backwaters.
- Fish sampling in the channel was incomplete because dense bank vegetation prevented effective seining; shoreline species were underrepresented.
- Backwaters support a much larger benthic biomass than the channel, largely due to [[Asiatic Clam]] at sites near [[Imperial Dam]].
- Giant African reed and spiny naid strongly shape habitat structure, with dense mats and beds contributing to elevated dissolved oxygen and pH.
- Chlorophyll levels in 1975 were 2-5 times higher than upstream, indicating high algal productivity in this arid lower-river reach.
- Winter backwater temperatures (7.9 °C in February 1975) fell to levels lethal to threadfin shad, indicating seasonal thermal stress for some species.
- Lower nitrate and phosphate levels in backwaters may help explain similar pigment values across habitats.
- The reach has a low-gradient, arid-climate character with high evaporation and summer temperatures held near 30 °C in the mainstream.
