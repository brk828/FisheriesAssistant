# TaqMan Probe Detection of Fish Eggs and Larvae in Teleost Predator Stomachs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study tested whether species-specific [[TaqMan Real-Time PCR]] probes can detect prey DNA from fish eggs and larvae after ingestion by a teleost predator, the [[Whiting]] (*Merlangius merlangus*). Prey were [[Atlantic Cod]] (*Gadus morhua*) eggs and yolk-sac larvae, and [[European Plaice]] (*Pleuronectes platessa*) eggs. Estimated half-life detection times (T50) were 31 h for eggs and 26 h for larvae. Some positive detections persisted even after visual inspection showed complete gut clearance. The authors conclude that the method offers a rapid, taxon-specific way to detect predation on fish eggs and larvae that visual stomach-content analysis cannot resolve.

## Background & Description

Predation on fish eggs and larvae is considered a major source of early mortality and a possible control on [[Year-Class Strength]]. Despite this, it has rarely been quantified. Traditional [[Stomach Content Analysis]] relies on visual identification, which performs poorly for this kind of prey:

- **Eggs:** The chorion resists digestion, but identification to species is possible only for eggs with distinctive sizes or shapes.
- **Larvae:** Digestion is fast. More than 50% of small [[Capelin]] (*Mallotus villosus*) larvae became visually unidentifiable within 1 h of ingestion by 0-group cod. Herring (*Clupea harengus*) larvae were unidentifiable after about 2 h.

Molecular approaches using genetic probes target unique sequences in prey DNA. Earlier marine applications include detecting toxic algae, fish parasites and crab larvae in plankton, identifying algae in copepod guts, and detecting larval cod in predator stomachs. A TaqMan assay for plaice DNA had previously been used to detect predation on juvenile plaice. Before field use, two conditions must be verified: assay specificity, and the ability to detect target DNA after digestion.

The study used species-specific TaqMan assays for cod (Taylor et al., 2002) and plaice (Albaina et al., 2010). Fish were fed prey and tracked over time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study animals and husbandry**
- Whiting were caught locally with fixed gillnets during winter 2006–2007.
- Fish were acclimated in communal tanks of local sea water at ambient temperature at the [[Centre for Environment, Fisheries and Aquaculture Science]] (Cefas), Lowestoft, and fed sandeel (*Ammodytes* sp.) three or four times a week.
- One week before each trial, individual whiting were moved into thirty-two 50 L glass observation tanks in a constant-temperature room with running, gently aerated sea water. Room temperature was gradually lowered to the experimental temperature.
- Food was withheld for 48 h before feeding trials.
- Work was conducted under a UK Home Office licence and approved by the Cefas Ethical Review process.

**Prey sources**
- Cod eggs came from a commercial farm (Viking Fish Farms Ltd, Ardtoe Marine Laboratory, Argyll). Some eggs were hatched in the laboratory to produce larvae.
- Plaice eggs came from a Cefas broodstock. These eggs did not develop beyond the lateral-line/hatching stage, so plaice larvae could not be included.

## Key Findings

**Feeding behaviour**
- Initial trials tested whether whiting would voluntarily eat eggs and recently killed larvae, either inserted into whole sandeel pieces or into ground sandeel set in agarose.
- Feeding success varied strongly between individuals, and regurgitation was often seen in fish that did ingest food. This prevented confirmation of the amount of eggs or larvae consumed, and stomach analyses of these fish were negative.
- The fish showed consistent refusal to voluntarily ingest eggs and larvae, so prey were delivered in a controlled way for the main experiments.

**Detection kinetics**
- Estimated half-life detection rates (T50) were **31 h for eggs** and **26 h for larvae**.
- Some positive detections occurred after visual inspection indicated complete gut clearance, so visual inspection alone underestimates recent predation.
- Detection rates decreased during the first 12 h of the experiment. The authors suggest this could limit detection of predation events in field samples collected within 24 h, unless sampling is designed around this window.
- There was no significant difference in DNA detection between cod and plaice eggs, and no significant effect of predator group or batch on detection efficiency. Residual checks of the model were acceptable.

**Statistical approach**
- Detection data were fitted with a binomial generalized linear model (GLM) using a logit link. Model selection was done by stepwise procedures, and significant differences between treatments were tested.
- Specificity was confirmed because the TaqMan probes detected only the target DNA in the assays.

**Implications and conclusions**
- Because TaqMan probes are taxon-specific, the method can rapidly and unambiguously detect predation by teleosts on fish eggs and larvae.
- The authors note that detection time depends on predator and prey species. They point to the need for more comparisons across predator groups. Earlier studies on [[Molecular Detection Efficiency in Non-Teleost Predators]] have reported conflicting results.
- The rapid, species-specific molecular approach is well suited to screening large numbers of potential predators in marine field surveys.
