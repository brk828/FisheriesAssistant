# Leslie Canyon Lake: Proposed Reservoir and Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #yaqui-river-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department planning document (prepared July 1972, amended November 1976) evaluates a proposed multi-use reservoir on Leslie Creek in Cochise County, southeastern Arizona. The project was intended to meet demand for water-based recreation in southern Arizona. Hydrologic data from 1970 to 1975 showed the watershed could support only about half the originally planned lake, so the design was reduced from 250 to roughly 125–150 surface acres.

The document weighs recreational benefits against serious ecological and social costs. These include loss of a rare riparian stand, threats to a population of rare native fishes, wildlife losses from land development, and the destruction of archaeological sites. It also sets out four alternatives and mitigation measures. A 1985 letter from the Department of Water Resources reports that the Department's water right application and permit were cancelled effective February 22, 1985.

## Background & Description

**Project purpose and funding.** The [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] began a lake-construction program in 1954 to meet public demand for fishing and boating. Most lakes were built in east-central Arizona, so the Department sought more sites in the south. The Arizona Outdoor Recreation Coordinating Commission (1967) projected southern Arizona fishing water rising from 409 surface acres (1970) to 762 acres (1985). About 535 acres were available after [[Arivaca Lake]] and [[Lake Patagonia]] were completed in 1971. Leslie Canyon Lake was proposed to close the gap. Funding came from state and federal sources, including the [[Dingell-Johnson Fund]] (federal, with a 25% Department contribution).

**Site and watershed.** The site lies at the southern end of the [[Chiricahua Mountains]]. The 81.5 square mile watershed is bounded by the [[Swisshelm Mountains]] and the [[Pedregosa Mountains]]. Elevations range from 7,185 feet in the Swisshelms to 4,700 feet at the spillway crest. Two drainages feed [[Leslie Creek]]: Big Bend Creek and Mesa Draw.

**Design evolution.** The original design would have impounded 5,400 acre-feet across 250 acres, with a maximum depth of 60 feet at the dam. Runoff analysis led to a 125–150 acre redesign with a multi-stage dam for future enlargement. The Department considers 2–3 feet of average annual water-level fluctuation the practical maximum. Road relocation and land acquisition costs have made the cost-benefit ratio questionable.

**Vegetation.** The site is desert grassland, an ecotone of [[Chihuahuan Desert]] grassland and Encinal–Mexican oak woodland. Tobosa grass dominates the bottomlands, and sacaton the floodplain. Only about 12 acres of tree-form [[Riparian Vegetation]] occur through the project site, mostly willow, velvet ash, and cottonwood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian habitat.** Leslie Creek is a live stream fed by a spring above the dam site. An old silted rock dam creates shallow ponds and supports a moist, lush understory rare in desert and grassland biomes. Downstream, the riparian band narrows, with ash and netleaf hackberry increasing toward the lower reaches.

**Birds.** Table 1 lists birds recorded during a May 3–4, 1972 reconnaissance, and Table 2 lists species with suitable riparian habitat. Notable species include the [[Prairie Falcon]] (nesting cliffs at the dam site), golden eagle, and [[Mexican Duck]] (classed as endangered). Grassland species include scaled quail and several sparrows, some of which are only moderately common.

**Mammals.** No rare or endangered mammals are known from the lake area. Table 5 lists mammal species known or presumed present. The area was historic range for pronghorn and black-tailed prairie dog, both extirpated. Big game (javelina, mule deer, white-tailed deer) is most vulnerable to development pressure because of low population levels and slow reproduction.

**Fishes.** Four native fish species are documented in Leslie Creek (Table 6). This population is one of the few remaining live tributaries of the [[Yaqui River System]] in the United States. Status and origin details are summarized in Key Findings.

**Reptiles.** The area's reptile fauna is unusually rich, reflecting its position between the [[Sonoran Desert]], the Chihuahuan Desert, and the Sierra Madre of Mexico. Six species are on the Department's Threatened Wildlife of Arizona list, including the [[Gila Monster]]. Snakes are highly vulnerable to road mortality, which is expected to rise with traffic.

**Archaeology and geology.** Field surveys in March 1976 found substantial artifacts (metates, manos, points, a drill shaft holder) on a terrace above the flood plain, within the area to be inundated. The Arizona State Museum (Appendix A) confirmed at least one known site would be affected and recommended intensive survey. Watershed geology includes Gila conglomerate and deep alluvium of silt, sand, and gravel.

**Land ownership.** About 65% of the township (15,080 acres) is privately owned. The Lamberson-Riggs family controls 8,680 acres around the lake site. Land exchange and acquisition depend on resolving a downstream water-rights challenge.

## Key Findings

**Water yield and lake size**
- Watershed yield was estimated by transposing data from the Sonoita Creek and upper Santa Cruz watersheds. Average annual rainfall was taken as 16.0 inches.
- The predicted yield was 1,607 acre-feet/year, plus 195 acre-feet/year of spring base flow, for a total of 1,900 acre-feet/year. This supports a 250-acre lake with an annual spill probability of 64% and a mean fluctuation of 2.02 feet.
- Gauging since 1970 found runoff averaged only 1.2% of rainfall, about 52% of the predicted ratio. Precipitation averaged 14.9 inches (93% of prediction). No correlation between rainfall and runoff was found for 1970–1975.
- The watershed now supports a 125-acre lake with about 65% annual spill probability. A 148-acre lake would drop spill probability to near 50% and increase fluctuation enough to cause problems with boat ramps and shoreline facilities. Evaporation was calculated at 5.375 feet per surface acre per year.

**Negative impacts on habitat and wildlife**
- Inundation would remove about 125 acres of grassland, a small share of regional grassland.
- Almost 70% of the 12-acre riparian strip would be lost. Trees in the lake bed (5.9 acres), at the dam site (0.7 acres), and cleared for dam construction (about 1.7 acres) would be removed. About 3.8 acres of tree borders downstream would remain.
- Bird populations of both riparian and grassland communities would decline. Development would harass nesting falcons and golden eagles in the Swisshelms.
- Scaled quail would be hit hardest among game birds. Gambel's and Mearn's quail would be less affected.
- Big game (javelina, mule deer, white-tailed deer) would decline, and hunting in Area I would eventually disappear.
- Non-game fishes would be negatively affected. Habitat would be reduced, and warm-water non-native species would be introduced.

**Fish populations of concern (Table 6)**
- **[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): listed by Arizona as Status IV (distribution so limited that major disturbance could jeopardize it). A substantial population with several age classes is present. This is considered the most prolific and stable of the two US populations.
- **[[Yaqui Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): Status II (in danger of elimination from Arizona). It is a subspecies of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and reproduces healthily in Leslie Creek. It is documented in only one other US locality, Astin Spring.
- **[[Mexican Stoneroller]]** (*Campostoma ornatum pricei*): Status II. Identified by McNatt (1974). The only other viable Arizona population is in Rucker Canyon.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*): common in Arizona and numerically dominant in Leslie Creek. McNatt (1974) suggested Leslie Creek fish might be a distinct taxon because of morphological differences and the isolation of the Yaqui system.
- Mosquitofish records were attributed to misidentification of the Yaqui topminnow. The origin of the Yaqui chub and topminnow is somewhat controversial, but both are native to the Yaqui system, which is largely unsurveyed in Mexico.

**Recreation and use**
- Cochise County had about 76,000 residents in 1975. Douglas, with about 14,700 residents, is the nearest center, about 25 road miles away.
- Using a statewide 22.8% license-holder rate, the 125-acre lake would provide about 55 fishermen per surface acre. A 250-acre lake would give about 28. The comparison figure for Pima and Santa Cruz Counties is 204.
- Southeastern Arizonans valued cold-water fishing at $731 per surface acre versus $380 for warm-water fishing.
- Summer recreational use could exceed 300 man-days per surface acre. Recreationists stop considering water recreation a quality experience above this level.

**Planned fish stocking**
- Largemouth bass, channel catfish, and a redear × green sunfish hybrid are planned. Threadfin shad may be added later as forage, and rainbow trout may be stocked in winter if conditions allow.

**Irreversible commitments**
- All land under the lake, plus large private blocks, would be urbanized or lost to livestock and wildlife production.
- Over 8 acres of riparian vegetation would be lost to dam construction and inundation. About 1.1 miles of road relocation would commit rock, soil, and about 3 acres of vegetation.
- The loss of a short stretch of live stream, which is rare in southern Arizona, is considered the most significant loss. It would likely eliminate a healthy population of rare non-game fishes.

**Mitigation proposals**
- Reseed all disturbed construction areas with grass and browse species, with emergent vegetation planted in lake shallows.
- Build several small impoundments and check dams 2.5–3.0 miles east of the dam on Big Bend Creek to create cienega habitat and trap silt.
- Guarantee minimum flows below the dam to protect the fish population.
- Keep introduced game and forage fish out of the creek. Fence the stream to exclude cattle and give the area protected status.

**Alternatives considered**
- **Alternative A:** Do not build the lake. Development could proceed slower, or a private lake could be built with fewer public users but no buffer zone.
- **Alternative B:** Build smaller lakes elsewhere. [[Pack Saddle Lake]] (70 acres, on [[Coronado National Forest]] land) would give much of the benefit with less impact, though it requires a Forest Service special use permit.
- **Alternative C:** Build the lake but acquire much more surrounding land (Sections 15, 16, 21, 22) for public control, keeping recreation away from the shoreline. Wildlife impacts would be reduced, though recreational pressure would still harass game animals.
- **Alternative D:** Purchase only the state trust land on the north shore, and let the landowners build the lake under a public-access arrangement. Public cost would be lower.

**Water rights status**
- Land acquisition is on hold pending the outcome of litigation brought by downstream ranchers over Leslie Creek water rights.
- In a March 4, 1985 letter, the Department of Water Resources reported that Application to Appropriate No. R-2742 and Permit No. R-1952, filed by the Department, were cancelled effective February 22, 1985.
