# Young-of-the-Year Striped Bass Growth Variation in the Sacramento-San Joaquin System, 1956-1960

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This Inland Fisheries Administrative Report, submitted in August 1966 by [[Harold K. Chadwick]] of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], describes fall length variation in young-of-the-year [[Striped Bass]] collected by [[Beach Seine Sampling]] throughout most of their range in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] system from 1956 through 1960. Annual mean lengths were similar across years, indicating that ecological variation, including substantial variation in runoff and salinity, did not noticeably alter mean growth. Within the fall samples, mean lengths were greatest in the lower [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]] and in [[Suisun Bay]], and declined upstream and downstream. Growth patterns were only partly explained by food supply, and the author states that the factors controlling these differences could not be adequately explained at the time.

## Background & Description

The study is part of the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] (California F-5-R, "A Study of Sturgeon and Striped Bass"), funded by Federal Aid to Fish Restoration funds, and was performed in conjunction with the Delta Fish and Wildlife Protection Study.

Striped bass typically reach a mean length of about 1 inch in late June or early July in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system. Previously reported mean back-calculated lengths at the end of the first year were 4.2 inches in 1925 and 1928 (Scofield, 1931) and 4.1 inches in 1957 and 1958 (Robinson, 1960). Little was known about how growth varied among habitats and years, and such variation could indicate which ecological conditions are most favorable to young bass.

**Methods**
- Young bass were collected with 100-by-6-foot stretched-mesh beach seines.
- Sampling extended from western San Pablo Bay to the mouth of the [[Feather River]] on the Sacramento River, and to about 10 miles above [[Mossdale]] on the San Joaquin River.
- Most locations were sampled each year; collections were made in October each year except 1959, when they were made during the first half of November.
- [[Fork Length]] was measured to the nearest tenth of an inch. When several hundred fish were caught, a grab sample of 50 to 100 fish was measured.
- Fish under 6.5 inches were assumed to be young-of-the-year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and salinity:** Runoff and [[Salinity Intrusion]] varied widely during the 1956-1960 period, yet overall annual mean lengths remained similar.
- **Food supply:** Stomach analyses from the upper San Joaquin River indicated significantly less food than in lower portions of the river. The primary foods of young bass, including [[Neomysis]] and other crustacean plankton, are less abundant in the lower Mokelumne River and in the Sacramento system above the mouth of Cache Slough than in the San Joaquin and lower Sacramento rivers.
- **Downstream movement:** Larger young bass in the lower San Joaquin River may have been swept downstream by high spring river flows, since they were older and had more time to move before fall.
- **Spawning and early growth:** The mechanisms controlling early growth, including [[Spawning Season]] timing and larval growth rate, remain unknown.

## Key Findings

1. **Stable annual growth:** Overall annual mean lengths were similar across the five study years, indicating that ecological variation did not affect growth rates differentially. The exception was 1959, with a significantly greater mean length.
2. **Early 1959 growth:** In 1959, young bass reached a mean length of 1 inch earlier in the summer and were less numerous than in any other study year. Because fall mean length and mid-summer abundance were not related in other years, the author concluded the greater 1959 length was most likely associated with earlier achievement of 1-inch growth rather than with the later collections.
3. **Spatial pattern:** Mean lengths were greatest in the lower Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers and [[Suisun Bay]], and declined upstream and downstream. The largest deviation from the population mean was a lower mean length in samples from the San Joaquin River below [[Stockton]] and above [[Antioch]].
4. **Food hypothesis (partial support):** [[Donald E. Stevens]] hypothesized that the lower mean length in the upper San Joaquin River resulted from lower food supply, since stomachs from bass in that reach contained significantly less food. [[Shoken Sasaki]] observed a similar pattern. Stevens also suggested that the low length below Stockton resulted from young bass migrating downstream during their second summer.
5. **Limits of the food explanation:** Food supply does not explain the rest of the system. Growth rates are substantially better in the lower Mokelumne River and upper Sacramento River, even though food production is lower there. The author concludes that the food-growth correlation seen in the San Joaquin River does not apply to the rest of the system, and that available facts are insufficient to explain these variations.
6. **Overall conclusion:** Under present conditions, biological conditions and the physical factors that affect them appear to have little effect on overall growth, but significant spatial differences in growth persist and remain unexplained.
