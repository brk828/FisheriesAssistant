# El Niño and La Niña: A Global Perspective on Stream Ecology

#benthology #stream-ecology #hydrology #enso-climatology

## Executive Summary

This *Journal of the North American Benthological Society* perspective piece by Molles and Dahm tests whether the [[El Niño-Southern Oscillation]] (ENSO) cycle predicts spring streamflow in two mountain rivers of New Mexico. Using long-term records from the [[Gila River]] (60 years) and [[Pecos River]] (68 years), the authors show that spring runoff during El Niño years was significantly higher, and during [[La Niña]] years significantly lower, than in medial years. The authors argue that ENSO forecasts, which can be issued months in advance, could make stream disturbance studies more predictive. They also suggest that streamflow integrates precipitation and may be more predictable than local rainfall.

## Background & Description

The paper is a Perspectives contribution, which the journal describes as less formal than a standard research paper and open to speculation intended to stimulate discussion. The authors frame [[Stream Discharge]] as a central organizing variable in stream ecology. Extreme flows, both [[Flood Disturbance]] and [[Drought Disturbance]], alter stream function, community structure, and population dynamics. The authors note that drought may be as important an organizing factor as flooding in some streams, and that much year-to-year variability in stream systems is linked to discharge.

The ENSO cycle links sea surface temperature and barometric pressure across the Pacific. An El Niño (named after a warm coastal current off Peru at Christmas) is marked by elevated sea surface temperatures and reduced pressure in the eastern tropical Pacific. La Niña reverses these conditions and suppresses storm formation. The [[Southern Oscillation Index]], calculated from Tahiti–Darwin pressure differences, serves as the monitoring tool.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood and drought effects reviewed by the authors**
- Floods alter nitrogen dynamics, nitrification, and seston movement, and reduce algal biomass and invertebrate numbers and diversity. Post-flood recovery appears to depend on local flood frequency.
- Flow mediates competition among stream invertebrates and fishes.
- A severe flash flood eliminated an endangered fish population, *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, in a Sonoran Desert stream (Collins et al. 1981). Floods also reduced trout in Sagehen Creek (Seegrist and Gard 1972).
- Drying affects leaf decomposition, primary production, respiration, water chemistry, algal communities, and fauna in intermittent versus perennial reaches.

**Study rivers**
- Gila River: catchment area 4843 km², records 1928–1987.
- Pecos River: catchment area 490 km², records 1920–1987.
- Both drain high-altitude wilderness with little human impact on runoff. They sit at opposite ends of New Mexico, so they were chosen to represent the range of ENSO influence.

**Broader implications noted by the authors**
- ENSO affects precipitation across much of North America and tropical and subtropical regions. It also influences Amazon basin hydrology (Richey et al. 1989).
- Other large-scale drivers, including Eurasian snow cover and volcanism (Krakatau, El Chichón), may also affect regional climate.
- ENSO may influence stream heat budgets and water chemistry. In 1983, sulfate deposition from an El Niño period reduced alkalinity in alpine streams of northern New Mexico (C. S. White, unpublished data).
- Current [[Global Circulation Models]] do not simulate ENSO, so projections of regional precipitation and streamflow in ENSO-affected areas are considered highly suspect.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- El Niño years were taken from the Quinn et al. (1987) reconstruction, a 450-year history off Peru. Only strong to very strong events that persisted into a second year were used.
- The effect of each El Niño was assigned to the following winter–spring snowmelt. For example, the 1972–1973 event was assigned to 1973.
- La Niña years were identified from a Tahiti–Darwin pressure record and the monthly *Climate Diagnostics Bulletin*. A five-month running mean of the Southern Oscillation Index of about +1.0 standard deviation or greater indicated La Niña.
- Spring flow was defined as total flow during April–June. Flow data were ln(n + 1) transformed and analyzed with ANOVA.

**Sample sizes**
- Gila River: 4 El Niño years, 12 La Niña years, 44 medial years.
- Pecos River: 5 El Niño years, 13 La Niña years, 50 medial years.

**Results**
- Flow differences among ENSO categories were significant in both rivers (p < 0.001).
- Mean spring runoff in El Niño years was 2.3–3.2× that of medial years and 6.0–7.4× that of La Niña years.
- Flow differences were roughly twice as large as the corresponding precipitation differences. The authors interpret this as stream systems amplifying precipitation variation.
- On the Gila, the El Niño years 1941, 1958, 1973, and 1983 produced the four highest spring flows on record. On the Pecos, these years produced four of the five highest spring flows.
- La Niña periods were associated with reduced flow, especially on the Gila. The extended droughts of 1950–1951 and 1954–1957 were notable.
- The Gila record showed a clearer ENSO signal than the Pecos. The authors attribute this to the Gila basin's more southerly and westerly position, with the Pecos near the northern limit of ENSO influence.

**Forecasting example**
- The Southern Oscillation Index began signalling strong La Niña conditions in late 1988. Precipitation was below normal across central and southern New Mexico in winter–spring 1989, and spring discharge was below average on both rivers.

**Application to stream ecology**
- McElravy et al. (1989) recorded reduced macroinvertebrate numbers and diversity in 1978 and 1983, the years of highest discharge and storm frequency in their California study. Those winters followed strong negative pressure differentials that could have served as an early warning.

**Principal conclusions**
- ENSO shows a strong correspondence with spring streamflow in New Mexico, and annual flow variability may be predictable from the Southern Oscillation Index.
- Long-term ENSO-linked records offer a temporal framework for stream ecology, and forecasts could place disturbance research on a more predictive footing.
- Future climate projections for ENSO-affected regions remain uncertain until ENSO is represented in global models.
