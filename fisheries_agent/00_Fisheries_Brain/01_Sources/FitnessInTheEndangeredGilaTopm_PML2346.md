# Fitness in the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Sheffer]], [[Hedrick]], [[Minckley]] and [[Velasco]] (Arizona State University, *Conservation Biology* 11(1), 1997) compares fitness correlates among four remnant populations of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in four separate watersheds of the United States. The work tests a previously reported positive association between allozyme heterozygosity and fitness, reported by [[Vrijenhoek]] and colleagues and by [[Quattro and Vrijenhoek]] (1989), which favored [[Sharp Spring]] stock for reintroductions. The authors could not confirm that association. Under laboratory conditions the population with the highest heterozygosity had no advantage in survival, growth, fecundity or bilateral asymmetry. The authors attribute the discrepancy most plausibly to differences in laboratory stress, and they support the draft recovery plan's recommendation to use hydrographically nearest stock.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Gila topminnow is a small, live-bearing fish of the family Poeciliidae, one of two subspecies of Sonoran topminnow in Arizona. It was federally listed as endangered in 1967 and listed as threatened by the State of Arizona in 1988.
- **Historical status:** Once among the most abundant fishes in the lower Gila River basin, it persisted at only a few isolated sites by the 1990s. Loss and fragmentation of shallow-water habitat and introduction of the non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) are the main causes of decline. The species also persists in Sonora, Mexico, where introduced fishes and habitat alteration threaten it.
- **Study populations:** Remnant populations exist in at most nine locales. Samples were taken from four subdivisions:
  - [[Bylas Springs]] complex (Gila River mainstem, San Carlos Apache Reservation), isolated in 1914.
  - [[Ciénega Creek]] (Pima County), the largest remaining U.S. population.
  - [[Sharp Spring]] (tributary to the upper Santa Cruz River).
  - [[Monkey Spring]] (tributary to Sonoita Creek), the most chemically and thermally constant habitat, at 27.4 ± 0.6 °C.
- **Mosquitofish status:** Six of the nine habitats are infested with mosquitofish. Monkey Spring and Ciénega Creek are unaffected.
- **Historical reintroductions:** Before 1986, reintroductions used captive stock from Monkey Spring. Following the allozyme-based recommendation, stock was switched to Sharp Spring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population structure:** Historically, populations probably formed nearly continuous distributions along the lower Gila River. Droughts and cold winters caused range contractions, and recolonization in wetter periods promoted gene exchange. Distances between sites are large: the shortest is about 70 km (Monkey Spring to Sharp Spring) and the longest about 700 km (Bylas Springs to Sharp Spring).
- **Habitat:** Sharp Spring is fed by springs but fluctuates in temperature and physico-chemistry. It consists of pools joined by marsh channels, with topminnows persisting in low frequency in upper pools but largely extirpated by mosquitofish from lower pools. Ciénega Creek was drained by arroyo cutting in the late 1800s, and its lower reaches have been dry for decades except in major floods.
- **Limiting factors:** Predation (including cannibalism), intraspecific density effects and food limitation are considered the main natural causes of mortality and brood-size variation. Physico-chemical conditions differ strongly between the southwestern U.S. (high total dissolved solids and pH) and the northeast, where the original laboratory was located.
- **Genetic markers:** [[Allozyme Heterozygosity]] was measured at 25 loci. Monkey Spring, Bylas Spring and Ciénega Creek were monomorphic. Sharp Spring was polymorphic at Pgd and Est-4, with an estimated heterozygosity of 0.037. Mitochondrial DNA showed all Arizona samples shared one haplotype.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview:** Wild-caught gravid females from four sites (Sharp Spring, Monkey Spring, Ciénega Creek, Bylas Spring, the last with only 17 females) produced Fo offspring held at 25.5 ± 1 °C on a 14:10 light cycle. Eight broods per population were randomly chosen. Traits measured were survivorship to 12 weeks, standard length at 12 weeks, bilateral asymmetry (fluctuating asymmetry index) and brood size, analyzed with ANOVA and ANCOVA.

**Survival**
- Survival to 12 weeks averaged 93% (90–96%) across the four populations, with no significant heterogeneity.
- Quattro and Vrijenhoek (1989) reported much lower survival: 45% for Monkey Spring and 56% for Sharp Spring.

**Growth**
- Size at 12 weeks differed significantly among populations for both sexes (p < 0.01). Monkey Spring fish were the longest, the opposite of the earlier result.
- Monkey Spring males were 21.5% smaller in the earlier study, while Sharp Spring males were nearly identical in size across studies.

**Bilateral asymmetry**
- No significant fluctuating asymmetry was found for lateral line scales, pectoral fin rays or pelvic fin rays. About 80% of fish were symmetrical for all three traits.
- Quattro and Vrijenhoek's Monkey Spring values were 3.5, 6.2 and 30.7 times higher than this study's averages.

**Brood size and fecundity**
- Wild-caught brood size differed significantly among populations (p < 0.01). Monkey Spring was smallest (12.1 ± 1.3) and Ciénega Creek largest (17.2 ± 1.1), slightly larger than Sharp Spring (15.9 ± 1.2).
- Brood sizes of Fo (laboratory-raised) progeny did not differ significantly among populations.
- Brood size declined in the first laboratory generation for all populations.

**Heterozygosity and fitness**
- The population with the highest allozyme heterozygosity (Sharp Spring) had no clear fitness advantage over monomorphic populations.
- The authors offer four explanations for the discrepancy with earlier work:
  1. Differences in laboratory stress. Quattro and Vrijenhoek's laboratory mortality was about eightfold higher, and the unknown stressors may act differently on each population.
  2. Evolutionary change over the eight years between studies, which the authors consider unlikely given stable habitats.
  3. Changes in allozyme heterozygosity over time, which the authors consider unlikely.
  4. An underlying lack of correlation between allozyme heterozygosity and fitness, which they regard as the most likely explanation.
- They also argue that allozymes (25 loci) may be inadequate measures of genetic variation, and that the Yaqui topminnow sample used by Quattro and Vrijenhoek is a distinct subspecies and therefore not a valid comparison.

**Recommendations**
- The draft recovery plan (USFWS 1993) recommends using hydrographically nearest natural population stock for reintroductions, following American Fisheries Society guidelines. Exclusive use of Sharp Spring stock is not recommended.
- The authors support the following stock assignments: Ciénega Creek stock in the Ciénega Creek drainage, Sharp Spring stock in the upper Santa Cruz River, Bylas Spring stock in the Gila River drainage, and no further use of Monkey Spring stock, which is already widely distributed.
- Future work should use more polymorphic loci and molecular markers, including the major histocompatibility complex and microsatellites, to better assess adaptive genetic variation.
