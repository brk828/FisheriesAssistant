# Lotic Zoobenthos of the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by [[James V. Ward]], [[Henry J. Zimmermann]] and [[Leo D. N. Cline]] of Colorado State University, is a basin-wide review of benthic macroinvertebrates (zoobenthos) in running waters of the [[Colorado River System]]. It synthesizes mostly qualitative mainstem data, quantitative tributary data from 34 locations, and the effects of [[Dam Impoundment]] on downstream communities. The authors conclude that the basin's zoobenthos is depauperate in several groups, that a specialized riverine mayfly fauna once existed in the [[Green River]] and is now lost, that regulated tailwaters are dominated by chironomids and baetid mayflies, and that abiotic factors have shaped mountain-stream communities more than biotic interactions. The [[River Continuum Concept]] is tested and only partly supported.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River Basin spans extreme climatic, vegetational and geological gradients, from cool, clear, forested headwaters on crystalline bedrock to warm, silt- and salt-laden desert rivers near sea level. Cold, clear tailwaters also occur in deserts below high dams. Prior literature on lotic zoobenthos, particularly in the Lower Basin, is sparse and largely found in agency or consulting reports; much Upper Basin data come from the senior author's laboratory.

Sampling sites were grouped by the [[Illies and Botosaneanu Classification]] (1963):
- Crenon and upper rhithron: springs, springbrooks and headwaters, roughly stream orders 1-3.
- Middle and lower rhithron: stream orders 4-6.
- Potamon: rivers, stream order 7 or greater.

The mainstem review covers eight sites (A-H), from the [[Colorado River Headwaters]] on the Continental Divide (3105 m a.s.l.) to Black Canyon below [[Hoover Dam]]. Tributary data cover 34 locations: 18 crenon/upper rhithron, 11 middle/lower rhithron and 5 potamon. The chapter explicitly excludes assessment of mining, grazing and organic pollution effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Headwaters (Sites 1 and A):** Cold stenothermic, high-gradient streams with about 7 months of ice and snow cover. Five insect orders made up over 97% of zoobenthos. Dominant taxa included [[Baetis Bicaudatus]], [[Rhyacophila]] caddisflies, chironomids and tipulids, and the elmid beetle [[Heterlimnius Corpulentus]], which occurs at the highest elevations.

**Mainstem potamon reaches (Sites C-E):** Heavy silt loads and shifting sand limit colonization. Before the construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], the Glen Canyon reach yielded only 18 zoobenthic species from the main river, against more than 91 aquatic insect species in the preimpoundment survey, most found in tributaries.

**Grand Canyon (Site F):** Preimpoundment temperatures ranged from 0 to 29.5°C. Current velocities reached up to 5 m/s, and very few mainstem invertebrates were collected.

**Sonoran Desert streams (Sites 33 and 34):** [[Sycamore Creek]] and [[Aravaipa Creek]] show Neotropical-Nearctic mixtures, rapid development (1-3 weeks egg to adult), continuous reproduction, and a rich hemipteran and coleopteran assemblage. Amphipods were absent.

**Piceance Creek spring systems (Site 10):** Non-insects dominated rheocrene habitats, reaching 57% of the fauna in crenon habitats.

**Lower Colorado River:** Prior to dam construction, suspended solids sometimes exceeded 30,000 mg/l and temperatures reached 32°C. Chironomids and oligochaetes were probably dominant.

## Key Findings

- **Headwater composition:** Insects comprise almost all zoobenthos at first-order high-elevation streams. Stoneflies reached 16% at Site 1 and 14% at Site A.
- **Mainstem gaps:** Unionacean clams are virtually absent from the whole Colorado River Basin, and no Plecoptera were recorded at Glen Canyon. The introduced [[Corbicula Manilensis]] became established in the Lower Colorado River in the 1940s.
- **Mainstem sampling in the Grand Canyon:** Post-dam densities were 5 to 20 organisms per m². Gammarid amphipods (*Gammarus lacustris*) were introduced in 1932 and 1965; in Black Canyon, *Gammarus* was later largely replaced by [[Hyalella Azteca]].
- **Tributary density and biomass (Pennak 1977):** Across 14 stations on 8 Rocky Mountain trout streams (1552-2805 m), mean density ranged from 149 to 1450 organisms/m² (grand mean 639). Biomass ranged from 1.3 to 8.3 g/m² wet weight (grand mean 4.7 g/m²).
- **Sycamore Creek density:** Mean annual total zoobenthos density approximated 80,000 organisms/m².
- **Diversity:** Shannon-Weaver values at unregulated sites fell within the range expected for unpolluted streams. Diversity peaked in middle reaches, consistent with the [[River Continuum Concept]].
- **Dominant groups:** Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera each exceeded 10% of total fauna in all unregulated reaches. Plecoptera and Coleoptera stayed below 10%.
- **Lost riverine fauna:** Before the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962, the Green River hosted a specialized community, including the carnivorous siphlonurid *Analetris*, sand-dwelling heptageniids, *Ametropus albrighti* and *Lachlania powelli*. Impoundment inundated 145 km of this potamon habitat. The authors state that this community "has been lost forever."
- **Surviving potamon mayflies:** On the White and Yampa Rivers, mayflies made up 67% and 56% of zoobenthos respectively (Ames 1977). Burrowing species such as *Ephoron album* remain.
- **Thermal sensitivity:** *Ephoron album* requires freezing temperatures to break egg diapause and at least 10°C for hatching. Regulation can therefore disrupt its life cycle.
- **Functional feeding groups:** Shredders are scarce in both headwaters and tailwaters. Scrapers are less abundant than the River Continuum Concept predicts, probably because scouring flows and the absence of submerged angiosperms limit attachment sites. Collectors reached 80-90% of zoobenthos in lower potamon habitats.
- **Concept evaluation:** The authors conclude that the basin shows less structural and functional integrity than eastern woodland streams, so stochastic physical factors probably outweigh coactive patterns.
- **Regulation effects:** Deep-release dams reduce flow variation, cause clear-water erosion and enlarge substrate particles, and allow [[Cladophora Glomerata]] mats to cover surfaces for several kilometres. Post-dam Grand Canyon annual temperature range fell from 0-29.5°C to 6-15°C. Below dams, collectors and predators are essentially the only functional groups present; chironomids and baetid mayflies dominate.
- **Tailwater trout fisheries:** Productive trout fisheries developed below Glen Canyon Dam, Lake Mead (Hoover Dam) and the Navajo Dam tailwater, where the chironomid and baetid fauna was prominent.
- **Final conclusion:** The alternating series of reservoirs and tailwaters is described as obscuring "forever" any remaining indigenous lotic zoobenthos of the Colorado River System.
