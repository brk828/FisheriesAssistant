# Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis): Final Rule Determining Threatened Status

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final rule (Federal Register, Vol. 51, No. 208, 28 October 1986) lists the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) as a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The species has been reduced to roughly 380 km of occupied stream, about 15 percent of its former range, through habitat destruction, water diversion, groundwater pumping, channel downcutting, sedimentation and predation or competition by [[Exotic Fish Species]]. The rule includes a [[Special Rule]] allowing limited take for scientific, educational and propagation purposes under New Mexico and Arizona state permit systems. Critical habitat designation was postponed until June 1987 because of the complexity of the required economic analysis.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** First collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro, Arizona, and described by Girard in 1856. It is the only species in the genus *Tiaroga*.
- **Morphology:** A small (under about 80 mm, or 3 inches), slender, elongated olivaceous fish with dirty white spots at the base of the dorsal and caudal fins. It has a highly oblique terminal mouth and upward-directed eyes. Breeding males develop vivid red-orange markings.
- **Habitat preference:** Small to large perennial streams with shallow turbulent [[Riffle Habitat]] over cobble substrate, swift current and filamentous algae. [[Flood Pulse Dynamics]] keep substrate free of embedded sediments and help maintain its competitive edge over exotic species.
- **Historic distribution:** Once locally common throughout the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco and upper Gila river systems, up to about 2,200 m elevation (Minckley 1973). It was also historically present in the upper San Pedro River in Sonora, Mexico.
- **Current distribution:** About 24 km of Aravaipa Creek (Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona); about 93 km of the upper Gila River (Grant and Catron counties, New Mexico); about 167 km of the San Francisco and Tularosa rivers (Catron County, New Mexico); 1.5 km of Whitewater Creek; about 95 km of the Blue River (Greenlee County, Arizona); and a short stretch at the White and East Fork confluence (Navajo County, Arizona).
- **Land ownership:** Mixed. Key holdings include the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness), [[The Nature Conservancy]] and [[Defenders of Wildlife]] (Aravaipa and upper Gila lands), the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (Gila National Forest, Gila Wilderness, Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest), and extensive private land along the Cliff-Gila Valley and San Francisco River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Dams (including proposed [[Conner Dam]] and [[Hooker Dam]] alternatives), water diversion, groundwater pumping, channel downcutting, sedimentation, grazing, timber harvest, pesticide use upstream of Aravaipa Creek, and predation or competition by [[Exotic Fish Species]] such as [[Red Shiner]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] (small mouth bass cited in the text).
- **Native fish context:** 35 percent of native Gila system fish were federally listed as endangered, and another 35 percent were considered threatened or endangered by states or the American Fisheries Society.
- **Range loss:** Of about 2,000 km of historically occupied habitat, 2,220 km (1,364 miles per the notice; the rule's own figures are inconsistent) no longer supports the species, a reduction of about 85 percent. The species was last found in the San Pedro River (except Aravaipa Creek) in 1961 and in the Verde River drainage around 1938.
- **Drought and flooding:** Historically, the species survived drought by moving into residual pools. Flooding is important to its biology, though severe flooding of already degraded systems can cause erosion and habitat loss.
- **Conservation status:** Listed by the American Fisheries Society (1979 special concern list), the IUCN Red Data Book (Vol. 4), and by Arizona and New Mexico state lists.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The Service determined that threatened status is appropriate because of the greatly reduced and fragmented range and ongoing threats. Endangered status was not warranted because the species remains across about 380 km and does not appear in imminent danger of extinction.
- **Five listing factors (Section 4(a)(1)):** (A) habitat destruction and alteration, the primary cause; (B) overutilization, not a significant factor; (C) disease or predation, where exotic species are a significant secondary threat; (D) inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms, since state collection permits are adequate only for scientific taking; (E) other natural or manmade factors, including introduced exotic species.
- **Special rule:** Take is allowed for educational, scientific, propagation or survival enhancement, zoological exhibition and other conservation purposes, provided a state collection permit is obtained. Incidental take by licensed anglers is not a violation if the fish is immediately returned to its habitat.
- **Critical habitat:** Postponed until June 1987 under Section 4(b)(6)(C)(ii). Economic analysis was still in progress.
- **Public comment:** 95 letters from 89 parties were received: 67 supportive from 62 parties, 12 opposed from 10 parties, and 16 neutral or economic-only. Three public hearings (Silver City, Safford, Phoenix; Thatcher was also a hearing site) drew 107 attendees with 33 formal statements.
- **Key disputed points:** The Hooker Dam Association argued the range was larger and the 85 percent loss overstated; the Service countered that historic data are spotty and that elusive, fluctuating populations were likely more widespread historically. Opponents also claimed that habitat improvement could replace listing, which the Service rejected because too little is known about the species' habitat needs.
- **Federal consultation:** Section 7 consultation applies to federally funded or permitted actions, including [[Upper Gila Water Supply Study]] projects, Bureau of Reclamation activities, and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers flood control work. Private land activities without federal nexus are not covered.
- **Recovery:** Predator control, habitat enhancement and possible reintroduction (the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] does not currently hold stocks) are to be pursued during recovery planning.
- **NEPA:** No Environmental Assessment was required, per a notice published in the Federal Register on 25 October 1983.
