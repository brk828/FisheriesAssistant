# Saving Endangered Species: Implementation of the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1984

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This Defenders of Wildlife report (May 1985) assesses how the Reagan administration and Congress implemented the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) during 1984. It is the first annual report in a series that follows a ten-year analysis published in 1984. It covers three areas: budget and funding, species [[Listing]] (the process of designating species as endangered or threatened), and [[Recovery Plans]] with their implementation. A report card grades performance on an A-to-F scale.

The overall verdict is "mixed." Funding for FY 1985 rose, but the FY 1986 request cut or froze most programs, and habitat acquisition was proposed at zero. Listings increased, but the backlog of roughly 4,000 candidate species barely changed. Recovery plans were written at a faster pace, but implementation remained weak. The report warns that slow listing and recovery could turn the listing process into "the chronicling of extinctions" rather than protection.

Ten representative species were chosen for a 1984 public campaign. They include the lower 48 wolf and grizzly bear, [[Desert Tortoise]], lynx, [[Black-Footed Ferret]], western yellow-billed cuckoo, Gulf of California harbor porpoise, pine barrens buck moth, small whorled pogonia, and [[Red-Cockaded Woodpecker]].

## Background & Description

The report was produced by [[Defenders Of Wildlife]], with research by Dr Martha Stout and writing by Dr Stout and William Painter. Its analysis draws on [[Federal Register]] listing rules, Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) data, and recovery implementation reports.

The ESA is administered by several agencies:

- **[[Fish And Wildlife Service]]** (Department of the Interior): the primary implementing agency. Its budget covers listing, research, consultation, recovery, law enforcement, and Section 6 grants to states.
- **[[National Marine Fisheries Service]]** (NMFS, Department of Commerce): responsible for 19 listed marine species, including all six sea turtle species, the [[Shortnose Sturgeon]], several pinnipeds, and most great whales. NMFS also administers the Marine Mammal Protection Act.
- **[[Forest Service]]** and **[[Bureau Of Land Management]]** (BLM): manage public lands that contain listed species.

Funding for habitat purchases comes from the [[Land And Water Conservation Fund]] (LWCF), which draws on surplus property sales, motorboat fuel taxes, and offshore oil and gas revenues.

The report grades performance on an A-to-F scale. Congress was not graded on listing, recovery, or attitude because it took no direct action on those areas in 1984.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames declining species as an urgent problem. It describes a "nosedive" that must be detected and reversed early, because populations near extinction have little room to recover.

Habitat loss and modification are the main causes of decline for most listed species. Section 7 of the ESA requires federal agencies to avoid actions that could jeopardize listed species or destroy critical habitat. Section 9 prohibits "taking," which includes significant habitat modification. The report argues that Section 7 is often weakly enforced.

Several aquatic examples illustrate the stakes:

- **Colorado River system:** Existing water projects had already reduced flow by 35 percent. Four listed fishes (the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]) are threatened by habitat impacts. Since 1981, FWS has issued 32 biological opinions finding that upper Colorado basin water projects would not jeopardize the fishes, even though they would deplete flows by an additional 415,000 acre-feet annually. Approval was pending for 28 more projects.
- **Smoky madtom:** A fish presumed extinct for 23 years was rediscovered in Citico Creek, Tennessee, in 1980 and listed as endangered in 1984. A reintroduction into Abrams Creek in the Great Smoky Mountains National Park is planned to reverse an inadvertent local extirpation in 1957, caused by a fish toxicant treatment.
- **Desert fishes of Texas:** Recovery is described as effective. Wells were dug at Big Bend National Park to supplement springs, and introduced competitors and predators were removed. Fish, including the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], are held at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery for reintroduction.
- **[[Moapa Dace]]:** Land acquisition created the Moapa National Wildlife Refuge in Nevada, and a new stream channel was built and stocked.
- **Platte River:** Proposed water developments threaten the [[Whooping Crane]], which still numbers fewer than 150 birds. The cranes rest and feed on this river during migration.
- **[[Gulf Of California Harbor Porpoise]]:** Listed as endangered after a petition from Defenders. The text gives the listing date as January 1986, which conflicts with the report's May 1985 publication date and is likely an error.

## Key Findings

### Funding

- **FY 1985:** Congress appropriated amounts above the President's request for basic FWS programs and habitat acquisition. Section 15 programs reached $23.1 million, after the President requested $21.2 million. Congress also restored FY 1985 funding for the Forest Service, BLM, and NMFS, which had been targeted for cuts.
- **FY 1986:** The FWS request essentially freezes basic programs at 1985 levels, a real cut after inflation. Total Sections 6 and 15 funding requested was $26.9 million (Table 1). Defenders recommended at least $53.7 million for FY 1986 and at least $72 million by FY 1988.
- **Authorization ceiling:** Appropriations stayed below the $33 million congressional ceiling for Sections 6 and 15. Defenders recommended raising the authorized limits during the 1985 reauthorization.
- **Consultation funding:** Flat since 1979, even though the number of consultations tripled over that period.
- **Recovery:** Defenders estimated $15.5 million was needed for FY 1986 recovery work (including $9.5 million for new and revised plans), rising to at least $20 million annually.
- **NMFS:** $11.2 million was appropriated for FY 1985. The FY 1986 request was about $7 million, a 38 percent cut, with research cut by more than 50 percent. Defenders recommended $11.2 million plus $2.6 million.
- **Forest Service:** Congress appropriated $2.5 million for FY 1985 (request: $1 million). The FY 1986 request is $1.6 million, against a recommended $5 million. About 73 listed species occur on Forest Service land.
- **BLM:** Congress appropriated $2.65 million for FY 1985 after a proposed $1 million cut. The FY 1986 request is $2.35 million, against a recommended $5 million. BLM has implemented only 26 of 44 recovery plans covering its lands.
- **Land acquisition (LWCF):** FY 1985 request was $14.5 million; Congress appropriated $31.6 million, close to the $33.8 million Conservation Alternative. FY 1986 request was zero funding (a three-year moratorium), with $1.5 million for administration only. Defenders' Conservation Alternative proposed $47.7 million for 30 species. The administration's own plan averaged $43.8 million per year over six years.

### Listing

- 124 listing rules were published in the 1984 presidential-term year, the most since 1978. 47 species were listed and 60 proposed.
- Under the Reagan administration, 82 species were listed in its first four years, compared with 173 under Carter and 237 under Nixon-Ford.
- The average time from proposal to final rule was 369 days. Twelve final rules are overdue, two by more than three years.
- The candidate backlog stands at 3,827 species: 1,116 Category 1 (well-documented vulnerability) and 2,711 Category 2 (needs more research). Plants make up 2,588, invertebrates 876, and vertebrates 363.
- Invertebrates lag behind. Only two invertebrate proposals were published in 1984, one of which (the bay checkerspot butterfly) came almost four years after petition. NMFS has rejected three invertebrate petitions and has yet to list any marine invertebrate or plant.
- Status surveys covered 72 species in FY 1984 ($207,000), up from 29 species in FY 1983. At that pace, resolving the status of all candidates would take 38 years.
- At the current listing rate, Category 1 candidates alone would take about 25 years to list, and about 60 years if a third of Category 2 is excluded.
- Several candidates became extinct while awaiting listing, including the Florida sand flax and the Texas Henslow's sparrow.

### Recovery

- 165 recovery plans are approved, covering 195 species (about 60 percent of the 329 U.S. listed species). 55 plans were approved in 1984, averaging 31.5 per year under the Reagan administration.
- Of 234 species with FWS status reports: 33 are extinct or at crisis levels, 18 are declining, 23 are increasing, 10 are stable, and 154 (nearly two-thirds) have unknown status.
- Only about 25 percent of listed species receive even minimal attention, and only about 10 percent are known to be stable or increasing.
- The report describes the [[Palos Verdes Blue Butterfly]] as probably the first species extinct after listing. Its last habitat was rototilled for a ball field despite repeated warnings.
- The [[California Condor]] continues to decline by about two birds per year. Causes include an M-44 cyanide device and lead poisoning. Its critical habitat was found to be too small.
- Alligator populations in Louisiana and Texas recovered and were delisted, but the report warns that delisting removes Section 9 protection from nesting and wetland habitat, leaving them vulnerable to renewed decline.
- Only 25 of 30 Virginia round-leaf birch seedlings survived at the last wild site, and several seemed intentionally destroyed.

### Overall Assessment

- The report grades 1984 as mixed, with improvement in FY 1985 funding and listing offset by proposed FY 1986 cuts and the continuing backlog.
- Defenders' core recommendations are: raise FWS ESA funding to at least $53.7 million immediately; clear the candidate backlog by 1994; add limited protection for Category 1 candidates; increase recovery-plan implementation; use Section 7 more effectively; and extend protection to plants.
