# Husbandry, Life Span, and Growth Rate of the Annual Fish Cynolebias Adloffi

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cynolebias

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by [[Roy L. Walford]] and Robert K. Liu evaluates [[Cynolebias Adloffi]], a large [[Annual Fish]] from South America, as a short-lived vertebrate model for [[Gerontology]]. The authors describe husbandry methods, laboratory life span, disease patterns, and growth at two rearing temperatures (22°C and 16°C). At 22°C, the 50% survival time was 11–11.5 months and no fish survived beyond 14 months, supporting senile changes within about one year. The 16°C population could not yield reliable life tables because of a severe outbreak of [[Tuberculosis]]. Fish at 16°C grew significantly faster than those at 22°C, and the authors suggest that slower aging may occur at the lower temperature.

## Background & Description

The study is motivated by the need for biochemical markers of physiologic aging in a vertebrate that is immunologically competent. [[Teleost Fishes]] were chosen as a [[Poikilothermic Vertebrates]] model because their life span can be shifted by mild changes in environmental temperature, which is less drastic than [[X-Irradiation]] or juvenile starvation. Most fish are long-lived, but [[Annual Fish]] are reputed to live about one year, which would be advantageous for aging research. Commercially available annual fish are usually too small for biochemical work, so a 1962 field expedition to South America, with [[Walford]] and [[Hildemann]], collected larger species, including Cynolebias adloffi, to establish laboratory colonies.

**Husbandry methods**
- Adults were kept in about 30 gal tanks at roughly one adult fish per gallon.
- Water was a 1:1 mix of distilled water and acidified tap water, made acid with [[Peat Moss]]. Hydrogen ion concentration was kept between pH 4.8 and 7.0.
- A half-inch peat layer on the bottom served as the spawning substrate.
- Fish were fed twice daily with [[Tubifex Worms]] and/or adult [[Brine Shrimp]].
- One-fifth of the water was replaced monthly, and peat was sieved monthly (2 mm and 1 mm mesh) to harvest eggs.
- Eggs were stored in moist peat for 2–4 months before hatching in peaty water.

**Life span experiment**
- Fish were reared at 22 ± 1°C to sexual maturity at 2 months, then divided into two lots.
- The 22°C lot comprised 21 males and 23 females (44 fish).
- The 16 ± 1°C lot comprised 16 males and 19 females (35 fish), held in a separate room. The temperatures were chosen to match field recordings of 13–20°C.
- Fish were observed until spontaneous death or were killed when moribund. Complete autopsies and histology (step-cut sections, haematoxylin and eosin) were performed where tissue allowed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors view temperature as the main environmental variable. Field temperatures of 13–20°C guided the choice of the 16°C treatment. Water exchange between the two rooms was stopped after a tuberculosis outbreak in the 16°C tank. This tank's pH ran 0.5–1 unit higher than the 22°C tank, particularly during the last 4–5 months, which is a confounding factor.

The authors also consider possible disease sources. They concluded that tuberculosis was most likely introduced by feeding [[Tubifex Worms]]. They propose that a diet of adult live brine shrimp, hatched in 6% saline and maintained in 3% saline, would be largely free of usual fish pathogens and could prevent the disease in future colonies.

## Key Findings

**Life span and senescence (22°C)**
- Mean survival was 11–11.5 months; the longest-lived of 44 fish survived less than 14 months.
- Warm-room survivors showed senile changes, including spinal curvature (humped back), raised scales, clouded corneas, [[Exophthalmos]], and a ragged appearance. These changes were far less common at 16°C.
- Tuberculosis caused 36% of deaths at 22°C, mostly in the last half of the survival curve, which was beyond the 50% survival point.
- Other causes included [[Chronic Renal Disease]], [[Lymphosarcoma]], [[Dropsy]], [[Mycotic Infection]], and [[Reticulo-Endothelial Hyperplasia]].

**Disease patterns**
- Tuberculosis was the dominant disease, with granulomas containing acid-fast bacilli in the liver and kidney, and dissemination to spleen, ovaries, gut, and muscle in severe cases.
- The tuberculosis rate in the 16°C population was extremely high, which prevented life table analysis.
- Other findings included [[Fatty Liver]] change, which was associated with infection and not established as independent of it. Chronic renal cystic disease was not closely tied to chronological age.
- Reticulo-endothelial hyperplasia was common in middle-aged 22°C fish, infrequent in old 22°C fish, and seen in only one 16°C fish. It was not regarded as neoplastic and its cause remains unexplained.

**Growth rate**
- Fish at 16°C grew faster. In the first experiment, the average difference was 7 mm in length and 1.45 g in weight, about a 60% difference in total weight.
- In a second, larger experiment with 70 fish per temperature, the 16°C groups were larger at 6 months (one month after the 5-month transfer). Males were 39.1 mm and 1.51 g at 16°C versus 34.0 mm and 1.01 g at 22°C. Females were 33.0 mm and 0.98 g at 16°C versus 30.0 mm and 0.70 g at 22°C.
- Growth-curve weights for the most advanced ages reflect shrinking sample sizes and are not valid population measures.

**Mortality and reproduction**
- In the later experiment with tuberculosis-free fish, mortality at 9 months was 50% at 22°C and 15% at 16°C.
- Egg production per female was higher at 16°C in later months (Table 3). For example, in the 10–11-month interval, 16°C females averaged 11.2 eggs (n = 4), whereas 22°C females averaged 1.0 (n = 16).
- The authors interpret this as evidence that slower aging, not faster growth, occurred at the lower temperature.

**Conclusions**
- Cynolebias adloffi is a relatively short-lived species with senile changes in about one year under laboratory conditions at 22°C.
- Lower temperature increases growth rate and may slow aging, but the 16°C life span could not be determined because of tuberculosis.
- The authors conclude that annual fish may offer unique advantages for gerontologic research, and that disease-free husbandry (especially the brine shrimp diet) is essential.
