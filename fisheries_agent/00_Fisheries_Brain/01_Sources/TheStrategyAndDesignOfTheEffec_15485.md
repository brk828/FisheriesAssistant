# Strategy and Design of the Effectiveness Monitoring Program for the Northwest Forest Plan

#ornithology #ecosystem-monitoring #forest-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Final Prepublication Draft (November 13, 1997), prepared by the [[Effectiveness Monitoring Team]] (EMT), sets out the strategy and design for effectiveness monitoring of priority resources under the [[Northwest Forest Plan]]. The Plan is a large-scale ecosystem management plan covering 24 million acres of federally managed forest across 18 National Forests and 7 Bureau of Land Management Districts in northern California, western Oregon, and western Washington. The Plan mandates three types of monitoring: implementation, effectiveness, and validation.

The primary question effectiveness monitoring is designed to answer is: "To what extent are the goals and objectives of the Forest Plan being achieved?" The report proposes a predictive, integrated, [[Habitat-Based Monitoring]] approach that tracks the status and trends of selected resources. Its stated aim is to produce useful, timely results more efficiently and cost-effectively than past programs.

The report provides the scientific framework and template for monitoring modules on [[Late-Successional Old-Growth Forest]], the [[Northern Spotted Owl]], the [[Marbled Murrelet]], and [[Aquatic and Riparian Ecosystems]]. It also anticipates future modules on socioeconomic, tribal, and [[Survey-and-Manage Species]] issues. The initial goal is to produce the first regionwide interpretive monitoring report at the end of 1999.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and context.** The report is the second in a series on effectiveness monitoring. The first report, "Effectiveness Monitoring: An Interagency Program for the Northwest Forest Plan" (July 1995), set out the general framework, which the Intergovernmental Advisory Committee (IAC) accepted. The present report incorporates peer, agency, and other reviews, but its strategy has not changed from the version the IAC accepted. It concludes the EMT's development of the overall strategy and guidance.

**Policy lineage.** Monitoring under the Forest Plan traces to the adaptive-management role described in the [[Forest Ecosystem Management Assessment Team]] (FEMAT) report (July 1993), the interagency "Interagency Framework for Monitoring the President's Forest Ecosystem Plan" (March 1994), and the Record of Decision (April 1994).

**Team structure.** The EMT was led by Barry S. Mulder (Fish and Wildlife Service) and included technical coordinators from the Forest Service, the Environmental Protection Agency, and other agencies. Monitoring workgroups covered aquatic and riparian, late-successional and old-growth forest, northern spotted owl, and marbled murrelet resources. The Federal research agencies (Forest Service, EPA, and the U.S. Geological Survey Biological Resources Division) took responsibility for the scientific framework and monitoring options at the IAC's request. Agency selection of options will trigger the final stage: assigning plans, people, and funding.

**Scope of the program.** The program will consist of many modules and supporting guidance. Its scope and complexity mean it will differ substantially from how agency monitoring has traditionally been conducted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Challenges of ecosystem monitoring.** The report notes that natural systems are dynamic and spatially heterogeneous, and that many changes are not caused by human actions or are not amenable to management. Few successful ecosystem-scale monitoring programs exist. Common weaknesses include vague objectives, too many attributes measured, weak theoretical foundations, and no link to decision-making. Monitoring is often the first activity cut during budget reductions.

**Prospective monitoring.** The report adopts a prospective (predictive) approach. It starts by characterizing stressors and their effects on ecological integrity, builds a [[Conceptual Model]] linking stressors to ecological effects, and selects indicators that reflect the expected changes. The approach is only as valid as the assumed cause-effect relationships.

**Seven essential steps** drawn from the literature:
1. Specify goals and objectives.
2. Characterize stressors and disturbances.
3. Develop conceptual models.
4. Select indicators.
5. Determine detection limits for indicators.
6. Establish trigger points for management intervention.
7. Establish clear connections to the management decision process.

**Habitat as a surrogate.** Monitoring all biotic components is impossible, so the report uses habitat structure and dynamics as surrogates for biota. The justification combines pragmatic and theoretical arguments. Habitat loss and fragmentation were the primary drivers behind the Forest Plan. The theoretical basis is that animals adaptively select habitat in response to landscape structure and composition. Predictive [[Habitat Suitability Model]]s must account for landscape pattern, species life history, and population-scale dynamics. The assessment strategy combines remotely sensed and ground-plot data to infer habitat quality at several spatial scales. [[Focal Species]] serve as ecological indicators within this framework.

## Key Findings

**1. Primary question and purpose.** Effectiveness monitoring asks to what extent the Forest Plan's ecological and socioeconomic goals are being achieved. Its goals are to evaluate Plan success by assessing status and trends of selected resources, consistent with emerging national and international monitoring frameworks.

**2. Shift to ecosystem-based monitoring.** The program will move gradually from an intensive, species-by-species focus to an extensive, ecosystem-based approach. This replaces crisis response to individual endangered species with prospective evaluation of management decisions. The report argues the habitat-based approach offers these advantages:
- Monitoring vegetation change is more cost-effective than directly monitoring populations of all relevant species.
- Existing forest inventory programs can form the foundation for monitoring.
- A habitat focus matches the Forest Plan's mandate to manage vegetation communities rather than species directly.
- Estimating habitat trends is an anticipatory rather than retrospective approach, and it allows evaluation of alternative management strategies.

**3. Build on existing data.** Many inventory, monitoring, and research projects already collect data relevant to effectiveness monitoring. The report recommends building on them rather than duplicating them. Coordination will use direct staff linkages, direct data linkages, and quality assurance systems. [[Forest Inventory and Analysis]] and the [[Environmental Monitoring and Assessment Program]] are examples of existing programs the report names as possible foundations.

**4. Reporting structure.**
- **[[Data Summaries]]:** brief, comprehensive reports of essential data, produced annually for each monitored resource.
- **[[Interpretive Reports]]:** periodic regionwide reports on 5-year intervals. These are the program's key products. They evaluate the ecological significance of emerging status and trends relative to the Forest Plan and give management the implications of monitoring results.
- Interpretive results are meant to support adaptive management, including changes to plans, direction, policies, and budgets.

**5. Supporting functions.** The report identifies several ongoing needs: research support for new indicators and monitoring designs, pilot and test studies, and an information management and quality assurance system. The last is required so that monitoring results are legally defensible.

**6. Implementation timeline and risks.** The initial goal is the first regionwide interpretive report at the end of 1999. This report will test the program, establish the baseline for future trends, and allow program adjustment. The report flags several challenges:
- Diverse cooperating agencies.
- Many resources to monitor.
- Numerous monitoring groups.

**7. Institutional requirements.** Success depends on institutionalizing the program. The report calls for:
- Assigning permanent monitoring staff.
- Establishing core agency teams with program managers.
- Securing long-term base funding.

If staffing, data sharing, and quality assurance approaches are followed, the report expects that monitoring efforts will integrate and that the information needed for adaptive management of the Forest Plan will be available.
