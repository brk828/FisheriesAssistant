# Freshwater Triclads of North America I: The Genus Planaria

#turbellaria #tricladida #planariidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper by [[Roman Kenk]] revises the freshwater triclad genus [[Planaria]] (Turbellaria) in North America. It traces the history of the genus from [[Otto Friedrich Müller]] (1776) to its modern restriction, and it confirms the narrowed genus contains very few species. The paper redescribes [[Planaria Dactyligera]] and formally describes a new subspecies, [[Planaria Dactyligera Musculosa]], distinguished by a thick circular muscle layer on the penis papilla and a glandular genital antechamber. It also describes a new species, [[Planaria Occulta]], known from a single subterranean well in Virginia. The work relies on sectioned anatomy, because external features alone cannot separate closely related species.

## Background & Description

**Generic history.** The name *Planaria* originally covered all Turbellaria, including nemerteans. Successive revisions by Dugès, Ehrenberg, and Örsted narrowed it, and by the late nineteenth century it was restricted to freshwater triclads. The common name "planarian" still can refer to any triclad, including freshwater, land, and marine forms.

**Family-level classification.** Kenk (1930) set out the system still followed today. The suborder Paludicola (Probursalia) of the Tricladida contains two families, distinguished by pharyngeal musculature:
- **[[Planariidae]]:** the internal pharyngeal muscle zone has two distinct layers (inner circular, outer longitudinal). This character can be seen even in sexually immature specimens.
- **[[Dendrocoelidae]]:** a single layer of intermingled circular and longitudinal fibers.

A third family, [[Kenkiidae]] (Hyman, 1937), was not accepted by later workers.

**Type species.** Komárek (1926) restricted *Planaria* to *P. alpina*, but Kenk (1930) selected *Fasciola torva* Müller (1774) as the type species, and later emended the generic definition (1935). Under this narrower sense, the genus has few species: *P. torva* (Europe and possibly Asia, with synonym *P. onegensis*), *P. kempi* (India), and *P. dactyligera* (North America). The status of *P. torva* was recently reviewed by Ball, Reynoldson, and Warwick (1969).

**Diagnostic reproductive anatomy.** Species identification depends mainly on the reproductive system: testes, vasa deferentia, penis, copulatory bursa, and [[Adenodactyl]]. Key features include:
- Oviducts that unite into a common oviduct opening into the genital atrium.
- A male atrium without radial muscle plates.
- A hollow adenodactyl of the *Planaria torva* type.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and collection sites.** The taxa occur in freshwater, in streams, springs, lakes, and (for *P. occulta*) a groundwater well:
- *P. dactyligera dactyligera*: type locality Mountain Lake, Giles County, Virginia, near the south bank. Also reported from Rockbridge, Highland, and Albemarle counties, Virginia. Fite (1952) collected at Twin Springs near the [[Mountain Lake Biological Station]].
- *P. d. musculosa*: from the "Sawdust Pile Location" in Ossipee, Alamance County, North Carolina (November 1966), and from two localities at the [[Carolina Biological Supply Company]] Warren Laboratories, Warren County, North Carolina (13 June 1968). Longest (1966) recorded it from Abita Springs State Park, Louisiana.
- *P. occulta*: a hand-dug well about 4 m deep, east of Duffield, Scott County, Virginia.

**Well conditions.** On 26 November 1968 the water temperature in the well was 11.8 °C. The well is the only known locality for *P. occulta*, and whether the hypogean occurrence is obligate is unresolved, because surface waters in the area have not been surveyed systematically.

**Associated organisms.** Parasites and epibionts noted in the paper include:
- Small nematodes in the pharyngeal mesenchyme, moving freely without encystment (fite 1952 studied a related nematode).
- The holotrichous ciliate [[Sieboldiellina Planariarum]], found in the intestine and pharyngeal pouch of both subspecies and of *P. occulta*, where it was heavily infesting all sectioned specimens.
- The peritrichous epizoite *Urceolaria mitra*, attached mainly to dorsal surfaces.

**Laboratory culture.** Worms were kept in spring water at about 14 °C and fed beef liver and/or *Tubifex* (readily taken). Cocoons were produced year-round in *P. d. musculosa*.

## Key Findings

**1. Restoration of the type species.** *Fasciola torva* Müller (1774) was designated type of *Planaria* (Kenk 1930). The genus is therefore narrowly defined, containing *P. torva*, *P. kempi*, *P. dactyligera*, and the new *P. occulta*.

**2. *Planaria dactyligera dactyligera* (redescribed).** Holotype (USNM 39461) from Mountain Lake, Virginia. Mature animals reach 13 mm × 1.75 mm. The dorsum is gray, brown, or black; the eyes are two and close together.

**3. *Planaria dactyligera musculosa* (new subspecies).** Holotype (USNM 39462) from Ossipee, North Carolina. Mature animals reach 11 mm × 1.3 mm. It is externally indistinguishable from the typical form, and it closely resembles pigmented *Phagocata* species and *Hymanella retenuova*. Diagnostic features:
- An extraordinarily developed external circular muscle layer at the base of the penis papilla, several times thicker than the muscle layers of the male atrium (in the typical form, these layers are about equal).
- A highly glandular antechamber (genital atrium compartment) between the gonopore and the adenodactyl.

**4. *Planaria occulta* (new species).** Holotype (USNM 39463) from Duffield, Virginia; 7 paratypes. Key findings:
- Unpigmented and white, reaching 9 mm × 1.5 mm. The anterior intestinal ramus ends in front of the eyes, a feature separating it from adult *Phagocata morgani* and *P. oregonensis*.
- Smooth gliding locomotion, with no crawling movements.
- Six to nine anterior intestinal branches and 13 to 19 posterior branches.
- A convoluted ejaculatory duct that may be capable of eversion, with partial eversion seen in one of eight sectioned specimens.
- Taxonomically isolated within *Planaria*, although it shares the genus's core copulatory architecture.

**5. Sexual maturity and reproduction.**
- *P. occulta* was collected sexually mature in March, April, and November, suggesting non-seasonal maturity. Roughly 100 specimens were taken in November 1968.
- In culture, *P. d. musculosa* produced cocoons year-round. Cocoons measured 0.8–1.7 mm (long axis) and 0.6–1.0 mm (short axis). Three to 14 young hatched after 3–4 weeks, measuring 1.5–3 mm.
- Neither species showed asexual fission (28 months of culture for *P. d. musculosa*).
- *P. occulta* produced no cocoons during five months of culture.

**6. Morphological resemblance and identification.** Preserved specimens are unreliable for distinguishing these species. Subtle differences in living gliding animals, such as the anterior intestinal extent, separate *P. occulta* from *Phagocata morgani* (Rock Creek Park, Washington, D.C.) and *P. oregonensis* (Portland, Oregon).

**7. Misidentifications and records.** A Bloomington, Indiana, record by Chandler (1966) proved erroneous on slide examination. Reynierse and Ellis (1967) likely used *P. d. musculosa*, since their animals came from the Carolina Biological Supply Company. Longest's (1966) Louisiana material also proved to be *musculosa*.

**8. Evolutionary note on the bursa.** Ciliates in the copulatory bursa of *P. occulta* support Steinböck's (1966) view that the bursa is derived from the intestine, retaining some intestinal functions and environment.

**9. Documentation.** The paper closes with a reference list of about 20 works, from Müller (1774, 1776) to Zabusov (1901), including Kenk's own Virginian and Dinaric studies (1930, 1935, 1936). Type specimens are deposited in the [[Smithsonian Institution]]'s U.S. National Museum.
