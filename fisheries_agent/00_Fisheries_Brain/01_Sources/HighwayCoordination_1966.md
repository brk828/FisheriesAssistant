## Executive Summary
This 1965–1966 completion report describes efforts by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to improve coordination with the Arizona Highway Department during highway planning, in order to reduce impacts on fish and wildlife populations. Key outcomes included revised fencing specifications for [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] range, a compiled list of mitigation/enhancement measures for highway projects (sourced from the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife and the Bureau of Public Roads), and streamlined review of 103 highway projects (38 interstate, 26 primary, 31 secondary, 18 urban secondary). Recommendations for fish and wildlife protection were submitted on 38 of these projects. The report emphasizes that early-stage involvement in highway planning is critical to securing acceptance of conservation recommendations, and identifies interstate highway access restrictions as the primary ongoing conflict between transportation infrastructure and wildlife/hunter access.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Although not framed in traditional habitat-ecology terms, the report implicitly addresses several ecological concerns tied to fish and wildlife habitat fragmentation caused by highway infrastructure:

- **Migration corridors**: [[Interstate 17]] was found to bisect established [[Elk]] migration routes near Sedona Road Junction and Munds Park; box culverts were recommended to reduce elk-vehicle collisions and preserve connectivity.
- **Bighorn sheep habitat**: The Parker-Topock Highway Project threatened prime [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] habitat in the Aubrey Hills segment; a highway realignment was proposed to bypass this habitat, alongside a two-strand smooth-wire fence design (per [[Manes 1965]]) intended to allow bighorn passage while restricting cattle movement — a design point of disagreement with the Highway Department.
- **Fish habitat/stream crossings**: Recommendations addressed specialized bridge construction features for streams crossed by highways, intended to prevent degradation of fishing stream quality or to enhance in-stream fish habitat.
- **Water resource creation**: Proposals included converting abandoned borrow pits into lakes/ponds for fish and wildlife use, creating new waterholes for game cut off by fencing, and using highway surfaces as water-catchment aprons piped to remote troughs — indirect habitat enhancement measures for wildlife and potentially fish-bearing waters.
- **Access barriers**: Controlled-access interstate design (a Bureau of Public Roads safety requirement) was identified as restricting hunter and angler access to game and fish resources, partially mitigated via locked-gate access for department personnel (e.g., on Interstate 17) but not resolved for the public.

## Key Findings
- Adoption of an improved highway project review system (per [[Curtis 1964]]) reduced pending project backlog from 33 (end of Segment 4) to 6 (end of Segment 5), improving early-stage input into highway design.
- 103 highway projects were reviewed August 1965–July 1966; fish and wildlife recommendations were submitted for 38 of them (~37%).
- Interstate highways posed the greatest wildlife/access conflict due to Bureau of Public Roads controlled-access mandates; frontage roads were identified as a costly but effective mitigation option.
- Revised bighorn sheep fencing specifications were agreed upon between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and Highway Department, though disagreement persisted over fence design adequacy for cattle control on the Parker-Topock project.
- A standardized 11-item checklist of mitigation/enhancement measures (fencing, gates, underpass tunnels, signage, land transfer, borrow-pit pond conversion, bridge design, waterhole creation, highway water catchment) was distributed to regional field personnel to guide future recommendations.
- Urban secondary highway projects presented no documented fish/wildlife impacts during this period, but continued review was recommended as a precaution.
- The report recommends continuing this coordination job and initiating a new study to determine factors influencing acceptance or rejection of fish and wildlife recommendations by highway planners — indicating a data gap on institutional/policy-level effectiveness of past mitigation efforts.
