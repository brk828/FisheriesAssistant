# The Legacy of Tethys: An Aquatic Biogeography of the Levant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-biogeography

## Executive Summary

This is the front matter, table of contents and preface of a 1989 monograph by F.D. Por (with Ch. Dimentman), published as Volume 63 of [[Monographiae Biologicae]] by Kluwer Academic Publishers. It presents a synthesis of the aquatic biogeography of the Levant, an area defined as the Asian branch of the [[Great Rift Valley]] together with the adjacent eastern Mediterranean, the northern [[Red Sea]] and its two northern gulfs, and northern Sinai. The book integrates historical (Tethyan and Pleistocene), distributional, and site-level information. The author states that the synthesis relies largely on second-hand sources and that taxonomic knowledge across groups is very uneven. The extracted portion also includes the author's postface and a partial bibliography and index.

## Background & Description

- **Author and scope:** Francis Dov Por, with the assistance of Chanan Dimentman. The preface says the book was written by one author to give a coherent structure rather than an edited volume, and it treats the subject on three levels: historical, present distribution, and individual waterbodies.
- **Geographic framing:** The Levant is defined as the province of basins that drain or once drained to the Rift Valley. The Suez Canal and Gulf of Suez are included; the [[Nile]] and [[Tigris-Euphrates]] basins are excluded as artificial borders. The northern Red Sea boundary is set near 24°N, with a possible southern limit at 20°N.
- **Structure:** Chapters cover early formative history (Tethys closure, [[Messinian Salinity Crisis]], Pliocene normalization), the Pleistocene, the eastern Mediterranean, the northern Red Sea and gulfs, halmyric (variable-salinity) environments, and continental waters (Dead Sea, [[Lake Kinneret]], [[Lake Hula]], Orontes, Litani, Jordan, Lebanese rivers, temporary pools, polluted waters).
- **Sources:** The book cites a very large bibliography (roughly 180 pages in the source), spanning mollusks, crustaceans, insects, fishes and geology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Most Levantine rivers are described as a "hydrological skeleton with little flowing life." The [[Jordan River]] is called a "limnologic hoax." Major karstic springs, such as those of the [[Jurassic Aquifer]] on Mount Hermon, are non-seasonal. The Hermon aquifer supplies about 750 million m³ per year, of which the [[Dan Spring]] takes one third; Dan alone supplies roughly half the minimal summer flow of the Jordan.
- **Salinity gradient:** The Jordan Valley shows increasing salinity from north to south. The [[Dead Sea]] is about 320 ppt; Lake Kinneret is oligohaline at about 0.6 ppt. Saline "N-T" (Tiberias-Noit) springs, of Pliocene-marine-gulf origin, feed the lakes.
- **Biogeographic origin:** The euryhaline fauna is considered the oldest, followed by the Mesopotamian primary freshwater fauna, the Ethiopian (Afrotropical) element, and the Palearctic influx. The Mesopotamian basin is proposed as a major center of origin for inland water fauna.
- **Historical barriers:** No continuous riverine connection existed between the Levant and Africa since pre-Messinian times. The Palearctic fauna is restricted to cold, stenothermic springs and streams, with a southern limit called the [[Nehring Line]] (terrestrial and aquatic).
- **Dead Sea decline:** The lake fell from -391.5 m (1929) to 403.5 m (1979), about 30 cm per year on average, partly due to climate and partly to Jordan water diversion.
- **Human impacts:** Diversion of the Jordan (flow reduced by more than 90%), the [[National Water Carrier]], the [[Aswan High Dam]], drainage of Lake Hula (completed by 1958), drainage of Lake Amiq and the Ghab swamps, and sewage-fed streams such as Nahal Soreq.

## Key Findings

- **Central thesis:** The Levant is not a center of origin for aquatic biota but a meeting zone of dispersing populations from at least three centers. Its biogeographic dynamism is its principal asset, and many "endemics" may be relic or secondary occurrences rather than true evolutionary endemics.
- **Lake Kinneret:** 19 fish species, of which 10 are secondary freshwater or euryhaline species with mostly Ethiopian affinities; 7 cichlid species and subspecies. The lake is warm-monomictic and has a "nematode lake" profundal dominated by *Eudorylaimus andrassyi*, with a benthic biomass dip below 15–30 m.
- **Lake Hula:** Once the most diverse aquatic system in the Levant. More than 500 invertebrate and fish species were listed from the lake. Drainage (1951–1958) caused major losses. The frog *Discoglossus nigriventer* has not been reported since 1955.
- **River Dan:** More than 150 animal taxa recorded from the torrential part alone. A stenothermic site at 15.5°C with a flow of 250 million m³ per year; the fauna is predominantly Palaearctic.
- **Dead Sea:** Reported as nearly abiotic; the only alga is *Dunaliella parva*. Experiments by Dimentman showed that a 10% admixture of Dead Sea water was lethal to marine organisms.
- **Aphanius:** Several euryhaline species include *A. mento*, *A. dispar richardsoni*, and the recently described *A. sirhani* (El Azraq oasis, Jordan).
- **Jordan River outflow:** Reduced from 1,100 million m³ per year to 100 million m³ per year at the Dead Sea.
- **Lebanon:** Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera are better diversified in coastal rivers; the fish fauna is poor, with mostly mountain cyprinids and cobitids.
- **Orontes:** Shares 18 of its 32 primary freshwater fish species with the Euphrates; the Jordan shares 7 species with the Orontes.
- **Introduced species:** Including *Tilapia zillii*, *Clarias gariepinus*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Salmo gairdneri*, *Gambusia affinis* and Chinese carps.
- **Postface conclusion:** The "legacy of Tethys" persists in the dynamic biogeography of the region. Artificial hydraulic engineering has repeated natural biogeographic change, and the author argues that environmental management should learn from this history before repeating it.
