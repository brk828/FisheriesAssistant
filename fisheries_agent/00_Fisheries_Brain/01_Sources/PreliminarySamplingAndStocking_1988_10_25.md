## Executive Summary

This 1988 field memorandum from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) documents baseline fish surveys and subsequent conservation stocking of three imperiled Colorado River Basin native fishes — [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] — into artificial ponds at the ASU Golf Course and ASU Research Park in Tempe, Arizona. Pre-stocking surveys (September 1988) found the ponds nearly devoid of fish, occupied only by low numbers of non-native [[Red Shiner]] and [[Western Mosquitofish]] that had colonized via connected Salt River Project irrigation canals. Following stocking, a follow-up survey in October 1988 documented survival of all three native species alongside a diverse non-native assemblage dominated by [[Redbelly Tilapia]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. High summer water temperatures (~35°C) were implicated in significant mortality of stocked razorback sucker, though survivors were expected to be sufficient for the 1989 spawning season, setting the stage for planned larval-fish and predator-interaction studies.

## Background & Description

- **Bonytail** (single recaptured specimen, 13 October stocking cohort): 344 mm total length (304 mm fork length, 262 mm standard length), weight 288 g. Sex undetermined but presumed male based on coloration. Moderate breeding-type coloration at paired fin bases and belly. Good overall condition, with minor fungal growth on the dorsum and several *Lernaea* (anchor worm) parasites at the dorsal fin base.
- **Razorback Sucker** (n = 8, all males recaptured in October): total length range 402–503 mm (mean 451 ± 38 mm); weight range 584–1,200 g (mean 849 ± 245 g). Most individuals were in poor, emaciated body condition, attributed to thermal stress during peak summer temperatures.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow**: no morphometric data reported; ~1,500 juveniles stocked with no recaptures noted in the October follow-up sampling.
- Stocked fish were acclimated via gradual transport-water temperature adjustment to near-pond temperatures, minimizing thermal shock; total transport/stocking mortality was <1% across all three stocking events.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System type**: Shallow, clear, artificial ponds with minimal algal or macrophytic growth, connected to and recharged by Salt River Project canal water — creating an open pathway for non-native fish invasion.
- **Invertebrate community**: Larval [[Anisoptera|dragonfly naiads]] abundant on solid substrates; [[Notonectidae|backswimmers]] common in shallow, warm nearshore water.
- **Pre-stocking fish community**: Sparse, limited to low densities of invasive Red Shiner and Mosquitofish, both presumed canal invaders; no native species present prior to stocking.
- **Post-stocking community structure** varied markedly among the three interconnected ponds (north, middle, south lakes) at ASU Research Park:
  - **North Lake**: Overwhelmingly dominated by Red Shiner (90.6% of seine catch, CPE 231 fish/haul), with Bluegill and Mosquitofish as minor components.
  - **Middle Lake**: More diverse tilapia-centric assemblage — Redbelly Tilapia (35%), Bluegill (29%), and Tilapia hybrids (21%); lower CPE (5.7).
  - **South Lake (South Pond)**: Lowest catch and diversity (CPE 0.8, 5 species), dominated by Bluegill (31%), Largemouth Bass (25%), Threadfin Shad and Green Sunfish (19% each); notably lacked Red Shiner in the seine catch.
- **Thermal stress**: Water temperatures near 35°C recorded in the south lake during August coincided with a die-off of razorback sucker, indicating the ponds' thermal regime approaches or exceeds tolerance limits for this species — a key abiotic constraint on native fish persistence in this artificial habitat.
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: Abundant non-native predators (Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish, Bluegill) co-occur with native stock, setting up planned research into interactions between anticipated native larvae (Bonytail, Razorback Sucker) and non-native predators during the 1989 spawning season.

## Key Findings

- Ponds at ASU Golf Course were essentially fishless prior to stocking, containing only incidental non-native Red Shiner and Mosquitofish likely derived from adjacent SRP canals.
- Trammel and gill netting (270 total linear meters across both ponds) captured zero fish, while seining showed extremely low catch rates relative to comparable regional lakes/ponds.
- Three native, imperiled Colorado River Basin fishes were experimentally stocked in fall 1988:
  - ~1,500 juvenile Colorado Pikeminnow (07 September) → North Pond
  - ~1,500 juvenile Bonytail (13 October) → South Pond
  - ~2,000 juvenile Razorback Sucker (24 October) → South Pond
  - Source hatcheries: USFWS Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico) for pikeminnow/bonytail; Arizona Game and Fish Department Page Springs State Fish Hatchery for razorback sucker.
- A one-month post-stocking survey (10–12 October 1988) confirmed survival of all three native species via trammel netting, though at reduced/low abundances compared to historical autumn samples.
- Razorback sucker showed signs of substantial summer heat-related mortality (dead specimens recovered nearshore in August at ~35°C water temp), with survivors mostly emaciated; however, remaining stock was judged sufficient to support winter–spring 1989 spawning.
- Non-native fish community was highly diverse and pond-specific, comprising ten taxa in seine samples overall, with tilapia species (Redbelly Tilapia, Blue Tilapia, Tilapia hybrids), centrarchids (Bluegill, Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish), Red Shiner, Threadfin Shad, and Common Carp.
- Study establishes baseline for a planned April 1989 follow-up survey and future investigations into larval native fish recruitment and interactions with non-native predator communities, framing this as an early field trial in [[Native Fish Conservation]] via [[Refuge Pond Stocking]] strategies for endangered Colorado River Basin fishes.
