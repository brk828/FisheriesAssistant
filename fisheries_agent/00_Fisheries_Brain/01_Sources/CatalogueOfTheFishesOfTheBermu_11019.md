# Catalogue of the Fishes of the Bermudas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bermuda-fish-fauna

## Executive Summary

This Smithsonian publication (1876), the fifth in a series illustrating the [[National Museum]] natural history collections, is an annotated catalogue of the fishes of the Bermudas prepared by G. Brown Goode, Assistant Curator of the United States National Museum. It is based chiefly on specimens and notes gathered during a visit in February and March 1872. The author enumerates **75 species** in total, most personally observed, and supplements earlier descriptions with living colours, size and proportions, habits, popular-name origins, capture methods and economic value, and geographic distribution. The fauna is shown to lie at the northern and eastern edge of the [[West Indian Region]], with its character strongly shaped by the [[Gulf Stream]].

## Background & Description

- **Purpose and method:** The work was written at the request of the Smithsonian Institution and follows the family classification of [[Theodore Gill]]. Goode notes that the list is "necessarily far from exhaustive" because winter storms limited reef exploration.
- **Prior knowledge:** Only seven fish species had been recorded before the visit. The main earlier source was [[John Matthew Jones]]'s *The Naturalist in Bermuda* (1859); the list in [[T. L. Godet]] (1860) was judged unreliable because it was largely copied from [[Philip Henry Gosse]]'s *Naturalist in Jamaica*.
- **Islands:** The atoll forms a sunken ellipse (major axis about 25 miles, minor about 13). Main islands are linked by channels 15 to 20 feet deep. Water inside the encircling reef rarely exceeds 12 to 14 fathoms, and the bottom drops steeply beyond the reef.
- **Fisheries:** Fishing is central to the islanders' diet. Fish are sold live from boats at the quay in Hamilton and St. George's, killed by an awl thrust into the brain, and kept fresh. The legal price was fixed at fourpence a pound. Capture methods include lines (for the best food fish), split-cane fish-pots, shallow seines tanned with mangrove bark, casting-nets, and the two-pronged "grains" spear. Winter stock was kept in natural fish-ponds, most famously the [[Devil's Hole]] on Harrington Sound.
- **Popular names:** Of 33 names recorded by [[Mark Catesby]] in the Bahamas (1724–25), 26 are still applied to Bermudian species. Goode also lists about 40 fishermen's names he could not identify.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biogeography:** The Bermudas lie on the northern and eastern boundary of the West Indian Region. Goode argues that the [[Gulf Stream]] is the key driver, since it both warms the waters and transports tropical drift-wood, seeds and fishes from the Antilles. The reef-building polyps reach their farthest northern limit here.
- **Faunal relationships (57 species of restricted range):** Of the 75 species, 18 are widely distributed and set aside. Of the remaining 57:
  - 50 (86%) are common to the Bermudas and the West Indies.
  - 18 (32%) occur on the coast of Brazil.
  - 8 (14%) occur on the eastern United States coast north of Georgia, of which 4 are considered accidental.
  - 13 (23%) occur in the Eastern Atlantic, and three of these are treated as wanderers from the Mediterranean fauna.
- **Endemism:** Four marine species and one brackish-water species are considered peculiar to the group: [[Syngnathus jonesii]], [[Lefroyia bermudensis]], [[Engraulis chirostomus]] and [[Fundulus bermudae]].
- **Habitats:** Tide pools and shallow coves hold many reef and juvenile fishes. Pelagic species such as [[Pterophryne picta]] live in [[Sargassum]] mats thrown ashore by winter storms. The Blenny [[Labrosomus nuchipinnis]] inhabits tide pools in colonies, and brackish ditches hold [[Carassius auratus]] and [[Anguilla bostoniensis]].
- **Economic and ecological notes:** The most prized food fish are [[Holacanthus ciliaris]] (Angel-fish), Pristipomatidae, Serranidae, Sparidae and [[Lachnolaimus falcatus]] (Hog-fish). Baitfish include [[Harengula macrophthalma]] and [[Decapterus punctatus]]. Several species (for example, the [[Cow-fish]] and [[Puffer-fish]]) are poisonous or eaten only locally.
- **Reptiles noted:** Four sea turtle species and the small saurian *Eumeces longirostris* were also observed.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** The catalogue lists 75 species, supplemented by an appendix of additional species cited on the authority of Jones (not personally observed by Goode).
- **Dominant West Indian affinity:** 86% of the restricted-range species are shared with the West Indies, supporting the Bermudas as a northern outlier of the West Indian Region.
- **Gulf Stream influence:** Goode attributes both the warm-water fauna and the introduction of tropical life to the Gulf Stream.
- **Taxonomic revisions:** Several names are corrected. [[Malthe vespertilio]] is reassigned to subspecies status, and [[Pseudoscarus vetula]] is shown to rest on Parra's figure. Several species previously believed to be the same are discussed, such as the Blue-fish (*Cheilojulis radiatus*) and the Clamacore.
- **New or notable records:** [[Syngnathus jonesii]] and [[Lefroyia bermudensis]] are described as new to science. A 16-foot [[Regalecus gladius]] (oarfish) came ashore in 1860. A [[Sphyraena sphyraena]] record marks a westward extension of a Mediterranean species.
- **Conservation/fishery notes:** The fishery depends heavily on fish-ponds and lines, and Goode warns that many older identifications are uncertain.
- **Research gap:** Goode calls for a resident naturalist to do for Bermudian ichthyology what [[Felipe Poey]] and [[Pieter Bleeker]] had done for the West and East Indies.
