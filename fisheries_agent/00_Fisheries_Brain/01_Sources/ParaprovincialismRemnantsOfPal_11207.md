# Paraprovincialism: Remnants of Paleoprovincial Boundaries in Recent Marine Molluscan Provinces

#malacology #marine-biology #gastropoda #biogeography #polychaeta

## Executive Summary

This Proceedings of the Biological Society of Washington paper by [[Edward J. Petuch]] (1982) introduces **paraprovincialism**, a biogeographic pattern in which congeneric species pairs occupy mutually exclusive ranges that meet along sharply defined boundaries. Petuch argues that these pairs are not true siblings. Each member descends from a separate Neogene ancestor, and these ancestors were themselves interprovincial sibling pairs, which he calls [[Ancestromas]]. The Recent Caribbean pattern therefore preserves the boundary between two Pliocene provinces: the [[Gatunian Province]] to the south and the [[Caloosahatchian Province]] to the north. The paper extends the idea to the central South Pacific and the Malaysian Archipelago, and proposes it as a tool for reconstructing Neogene provincial boundaries where the fossil record is poor. A short appended description of a deep-sea eunicid polychaete from Pacific [[ALVIN Submersible]] collections is also included.

## Background & Description

Petuch's work builds on earlier discoveries of [[Geographical Heterochrony]] and relict Pliocene molluscan faunas in the Caribbean (Petuch 1981a, 1981b, 1982). He notes that many Caribbean gastropod genera show a bipartite distribution: two congeners have non-overlapping ranges that abut along a well-defined line. When this occurs in a province, it appears across whole suites of species.

Malacologists had long divided the [[Caribbean Molluscan Province]] into northern and southern components, each marked by indicator congeners (Warmke and Abbott 1962; Vermeij 1978). Briggs (1974) further proposed subprovinces based on this bipartite pattern, attributing them to insular versus continental ecological differences. Petuch rejects an explanation based only on present-day ecology and attributes the pattern to paleoprovincial history.

Table 1 lists paired examples (about 17 pairs), with northern members such as *Tegula lividomaculata*, *Murex anniae*, *Murex tryoni*, and *Conus spurius atlanticus*, and southern counterparts such as *Tegula viridula*, *Murex olssoni*, *Murex blakeanus*, and *Conus lorenzianus*. The paper also cites similar patterns in Indo-Pacific faunal lists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Caribbean faunal boundary.** The northern–southern shift lies along the Honduras–Nicaragua coastline in the west and the Lesser Antilles in the east (Fig. 1). Species compositions change abruptly along this line, although Petuch notes that most sister pairs are ecologically similar. The key example is *[[Conus jaspideus]]*–*[[Conus puncticulatus]]*. The two coexist from Martinique to the Grenadines, but *C. jaspideus* occurs only north of this zone and *C. puncticulatus* only to the south. A second example is *[[Oliva reticularis]]*–*[[Oliva oblonga]]*. *O. reticularis* is found only north of the Leeward Islands, while *O. oblonga* is restricted to the south and is most prevalent along northern South America.

**Habitat association.** Northern members such as *O. reticularis* and *C. jaspideus* are consistently found together in shallow, sandy areas of the northern Caribbean and Greater Antilles. Their southern counterparts, *O. oblonga* and *C. puncticulatus*, occupy the same environments in the southern Caribbean. Petuch therefore concludes that the boundary reflects historical provincial differentiation rather than habitat partitioning.

**Pliocene paleoprovinces.** Figure 2 (after Petuch 1982) maps the Gatunian Province (southern) and Caloosahatchian Province (northern). The two provinces together gave rise to the modern Caribbean fauna, and their shared boundary ran roughly along the same Nicaraguan and Lesser Antillean line.

**Central South Pacific.** Cypraeid cowries define a sharp north–south boundary from the Phoenix–Canton Atolls to east of Samoa and west of Tonga (Burgess 1970). East of this line are wide-ranging species such as *[[Cypraea serrulifera]]*, *C. cumingii*, and *C. obvelata*. West of the line are the similar *C. minoridens*, *C. catholicorum*, and *C. annulus*. No two members of a pair have been found sympatric along the boundary. The fossil record here is poorly known, so ancestromas can only be inferred.

**Malaysian Archipelago.** Western (Indian Ocean) species, including *Strombus decorus*, *Strombus sibbaldi*, *Lambis indomaris*, *Harpa crassa*, *Oliva ponderosa*, *Conus maldivus*, *Conus sumatrensis*, and *Conus fuscatus*, abut eastern (Pacific) sibling species such as *Strombus luhuanus*, *Strombus plicatus*, *Lambis scorpio*, *Harpa amouretta*, *Oliva miniacea*, and *Conus generalis*, *C. vexillum*, and *C. imperialis*. Petuch links these pairs to sea-level fluctuations in the Upper Pliocene and Lower Pleistocene.

**Eunicid polychaete (appended description).** The second section describes a eunicid polychaete from Pacific [[ALVIN Submersible]] collections at 2633 m depth. The specimen is described as a cylindrical worm with a dorsal arc, and one specimen (USNM 74305) has 353 setigers. The prostomium is somewhat flattened, with two antennae and a cleft between the inner lateral antennae. Specimen details are partial in the source text, and the species name is not given in the excerpt.

## Key Findings

1. **Paraprovincialism is a recurring pattern.** Mutually exclusive congeneric pairs abut along sharp lines in the Caribbean, the central South Pacific, and the Malaysian Archipelago.

2. **Recent pairs are pseudosiblings.** Each Recent Caribbean pair descends from two separate Pliocene ancestral species rather than from one common ancestor. Those ancestors were themselves interprovincial sibling species, termed [[Ancestromas]].

3. **Provincial origin of the Caribbean fauna.** One member of each ancestroma was restricted to the Gatunian Province and the other to the Caloosahatchian Province. The Recent southern species descend from the Gatunian member and the northern species from the Caloosahatchian member.

4. **Table 2 documents 17 ancestromas.** Examples include *Astraea precursor*/*A. aora*, *Murex aff. recurvirostris*/*M. polynematicus*, *Oliva carolinensis*/*O. brevispira*, *Conus waccamawensis*/*C. caboblanquensis*, and *Conus cherokus*/*C. longitudinalis*. Sources span Dall (1889, 1890) through Woodring (1928).

5. **Ecology is not the primary explanation.** Petuch proposes that the mechanism is most likely ecological, involving competitive exclusion at the provincial level. He acknowledges that this remains conjectural.

6. **Predictive value.** Where the Caribbean fossil record is well preserved, paraprovincialism is unnecessary for reconstruction. In poorly sampled regions, however, Recent pairs can map former provincial boundaries.

7. **Indo-Pacific origin.** The Indo-Pacific Molluscan Province may have formed from the post-Pleistocene combination of at least three Neogene provinces. Petuch suggests that the search for paraprovincialism could support worldwide reconstruction of Neogene marine provinciality.

8. **Appended polychaete description.** A eunicid polychaete from 2633 m in the Pacific is described from ALVIN material. It is included in the same document but is not connected to the paraprovincialism argument.
