# Geohydrology and Water Resources of Alluvial Basins in South-Central Arizona and Parts of Adjacent States

#hydrogeology #groundwater #aquifer-system #water-resources #arizona

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper (1406-B), by T.W. Anderson, Geoffrey W. Freethey, and Patrick Tucci (1992), is the interpretive report of the [[Southwest Alluvial Basins]] (SWAB) study, one of the [[Regional Aquifer-System Analysis]] (RASA) program studies. It describes the geohydrologic framework, flow system, water budget, and development effects of 72 alluvial basins covering about 82,000 square miles. The basins are treated as largely independent hydrologic systems that share a common development response. Before development, inflow was about 2.5 million acre-feet per year; by 1980, 184 million acre-feet had been withdrawn, about half of it from aquifer storage. The report groups basins into five categories and documents land subsidence, earth fissures, and water-level declines, and reviews the [[Arizona Groundwater Management Act of 1980]].

The document is a hydrogeology report, not a fish or biological study. It contains no species-level data.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 82,000 mi² in southern and central Arizona, western New Mexico, and small parts of southeastern California and Nevada. It includes the Colorado River drainage between [[Hoover Dam]] and the Mexican border near Yuma, and basins draining to Mexico (San Bernardino and Douglas basins).
- **Physiography:** Part of the [[Basin and Range Province]], with broad valleys separated by sharply rising mountain ranges. Fenneman's Sonoran Desert and Mexican Highland sections are used, with valleys ranging from under 500 ft to over 4,000 ft above sea level.
- **Climate:** Arid to semiarid. Precipitation runs from under 3 in./yr (Yuma area) to about 30 in./yr (high-elevation headwaters of the Salt and Gila Rivers). Potential lake evaporation is 60 to 86 in./yr. Winter precipitation is generally low-intensity and long-duration; summer precipitation comes as short, high-intensity thunderstorms.
- **Basin structure:** The 72 basins are structural depressions formed during the Basin and Range disturbance (began about 15 to 12 m.y. ago). Basins are generally oriented north to northwest. The deepest basins lie in the [[Gila Low]] area.
- **Geohydrologic units:**
  - Rocks of the mountains: largely yield little water and form flow boundaries.
  - Pre-Basin and Range sediments: Tertiary, up to 15,000 ft thick; consolidated; generally a local source of water.
  - Basin fill, lower (Miocene, about 15 to 8 m.y. ago): finer grained, evaporite-bearing, closed-basin deposits.
  - Basin fill, upper (Miocene to Pliocene, about 8 to 3 m.y. ago): coarser, transitional to integrated drainage.
  - Stream alluvium (late Pliocene to Holocene): the most productive unit where saturated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report addresses the hydrologic environment rather than biota, but it notes several ecological links:

- **Riparian and phreatophyte vegetation:** Mesquite and cottonwood indicate shallow ground water. Before development, evapotranspiration along the [[Colorado River]] was estimated at about 1.3 million acre-ft/yr (from 1930s aerial photography), and along the Gila River and tributaries about 800,000 acre-ft/yr.
- **Perennial stream loss:** Pumping and dam construction dried reaches that formerly flowed perennially. Reduced base flow is described as critical to maintaining riparian habitat.
- **Water quality:** Dissolved solids generally increase with depth where mudstone or gypsiferous mudstone is present. Salinity is also driven by evaporite dissolution. Pumping problems include variable water quality with depth.
- **Land subsidence:** Compaction of fine-grained sediments (permanent loss of storage) causes earth fissures that widen by erosion and damage infrastructure.

## Key Findings

**Water budget**
- Before development (about 1940), total inflow was about 2.5 million acre-ft/yr: streamflow infiltration about 1.7 million, mountain-front recharge about 0.8 million, and underflow about 13,000 acre-ft/yr from New Mexico. Outflow was evapotranspiration about 2.1 million, discharge to streams about 0.8 million, and underflow leaving the study area about 130,000 acre-ft/yr.
- Recoverable water stored in the upper 1,200 ft of basin fill was estimated at about 900 million acre-ft, roughly 400 times the annual depletion rate.
- Mountain-front recharge follows log Q = -1.40 + 0.98 log P, where P (precipitation) exceeds 8 in./yr. The regression correlation index is R = 0.95, versus 0.85 for total precipitation.

**Development and depletion**
- Ground-water pumpage was 1.7 million acre-ft in 1942, 3.8 million in 1952, and 4.8 million in 1962. Average pumpage for 1950–80 was 4.8 million acre-ft/yr; about 4.5 million was pumped in 1980, of which about 2.5 million came from storage.
- Cumulative withdrawal through 1980 was 184 million acre-ft. About 100 million acre-ft came from aquifer storage (slightly more than 50 percent).
- Water-level declines range from under 50 ft to more than 450 ft in developed basins. The maximum long-term decline exceeds 450 ft in the Stanfield area.
- Depletion of storage through 1980 ranged from under 1 percent to about 15 percent of stored water among basins.
- Gila River flow near Dome fell from an average of 620,000 acre-ft/yr (1903–40) to less than 22,000 acre-ft/yr (1941–78).

**Basin categories**
- Basins are grouped into five categories by lithology and flow system: southeast, central, west, Colorado River, and highland. Within each, development response is expected to be similar.
- Basins are also classed by annual unit downvalley flow into three groups: river-dominated (above 1.0 (acre-ft/acre)/yr), multiple source-sink (0.01 to 1.0), and storage-depletion (below 0.01). Highland basins are excluded.
- Basins tend to evolve from river-dominated or multiple source-sink types toward storage-depletion as development increases.

**Hydraulic properties**
- Upper basin fill hydraulic conductivity: about 1 to more than 100 ft/d (central Arizona typically 30 to 90 ft/d).
- Lower basin fill: generally under 20 ft/d; less than 1 to about 50 ft/d.
- Stream alluvium along the lower Colorado River: 200 to 400 ft/d, locally near 1,000 ft/d.
- Fine-grained content is about 10 to 50 percent at basin margins and 60 to 90 percent at basin centers.

**Land subsidence and fissures**
- Near Eloy, total subsidence during 1952–77 reached 12.5 ft at one benchmark. More than 120 mi² subsided at least 7 ft, and more than 1,000 mi² subsided at least 3 ft.
- A fissure system in the Eloy area is more than 8 mi long.

**Management**
- The Arizona Groundwater Management Act of 1980 established four Active Management Areas (Phoenix, Prescott, Tucson, Pinal) and two Irrigation Nonexpansion Areas. Phoenix, Prescott, and Tucson aim for safe yield by 2025.
- Agriculture used 82 percent of ground water pumped in 1980.
- A pump tax of up to $5.00/acre-ft was provided for. The [[Central Arizona Project]] will import Colorado River water to partly offset overdraft.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Subsurface data are sparse. Data cover typically only the upper 20 to 30 percent of aquifer thickness, and wells rarely exceed 2,000 ft.
- The mountain-front recharge equation is not for small watersheds or isolated areas and needs further testing.
