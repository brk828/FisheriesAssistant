# Summer Habitat Use by Sonora Chub in Sycamore Creek, Santa Cruz County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under a contract on the habitat of the federally threatened [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*), examines summer habitat use in [[Sycamore Creek]], the only U.S. population. The study ran over two summers (1990 and 1991) and analyzed habitat at two spatial scales: macrohabitats (whole pools and reaches) and microhabitats (individual points used by fish). Persistence of habitat through drought, rather than fish density, was used as the index of habitat quality. The author concludes that deep pools that resist drying are the most suitable habitat, that adult chub select such pools, and that sedimentation and drought pose the main risks to the population.

## Background & Description

The Sonora chub is a small, dark minnow with two prominent black lateral lines. In Arizona it is rarely larger than 125 mm, while in Mexico it may reach 190 mm. It is endemic to the [[Rio de la Concepcion Basin]], occurring in the Rio Magdalena and Rio Altar in Mexico. The U.S. population is restricted to Sycamore Canyon, which is the headwater of the Rio Altar. The species is federally listed as threatened (USFWS 1986), with critical habitat designated in the watershed, and is state listed as endangered (AGFD 1988).

Published habitat information was previously qualitative and drawn mainly from Mexico. The study uses the following life-stage definitions:

- **Juveniles:** fish under 25 cm total length (TL), with a recognizable size cohort.
- **Subadults:** fish 2.5–5.0 cm TL with adult markings but no breeding colour.
- **Adults:** fish over 5.0 cm TL, treated as potentially breeding (the smallest observed with breeding colour was 5.8 cm TL).
- **Immature:** subadults alone or with juveniles.

Density estimation was impractical because of handling restrictions on the species, and snorkelling failed because chub were attracted to submerged observers. Surface (shoreline) observation was therefore used.

The study area lies in the [[Pajarito Mountains]], about 40 km west of Nogales, and includes Sycamore Canyon, a [[Research Natural Area]] established by the [[U.S. Forest Service]] by 1988. The canyon is about 11 km along the thalweg, running from Hank & Yank Spring (1220 m) to the Mexican border (1035 m). It forms a natural corridor for Mexican flora and fauna.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** Sycamore Creek is an interrupted stream in low-flow years, consisting mainly of isolated pools. Surface water is continuous only after heavy rain, and the lower reaches may stay dry. Habitat conditions are most limiting in May and June, when temperatures are highest and flow is lowest. Most annual precipitation falls in July and August. Water temperature closely follows air temperature at [[Nogales]] (r² = 0.817). The maximum recorded water temperature was 29.7 °C (28 June 1990), and the minimum was −2.4 °C (23 December 1990). A field thermometer recorded 37 °C at a chub location in 1990.

**Other fishes and threats.** Three other species have been reported in the canyon: [[Longfin Dace]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Green Sunfish]]. The author found no longfin dace in the study and concluded that earlier records were probably erroneous. Green sunfish and mosquitofish were found in deeper pools and near storage tanks. Potential predators included coatis, herons, garter snakes, bullfrogs, and water beetles. Sedimentation from the 1983 flood and livestock grazing were identified as the main habitat threats.

**Food.** Food availability in isolated pools is likely limited because invertebrate drift stops when surface flow ceases. Fish were observed feeding on chironomid larvae and other aquatic insects, and grazing on attached algae.

## Key Findings

**Methods and objectives**
- Objectives: (1) quantify macrohabitat characteristics and persistence used by different life stages through summer; (2) quantify microhabitat use and availability.
- Macrohabitat surveys were conducted from 11 May to 30 June 1990 and from 16 May to 7 July 1991.
- Microhabitat data were collected at 8 sites (2 in each of 4 stream sections) from 13 June to 27 July 1991, with 50 random points per site surveyed for 15 seconds each.
- Statistics: G-tests for categorical data, Kruskal-Wallis and Tukey tests for continuous macrohabitat data, and multiresponse permutation procedures (MRPP) for microhabitat use versus availability.

**Macrohabitat and persistence**
- In 1991, 65% of areas occupied by fish in May had dried up by July.
- Adult-occupied habitats (Category A) made up 16% of May surface area; only 19.3% of that area dried. Immature-fish habitats (Category B) made up 21% of May surface area, but 70% of it dried. Juvenile-only habitats (Category C) made up 9% of surface area, and 84% of it dried. Areas without fish (Category D) made up 39% of surface area, with 93.8% drying.
- Fish losses: at least 20% of the May fish estimate was in pools that dried. Only 12% of fish in adult habitats were lost, compared with 31% of immature fish and 54% of juveniles.
- Adult habitats were deeper and larger in both May and July. In July, adult habitats averaged 0.37 m depth and 29.89 m² surface area, against 0.15 m and 7.31 m² for unoccupied areas.
- Dominant substrate differed significantly (G = 8.5, df = 3, P < 0.0001). Bedrock/sand areas persisted more often than expected; rubble/boulder and gravel areas persisted less often.
- In 1990, persistent habitats had less floating cover, with 75% containing less than 50% floating cover.

**Microhabitat selection**
- Sonora chub generally selected slow or zero-velocity water, rock or fine-substrate cover, and deeper water as they grew larger. Bedrock was selected where available, otherwise sand and fine gravel.
- Shade and distance to cover showed no consistent patterns.
- Microhabitat use usually differed among life stages. Juveniles used shallower water and margins; adults selected deeper water (>10 cm at Site 24) and were dispersed in the water column.
- Selection differed among pools. Of the comparisons, only about a few per variable were significant, and P-values above 0.013 were treated cautiously.

**Life history and reproduction**
- Breeding colour has been observed in August, October, and March. Young have been found in early spring and November.
- Fry (15–18 mm) were observed in April, May, and September 1990–1991, suggesting spawning after spring and monsoon rains.
- Post-flood spawning appears to be a survival mechanism, though the chub may also breed year-round under suitable conditions.

**Limiting factors**
- Likely limits include drying pools, flash floods, low dissolved oxygen (as low as 1.0 ppm), and loss of drift food in shrinking pools.
- About 14 dead chub were found on 29–30 June 1990, after record air temperatures and cloudy skies; hypoxia was suspected.
- High temperatures were judged unlikely to be a major problem, as most pools stayed below 30 °C.

**Management implications**
- Sediment loading after the October 1983 flood filled many pools; in 1990, pools with adult chub were filled with sand.
- About 17% of the population seen in areas with adults was lost in 1991, partly because fish were isolated in pools that did not persist.
- Grazing inside and upstream of the Research Natural Area, possible mining, and unexplained die-offs of the Tarahumara frog are noted as concerns.
- Recommended monitoring: a late-June dry-season survey and a post-flood survey in September to October.
- Translocation into cattle tanks or tinajas with steep slopes would probably support adults, but recruitment would require shallow areas and cover for juveniles.
