# Biological characteristics of interidal and fresh-water spawning pink salmon at Olsen Creek, Prince William Sound, Alaska, 1962-63

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Special Scientific Report (No. 602, 1970) by [[John H. Helle]] examines [[Pink Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus gorbuscha*) spawning in both the [[Intertidal Zone]] and fresh-water reaches of [[Olsen Creek]] in [[Prince William Sound]], Alaska, in 1962 and 1963. Prince William Sound differs from other pink salmon areas because a significant share of spawning occurs in stream intertidal zones. The study covers streambed quality, spawner abundance and timing, spawner length, and fecundity, using a [[Foot Survey Method]] for abundance estimation.

## Background & Description

Pink salmon from Prince William Sound support runs worth millions of dollars, ranging from 0.6 to 8.8 million fish between 1956 and 1963. Spawning in the intertidal zone is a distinctive feature of this population. Pink salmon spawn and die at age 2, so odd- and even-year spawners form separate genetic lines.

Olsen Creek is one of the major pink and chum salmon spawning streams in the sound. It lies at the head of Olsen Bay in Port Gravina, with East and West Forks converging at the 11-foot tide level. The boundary between intertidal and fresh-water zones was set at the 12-foot (about mean high tide) level. Olsen Creek was divided into five study sections: upper intertidal, holding pool, low gradient, middle intertidal, and lower intertidal. Middle Slough and Little Creek, two small entirely intertidal streams nearby, were used for comparison.

The Bureau of Commercial Fisheries began studying these intertidal populations in 1960. The present report builds on [[Helle Et Al. 1964]] and related work by [[Kirkwood]] and [[McNeil]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Streamflow and precipitation.** The watershed is steep and has no lakes, so streamflow closely tracks rainfall. A drought in 1962 lowered flows enough that the West Fork recorder failed, and only East Fork data were analysed. Precipitation at Olsen Creek was measured July to September only and was compared with [[Cordova]] records, about 48 km away.

**Streambed quality.** Fines (material passing a 0.833-mm sieve) increased from higher to lower tide levels, meaning higher intertidal areas had coarser gravel. The low-gradient section was an exception. Fines were significantly lower after spawning in the upper intertidal area (7.4% vs. 11.0% before spawning; t = 3.13, 26 d.f.), though the cause, spawning or flooding, was not determined. Previous work by [[McNeil And Ahnell]] linked low streambed permeability to fewer salmon.

**Intertidal physical stresses.** Tidal action changes intragravel flow, temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen. Survival of pink and chum salmon was reported by Kirkwood to be higher at 8 to 12 feet than at 4 to 8 feet, and negligible below 4 feet. Egg survival over winter declined from higher to lower tide levels.

**Drought effects (1962).** Low water isolated fish in shallow pools on Little Creek. Foot surveys recorded 22 males and one female on July 27, and seven males and two females on August 6. These fish were vulnerable to [[Black Bear]] and [[Glaucous-Winged Gull]] predation. On the West Fork, the first falls became a migration barrier. Counts of 5,924 fish below the first falls on July 27 dropped to 404 between the falls.

## Key Findings

**Spawner abundance and distribution**
- Although less than one-third of the Olsen Creek drainage spawning area is tidally influenced, 70% of the total spawners used it in 1962 and 34% in 1963 (30% in some summaries).
- The 1962 run (total 147,557 by foot survey; 103,995 intertidal) was bimodal. The early run spawned in both areas and the late run only in the intertidal zone, matching the parent 1960 year.
- The 1963 early and late runs both spawned throughout the stream, with 20,949 total fish in the 1963 foot survey counts for the intertidal area and fresh water combined (20,084 intertidal).
- Olsen Creek received 7% of the Prince William Sound pink salmon escapement in 1960 and 3% in 1962.

**Estimation method**
- The foot survey method was compared with weir counts, peak counts (1960), and tag ratio estimates (1961). Differences ranged from 6% to 22% for individual areas and totalled 15–18% across the drainage.
- The author concluded that the foot survey method gives reasonable estimates. Foot survey counts were larger than minimum weir counts four of five times.
- The tag ratio estimate for the 1961 intertidal run may have been high, but this was not confirmed.
- Foot surveys of 1963 used redd life (8.9 days early run; 6.2 days late run) rather than stream life, because pool counts were excluded.

**Length**
- In 1962, 984 adults were measured (MEHP length). Fish in small intertidal creeks (Middle Slough, Little Creek) were significantly smaller than those in the main stream (P < 0.01 for most comparisons).
- In 1963, 622 adults were measured. Late-run females in Little Creek were significantly shorter than in the main stream (P < 0.05), while male lengths did not differ.
- Two explanations are proposed: genetically distinct stocks homing to specific sections, or behavioural segregation by size.
- A regression for converting MEHP to mideye-to-fork-of-tail length is given as Y = 4.7 + 1.01X (n = 97 females).

**Fecundity**
- In 1962, mean fecundity was 1,815 eggs (early run, n = 49) and 1,846 eggs (late run, n = 41). The combined mean was 1,829 eggs (n = 90), with a significant correlation with length (r = 0.335).
- In 1963, mean fecundity was 1,929 eggs (n = 52), with a highly significant correlation (r = 0.634). The regression was Y = -1,238 + 73.5X.
- Mean female length was about the same in both years (43.2 cm in 1962; 43.1 cm in 1963), but fecundity was higher in 1963 (about 100 eggs more).
- Left and right ovaries did not differ significantly in 1963 (mean 954 vs. 978 eggs; n = 50).

**Egg retention**
- Egg retention was about 10% in 1962 and 1963, versus 41% during the 1961 late run (linked to high spawner density) and 7% in 1960.

**Conclusions**
- Intertidal spawning is a major and variable component of pink salmon production in Prince William Sound. It is shaped by streambed quality, tidal stress, drought, and spawner size and timing.
