# Biological Characteristics of Intertidal and Fresh-Water Spawning Pink Salmon at Olsen Creek, Prince William Sound, Alaska, 1962-63

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #spawning-ecology #intertidal-ecology

## Executive Summary

This report documents biological characteristics of [[Pink Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus gorbuscha*) spawning populations in [[Olsen Creek]], [[Prince William Sound]], Alaska during 1962-63. [[Prince William Sound]] is unique among major pink salmon-producing areas in that a significant portion (35-77%) of spawning occurs in intertidal zones of streams. The study examined streambed quality, spawner abundance, timing, distribution, length, and fecundity in both intertidal and freshwater spawning areas. Key findings include progressive increases in fine sediments from higher to lower intertidal levels, differential use of intertidal zones between even-year (70% intertidal) and odd-year (30% intertidal) spawning lines, and significant positive correlations between female length and egg number. A repetitive stream survey technique was developed and validated for estimating spawning populations, showing 6-22% variation compared to weir counts and other methods.

## Background & Description

[[Olsen Creek]] is located in [[Olsen Bay]], [[Port Gravina]], on the eastern side of [[Prince William Sound]], Alaska. The creek has two branches ([[East Fork]] and [[West Fork]]) that converge at the 11-foot (3.4 m) tide level to form the main channel in the intertidal zone. Salmon can ascend each fork approximately 2.5 km, but the study focused primarily on areas below the 12-foot (3.7 m) tide level, considered the boundary between intertidal and freshwater zones. The stream was divided into five study sections based on ecological differences: upper intertidal, holding pool, low gradient, middle intertidal, and lower intertidal.

Two small nearby intertidal streams, [[Middle Slough]] and [[Little Creek]], drain the east side of the tideflat parallel to [[Olsen Creek]] and were included for comparative purposes. These streams are entirely intertidal with no freshwater spawning areas.

Pink salmon in [[Prince William Sound]] are unique in that they exhibit bimodal spawning runs (early and late) and maintain separate genetic lines in odd and even years, as pink salmon spawn and die at age 2. Annual runs from 1956-1963 ranged from 6 to 8.8 million fish. [[Olsen Creek]] received 1-2% of the total pink salmon escapement in [[Prince William Sound]] during the study period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Streambed Quality**: Gravel sampling using the [[McNeil-Ahnell]] method revealed that the percentage of fines (materials passing through 0.833 mm sieve) increased progressively from higher to lower intertidal levels. The upper intertidal area showed 11.0% fines before spawning and 7.4% after spawning (significant at P<0.01), though this reduction may have resulted from flooding rather than spawning activity alone. The lower intertidal area, serving as a settling basin where [[Olsen Creek]] meets tidewater, contained the highest percentage of fines. The low-gradient area was an exception to this progression, containing intermediate fine sediment levels despite its position.

Previous studies by [[Kirkwood]] (1962) demonstrated that survival of pink and [[Chum Salmon]] was highest in the 8-12 foot (2.4-3.7 m) tide levels, lower in 4-8 foot (1.2-2.4 m) levels, and negligible below the 4-foot (1.2 m) tide mark. This survival pattern correlates with streambed permeability, which [[McNeil and Ahnell]] (1964) found inversely related to the proportion of silt and fine sand.

**Hydrological Conditions**: Stream flow in [[Olsen Creek]] is closely related to rainfall due to the steep watershed and absence of lakes for water storage. Daily stream level and discharge measurements were recorded for the [[East Fork]] during July-September 1962-63. A severe drought occurred in September 1962, resulting in extremely low stream flows, though a September 1963 drought occurred late enough that it did not affect spawner abundance or distribution. Precipitation data for [[Olsen Creek]] (July-September only) were similar to those for [[Cordova]], located 48 km away, suggesting annual precipitation patterns at [[Cordova]] are indicative of conditions at [[Olsen Creek]].

**Intertidal Environment**: Tidal action causes changes in flow, temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen of intragravel water that affect the biology of fish in the intertidal environment. These factors have been studied extensively in freshwater environments but comparatively little in intertidal zones. The percentage of live eggs in the stream in fall and survival of larvae over winter decrease from higher to lower tide levels, probably caused by lower streambed permeability and increasingly severe effects of tide-associated changes in environmental parameters.

## Key Findings

**Population Estimation Methods**: A repetitive foot survey method was developed and validated for estimating spawning populations. The method involved periodic walking surveys (every 7-10 days) counting live and dead fish, plotting counts over time, calculating the area under the curve (fish-days), and dividing by average stream life to estimate total spawners. Comparisons with 1960-61 data from weir counts, tag ratio estimates, and peak count methods showed differences of 6-22% between estimation methods. The foot survey method consistently produced higher estimates than weir counts for freshwater populations, which were known to be low due to weir damage during floods. The 1960 peak count estimate for intertidal spawners was lower than the foot survey estimate, likely because freshets, tidal action, and bears removed many carcasses before they could be counted.

**1962 Spawning Population (Even-Year Line)**: Total estimated spawners: 10,800 fish. Distribution: 70% (7,560 fish) spawned in the intertidal zone, 30% (3,240 fish) in freshwater areas. The run was bimodal with an early run (July-early August) and late run (mid-August-September). Early-run fish (2,700 total) spawned in both freshwater and intertidal areas. Late-run fish (8,100 total) spawned exclusively in the intertidal zone. Peak spawning occurred in late August. Average stream life was 8.9 days for early-run fish and 6.2 days for late-run fish. This distribution pattern was identical to the parent year (1960).

**1963 Spawning Population (Odd-Year Line)**: Total estimated spawners: 8,900 fish. Distribution: Only 34% (3,026 fish) spawned in the intertidal zone, 66% (5,874 fish) in freshwater areas. The run was bimodal with similar timing to 1961 (parent year). Unlike the even-year line, both early-run (3,000 fish) and late-run (5,900 fish) fish spawned in both intertidal and freshwater areas. Peak spawning occurred in late August-early September. Average redd life was 8.9 days for early run and 6.2 days for late run.

**Length Comparisons - 1962**: MEHP (mid-eye to hypural plate) lengths were measured for 984 adult pink salmon. Males showed more widely dispersed lengths around the mean than females. In [[Middle Slough]], both males and females from the early run were significantly shorter (P<0.01) than other early-run intertidal spawners. Late-run fish in [[Middle Slough]] and [[Little Creek]] were significantly shorter than those in the mainstream of [[Olsen Creek]] (P<0.01 for males and most female comparisons). This tendency for fish in small intertidal creeks to be shorter was also noted in the parent year (1960). Early-run females in the [[East Fork]] (freshwater) were significantly shorter (P<0.05) than early-run females in the mainstream, but males showed no significant difference. Mean lengths of early-run fish were greater (but not significantly) than late-run fish from corresponding areas.

**Length Comparisons - 1963**: MEHP lengths were measured for 622 adult pink salmon. Length differences between late-run and early-run fish of the same sex were not significant, contrasting with 1961 when late-run fish were significantly longer. Males from [[Little Creek]] did not differ in mean length from late spawners in the mainstream, but females in [[Little Creek]] were significantly shorter (P<0.05) than mainstream females. In the parent year (1961), mean length differences between [[Little Creek]] and mainstream spawners were not significant.

**MEHP to MEFT Length Relationship**: For 97 unspawned females collected at the creek mouth, the relationship between MEHP and MEFT (mid-eye to fork of tail) measurements was: Y = 4.7 + 1.01X, where Y is expected MEFT length in cm and X is MEHP length in cm. This relationship facilitates comparison with other studies using MEFT measurements.

**Fecundity - 1962**: Mean fecundity for 49 early-run females was 1,815 eggs (mean length 43.0 cm). Mean fecundity for 41 late-run females was 1,846 eggs (mean length 43.5 cm). The relationship between egg number and length was significant for early-run females (r=0.347) but not for late-run females (r=0.294). For combined early and late runs (n=90), mean length was 43.2 cm, mean fecundity was 1,829 eggs, and the correlation between egg number and length was highly significant (r=0.335). The parent year (1960) showed similar mean length (43.0 cm) and fecundity (1,815 eggs) but a non-significant correlation (r=0.210).

**Fecundity - 1963**: For 52 females (early and late runs combined), mean length was 43.1 cm and mean fecundity was 1,929 eggs. Although mean female length was similar between years, mean fecundity was 100 eggs greater in 1963 than 1962. The relationship between egg number and length was highly significant (r=0.634), showing a stronger correlation than 1962 despite a smaller range in lengths and egg numbers. Five average to large females with considerably fewer than average eggs in 1962 may partially explain the lower correlation that year.

**Ovary Comparison - 1963**: For 50 females examined, mean egg number was 954 for left ovaries and 978 for right ovaries; the difference was not significant. The right ovary contained more eggs than the left in 29 fish (58%) and fewer in 21 fish (42%).

**Egg Retention**: Approximately 10% of eggs were retained in carcasses of spawned-out females in both 1962 and 1963. This contrasts with 41% retention during the late run in 1961, which was associated with high spawner density. The 1960 retention rate was only 7%, similar to 1962-63 when spawner densities were comparable.

**Population Differences**: Two possibilities are suggested to account for size differences between fish spawning in small intertidal creeks versus the mainstream: (1) genetically different stocks may home to specific portions of the [[Olsen Creek]] tideflat, or (2) behavioral patterns may cause segregation of large and small fish, with large adults either finding it difficult to spawn in smaller channels or driving small adults away from preferred redd locations in the mainstream.
