# Upper and Lower Duddon: Benthic Invertebrate Distribution in an English Mountain Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #plecoptera #ephemeroptera #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by [[G. Wayne Minshall]] and [[Robert A. Kuehne]] (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside) surveyed the benthic macroinvertebrates of the [[River Duddon]], a radially drained, acidic mountain stream in the English [[Lake District]], across a complete year. The basin divides sharply into an upper area dominated by Plecoptera (stoneflies), with Ephemeroptera (mayflies) and [[Gammarus pulex]] rare, and a lower area dominated by Ephemeroptera, with Plecoptera common and Gammarus common. The authors conclude that nutrients, detritus quality or other water-quality factors are the most likely cause of this faunal discontinuity, with temperature possibly contributing. Physical barriers, substratum, discharge, current velocity and wind were judged unimportant.

## Background & Description

The study aimed to describe an entire river system's invertebrate community over a full year, a scope not matched in England since the [[River Tees Survey]] (Butcher, Lonsdale & Penlow, 1937). The Duddon was chosen for year-round accessibility, low human disturbance, no large lakes interrupting the stream pattern, and a considerable altitudinal gradient.

The basin covers 8636 hectares, lies largely under the [[Forestry Commission]] or the [[National Trust]], and drains roughly 18 km from headwaters rising to 735 m on the fells south of Crinkle Crags to the tidal Duddon Sands. Major tributaries include Tarn Beck, Crosby Gill, Holehouse Gill and Logan Beck; [[Gaitscale Gill]] is the highest tributary. Bedrock is acidic lower Silurian volcanic rock, and the valley is glacially modified.

Land use differs between basins. The upper basin has two low-productivity farms and is dominated by sheep grazing, moor-rush and sedges. The lower basin has over a dozen farms, at least one dairy, fertilised pastures and cattle, and oak and conifer cover. The authors attribute these differences ultimately to climatic contrasts between the basins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** Stream temperatures ranged from 0° C (January) to almost 20° C (June–July). Maximum weekly variation was about 10° C (May), and weekly ranges of about 8° C occurred April through August. Upper-basin conditions were harsher, with longer-lasting snow and a shorter growing season.
- **Precipitation and discharge:** Mean annual precipitation at Ulpha Water Works was 203 cm (range 149–278). Discharge ranged from 41,760 to 1,612,000 m³/day and fluctuated rapidly, closely following precipitation. Flow is regulated below the Ulpha intake.
- **Water chemistry:** Rainfall was considered the primary source of sodium, potassium, chloride and sulphate. Total cations averaged about 0.240 meq/L near the source and about 0.420 meq/L near the mouth. Upper-basin total cations were usually 0.300 meq/L or less (range 0.186–0.335), against 0.400 or more in the lower basin (range 0.348–0.691). Alkalinity was undetectable at the headwater station, and Gaitscale Gill stayed below pH 6.0.
- **Detritus:** Terrestrial leaf litter and plant debris accumulate in winter and are removed by spring floods. Invertebrates aggregated in packets of this debris, which the authors regard as a major food base.
- **Sampling design:** The study used 28 numbered and lettered stations, with an autumn series (November 1965, plus October 1965 and February 1966 for lettered sites) and a summer series (1–2 July 1966). Collections used a 25 cm square metal-framed net with 265-micron mesh, and were preserved in formalin and 70% ethanol.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna:** 62 benthic macroinvertebrate taxa were recorded, 44 of them to species level.
- **Dominant taxa:** [[Leuctra inermis]] was the most abundant. Together with Simulium spp., Chironomidae, [[Amphinemura sulcicollis]], [[Baetis rhodani]], [[Leuctra hippopus]], Gammarus pulex and [[Protonemura meyeri]], these made up 71% of the total autumn and summer collection.
- **Ubiquity:** Only Chironomidae and Simulium spp. occurred at all 28 stations. A. sulcicollis, P. meyeri, L. inermis and L. hippopus each occurred at more than 25 sites.
- **Basin contrast:** Ephemeroptera were almost absent above station D, except two specimens of Siphlonurus lacustris and four of B. rhodani. Gammarus pulex was confined mostly to lower-basin tributaries. Only three taxa, all plecopterans, were largely restricted to the upper basin. The lower basin held 36 additional taxa, 16 of them common or abundant.
- **Seasonality:** Two collections a year, one in winter and one in summer, are needed for a reliable picture (following [[Macan]], 1957). Five species would have been missed by an autumn-only sample.
- **Comparison with other streams:** The mayfly fauna of [[Ford Wood Beck]] closely resembled the lower Duddon, with six species common to both. Plecopteran fauna also overlapped strongly. [[Whelpside Ghyll]] combined upper-Duddon Plecoptera with lower-Duddon mayflies and Gammarus.
- **Factors tested:**
  - Physical barriers, lithology and substratum were judged unlikely explanations, since occasional upper-basin Ephemeroptera and Gammarus records indicate physical continuity.
  - Discharge variation and current velocity were not supported as explanations, since velocities overlapped between basins and fauna did not track them.
  - Wind was considered unlikely because one of the windiest sites still held mayflies.
  - Temperature was considered a plausible but insufficient explanation, because high-altitude Whelpside Ghyll has a mayfly fauna despite being at least as cold as the upper Duddon.
  - Water chemistry and detritus nutritive value were judged the most likely explanation. Relevant factors include low bicarbonate in the upper basin, low total cations (cf. [[Ecological Survey of Scottish Streams]] by Egglishaw & Morgan, 1965), and low-quality coarse detritus from high pastures (Festuca ovina dominated), which yielded 559–915 kg dry weight/ha/yr versus 1788–1947 kg at lower pastures.
- **Conclusion:** The authors identify nutrients or other water-quality factors as the most probable cause of the faunal break, with temperature possibly important. Future intensive study is suggested in these areas.
