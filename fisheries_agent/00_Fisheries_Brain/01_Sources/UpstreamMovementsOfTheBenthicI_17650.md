# Upstream Movements of the Benthic Invertebrates in the Speed River, Ontario

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study by [[John E. Bishop]] and [[H. B. N. Hynes]] (University of Waterloo, 1969) quantified the upstream movement of benthic invertebrates in the Speed River, Ontario, between June 1966 and September 1967. Using solid-walled [[Box Traps]] with induced current and [[Terylene Net Traps]], the authors detected substantial upstream migration. Upstream movement offset 6.5% of downstream drift by number and 4% by weight. The authors conclude that this movement is sufficient in quantity and diversity to account for recolonization of dried-out or erosion-denuded areas, though it is not a serious counter-mechanism to downstream [[Invertebrate Drift]].

No fish were studied; the document addresses the aquatic invertebrate fauna and its habitat only.

## Background & Description

- **Research focus:** Quantitative and temporal assessment of upstream migration of stream invertebrates, and its relationship to benthic density and downstream drift.
- **Prior knowledge:** The authors note that little was known of invertebrate movements within streams. Earlier reports described upstream migration of mayflies (e.g., [[Leptophlebia Cupida]]), gammarid amphipods, and immature caddisflies and stoneflies, usually attributed to positive rheotaxis, food search, or repopulation after disturbance. Prior work had produced no quantitative data on upstream dispersal.
- **Study site:** The Speed River rises near Orton in East Garafraxa Township, Wellington County, and flows about 65 km south to join the [[Grand River]] near Preston. The main sampling area was about 7.5 km from the source at 411.8 m elevation: a 125 m riffle with a 40 m shallow pool above, 5-10 m wide, 8-15 cm deep at modal flow.
- **Setting:** Bedrock is [[Guelph Dolomite]]; soils are podzols and humic gleysols over outwash and glacial till. About 80% of the watershed is open agricultural land, so flooding and rapid water-level fluctuations are common.
- **Substrate and vegetation:** Mixed gravels up to 10 cm with dolomite slabs and sand/silt in depositional areas. Emergent vegetation (Lemna, Potamogeton) occurred in backwaters, and [[Nasturtium]] beds lined partly shaded banks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Discharge:** 0.1 m/s in midsummer to about 20 m³/s during spring run-off.
- **Water chemistry:** Conductivity ranged from 150 µmhos (May) to 398 µmhos at minimum flow (August-September). Sulfate-sulfur measured 10-14 mg/L on four occasions. PO₄-P and NO₃-N tracked water volume, mainly from fertilizer and manure run-off. A winter diatom bloom before ice formation was thought to explain the apparent winter loss of dissolved SiO₂ and low NO₃-N.
- **Climate:** The summer of 1967 was cooler and much wetter than 1966.
- **Hyporheic zone:** Substantial fauna lives in deeper interstitial layers. A colonization-pot study (Coleman, unpublished, 1968) found 40-50% of the fauna below 15 cm, which led the authors to bury traps 9 cm below stream-bed level.
- **Benthic density:** Coleman (unpublished) reported 101,397/m² in June and 950,992/m² in July 1966.

## Key Findings

1. **Upstream movement is substantial.** Traps caught large numbers of upstream migrants. In the 3.3 m net traps of June and July 1966, the downstream 2 m held more than 50% of the catch. Total catches were 5,049 (June 23, 1966) and 13,624 (July 8, 1966).

2. **Darkness increases upstream movement.** Artificially darkened traps captured more biomass and more Trichoptera (Hydroptilidae, Psychomyiidae), Hydracarina and, to a lesser extent, Ephemeroptera. Covered 3.3 m traps caught about 129% of the uncovered-trap total, and covered 1 m traps about 128%.

3. **Seasonal and flood effects.** Numbers dropped after floods that scoured the bottom (December 20, 1966 and September 5, 1967). Diptera and "Others" (mainly mites and Copepoda) showed the largest losses. Recovery of the upper substrate was estimated at 4-8 weeks. Total catches over the year were 14,626 invertebrates (1,996 on November 20, 1966; 4,781 on July 9, 1967) with a total biomass of about 2.9 g.

4. **Position in the stream matters.** Winter movement (November-May) was greatest at the bank (P < 0.01) and greater midway than at centre (P < 0.05). In summer (July-September) the centre station contributed the most migrants (P < 0.05). Bank traps were dominated in winter by *Allocapnia pygmaea*, while summer peaks reflected emergence cycles.

5. **Vertical distribution.** Deeper substrate caught more insects in some cases, but the increase was far below the two- to threefold expected from added surface area. Upward migrants reached the top sections of the traps, and *Allocapnia* was found most often in the uppermost section, suggesting rapid movement.

6. **Taxa and life stages.** Less than 1.5% of the catch was over 5 mm long, and more than two thirds of the total was Diptera (mainly chironomids, some possibly passing the filters). Most insect groups were taken at all sizes and stages. The Plecoptera movement was dominated by *Allocapnia pygmaea*, with peak winter migration matching its growth phase. Taxa in Table 7 were numerous; the most abundant included Chironominae, Orthocladiinae, Hydracarina, Naididae, Copepoda and Polycentropus.

7. **Compensation for drift.** Upstream movement was highest when drift was lowest (January and August). Over the year it counteracted about 6.5% of drift by number (3.6 × 10⁶ upstream vs. 55 × 10⁶ drifting, extrapolated) and 4% by biomass (0.71 kg vs. 17.3 kg). The authors could not measure relative distances travelled.

8. **Mechanisms.** Positive rheotaxis is the main driver. Positive skototaxis and phototactic release, with night-time upstream movement and orthokinetic responses toward cryptic habitats, probably govern the distance covered. The darkened-trap results support this. Unstable substrates may also trigger migration.

9. **Conclusions.** Upstream movement is large enough, and diverse enough, to recolonize dried-out or erosion-denuded areas. The authors judge it not to be a serious counter-mechanism to downstream drift, in line with earlier studies (Stehr and Branson, Ball et al., Minckley, Waters, Miller).

10. **Methodological limits.** Net traps suffered colonization of the mesh by drifting chironomids and hydroptilids. Box traps had eddy swirl at the downstream mouth and uneven faunal distribution. Baffle tests of surface versus interstitial movement were inconclusive.
