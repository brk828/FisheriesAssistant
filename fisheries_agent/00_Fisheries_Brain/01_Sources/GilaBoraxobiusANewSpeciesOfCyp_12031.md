# Gila Boraxobius: A New Dwarf Cyprinid from Borax Lake, Southeastern Oregon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document formally describes [[Gila Boraxobius]] (Borax Lake Chub), a new dwarf species of cyprinid fish from [[Borax Lake]], a small spring-fed lake in the [[Alvord Basin]] of southeastern Oregon. The authors compare it morphologically with its closest relative, [[Gila Alvordensis]], known from [[Trout Creek]]. The species is restricted to a very small habitat that is threatened by drilling associated with [[Geothermal Energy Exploration]]. It has been proposed for federal threatened and endangered listing, is on the Oregon protected list, and is listed as threatened by the American Fisheries Society Endangered Species Committee.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context**
- The genus [[Gila]] is a diverse group of western North American cyprinids. The text states that fifteen species and numerous subspecies are currently recognized; the Relationships section later states that sixteen species are recognized.
- Many Gila taxa were described from allopatric populations in the [[Great Basin]].
- [[Gila Alvordensis]] was named by [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1972) from Trout Creek. Their diagnosis included the Borax Lake fish, which they noted as "usually greatly dwarfed." Further study showed the Borax Lake fish is specifically distinct.

**Physical description (Gila boraxobius)**
- Dwarf species; adults typically 33-50 mm standard length (SL).
- Large head with a prominently concave dorsal profile; large eyes; long jaw with an oblique mouth; noticeably slender caudal peduncle.
- Reduced lateral line, with pores restricted to near the head in 27 of 30 fish examined.
- Uniserial, well-hooked pharyngeal teeth, typically 5-4, occasionally 4-4.
- Fine, deeply embedded scales with well-developed radii in all fields.
- Typically 7 dorsal and anal fin rays, 8 pelvic rays, 13 pectoral rays, and 19 caudal rays.
- Modal vertebral counts: precaudal 19, caudal 18 or 19, total 37.

**Size and sexual dimorphism**
- Holotype: tuberculate male, UMMZ 203329, 50.6 mm SL.
- Of several hundred Borax Lake specimens in the Oregon State University collection, only four exceeded 60 mm SL: 61.8, 68.3, 90.4, and 92.7 mm, all female.
- Males as small as 28.6 mm SL are highly tuberculate; mature eggs were found in females as small as 31.8 mm SL.
- All fins are proportionately larger in males (see Key Findings).

**Coloration**
- Live fish are moderately dark, olive green dorsally, with silver sides showing purple iridescence and a silver venter.

**Diagnostic comparison with Gila Alvordensis**
- Gila boraxobius has a longer, wider, and deeper head, a larger eye, a longer snout and jaw, and a more slender caudal peduncle.
- Its dorsal head profile is concave, whereas that of G. alvordensis is convex.
- Its lateral line is reduced, whereas that of G. alvordensis is complete.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**
- [[Borax Lake]] (also called Hot Lake) is a relatively shallow, very clear natural lake of 4.1 ha.
- The lake receives water from several thermal springs issuing from a fault at 35-40°C. Lake temperatures range from roughly 17 to 35°C depending on season, weather, and distance from spring sources.
- The species avoids water above approximately 34°C.
- The water is slightly alkaline (pH near 7.3) with a specific conductance of 2,410. Sodium is the dominant cation; bicarbonate, sulfate, and chloride are the principal anions, in decreasing abundance (Mariner et al., 1974).
- The lake sits on salts deposited by its own waters. This process has raised the lake's elevation to 10 m above the surrounding land over hundreds to thousands of years.
- No other fish species inhabits these waters.
- The species also occurs occasionally in [[Lower Borax Lake]], an artificial lake fed from Borax Lake outflow. This lake usually dries in summer and never holds a viable population.

**Biogeographic history**
- During the Pleistocene, a pluvial lake of over 1,200 km² at its maximum extent occupied the [[Alvord Basin]] (Snyder et al., 1964).
- As [[Lake Alvord]] dried, fishes became isolated in remaining springs and creeks. Eleven allopatric Gila populations now occur in the Alvord Basin across Oregon and Nevada (Hubbs et al., 1974).

**Threats and conservation status**
- Drilling for [[Geothermal Energy Exploration]] threatens the Borax Lake population by possibly interfering with the hot springs that supply the lake.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Unique Wildlife Ecosystem Program ranks the Borax Lake area as the second most important and unique ecosystem in Oregon (pers. comm., Bruce Boccard).
- The species is on the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife protected list and is listed as threatened by the American Fisheries Society Endangered Species Committee (Deacon et al., 1979).

## Key Findings

- **New species:** Gila boraxobius is described as distinct from Gila alvordensis at the specific level.
- **Sample base:** Thirty specimens each of Gila from Borax Lake and Trout Creek were measured for morphometry and meristics.
- **Measurements:** Standard length ranged 41.7-90.4 mm (mean 48.9 mm, SD 9.1) in G. boraxobius, versus 47.4-119.5 mm (mean 64.8 mm, SD 22.1) in G. alvordensis.
- **Head proportions:** Head length averaged 318.1 per thousand SL in G. boraxobius, versus 270.0 in G. alvordensis. Orbit length averaged 64.3 versus 49.9.
- **Lateral line:** Pore counts averaged 13.2 (range 2-43) in G. boraxobius versus 44.2 (range 30-66) in G. alvordensis. Of the 30 Borax Lake fish, 27 had lateral line pores restricted to near the head; no specimens had lateral line development on the caudal peduncle.
- **Fin rays:** Modal pectoral ray count is 13 in G. boraxobius, versus 14 or 15 in G. alvordensis. Both species typically have 7 dorsal and anal rays, while G. bicolor typically has 8 or more.
- **Gill rakers:** First-arch gill rakers averaged 15.9 in 16 G. boraxobius specimens, versus 19.9 in G. alvordensis.
- **Scales:** Caudal peduncle scale counts averaged 29.8 in G. boraxobius, versus 32.1 in G. alvordensis.
- **Sex ratio:** In a sample of 90 individuals over 39.0 mm SL collected in August, 24.4% were male and 75.6% female. Spring, autumn, and winter collections showed nearly equal numbers of adults of each sex.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males have longer fins than females. Depressed dorsal fin averaged 229.5 (males, n=4) versus 190.8 (females, n=28) per thousand SL. Left pelvic fin averaged 172.3 versus 140.5. Male pelvic fins reach the anal fin origin; female pelvic fins usually do not reach the anus.
- **Nuptial tubercles:** Found only on males longer than 28 mm SL, restricted to the body and paired fins.
- **Intestine:** Simple sigmoid; length typically 80-90% of SL.
- **Vertebrae:** Modal precaudal 19, caudal 18 or 19, total 37.
- **Diet and ecology:** The text gives no feeding or reproductive-timing data beyond the sex-ratio and maturity observations above.
- **Type material:** Holotype UMMZ 203329 (50.6 mm SL) was collected by K. Howe and party from Borax Lake, Harney County, Oregon, on 5 August 1977. Paratopotypes total UMMZ 203330 (114 specimens), OS 4137 (12), and OS 4138 (182).
- **Etymology:** The epithet *boraxobius* combines "borax" and Greek *bios* ("life"), referring to life in the borate waters of Borax Lake.
- **Conservation:** The species is proposed for federal threatened and endangered listing, is on the Oregon protected list, and is listed as threatened by the American Fisheries Society (Deacon et al., 1979).
