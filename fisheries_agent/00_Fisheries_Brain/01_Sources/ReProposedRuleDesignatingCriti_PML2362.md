# Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund Comments on Proposed Critical Habitat for Four Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a formal public comment letter, dated January 10, 1994, from the [[Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund]] to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) on the proposed rule designating [[Critical Habitat]] for four endangered Colorado River fishes: the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The letter is submitted on behalf of six conservation organizations and supports the proposal in principle while urging a substantially broader and more protective final rule. Its central demands are: (1) explicit, reach-specific flow regimes approximating the natural hydrograph as constituent elements of critical habitat; (2) control of non-native predators and competitors; (3) inclusion of all proposed river reaches plus several additional segments; (4) no economic exclusions; and (5) stronger recovery mechanisms, water quality provisions and a summary of activities that would adversely modify habitat.

## Background & Description

The four species are described as being "on the verge of extinction." Their native habitat is being degraded by habitat loss, introduction of exotic species and other human activities. The letter notes that the plight of the fishes has worsened in the decades since they were listed, with listing periods cited as ranging from 14 to 27 years. The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) is the legal framework throughout, and the letter relies on its requirement that critical habitat be based on the best scientific information available.

The letter is a regulatory comment rather than primary research. It supplements comments from the Colorado Wildlife Federation, Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance, Four Corners Action Coalition, Colorado Environmental Coalition and Taxpayers for the Animas River. It also incorporates prior comments from [[Arizona State University]] researchers Dr. W.L. Minckley and Dr. Paul Marsh, and from Dr. Michael Kavanaugh, the author of the economic comments.

The FWS proposal is evaluated against the [[Biological Support Document]] (BSD), the [[Draft Economic Analysis]], and the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) for the Upper Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** The letter argues that the fishes evolved in a fluctuating ecosystem. Flows cue spawning, provide nursery habitat, inundate floodplains that supply nutrients, and influence the success of non-native competitors. Critical habitat therefore "need not be occupied year round or even annually" to be essential to recovery. The letter cites flow recommendations from [[Tyus and Karp]] (1989, 1991), [[Stanford]] (1993), [[Osmundson and Kaeding]] (1991) and [[Bliesner]] (1992), and argues that daily base-flow fluctuations pose substantial risk to young and larval fish and their food webs.

**Non-native species.** The letter states that introduced species pose a significant threat through predation and competition. It cites studies on predation by [[Catfish]] (ictalurid) species, including [[Pimental et al.]] (1985) and [[Marsh and Brooks]] (1989), and on competition from non-native fish, including [[Karp and Tyus]] (1990).

**Floodplains.** The BSD recognizes the hundred-year floodplain as biologically necessary. The letter stresses its role in fish reproduction and in maintaining the river's ecological health.

**Water quality and sediment.** The letter identifies temperature, sediment load, dissolved oxygen, nutrients and turbidity as primary constituent elements. It notes that the [[Paria River]] supplies sediment and warm water to the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

**Downstream connectivity.** Because water flows downstream, excluding any reach would harm all downstream reaches. The letter argues that fish migration, genetic exchange and buffering against catastrophic events, such as an oil spill, depend on connected segments.

## Key Findings

- **Critical habitat must be broad.** The letter argues that the ESA requires designation of habitat necessary to recover and delist the species, not merely to avoid jeopardy. Where knowledge is limited, decisions should "err on the side of species protection," citing [[Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill]] (437 U.S. 153, 1978).

- **Water quantity must be explicit.** The letter objects that the proposed rule leaves water quantity unaddressed. It asks that flows approximating the natural, historic hydrograph be made constituent elements for each reach. Flow levels developed in the Draft Economic Analysis (Vol. II, Ch. 5) should be included. Adequate water is to be achieved through the Section 7 process, with facilities operated together to produce flood, drought and average-year hydrographs.

- **Precedent for flow-based habitat.** The letter cites the Concho water snake critical habitat (50 C.F.R. § 17.95(c)) as precedent for defining habitat by flow levels.

- **Non-native fish control.** The final rule should specify decisions to eliminate predation and competition by non-native species throughout the basin. Non-native fish should not be stocked, and flow regimes that give native fish an advantage should be implemented.

- **Floodplain protection.** Floodplain lands, including sites already owned by FWS such as the Wildlife Refuge, should be identified and acquired or managed, and flows should inundate them regularly.

- **Additional reaches to include.** The letter urges inclusion of:
  - the [[Animas River]] up to the New Mexico border (for razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish);
  - the [[San Juan River]] upstream of Navajo Reservoir and the reach between Hogback Diversion, New Mexico, and Navajo Dam;
  - the [[Dolores River]], given a recent Colorado squawfish sighting and the failure to recover the species in more than 25 years;
  - the humpback chub reach upstream to Glen Canyon Dam, including several miles of the Paria River;
  - the humpback chub habitat in the [[Little Colorado River]] up to Blue Spring, where Arizona Game and Fish believes spawning occurs;
  - [[Lake Havasu]] for razorback sucker, citing the Lake Mohave population as evidence of the value of lacustrine habitat;
  - the [[Yampa River]], including Cross Mountain Canyon and the Little Yampa, where a viable humpback chub population has been identified.

- **No economic exclusions.** The letter argues that the economic analysis shows impacts too small to measure, and that it overestimates costs and underestimates benefits because it does not allow substitutions or account for non-market value. It concludes that the FWS cannot exclude any proposed area. The letter also objects that any new analysis at a county level would fail to capture the flowing nature of water. It further argues that under the ESA, economic exclusion is not legally permissible for species listed before 1978, which includes the Colorado squawfish and humpback chub.

- **Public notice.** The letter warns that any exclusion not previously noticed would violate the Administrative Procedure Act and cites [[Batterton v. Francis]] on adequate notice of significant rule components.

- **Water quality analysis.** The BSD fails to identify factors that influence water quality. The final rule should review biological links between water quality and fish vitality, identify actions affecting water quality in critical habitat areas, and identify remedial actions.

- **Recovery program weaknesses.** The letter argues that the RIP is not a legally adequate forum for developing recovery measures. It is described as riddled with conflicts of interest, too geographically limited, and unable to demonstrate significant progress. The letter asks that it expedite timetables and focus on these four fishes.

- **Section 7 consultations.** Re-initiation of consultation with parties responsible for existing projects should begin immediately, because the RIP alone cannot offset the impacts of existing and planned projects in the Upper Basin.

- **Adverse modification.** The final rule must summarize the activities that would adversely modify critical habitat (16 U.S.C. § 1533(b)(8)).

- **Population status.** The letter cites FWS documents that the razorback sucker is also "trending toward extinction," and a federal court ruling that it is "dangerously close to extinction" (*Colorado Wildlife Federation v. Turner*). The Green River subbasin is described as the last major stronghold for Colorado squawfish, though numbers there are not increasing. The San Juan subbasin is identified as an essential third wild population.
