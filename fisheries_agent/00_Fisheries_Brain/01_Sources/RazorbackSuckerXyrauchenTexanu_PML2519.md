# Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) Recovery Plan Draft

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan, prepared by Harold H. Tyus of the [[Center for Limnology]] (University of Colorado at Boulder) for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Region 6, addresses the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991 and had critical habitat designated in 1994. Its populations have declined sharply over the past 50 years, largely because of [[Flow Alteration]], [[Nonnative Fishes]] predation, and [[Recruitment Failure]]. Extant populations are dominated by old adults, and recruitment is too low to sustain them. The plan is written as a brief strategic document within a multispecies [[Aquatic Ecosystem Recovery Plan]] framework covering four listed Colorado River fishes.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and history.** The razorback sucker is the sole species of the monotypic genus *Xyrauchen*, within the tribe Catostomini and family [[Catostomidae]]. It was first described in 1860 as *Catostomus texanus* by Abbott, based on a specimen wrongly attributed to a Texas river. The genus was erected by Eigenmann and Kirsch (Kirsch 1889). Fossils of the lineage are known from the Pleistocene near the Salton Sea. The name refers to the species' bony dorsal hump ("razornape"). The fish was used as food by prehistoric and later human populations.

**Morphology.** Adults may reach about 1 m total length and 5–6 kg, though most are smaller. Females are larger than males; in Lake Mohave, females averaged 470–740 mm. A pronounced bony keel behind the head distinguishes the species from other New World catostomids. Meristic counts reported include dorsal fin rays usually 14–15 (range 12–17), anal fin rays 7, total vertebrae 45–47, lateral line scales 68–87, and gill rakers 44–50 on the first arch. Breeding males are dark dorsally and bright yellow to orange ventrally and develop nuptial tubercles.

**Ecological role.** Suckers are mainly detritivores and herbivores and are important links in transferring organic material into animal tissue, as well as prey for other fishes.

**Conservation status.** The fish was listed as threatened by the American Fisheries Society in 1972 and federally listed as endangered on October 23, 1991 (56 FR 54957). Critical habitat was designated on March 21, 1994 (59 FR 13374).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically, the species occurred throughout mainstem rivers and major tributaries in the basin, including the lower Colorado from the delta to Lee's Ferry, the Gila, Salt, Verde, Green, Yampa, Gunnison, and San Juan systems. Distribution has contracted to two major areas: large impounded reaches of the lower Colorado River and riverine reaches of the Green, Yampa, and upper Colorado rivers.

**Population status.**
- Lake Mohave: estimated at about 60,000 adults (Minckley et al. 1991), declining about 60% to roughly 25,000 fish in 1995 (Marsh 1995).
- Upper Green River: fewer than 1,000 adults estimated by Lanigan and Tyus (1989).
- Juveniles have been extremely rare in collections since the early 1950s.
- Over 12 million larval and juvenile fish were reintroduced in Arizona and California from 1981 to 1990. Most were apparently lost to predation, and no new populations were established.

**Life history.**
- *Reproduction:* Spawning in Lake Mohave occurs January–April in water temperatures of 11.5–18 °C, over coarse, wave-washed cobble in depths of 0.5–5 m. In the Green River, spawning occurs from mid-April to May, with ripe fish found in association with increased spring flows and water temperatures near 14 °C.
- *Movement:* Spawning migrations of 30.4–105.0 km have been recorded in the Green and lower Yampa rivers. Adults in Lake Mohave traveled 4–26 km.
- *Age and growth:* Otoliths were the only reliable ageing structure. Individuals reach 24–44 years of age (McCarthy and Minckley 1987). Adult growth is very slow, averaging 2.2 mm/year in Tyus (1988).
- *Diet:* Larvae initially feed on phytoplankton and zooplankton in lentic habitats and on chironomid larvae in streams. Riverine adults consume benthos, while reservoir adults are largely planktivorous.
- *Genetics:* Mitochondrial DNA shows greater diversity in Lake Mohave stocks (14 haplotypes) than in upper-basin fish (4 in the Green River, 1 in the Colorado River) (Dowling and Minckley 1993). Hybridization is considered rare and not a major threat.

**Threats and reasons for decline.**
- *Dams and flow alteration:* Over 20 major dams have been built on the mainstem. Peak discharges have been reduced by about 50% since 1942, while base flows increased by 21%. Diversions reduce habitat, and seasonal flooding of bottomlands has nearly disappeared.
- *Nonnative fishes:* More than 50 nonnative species have been introduced. Green sunfish, common carp, and catfishes prey on eggs and larvae. In one experiment, green sunfish consumed 90% of larvae present in 4-minute trials.
- *Habitat loss:* Channelization, diking, tamarisk invasion, and river channel degradation have eliminated backwaters and wetlands.
- *Barriers:* Seven structures block or partly block fish movement above Glen Canyon Dam. More than 15 mainstem dams restrict movement in the lower basin.
- *Water quality:* Cold tailwaters below dams have excluded the fish from some reaches. Elevated selenium (25–45 µg/g dry weight in some tissues) was found at Stewart Lake, and early tests showed 30–50% mortality of larvae exposed to agricultural drainage water.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment failure** is the primary cause of decline. Extant populations are aging, and few larvae survive beyond about 14 mm.
- **Recovery goals:**
  - *Short term:* prevent immediate extinction by reversing the decline of extant stocks in Lake Mohave, the Green River, and the Yampa River.
  - *Downlisting* requires stabilized Lake Mohave stocks, riverine populations in the lower Yampa and Green rivers reaching equilibrium at 2,000 or more adults for at least 5 years, and at least one additional population each in the upper and lower units.
  - *Delisting* requires downlisting plus two additional recovered or established populations.
- **Two recovery units** are proposed: a lower basin unit centered on [[Lake Mohave]] (a Class III, somewhat modified preserve) and an upper basin unit centered on the Green and lower Yampa rivers (a Class II, mostly natural preserve) (Moyle and Sato 1991).
- **Critical habitat** comprises 15 river reaches covering about 49% of historic habitat (1,724 river miles).
- **Recovery priorities** are assigned from Priority 1 (prevent extinction) through Priority 5. The stepdown outline covers protection of populations, control of nonnative fishes, habitat restoration, refugia, monitoring, new recovery areas, augmentation, management plans, quantitative goals, and public outreach.
- **Refugia:** Existing refugia are at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, and Ouray National Wildlife Refuge, Utah. Additional sites are proposed.
- **Reintroduction** has largely failed. Stocking in unsuitable habitats, predation, and insufficient environmental conditioning are cited. Imprinting and homing must be considered in future efforts.
- **Cost and timeline:** The plan projects that the three major populations can be stabilized and extinction avoided by 2000, downlisting possibly by 2010, and delisting as early as 2020 if criteria are met. The cost table in the draft is incomplete.
