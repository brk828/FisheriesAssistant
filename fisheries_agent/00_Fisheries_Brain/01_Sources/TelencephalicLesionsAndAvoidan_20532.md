# Telencephalic Lesions and Avoidance Behaviour in the Goldfish (*Carassius auratus*), with a Companion Study on Mouse Activity Sequences

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carassius

## Executive Summary

The excerpt contains two 1969 papers from *Animal Behaviour*, vol. 17. The first and main paper, by G. E. Savage, reports eight experiments on the role of the [[Telencephalon]] (forebrain) in [[Avoidance Conditioning]] in [[Goldfish]]. Forebrain-ablated fish learned and retained shock-avoidance poorly across light, sound, and discrimination tasks, but they showed normal feeding, locomotion, and olfactory-independent behaviour. The author concludes that the forebrain alerts the animal to new or feared stimuli and helps process reinforcement signals, rather than acting as the sole memory store. The second paper, by Guttman, Lieblich & Naftali, concerns activity sequences in inbred mice. It is terrestrial, not aquatic, and the excerpt ends during its methods section, so no results are available.

## Background & Description

**Study organism.** Goldfish (*Carassius auratus*) measuring 7.5 to 13 cm snout-to-tail length were obtained from a regular supplier, quarantined for one week, and randomly assigned to groups. Fish were housed two to three per tank (23 × 23 × 30 cm) with continuous water flow and aeration, and were fed heart ad libitum after training sessions.

**Surgical method.** Fish were anaesthetised in MS222 (1:5000), with gill irrigation at 1:10,000. The skull was trephined, the [[Olfactory Tracts]] were severed, and the forebrain was lifted out with the optic tracts left intact. Control operations followed the same steps without brain damage. Operated fish recovered for five days before training. Lesion extent was estimated from histology (Holmes's silver stain or haematoxylin and eosin) on a standard grid, with forebrain removal typically above 80 per cent.

**Training apparatus.** A shuttle box (28 × 23 cm approx., with a white door containing an elliptical hole) was used. Shock was a 0.5 s, 4 V a.c. pulse. Light (60 W lamp, 18 cm overhead) and a 400 Hz buzzer served as conditioned stimuli. Each fish received ten trials per day in a darkened room. Avoidance was defined as moving to the goal box within the CS/UCS interval, and escape as moving after shock. Intervals of 5 s and 15 s were used.

**Mouse study (Guttman et al.).** The mice were DBA/1J and C57BL/6J inbred strains, plus F1, F2 and backcross genotypes. Animals were observed for 5 min in a barrier cage, with barrier height lowered to 4 cm. Recorded activities were face washing, licking, peeking over barriers, jumping, and defecation, all in order of occurrence. The stated aim was to apply [[Multidimensional Scalogram Analysis]] to behaviour sequences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The goldfish work was carried out entirely in laboratory tanks, so no field habitat or natural population is described. The author emphasises that forebrain removal did not impair feeding, orientation, or locomotion, consistent with earlier reports by Desmoulins (1825) and Healey (1957). Experimental conditions were controlled: darkened room, constant low illumination, a dark glass screen to hide the observer, and foam rubber to reduce vibration. Olfaction was removed as a confound through [[Olfactory Tract Severance]].

The mouse study used standard laboratory housing, with unlimited Purina Laboratory Chow and water, and animals were reared with their mothers until testing at about 30 to 42 days of age (the scan reads "30-2 days"). No aquatic or ecological context applies.

## Key Findings

**Savage (1969), goldfish forebrain and avoidance**

- **Experiment 1 (15 s light CS):** Lesioned fish (n = 8, mean 85 per cent forebrain removed) showed a sharp post-operative drop in avoidance. Their first 50 post-operative trials differed from controls (T = 28, n = 14, P < 0.01; [[Mann-Whitney Test]], White's version). Post-operative shock-driven escapes were not impaired, and lesioned fish had mean escape times equal before and after surgery (Table I).
- **Retention:** Naive forebrain-ablated fish (n = 9, 87 per cent removed) trained for 25 days never matched controls, but still showed some retention (T = 51, n = 17, P < 0.05).
- **Activity:** Forebrainless fish stayed almost motionless during the stimulus-shock interval. Photocell counts for five fish were 0, 4, 2, 0, 4 passes over five post-operative trials, versus 0, 0, 1, 0, 2 for naive forebrainless fish. Conditioned "startle" responses were absent.
- **Experiment 2 (5 s CS):** Lesioned fish (n = 5, 95 per cent removed) showed the same near-total deficit and slow recovery as in Experiment 1.
- **Experiment 3 (sound CS):** Lesioned fish (n = 4, 90 per cent removed) and naive fish (n = 3, 92 per cent removed) showed deficits similar to the light-CS results. The author concluded that the mechanism is not specific to sensory modality.
- **Experiment 4 (olfactory severance):** Two fish with severed olfactory tracts learned the 15 s light task without the deficit, ruling out olfactory loss as the cause.
- **Experiment 5 (stimulus size and [[Interocular Transfer]]):** Lesioned fish (n = 6, 89 per cent removed) differed from controls post-operatively (T = 21, n = 15, P < 0.01). Escape times were 7.86 s before and 7.70 s after surgery. Naive forebrainless fish learned more slowly than trained-then-lesioned fish (T = 6, n = 9, P < 0.05), reaching pre-operative avoidance levels after about 200 trials. Interocular transfer was good in naive fish.
- **Experiment 6 (go/no-go [[Visual Discrimination]]):** Trained-then-lesioned fish (n = 6, 89 per cent removed) kept a near-chance discrimination, while avoidance climbed slowly to 15 to 20 per cent (T = 21, n = 21, P < 0.01). Naive forebrainless fish (n = 6, 90 per cent removed) reached avoidance of 20 to 30 per cent but still discriminated at chance. For avoidance, the trained-lesioned versus naive comparison was significant (T = 21, n = 12, P < 0.01). For discrimination it was not (T = 34, n = 12, P > 0.05). The author concludes that the forebrain controls which stimuli are avoided, not just whether avoidance occurs.
- **Experiment 7 ([[Commissure Splitting]]):** Splitting the forebrain commissure (four successful animals, Table II) reduced responses on the untrained side. Re-learning on that side later restored comparable scores, which the author takes as evidence of specific rather than unspecific forebrain involvement.
- **Experiment 8 ([[Unilateral Lesions]]):** Ten fish with electrolytic lesions (Table III) showed that dorsal lateral and central areas mediate avoidance. In several cases discrimination was impaired while avoidance remained high, which may indicate separate neural substrates.
- **Discussion:** The results agree with Aronson (1968) and Hainsworth et al. (1967), but contradict Ingle (1965) and Bernstein (1961a, b; 1962). Savage (1968b) reported that the avoidance deficit almost disappears at 30 trials per day. The author rejects a catatonia explanation (Regestein 1968) and a simple activation account.
- **Summary:** Forebrain ablation significantly impairs learning and retention of avoidance across modalities, independent of response time. The forebrain is proposed to alert the animal and to process reinforcement (pain) signals for memory storage. Splitting the commissure prevents interocular transfer.
