# The Influence of Floating Vascular Plants on the Diurnal Fluctuations of Temperature Near the Water Surface in Early Spring

#limnology #aquatic-botany #Lemnaceae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 study by Dale & Gillespie examines how mats of floating vascular plants (primarily [[Duckweed|duckweeds]] of the family [[Lemnaceae]]) alter the thermal microhabitat at the water surface during early spring in Ontario, Canada. Field measurements across three wetland sites and controlled experiments using paired plastic tanks demonstrate that floating plant mats create pronounced diurnal thermal stratification, with surface temperatures 4–11°C warmer than adjacent open water on clear days. This thermal effect has direct implications for the physiology, growth, and phenology of aquatic organisms — including fish, invertebrates, and plankton — that inhabit or interact with these floating plant microhabitats, since ambient open-water measurements poorly represent the "operative temperature" experienced within a few centimeters of the plant-water-air interface.

## Background & Description

**Taxa examined:**
- **Lemnaceae (duckweeds):** *Lemna minor*, *Lemna trisulca*, *Spirodela polyrhiza*, *Wolffia columbiana* — small free-floating vascular plants that achieve buoyancy in early spring via intercellular gas spaces produced by rising temperature and active photosynthesis.
- **Other floating/associated taxa:** *Riccia fluitans*, *Ricciocarpus natans* (liverworts), *Drepanocladus fluitans* (aquatic moss), *Sparganium eurycarpum* (bur-reed seedlings).
- **Larger floating-leaved plants:** *Nuphar variegatum* (yellow water lily) and *Potamogeton crispus* (curly-leaf pondweed), studied at Lake Opinicon.
- **Emergent marsh vegetation** (not directly measured but discussed as modifying influences): *Typha latifolia*, *T. angustifolia*, *Acorus calamus*.

**Mechanism of buoyancy:** Following [[Arber 1920]] and [[Jacobs 1947]], overwintering turions/thalli become buoyant as replacement of intercellular water by gas under rising spring temperatures and active photosynthesis overcomes the anchoring effect of stored starch. [[Dale 1957]]'s prior work on *Elodea canadensis* is cited as showing photosynthetic oxygen production pressurizes and distends intercellular air spaces, aiding thalli in reaching the surface.

**Field sites (Part I):** Three shallow wetland areas near Guelph, Ontario (Puslinch Township), sampled weekly April–May 1974, plus supplementary measurements at Lake Opinicon (Boathouse Bay and Hart Lake Channel).

**Experimental system (Part II):** Two polyethylene tanks (72 × 32 × 63 cm) insulated with aluminum foil, raised 63 cm above ground, one with clear spring water, one with turbid Grand River water — each paired with an open-water control and a duckweed-covered treatment. Thermistor probes recorded temperatures at the air-water interface, 2 cm, 10 cm, and 45 cm depths; net radiation, air temperature, and wind velocity were monitored via a nearby meteorological station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal stratification:** Floating plant mats absorb and re-radiate short-wave energy at the surface (high albedo: 0.24–0.27 vs. 0.11 for open water), transmitting very little light downward (0.4–6%), producing steep, "soil-like" thermal gradients dominated by conduction rather than convection.
- **Open water systems**, by contrast, allow radiant energy penetration to depth and are subject to wind-driven mixing, producing more homogeneous vertical temperature profiles and slower, air-temperature-linked surface fluctuations.
- **Wind and mixing:** Wind velocity strongly influenced time-lag of maximum temperature at depth in open water but had comparatively little effect under the insulating duckweed cover — reinforcing the buffering/insulating role of the floating mat.
- **Evaporation:** Energy budget analysis indicated greater evaporative loss from open water than from duckweed-covered surfaces, attributed to stomatal resistance in the floating plant tissue despite higher surface temperatures.
- **Turbidity and phytoplankton:** Differences in water turbidity (clear spring water vs. turbid Grand River water) did not measurably alter thermal patterns in this study, though the authors note prior work (Butler 1963; Culver 1974) linking turbidity/phytoplankton abundance to modified thermal budgets in natural water bodies.
- **Phenological cues:** The timing of duckweed emergence and vertical movement (e.g., *Lemna trisulca* rising and sinking in response to light/cloud conditions on April 3, 14, and 19) supports prior findings ([[Jacobs 1947]]) that light and temperature regulate spring emergence of floating Lemnaceae, though dissolved oxygen tension is proposed as an additional contributing factor.
- **Relevance to aquatic fauna:** The authors explicitly emphasize that the top ~2 cm of the water-air-plant interface represents a distinct, ecologically important microhabitat whose thermal regime is not well represented by conventional open-water or deep-water measurements — a critical consideration for interpreting "operative temperatures" for biological processes in organisms (including [[Fish|fishes]], invertebrates, and periphyton) associated with floating plant mats.

## Key Findings

- **Surface elevation of temperature:** On clear days, floating *Lemna* mats were **4.0–11.0°C warmer** than adjacent open water at solar noon, and up to **12.2°C warmer** than ambient air at 1 m height.
- **Rapid thermal response:** Duckweed mat surface temperature rose from 16.0°C to 20.0°C in 7 minutes following a passing cloud (May 27); infrared measurements showed rises of 0.5°C in 10 seconds up to 4.0°C in 2.5 minutes under direct solar exposure.
- **Depth-dependent fluctuation (tank experiments):**
  - Diurnal surface fluctuation: **3.5–7.7°C greater** under duckweed than open water.
  - At 1.5 cm above the surface (air): **1.1–1.4°C greater** fluctuation over duckweed.
  - At 2 cm below the surface: **2.4–3.5°C greater** fluctuation under duckweed.
  - At 10 cm and 45 cm depth: fluctuations were **smaller** under duckweed cover than in open water (e.g., 5.0°C and 3.8°C vs. 9.2°C at 2 cm in the same tank).
- **Albedo differences:** Open water albedo = 0.11; vegetation mat albedo = 0.24–0.27, indicating floating plants reflect more incoming short-wave radiation.
- **Light transmittance:** Thin vegetation mats transmitted 2–6% of incident light; thicker mats transmitted only 0.4–0.8% (cf. Ganning & Wulff 1970: 0.9% for *Lemna* cover).
- **Total energy budget:** On May 23, open water tank gained 212 cal/cm² vs. 197 cal/cm² for the duckweed-covered tank; on May 30, 324 cal/cm² vs. 292 cal/cm², respectively — indicating slightly reduced total energy absorption under floating vegetation despite higher peak surface temperatures.
- **Time-lag of maximum temperature with depth:** Greater and more variable under duckweed cover than in open water; wind mixing reduced this lag in open water but had little effect under vegetation.
- **Negative energy change onset (cooling):** Occurred earlier in duckweed-covered surface layers (as early as 1430–1530 h) compared to open water (1730 h), reflecting faster response to declining radiation.
- **Comparative floating/rooted plant data:**
  - *Nuphar variegatum* leaves showed elevated surface temperatures dependent on time of day, wind, and shading.
  - *Potamogeton crispus* stands (Lake Opinicon, Hart Lake Channel) showed highest sub-surface water temperatures near the center of the vegetated mass, decreasing toward the edge (e.g., 27.3°C at 20 m from edge vs. 24.0°C at 1 m under strong breeze conditions).
- **Seasonal substrate/water temperature data (Lake Opinicon, Boathouse Bay):** Water and substrate temperatures rose from ~2.0°C (water) / 4.0°C (substrate) in March to 17.9°C / 16.5°C by June 1, tracking overall spring warming relevant to *Lemna trisulca* buoyancy timing.
- **Evaporation asymmetry:** Qualitative energy-budget analysis indicates open water systems lose more energy via evaporation than duckweed-covered systems, both during day and night.
- **General model:** During the warming phase of the diurnal cycle, plant-covered water behaves thermally like a soil system (conduction-dominated, strong near-surface gradients), whereas open water behaves as a mixed fluid system (convection- and wind-mixing-dominated, more homogeneous vertical temperature).
