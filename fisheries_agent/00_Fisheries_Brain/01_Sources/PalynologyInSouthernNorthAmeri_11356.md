# Palynology in Southern North America I: Archaeological Horizons in the Basin of Mexico

#palynology #paleoclimatology #archaeology #pollen-analysis #lacustrine-sediments

## Executive Summary

This 1962 *Bulletin of the Geological Society of America* paper by Paul B. Sears applies [[Pollen Analysis]] to shallow lacustrine sediments in the [[Basin of Mexico]] to test whether climate changed during known human occupation and whether such changes correlate with [[Cultural Horizons]]. Pollen percentages of four upland forest genera (fir, pine, alder, oak) are used as moisture indicators: high oak or alder implies humid conditions, high pine implies dry conditions. The author concludes that both questions are answerable in the affirmative: alternating moist and dry intervals match archaeological phases from the [[Archaic Culture]] through the [[Nahua Culture]], and the sediments are consistent with soil profiles and former lake levels. The paper also argues that water supply, shaped by forest cover, was a limiting factor for [[Teotihuacan Culture]], and that later drainage and deforestation degraded the basin's economy.

## Background & Description

- **Method:** Samples from a sediment column are chemically treated (KOH; HCl for calcareous and magnesian material in older deposits) to concentrate pollen. A minimum of 100 grains per level was counted. Forest-tree pollen is expressed as percentages; all other pollen is counted per hundred forest-tree grains.
- **Indicator logic:** Three sample types were defined: (1) pine ≥90% (too dry for much oak, alder, or fir); (2) oak ≥10% (relatively moist); (3) all others, treated as indecisive. Fir (usually under 5%, above about 2800 m) was not used for interpretation because of recovery problems.
- **Vegetation context:** Highland forests at about 2300–4000 m consist of fir (*Abies*), pine (*Pinus*), alder (*Alnus*), and oak (*Quercus*). Oak is usually confined to ravines and moist soils, while pine occupies exposed ridges and drier soils. See [[Indicator Significance of Upland Forest Genera]].
- **Study geography:** The basin is a large enclosed volcanic and lacustrine basin, not a valley. Remnant Lake Texcoco lies at about 2235–2236 m, with ancient beaches up to about 2263 m. Mexico City stands at about 2236 m. Sediments extend under the city to several hundred meters.
- **Scope:** The paper treats only relatively shallow sediments correlated with human culture. Deeper cores (one of 68 m, another of over 70 m under the Palacio de Bellas Artes) are reserved for later reports. The study is framed as part of a broader series on palynology in southern North America, contrasting with glaciated regions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climatic and hydrological evidence**
- Alternating arid-climate caliches and dark organic soils indicate pronounced climatic pulsations in the valley (Bryan, 1948; Arellano, 1951).
- Glacial recessions and readvances on the slopes of Iztaccihuatl are recorded (de Terra, 1949).
- Lake level changes give general confirmation but must account for physiographic factors.

**Archaeological sites and their setting**
- Early and Middle Archaic occupation of upland beaches (Zacatenco, about 2242–2247 m).
- The Late Archaic site of [[El Tepalcate]] was occupied at low lake level (about 2235–2236 m) and later buried under a volcanic ash fall.
- Nahua settlements on promontories (Culhuacán, Tenochtitlan, Tlatelolco) coincided with restored water levels (about 2239 m) and dark organic soil formation.
- The [[Chinampa]] system of raised garden beds, canals, and aquatic fisheries supported the Nahua economy. Maguey terraces controlled upland erosion.

**Human impacts over time**
- Teotihuacán's heavy erosion and possible deforestation, linked to lime burning, were attributed by Vaillant (1941) to the city's ultimate collapse, with Cook (1949) documenting erosion and population pressure.
- Under Spanish rule from 1521, plow, livestock, and mining intensified soil erosion. Severe floods recurred, and the Tajo de Nochistongo (completed 1609) drained Lake Zumpango at about 2243 m.
- Thorough drainage, completed about 1900, lowered lake beds, produced dust storms (23 counted at one time in the Lake Chalco bed), and sharply reduced lake fisheries and chinampa horticulture.

**Pre-Archaic man**
- The [[Tepexpan Man]] and Peñón Man skeletal finds, in an elephant-bearing stratum, are considered by the author not to be dismissible, though pollen checks at Tepexpan were negative.

## Key Findings

- **Climate–culture correlation:** Pollen sequences show alternating moist (high oak) and dry (high pine) intervals. Moist intervals are labeled A, C, and the Nahua period, and dry intervals are labeled B, B1 (or Bs), B2, and D. These are correlated with archaeological horizons.
- **Archaic culture:** Flourished under moist conditions from about 2000–1500 to 500–400 B.C. on high beaches and dark organic soils. A dry interval with very low lake level and volcanic ash forced its move northward.
- **Volcanic ash marker:** A coarse, sandy, solidified ash layer overlies the Late Archaic horizon at El Tepalcate and is continuous across the eastern basin. It is treated as a stratigraphic key marking the end of the Late Archaic.
- **Teotihuacán:** Founded on higher ground (about 2266 m, roughly 30 km north of El Tepalcate) during a dry period. Its water supply probably came from ground water and springs protected by surrounding forests. Collapse around 800–900 A.D. followed deforestation and erosion, even though humid conditions had returned.
- **Nahua culture:** Occupied the basin from about 900 A.D. during humid conditions. It was based on chinampa horticulture, fishing, hunting, and terraced agriculture, and its forests were relatively well conserved.
- **Spanish and modern impacts:** Deforestation, overgrazing, floods, and drainage reduced the water economy that had sustained earlier peoples.
- **Pollen preservation limits:** Exposed soils, beaches, and volcanic ash contain little or no pollen. Archaeological layers are rich in charcoal. Several sites (Tlatilco, Copilco, Cuicuilco, Tlatelolco's lower layers) yielded no or minimal pollen.
- **Methodological note:** Fir pollen is underrepresented; older, more mineralized sediments required stronger chemical pretreatment than younger ones.
- **Conclusion:** Pollen analysis is consistent with soil profiles and former lake levels, and it supports a strong link between climate, water availability, and cultural development in the basin.
