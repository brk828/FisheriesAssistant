# Guide for Surveying Phreatophyte Vegetation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phreatophyte-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Department of Agriculture Forest Service handbook (Agriculture Handbook No. 266, May 1964), prepared by J. S. Horton, T. W. Robinson and H. R. McDonald, sets out a standardized method for surveying [[Phreatophyte]] vegetation: plants whose roots reach ground water and transpire large volumes of water in arid and semiarid western streams, flood plains and playas. The guide presents a seven-step, random-sample survey built on [[Line Intercept Method]] sampling, with sample sizes set by a [[Nomograph]] and checked by standard-error statistics. Its purpose is to provide the vegetation data needed to plan treatments that salvage water lost to uneconomic vegetation use. The work was sponsored by the Phreatophyte Subcommittee of the [[Pacific Southwest Inter-Agency Committee]].

Note: the document is a land-vegetation survey guide and contains no fish or aquatic-fauna data; the taxonomic tags above reflect the template default and do not describe the content.

## Background & Description

Water losses from stream channels, flood plains, bottom lands and delta areas are serious in the arid West. Besides evaporation, heavy losses result from transpiration wherever ground water is within reach of roots. Dense phreatophyte thickets also reduce stream velocity during overflow, raise channel beds with sediment, and create flood hazards, yet the same vegetation may have recreational, grazing, wildlife and bank-protection value.

The handbook is a practical field guide rather than a research report. It does not describe how water loss is computed from vegetation; it cites [[Gatewood Foliage Volume Method]] (Gatewood et al., 1950) as the most frequently applied approach, while noting that its validity has been challenged (Colman, 1953). The survey measures:

- Species composition and relative importance
- Percent crown cover
- Average height of trees and shrubs
- Crown depth
- Understory (ground cover) amount

Precision statistics are applied only to crown cover; height and crown depth are reported without precision estimates.

The model survey used throughout is a hypothetical but data-based example. Its vegetation types are dominated by [[Fivestamen Tamarisk]], [[Seepwillow]], [[Arrowweed]], [[Mesquite]] and [[Fourwing Saltbush]], with the [[Fremont Cottonwood]] appearing in sample lines.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flood plains and reservoir deltas:** Shallow water tables support a wide band of vegetation, often several miles across. Fivestamen tamarisk now dominates many southwestern flood plains (e.g., Gila River near Buckeye, Ariz.), with seepwillow, cottonwood, mesquite, arrowweed and willow. Saltbush and pickleweed occur on alkaline soils, and Bermudagrass, inland saltgrass and alkali sacaton are often abundant. Delta vegetation is much the same but on shallower water tables, and both areas are subject to periodic inundation.
- **Playas:** Wet playas have zonal vegetation, with the shallowest-rooted, most salt-tolerant species nearest the playa. A typical Great Basin sequence upslope is pickleweed, saltgrass, [[Black Greasewood]], rabbitbrush and sagebrush, with willow along the margin where water is not too saline.
- **Streambanks:** Perennial mountain streams support alders, cottonwoods and willows. Intermittent streams support evergreen oaks, sycamore, cottonwood and willow. In the Southwest these grade into desert washes dominated by deeply rooted mesquite.
- **Water relations:** Survey data serve as the basis for estimating potential water savings from vegetation treatment.

No aquatic animals, fish habitat, or water-chemistry measurements are reported beyond soil salinity context and water-table depth.

## Key Findings

**Survey procedure (seven steps)**
1. Determine the survey area and breakdowns; assemble aerial photographs and train workers on species.
2. Prepare preliminary office vegetation maps from aerial photographs, refined by aerial observation (helicopter preferred; fixed-wing flown 50–100 ft above vegetation).
3. Determine the number of sampling lines using a preliminary field survey and the nomograph.
4. Develop a random sampling system.
5. Collect field data on randomly selected lines and permanently mark them.
6. Determine data reliability with standard statistics.
7. Summarize the data for the study's purposes.

**Sampling design**
- Line-intercept sampling uses 100-ft lines in open cover and 50-ft lines in dense or shrubby vegetation. Crown intercepts are recorded to the nearest foot, and the sum equals percent cover on a 100-ft line.
- Preliminary lines: three or more open and three or more dense lines per type. The model used six preliminary lines per each of seven types.
- Minimum of 15 lines per type; 15–25 lines are increased by 10 percent; 30 or more are needed for the normal-approximation criterion.
- Random points are located with a three-digit scheme (overlay, east-west position, north-south position) from a random-number table.
- Dense brush with limited access: sample along access roads, starting about 100 ft into the brush. Edges of clearings must not be used as sample sites.
- Dense brush without access: aerial ocular estimates calibrated against four or five standard test plots (about one acre). Turner and Skibitzke (1952) covered 30 square miles in 5 days on the Salt River flood plain, and aerial densities matched ground transects closely.

**Model survey results**
- Survey area: about 7 miles of wide flood plain, mapped into seven types and subtypes after aerial refinement (tamarisk types merged into one; tamarisk-seepwillow type added).
- Nomograph example: a type with preliminary cover of 35–60 percent required 46 lines for ±5 percent, 15 for ±10 percent, 9 for ±15 percent and 6 for ±20 percent precision.
- The ideal 10 percent precision needed 263 lines; budget limits allowed 160 lines.
- Final allocation: tamarisk 40 lines (raised for importance), tamarisk-seepwillow 20, dense arrowweed 20, open arrowweed 30, dense mesquite 15, open mesquite 15, saltbush 20.
- Tamarisk (40 lines, mean crown cover 34.8%? as reported): twice SE was 9.6 percent of the mean, meeting the 10 percent goal. Calculation indicated 37 lines were needed.
- Open arrowweed (30 lines): 15.5 percent versus a 13 percent goal, not achieved; about 40 lines needed.
- Dense arrowweed: 11.8 percent versus 12 percent goal, achieved.
- Mesquite: open 14.2 percent versus 20 percent goal; dense 10.5 percent versus 13 percent goal; both achieved.
- Saltbush (20 lines): 21.9 percent versus 20 percent goal, not achieved; 24 lines needed.
- Tamarisk-seepwillow: individual species reached 17.0 and 16.3 percent; total cover 8.7 percent, showing mixed covers need more lines for co-dominant species individually.

**Worked example**
- Standard deviation of crown cover (14 lines, mean 80%) was 14.13 (long method) and 14.18 (shortcut). Standard error was ±3.65, and twice SE was 9.1 percent of the mean.
- Lines needed for 10 percent precision: about 13.

**Reporting and future surveys**
- Permanently mark line ends with painted metal posts or stakes; reference them to bench marks or section corners.
- Average species height over a type as sum of (height × feet) divided by total feet. Example: 2,653 ÷ 219 ft gives 12.1 ft for tamarisk.
- Area per type comes from dot grids (1 dot ≈ 0.4 acre at 4 inches to the mile) or planimeter. Results are reported as acres of type with mean cover, height and crown depth.
- Survey data are sent to the Phreatophyte Subcommittee or the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station (Tempe, Ariz.), using the standard inventory form (Figure 8).

**Scale and mapping standards**
- Detailed surveys use 6 inches to the mile (1:10,000); less intensive surveys use 4 inches to the mile (1:15,840).
- Smallest mapped area: one-eighth inch on a side (about 0.25 acre at 6 in/mile; about 0.67 acre at 4 in/mile).

**Sources of error and cautions**
- Line-intercept measurement minimizes observer differences, but all sampling is subject to individual observation limits.
- Minor species too sparse for statistical analysis are assumed to use water similar to the dominant species.
- Herbaceous measurements are not needed for water-loss estimates unless livestock use is a management factor.
