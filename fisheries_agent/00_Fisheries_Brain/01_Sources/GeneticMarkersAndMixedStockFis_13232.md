# Genetic Markers and Mixed Stock Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by Fred Utter and Nils Ryman (*Fisheries*, Vol. 18, No. 8, August 1993), examines how genetic markers can estimate the composition of mixed fisheries, where biologically distinct groups of fish are harvested together. The authors argue that such analysis requires baseline genetic data from contributing populations and an adequate sample from the mixture. Applications have been concentrated in [[Pacific Salmon]] and other [[Anadromous Fishes]], while marine species generally show less genetic subdivision and are therefore poorer candidates. The paper recommends the term [[Mixed Stock Analysis]] (MSA) over [[Genetic Stock Identification]] (GSI) and advises that molecular methods beyond [[Protein Electrophoresis]] be used mainly where protein data cannot resolve differences. The text also originated as a presentation to an FAO expert consultation on aquatic genetic resources (Rome, November 1992).

## Background & Description

- **Management problem:** Mixed fisheries complicate management through [[By-Catch]] (e.g., Pacific halibut in pollock trawls; *Sebastes* species grouped in one category in the North Pacific) and through species-level ambiguity, such as the [[Lake Malawi Chambo]] tilapia complex (*Oreochromis* spp.), where the number of species is unknown and morphology is ambiguous.
- **Intraspecific mixing:** Anadromous salmonids from isolated, genetically distinct populations are often harvested as a mixed stock at sea, before returning to natal streams. Managers need to identify origins to avoid overharvesting weaker populations.
- **Traditional marks:** Fin clipping, tagging, and freeze branding have been used, but have drawbacks: high cost, reduced survival, minimum size requirements, mark loss, and limited lifespan of marks. They cannot be applied where species are not yet clearly identified. Natural marks (meristics, scale and osteological characters, parasite patterns) have also been used; [[Parker et al. 1990]] reviewed these.
- **Genetic markers:** Interest in genetic characters has grown since the 1960s. Over the last 25 years, the ability to identify polymorphic genes has expanded, supporting a growing set of statistical procedures for stock mixtures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Genetic structure varies with life history.** Marine fishes generally show less subdivision than freshwater fishes, with anadromous species intermediate. Variation relates to inshore versus offshore habitat, viviparity versus pelagic larvae, fecundity, and larval dispersal.
- **Degree of subdivision in marine species (Table 2):** The authors classify species into three groups.
  - *High:* Black perch (*Embiotoca jacksoni*), with local population divergence comparable to that among congeneric species.
  - *Moderate:* Northern anchovy, Pacific herring (two major races, with gametic phase disequilibrium in the interracial zone), Atlantic herring (no east-west divergence, but distinct fjord populations in northern Norway), Pacific cod, Atlantic cod (Baltic versus Atlantic divergence), Pacific and Atlantic hake, Spanish mackerel, [[King Mackerel]] (eastern and western spawning groups), red drum, and yellowfin sole.
  - *Low:* Sardines (*Sardinops* spp.), South African hake, skipjack tuna, albacore tuna, halibut (*Hippoglossus* spp.), and anglerfish (*Lophius vomerinus*), with no detectable substructure in most cases.
- **Intermingling zones:** Even in species with little range-wide structure, genetic methods are feasible where distinct populations meet. Examples include Pacific herring in the Bering Sea/North Pacific overlap and king mackerel in the northern Gulf of Mexico, where intermediate allele frequencies indicate mixing.
- **Hybridization and cryptic species:** Steelhead and coastal cutthroat trout hybridize naturally in Puget Sound streams. Two *Carcharhinus* sharks in northern Australian waters were shown to be distinct species through fixed and nearly fixed differences.

## Key Findings

**Requirements for mixed stock analysis**
- Five requirements are listed: knowledge of potential contributing groups; genetic differences among those groups; the ability to identify some of these differences; reliable baseline data (genotype and allele frequencies at one or preferably multiple loci); and similar data from a catch sample, analysed with statistical methods.
- Almost all mixed fishery work to date uses [[Allozyme Markers]] from protein electrophoresis, which is inexpensive and allows large numbers of individuals and loci to be processed. Direct DNA methods (mitochondrial and nuclear) are expanding the marker range.

**Analytical procedures**
- *Fixed alleles:* Species-level mixtures with no shared alleles at some loci can be resolved unambiguously, as for rainbow trout versus coastal cutthroat trout (nearly fixed differences at two loci) and rockfish larvae and juveniles.
- *Nonfixed loci:* Within species, populations differ by allele frequency. The authors give a worked example: with baseline allele frequencies of 0.2 and 0.6 and a mixture frequency of 0.3, contributions are 0.75 and 0.25. The method assumes only two contributors, known true frequencies, and no sampling error; in practice, these assumptions rarely hold, and combining multiple loci improves precision.
- *Multi-population mixtures:* Estimators based on the [[EM Algorithm]] (maximum likelihood) have been developed and validated. The authors recommend against using the term GSI, because "stock" is ambiguous, the method estimates rather than identifies proportions, is not limited to genetic traits, and serves uses such as paternity, gene flow, and population size estimation.
- *Missing baseline groups:* Bias arises if major contributors are absent from baseline data. Simulated resampling of the mixture can help detect "out-populations."
- *Detecting mixtures without baseline data:* Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations (heterozygote deficiency) and tests for gametic phase (linkage) disequilibrium among loci can indicate population mixing; the latter is more sensitive when many locus pairs are examined.
- *Hierarchical structure:* When genetic variation is geographically hierarchical, estimating regional contributions may suffice, and adequate regional representation in baseline data may be enough.

**Salmonid applications (Table 1)**
- Origin estimates for chinook salmon fisheries vary by location, time, and capture method. Weekly monitoring tracked five stock groups in Puget Sound chum fisheries.
- [[Brodziak et al. 1992]] validated MSA on 220 coded wire-tagged fish from California and Oregon fisheries, using 35 polymorphic loci and baselines from 37 populations. Origins were accurately assigned to five major stock groups in a blind test.
- MSA supports in-season management of a Columbia River chinook fishery.
- Combining parasite data with a few polymorphic loci brought sockeye salmon estimates to acceptable precision.
- Other applications include estimating juvenile chum salmon population size using genetically marked releases (Seeb et al. 1986), evaluating an enhancement program where streamside incubator boxes were found ineffective (Seeb et al. 1990), identifying ancestral origins of lake trout repopulated from multiple sources in Lake Ontario (Marsden et al. 1989), and assessing relative reproductive success of restocked lake trout strains.

**Marine species and genetic marking**
- Marine species with low subdivision are poor candidates for natural-population mixture analysis, though this may change with extended sampling or discovery of genetic variants.
- *Genetic marking* can create detectable differences by altering allele frequencies in cultured stocks. Culturable marine species, such as [[Atlantic Cod]] released in Norway to augment declining populations (Svasand et al. 1990), are strong candidates, because marked fish stand out against a homogeneous wild background.
- Mandatory genetic marking of released cultured stocks is recommended as the only route to empirical data on long-term interactions with wild populations (Hindar et al. 1991).

**Conclusions**
- The authors emphasize that the current period offers unprecedented ability to distinguish populations and manage mixed stock fisheries.
- At present, cost effectiveness, ease of application, and sample size requirements mean that new molecular techniques should generally be used only when protein electrophoresis cannot resolve differences.
