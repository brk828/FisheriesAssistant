# Five New Species of Vejovis Scorpions from Cuatro Cienegas, Coahuila, Mexico

#arachnology #scorpiones #vejovis

## Executive Summary

This 1968 taxonomic paper by [[Stanley C. Williams]], published in the *Occasional Papers of the [[California Academy of Sciences]]* (No. 68), describes five new scorpion species of the genus [[Vejovis]] from the [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico. The descriptions rest on 227 specimens collected from 1965 to 1967. The author also notes that other specimens representing three genera and three families are held back pending more material, and suggests the basin may hold one of the richest scorpion faunas in North America. Four of the five species are described as endemic to the basin. The exception is *V. coahuilae*, which has a record from Pecos County, Texas.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Cienegas area is a small, horseshoe-shaped valley of roughly 40 × 30 km in central Coahuila, enclosed by ranges of the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]]. The surrounding mountains are steep, bare [[Cretaceous]] limestone. The valley sits at about 700 to 750 m elevation and has [[Chihuahuan Desert]] vegetation. It is known to biologists for its varied aquatic habitats and drainages, which include several fully internal drainage systems and an external drainage toward the [[Rio Grande]]. The basin is also noted for rich snail and fish faunas with many endemic species.

Measurements follow standard scorpion systematics, taken with a microscope and calibrated eyepiece micrometer. Diagnostic characters include:
- Total body length (carapace, mesosoma, metasoma and telson)
- Pectinal tooth and middle lamellae counts
- An index of hirsuteness, counting large reddish hairs on the inferior metasomal keels, vesicle, fulcra and middle lamellae
- Keel granulation and serration, and cheliceral and pedipalp finger structure

Specimens were mainly collected with [[Ultraviolet Detection]] at night, with some taken by [[Rock Rolling]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's habitat comments show a clear preference among the species for sandy and dune substrates:

- **Sandy dunes:** *V. pallidus* reaches its greatest densities in dune communities and is almost certainly a burrowing form, since surface cover is absent in the densest areas. *V. cazieri* also favors sandy habitats with fine, silty sedimentary soils.
- **Gypsum dunes:** The white gypsum dunes 13 km southwest of Cuatro Cienegas are the type locality of *V. gilvus*. Specimens of *V. minckleyi* from these dunes were distinctly paler, with reduced dark markings.
- **Rocky and cave habitats:** *V. minckleyi* occurred in both sandy and rocky habitats. The holotype came from inside a cave.

Co-collected arachnids and other taxa were not described in the paper. The species are tied to the basin's unusual geology and drainage history.

## Key Findings

**Specimen base and taxonomy**
- Five new species were described from 227 specimens, collected 1965–1967 and sampled from about 10 localities in the basin.
- *V. coahuilae* is the only species with a known record outside the basin (Pecos County, Texas).

**Species summaries**

| Species | Type locality | Notable diagnostic features | Pectinal teeth (males / females) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *V. gilvus* | White gypsum dunes, 13 km SW of Cuatro Cienegas | Small, pale yellow; faint markings; holotype 34.4 mm | 16–18 / 11–13 |
| *V. pallidus* | Dune community, 0.5 km SW of town | Large, pale yellow; holotype 58.1 mm; placed in subgenus *Paruroctonus* | 25–31 / 17–20 |
| *V. cazieri* | 0.5 km SW of town | Brownish-yellow with dark pedipalp fingers and caudal segments; holotype 45.8 mm | 16–19 / 14–16 |
| *V. coahuilae* | 0.5 km SW of town | Medium-sized; dark mesosomal stripes; related to *V. spinigerus* | 17–21 / 13–16 |
| *V. minckleyi* | Cave wall and rocky site, San Marcos Mountain area | Extremely large; uniform light yellow; holotype 62.5 mm; male pectine has 32 teeth | 32 (male) |

**Distribution and abundance**
- *V. gilvus*: found in 4 of 10 localities sampled. It was not abundant at any site. Specimens were mostly adult males.
- *V. pallidus*: found in 3 of 10 habitats and was numerically dominant in two of them. Males outnumbered females by more than 2:1. Known only from the basin.
- *V. cazieri*: found in 3 of 10 localities, and was numerically subdominant in one. Males outnumbered females by more than 4:1 (22 males, 4 females). Known only from the basin.
- *V. coahuilae*: the most widespread species in the paper for which a distribution is reported in detail. Found in 9 of 10 localities, in both sandy and rocky habitats. Females were encountered significantly more often than males.
- *V. minckleyi*: known from only two specimens, the holotype (from a cave) and one paratype (under a rock on powdery soil). A species-level distribution could not be established.

**Sexual dimorphism and variation**
- Pectinal tooth counts and the presence of genital papillae are the main sex-distinguishing characters across species.
- In *V. pallidus*, the paratopotypes (88 total; 64 males, 24 females) showed carapace-length modes of 5.6 mm for males and 7.2 mm for females.
- Juveniles generally show less swollen pedipalp hands and more contrasting dark pigment.

**Etymology**
- *V. gilvus*: named for its light yellowish coloration. *V. pallidus*: named for its pale yellow coloration.
- *V. cazieri*: honors Professor Mont A. Cazier of Arizona State University.
- *V. coahuilae*: named for the Mexican state where it was first collected.
- *V. minckleyi*: honors Professor [[Wendell L. Minckley]] of Arizona State University, who studied the Cuatro Cienegas basin and collected many of the first known scorpion specimens from the area.

**Significance**
- The paper concludes that the basin likely hosts one of North America's richest scorpion faunas, and that more specimens from other genera and families are awaiting description.
