# Symposium on Rare and Endangered Fishes of the Death Valley System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes a symposium held November 18-19, 1969 at [[Death Valley National Monument]], California, attended by 42 persons from federal and state agencies, universities, and conservation organizations. Coordinated by E. P. Pister of the California Department of Fish and Game and prepared by Robert Rush Miller for the [[National Park Service]], it reviews the status of the 11 native fish species of the [[Death Valley System]], ranks threatened forms by priority, and proposes refuge-based recovery actions. Human activity, mainly groundwater pumping and habitat destruction, is identified as the cause of the losses already recorded.

## Background & Description

The Death Valley System comprises the drainage basins of the Owens, Mohave, and Amargosa Rivers and Pahrump Valley in California and Nevada. Its 11 species fall into three families: minnows (1 dace and 2 chubs), suckers (1 species), and killifishes (5 pupfishes in the genus *Cyprinodon* and 2 killifishes in the genus *Empetrichthys*).

The report adopts [[IUCN Red List Criteria]] definitions. "Endangered" describes a species whose survival is unlikely without special protective measures. "Rare" describes a species not under immediate threat but present in so few numbers or so restricted a habitat that it could quickly disappear. The authors note that such status assignments remain partly arbitrary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Losses:** The [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] and the Shoshone pupfish (*Cyprinodon nevadensis shoshone*) are believed extinct. The status of the [[Tecopa Pupfish]] is uncertain but it may also be gone. Other *C. nevadensis* populations are badly depleted.
- **Critical species:** The [[Pahrump Killifish]] survives as a single population of a handful of individuals, threatening an entire genus.
- **Restricted habitat:** The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] lives in a habitat about the size of a backyard swimming pool, with an estimated 200 to 700 individuals. A 1968 [[United States Geological Survey]] report indicates its aquifer could be seriously altered within one year of pumping.
- **Stable areas:** Populations below Tecopa, especially in Death Valley proper, were reported in good condition at the time of writing.
- **Threats from development:** Agricultural pumping and land clearing at [[Ash Meadows]] have drastically altered aquatic and terrestrial habitats. The [[Amargosa River Project]] of the Bureau of Reclamation could affect springs within Death Valley National Monument, including the only Amargosa River pupfish population inside the Monument.
- **Exotic fishes:** [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and black mollies are established at several sites and are treated as predators to be removed from refuges.

## Key Findings

**Priority listing (most to least threatened):**
1. [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*): the most critically threatened fish, as the genus is in jeopardy.
2. [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): restricted habitat and a nearby proposed well.
3. [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*): nearly lost when its habitat dried in summer 1969. Recovery is underway and it may be removed from the world endangered list by the end of 1971.
4. [[Ash Meadows Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*): many populations lost, with others threatened by pumping.
5. [[Mohave Chub]] (*Gila mohavensis*): pure populations confined to Lake Tuendae at Zzyzx Resort.
6. [[Tecopa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*): status uncertain and needs taxonomic work.
7. [[Warm Spring Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*): very restricted range at Ash Meadows.

**Undetermined status (Owens Valley):** Owens chub (*Gila* sp.), speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), and Owens sucker (*Catostomus* sp.) have declined markedly but were not prioritized because their population sizes are unknown.

**Refuge plans (selected):**
- Pahrump killifish: enlarge spring seepage at Manse Ranch, develop a refuge at Saline Valley, and build an artesian-fed refuge near Mt. Wheeler, Nevada.
- Devils Hole pupfish: create a refuge at Upper Warm Spring, Saline Valley, and screen out mosquitofish, which must not be transferred there. Check additional springs in Nevada and at Gerlach.
- Owens pupfish: complete the 21-acre [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] in Fish Slough after removing exotic fishes, then reintroduce native species. Build a backup refuge at BLM Spring.
- Ash Meadows pupfish: seek purchase of Big Spring, remove exotic fishes, and rehabilitate Jack Rabbit Spring if feasible. Maintain stocks at UCLA.
- Mohave chub: a two-acre pond at the South Coast Botanic Garden, plus sites at San Felipe Creek, China Lake, and Paradise Spa, Las Vegas.
- Warm Spring pupfish: fenced refugium at School Spring completed. Broaden it to Mexican and Scruggs Springs, the entire known distribution, with a habitat management plan.

**Introduction guidance:** Past experiments were poor, with only 2 of 14 introductions of three species successful. All introductions should be monitored until establishment is assured, and pupfish should be stocked in late spring or early summer.

**Conclusion:** The symposium concluded that the survival of these fishes depends on protected refuges, restricted exotic species, and controlled groundwater pumping. Most recovery actions depended on Bureau of Land Management and state agency cooperation.
