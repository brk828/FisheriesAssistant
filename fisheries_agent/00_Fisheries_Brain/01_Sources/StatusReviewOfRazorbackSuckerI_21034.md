# Status Review of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 status review, prepared by Kevin R. Bestgen of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Upper Colorado Regional Office), compiles historic and present information on the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was once widespread in warmwater reaches from Wyoming to Mexico but has declined sharply. Remaining concentrations are largest in [[Lake Mohave]] (Lower Basin) and the upper [[Green River]] (Upper Basin). The report documents distribution and status, life history, reasons for decline, and current research and management, and closes with research and management recommendations within the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. Non-biological and legal questions, including the proposed federal listing, are explicitly outside its scope.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Xyrauchen* is one of three monotypic genera in the family Catostomidae. The species was described as *Catostomus texanus* by Abbott (1861) from a specimen labeled "Colorado and New rivers," Arizona. An Uncompahgre specimen was later shown by Hubbs and Miller (1953) to be a hybrid of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and razorback sucker.
- **Diagnostic features:** An abruptly rising bony dorsal keel behind the head (source of the common name) distinguishes it from other catostomids. Primary dorsal rays are usually 14-15 (range 12-17), anal rays 7, total vertebrae 45-47, lateral scales 68-87, and first-arch gill rakers 44-50.
- **Size:** Adults reach about 1 m TL and 5-6 kg but are more commonly 400-700 mm TL and under 3 kg.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Breeding males are bright yellow and orange ventrally with pronounced tuberculation. Females are larger and heavier on average, and may show a low, broad keel that can be mistaken for hybrid traits.
- **Feeding morphology:** Pharyngeal teeth in a comb-like arrangement and heavily ossified caudal skeleton are interpreted as adaptations to benthic feeding and strong currents.
- **Intraspecific variation:** No studies have addressed it. A qualitative comparison of Upper Colorado and Gila specimens found them similar.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and status.** The 1922 Colorado River Compact divides the basin at Lee Ferry, Arizona, into the Upper (UCRB) and Lower (LCRB) basins.

- **LCRB:** Historically abundant downstream of present Lake Mead, particularly around Yuma. Today it persists mainly in man-made lentic habitats: Lake Mohave, Lake Mead, Lake Havasu, and Senator Wash Reservoir. A spring 1989 multiple-census estimate put Lake Mohave at about 60,000 fish (range 15,000-120,000). Other LCRB populations are small and declining. The Gila River drainage has no substantiated wild records since 1926 for young fish, and recent captures are hatchery-produced.
- **UCRB:** Regularly found only in the upper Green River, Utah, and lower Yampa River, Colorado. Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 948 fish (95% CI 758-1,138) in the upper Green River, based on 1980-1988 capture-recapture data. Small concentrations occur in the Dirty Devil, San Juan, and Colorado arms of Lake Powell. The Colorado River near Grand Junction, once a relatively large concentration (234 adults collected 1974-1976), has declined sharply; only 12 were collected in a 53-km reach from 1985 to 1988.

**Habitat.** Warmwater mainstream reaches and associated low-velocity habitats (backwaters, sloughs, oxbows, gravel pits) were integral historically. Off-channel habitats remain important for spawning staging and larval development. Water temperature is critical: Bulkley and Pimentel (1983) reported optimal summer temperatures of 22-25 °C. Winter overwintering adults used deep, low-velocity areas with silt or sand substrate.

**Reproduction.** Spawning occurs over gravel or cobble in shallow (typically 0.3-2 m) areas. In Lake Mohave, peak spawning was January-March at 10-12 °C, and males outnumbered females 1.2-3.6:1. In the Green River, ripe fish were collected mid-April to mid-June, at 14-16 °C (range 9-20 °C), with spawning males outnumbering females about 2.5:1. Hatching time falls with rising temperature (19.4 days at 10 °C, 11.1 days at 15 °C, 6.8 days at 20 °C), and hatching success is highest at fertilization-matched temperatures.

**Movement.** The population appears mostly sedentary, with a migratory component that shows strong fidelity to spawning areas. In the upper Green River, 66 of 88 recaptured or radiotracked fish were local. Nineteen migratory fish moved 53-190 km, all during the spawning season.

**Diet.** Larvae feed on phytoplankton and zooplankton in lentic habitats and on chironomid larvae in lotic habitats. Lacustrine adults are planktivorous with occasional benthic feeding. Riverine adults eat mainly benthic invertebrates (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Chironomidae) along with algae and detritus.

**Disease.** In Lake Mohave, Minckley (1983) found 14% of fish blind in one eye and 6.8% blind in both. Bozek et al. (1984) reported injury or disease in one or both eyes for 32.3% of females and 28% of males (single eye). Flagg (1982) judged the pathogens found in five adults not a factor in the species' decline.

**Predators.** Historically, Colorado squawfish and roundtail chub were potential predators. Today, non-native fishes (channel catfish, common carp, green sunfish, flathead catfish) and birds (cormorants) are major sources of mortality, especially for young life stages.

**Hybridization.** Hybrids with flannelmouth sucker are widespread across the sympatric range, and Holden (1973) found hybrids nearly as common as pure adults in the UCRB (40 vs. 53). Buth et al. (1987) found low-level introgression in hatchery and Lake Mohave stocks. Some hybrid identifications may reflect misidentified low-keeled females.

**Importance.** The species was a significant aboriginal food fish and was commercially harvested for human, furbearer, livestock, and fertilizer use into the 20th century. A fishery existed in Saguaro Lake as recently as 1949.

## Key Findings

**Reasons for decline.** The report attributes decline to several interacting factors, which are difficult to separate:
- Mainstream dam construction, reservoir inundation, and altered flow and temperature regimes (cold hypolimnetic releases).
- Channelization and loss of off-channel, seasonally flooded habitat, which is needed for larval growth and pre-spawning staging.
- Introduction of more than 50 non-native fish species, many of them piscivorous.
- Dewatering and habitat degradation in the Gila drainage from 1905 onward.

**Recruitment failure.** The most consistent finding is that recruitment to the juvenile stage is severely limited:
- In Lake Mohave, large numbers of larvae are produced annually, but juveniles have been extremely rare since the early 1950s. Predation on larvae was judged the factor most likely limiting recruitment.
- Experiments support this: larvae in predator-free cages grew to 30 mm TL or more, versus disappearance at 10-12 mm TL in the reservoir (Langhorst 1987). Predator-free backwaters also produced better survival.
- Marsh and Brooks (1989) documented predation by ictalurids that eliminated stocked fish in the Gila River.

**Age and longevity.** Lake Mohave fish are presumed old. McCarthy and Minckley (1987) estimated ages of 24-44 years from otoliths, and 89% of 70 specimens hatched before or during the construction and filling of Lake Mohave. Growth in the UCRB is very slow (average 2.2 mm/yr in Tyus 1987).

**Stocking results.** Stocking has produced few recaptures. Only 118 fish were recovered from more than 12,000,000 stocked fish of all sizes in the Gila River through 1988. Hatchery-reared fish grown in predator-free ponds, however, reached 422 mm TL and 1,088 g after about 2.6 years (Osmundson and Kaeding 1989b), suggesting that grow-out ponds can produce fish large enough to escape most predators.

**Reproductive potential.** Adults are long-lived and fecund. Minckley (1983) estimated 100,800 total ova (74,600-144,000) for five Lake Mohave females. McAda and Wydoski (1980) estimated 46,740 ova (27,614-76,576) for ten Green River females.

**Current programs.**
- Brood stock is collected from the Colorado River (Colorado), Lake Powell arms, and the Green River, and held at the Colorado River Fishes Propagation and Experiment Station, Ouray, Utah (18,635 larvae produced in four years since 1987).
- Reintroduction in the UCRB has been limited to the Green River (about 2,000 juveniles in 1988 and about 5,000 in 1990).
- In the LCRB, Arizona (from 1981) and California (from 1986) run 10-year stocking programs.
- A genetic study funded by the Recovery Implementation Program is underway.

**Key recommendations.**
1. **Genetic diversity:** Resolve the question and preserve existing stocks, even if current methods show a single homogeneous population.
2. **Recruitment failure:** Identify causes through controlled experiments. Predation by non-native fishes is the main suspect in the LCRB; in the UCRB, predation, competition, habitat loss (floodplain inundation, cold releases, warm backwaters), and combined factors are all proposed.
3. **Movement and habitat:** Relate movements and habitat use to reproduction, flows, and migration barriers, with experiments in Lake Mohave and the upper Green River.
4. **Life history:** Collect basic riverine life-history data and resolve the contradictory temperature-hatching evidence.
5. **Brood stock and refugia:** Capture brood stock from all known areas and establish refugia populations.
6. **Reintroduction:** Refine stocking size, number, and timing. Select predator-free sites with suitable habitat and monitoring capacity.
7. **Monitoring:** Continue monitoring wild populations and survey remnant habitat (Gunnison River, Colorado River above Grand Valley Diversion).

The report stresses that stocking large numbers without identifying critical limiting factors "will not recover the species," and calls for controlled experimentation to identify one or a few key limiting factors.
