## Executive Summary
This 1986 Research Information Bulletin summarizes a Utah Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit evaluation of flooded gravel pits near Grand Junction, Colorado, as grow-out ponds for rearing the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The study tested whether extensive aquaculture in low-cost ponds could raise juvenile squawfish to a larger, more river-ready size before release, analogous to established grow-out practices for [[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]]. Results showed highly variable survival (0–60%) driven substantially by predation from resident [[Largemouth Bass]], but growth rates in a predator-free pond were more than double those documented in river or hatchery settings, suggesting strong potential for accelerated culture if predation and other mortality factors are controlled.

## Background & Description
- **Stocking size:** Squawfish stocked at 45–145 mm long.
- **Forage-sized fish threshold:** Defined as <70 mm long; included 13 species, dominated by [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Green Sunfish]].
- **Target production size:** Under favorable pond conditions, a 250-mm-long fish could be produced in two years, versus four years under current hatchery/river conditions.
- **Comparison release size:** Current hatchery-reared fish are stocked into the river at only 40–60 mm long after a few months of rearing, a substantially smaller and more predation-vulnerable size class.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Study system:** Five gravel-pit ponds (0.9–3.2 ha, depth 1.7–3.7 m) adjacent to but hydrologically disconnected from the [[Colorado River]], with little submerged vegetation or cover.
- **Predator community:** [[Largemouth Bass]] were the dominant predator in four of five ponds; [[Black Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], and [[Channel Catfish]] also present in varying abundance.
- **Prey/forage base:** Natural forage community of 13 small fish species (<70 mm) allowed squawfish to learn predatory feeding behavior on live fish prior to release — a proposed advantage over hatchery rearing on artificial diets.
- **Stocking regime:** Squawfish (45–145 mm) stocked at ~3,100 fish/ha in October 1983, October 1984, and July 1984; monitored for 2–6 months depending on stocking season.
- **Parasitism:** Observed infection by anchorworm (*Lernea* sp.) and tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus opsarichthydis*), potentially compounding mortality alongside predation and poor winter water quality.
- **Thermal growth threshold:** Growth calculated to begin at approximately 13°C; ponds supplied 1.8× the degree-days available in the adjacent river, indicating a substantially more thermally favorable growing environment than the river itself.
- **Release mechanism:** Proposed liberation method involves notching dikes separating ponds from the river, allowing direct fish passage without additional handling.

## Key Findings
- Survival of stocked squawfish ranged from 0% to 60% across ponds and stocking events, only partially correlated with bass density — indicating predation was significant but not the sole driver of mortality.
- Bass stomach content analysis directly confirmed predation on squawfish as a major mortality source.
- Additional mortality factors implicated: wintertime water quality degradation and parasitism (anchorworm, tapeworm).
- In a predator-free pond, squawfish growth rate was **more than 2x** the rate previously documented in river or hatchery environments.
- Ponds delivered **1.8x** the degree-days (>13°C) compared to the adjacent Colorado River, explaining the accelerated growth potential.
- **Management implication:** Extensive pond culture is only viable for growth acceleration if predators are excluded entirely from rearing ponds.
- **Conservation implication:** Larger release sizes (target ~250 mm in 2 years) are hypothesized to improve post-release survival compared to current practice of releasing 40–60 mm hatchery fish.
- The contrast between pond and river growth rates highlights that the **Upper Colorado Basin represents a thermally marginal, northern-edge habitat** for [[Colorado Squawfish]], contributing to slow growth and delayed age-at-first-reproduction — life history traits with direct consequences for population growth rates and recovery program success.
- Findings support broader relevance to [[Endangered Species Recovery]] strategies for [[Cyprinidae]] in the [[Colorado River Basin]], and parallel extensive culture techniques used in [[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]] management, with experimental precedent in [[Salmonids]] and [[Striped Bass]].
