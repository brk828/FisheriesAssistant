# Biological Opinion for Repair and Maintenance of a 4-Wheel Drive Road on the Lower San Francisco River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Biological Opinion]], issued April 15, 1997 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Arizona Ecological Services Field Office) under [[Section 7 Consultation]] of the [[Endangered Species Act]], evaluates the repair and maintenance of seven low-water crossings on an unnumbered high-clearance road along the lower San Francisco River upstream from Clifton, Greenlee County, Arizona. The [[Forest Service]], [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] (lead), and the [[Bureau of Land Management]] are the action agencies.

The Service concluded that the project, as proposed, is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) or the endangered [[American Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus anatum*). The opinion includes an [[Incidental Take Statement]] with quantified limits for peregrine falcons, indexed limits for loach minnow, mandatory terms and conditions, and a discretionary recommendation to explore reducing or eliminating use of the road.

## Background & Description

**Project.** The work would repair and maintain 7 of 26 [[Low-Water Crossings]] on an unnumbered track along approximately 8.7 miles of the [[San Francisco River]] (Township 3S, Range 30E). The seven work crossings (B, 4, 7, 8, 11, 14, and 15) lie within the most downstream 5.5 miles. The road provides access to the privately owned RU Ranch, an inholding within the national forest. The access standard is high-clearance 4-wheel drive only; the project is a one-time event expected to take about one week.

**Proposed method.** A County crew would use a bulldozer, loader, grader, and dump truck to:
1. Push blow sand off the ascension and descension points, exposing moist, hardened gravel.
2. Add gravel to restore a firm surface and prevent a funnel-shaped depression.
3. Obtain fill from nearby side drainages (Hackberry and Santa Cruz canyons) rather than importing material from outside the basin.
4. Place large rocks and boulders at key access points to discourage secondary travelways.

The only proposed work in the wetted channel is removal of some large rocks at the crossings. A Forest Service District biologist and the County roads supervisor would be present.

**Species status.**
- **[[Loach Minnow]]:** Listed as threatened October 28, 1986. A small, slender fish with upward-directed eyes that lives on the bottom of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble, and rubble. Its range has shrunk by about 85 percent. Lifespan is about 2 years, and it feeds exclusively on aquatic insects. Spawning occurs March through May, with autumn spawning reported under some conditions. Eggs are attached beneath rocks, and males may guard the nest. Critical habitat (designated March 8, 1994) has been enjoined by a New Mexico district court, so no critical-habitat consultation is required. The species is declining rangewide, and a reclassification to endangered is pending.
- **[[American Peregrine Falcon]]:** Listed as endangered October 13, 1970; no critical habitat designated. It nests on large cliffs (about 100 m or more) and feeds almost entirely on birds. The breeding season in the Southwest runs from March 1 to late June or early July. A June 30, 1995 notice proposed removing the subspecies from the list due to recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic degradation.** Beaver were abundant in the early 1800s. By the turn of the century, agriculture, grazing, roads, mining, timber harvest, and flooding had substantially altered the hydrologic regime and channel. Wood harvest for the Clifton-Morenci mines and local fuelwood decimated upland and riparian woodlands. Today the lower river is a wide, unvegetated braided expanse of cobble, gravel, and sand. Riparian vegetation is sparse, consisting mainly of seep willow, cottonwood seedlings, and salt cedar. Sedges are uncommon, and herbaceous cover is virtually absent on grazed BLM and State lands. Crossing locations can shift by a quarter mile or more with channel changes.

**Fish community.** The lower river's fish fauna is depauperate. In 1904 Chamberlain found no fish between the river's mouth and the Blue River. Surveys in 1979 found "few individual fishes and little biomass," and 1994–96 numbers were low but "not alarmingly so." Besides loach minnow, four native fishes remain: speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker, and Sonora sucker. Gila chub persists only in two tributaries. Eight native species have been extirpated from the river in the past century, more than 60 percent of the native fauna, attributed to habitat alteration and nonnative species.

**Nonnative species.** Red shiner, channel catfish, flathead catfish, mosquitofish, carp, fathead minnow, smallmouth bass, and softshell turtle occur in the river. They compete with and prey upon native fishes.

**Loach minnow distribution.** The first Arizona record was in 1977, and the species has since been found throughout the Arizona portion of the river, though in low numbers. Surveys found it at the Hickey Canyon confluence and at the Forest/BLM boundary. Its downstream limit is unknown, and no surveys were conducted below the boundary between 1983–84 and this consultation, despite suitable habitat for several miles downstream.

**Peregrine falcon status.** The Rocky Mountain/Southwest population stood at 559 breeding pairs in 1994, exceeding the recovery objective by 376 pairs. In the Intermontane Province, where the project lies, occupancy from 1992–1995 was the lowest regionally, at 78 percent, but productivity was 1.1 young fledged per occupied site. No active eyries are known within 10 miles of the project, and no falcons have been observed there. The nearest sighting was along the Blue River about 6 air-miles (12 river miles) away. Of the 5.5 river miles affected, only about 1 mile is estimated to be suitable nesting habitat. No protocol surveys had been done in the action area, so breeding birds were assumed potentially present.

**Consultation history.** Informal consultation began in winter 1995/96, and formal consultation was initiated September 27, 1996. The Forest Service's "not likely to adversely affect" finding for the peregrine falcon was not concurred with, because the project would now occur during the breeding season.

**Other species.** The Forest Service found the project "not likely to adversely affect" the razorback sucker, Mexican gray wolf, southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle, Mexican spotted owl, and proposed jaguar. The Service concurred for five of six, finding effects insignificant or discountable, but not for the peregrine falcon.

**Cumulative effects.** Most upstream land is Federal, so grazing and timber actions would undergo section 7 review. Private lands on the Blue and upper San Francisco rivers contribute ongoing effects. Downstream private lands suppress or eliminate loach minnow, increasing the vulnerability of the river-wide population.

## Key Findings

**Conclusion.** The Service found the project, as proposed, **not likely to jeopardize** either listed species.

**Anticipated effects on loach minnow.**
- Direct crushing by vehicles on crossings and during boulder removal, since loach minnow hide under cobble and boulders and may not flee.
- Harassment and displacement; juveniles in shallow edges may be swept into deeper, colder water.
- Only 1 percent of loach minnow habitat in the action area lies within the crossings, but about 23 percent lies in the riffles directly downstream, where sediment deposition is expected.
- Long-term effects from road-related sediment, bank instability, and channel destabilization. Riparian buffering is limited because the road lies in the floodplain and often within the stream.
- Toxic spills, particularly petroleum products.

**Anticipated effects on peregrine falcon.** Disturbance from heavy machinery within about one-half mile of potential cliff nests during the breeding season, which may cause nest abandonment or neglect of young. The Service noted that peregrines are least tolerant of disturbance during prelaying through incubation and are more tolerant after hatching. Johnson (1988) suggested core areas of about 1,300 feet (topographically screened) to 2,950 feet (unscreened), based on flight-response models rather than verified productivity impacts. Because the cliffs have little screening and the work would occur toward the end of the breeding season, disturbance was considered a concern.

**Incidental take limits.**
- *Loach minnow:* Take cannot be quantified and is indexed to the fish community and habitat. It is exceeded if (1) more than 20 dead fish of any species are found in the project area or within 500 yards downstream; (2) equipment enters the wetted channel more than 30 feet from the centerline of any low-water crossing; or (3) any toxic material spills in the Blue River or its floodplain.
- *Peregrine falcon:* No more than 2 adults and 4 young for the 1997 breeding season, from disruption within one-half mile of the work.

**Key terms and conditions (mandatory).**
- No on-site activity during loach minnow spawning season: March 1 to June 1 and September 1 to October 31.
- No more than 5 gallons of water may be removed from the river at one time; water should come from other sources, or from the river near Clifton, where loach minnow are not known to occur.
- Vehicles and heavy equipment limited to within 30 linear feet of the crossing centerlines in the wetted channel.
- Gravel borrowing from tributary alluvial fans must retain natural contours.
- Pollutants and toxic chemicals may not enter surface waters or be stored in the floodplain.
- A biologist must be present during activities, and monitoring for dead or dying fish must occur within 500 yards downstream. The Service must be notified by telephone if more than 20 dead or dying fish are found.
- Peregrine surveys using the Arizona Game and Fish Department protocol (Ward, 1994) to determine occupancy and reproductive status.
- A written report within 60 days of project completion, including all monitoring results and take records.

**Reinitiation triggers.** Consultation must be reinitiated if take limits are exceeded, new information reveals unconsidered effects, the action is modified, or a new species is listed or critical habitat designated. Operations must stop during reinitiation if additional take would cause irreversible, adverse impacts.

**Conservation recommendation (discretionary).** The Service recommends the Forest Service and BLM explore ways to restrict or eliminate use of the road, such as buying out the private inholding, providing an alternative access route, or restricting use to the inholding owner. The Service acknowledged possible non-biological constraints and asked that the suggestion be considered for long-term planning.

**Road use.** Current use is estimated at 12–20 vehicles per day on summer weekends and 0–5 on weekdays. Improved access could increase loach minnow mortality, sediment input, unauthorized routes into closed reaches, recreational impacts, fuelwood gathering, and the risk of toxic spills. A possible benefit would be increased fishing pressure on catfish, a predator of loach minnow, but this could also increase unauthorized nonnative introductions.
