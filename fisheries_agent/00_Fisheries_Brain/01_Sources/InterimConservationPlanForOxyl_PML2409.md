# Interim Conservation Plan for Kanab Ambersnail (*Oxyloma haydeni kanabensis*) and Related Ambersnails in Arizona and Utah

#malacology #succineidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Report (NGTR 192, April 2002) is an interim conservation plan for the federally endangered [[Kanab Ambersnail]] and related *Oxyloma* populations in Arizona and Utah. The plan stands in for a revised [[Recovery Plan]] (1995) that the authors consider out of date. It sets conservation goals and information needs, a funding list and an implementation schedule, and it was prepared with the [[Kanab Ambersnail Work Group]].

The Kanab ambersnail has two extant populations: [[Three Lakes]] near Kanab, Utah, and [[Vaseys Paradise]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona. Its listing (1992) was driven by loss of wetland habitat. The Vaseys Paradise population is threatened by flows from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]. Recent genetic work suggests that the Vaseys Paradise snails are genetically distinct, so the taxonomic identity of the population is unresolved.

## Background & Description

- **Status:** Emergency listing in 1991 (56 FR 37671). Final listing as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] on 1992 (57 FR 13661). No critical habitat was designated because economic information was lacking. Recovery priority is 6C.
- **Taxonomy:** Succineids are called "ambersnails" for their amber-colored, fragile shells. Identifying *Oxyloma* requires shell, genitalia and habitat assessment. *O. h. kanabensis* is distinguished from *O. h. haydeni* by a more slender spire and shorter aperture. Genetic data (see [[Genetic Analysis]]) conflict with these morphological designations.
- **Distribution:** The Kanab ambersnail is known from two extant populations. Other populations are *Niobrara ambersnail* (*O. h. haydeni*) at [[Indian Gardens]] and [[Minus 9 Mile Spring]] in Arizona, and in Kanab Canyon, Utah, plus *[[Oxyloma Retusa]]* in south-central Utah. Figure 1 maps known populations as of December 2001. Fossil records come from the San Pedro River Valley, Verde Valley and Lake Powell area.
- **Historical:** J.H. Ferriss first collected the species in 1909 at "the Greens" near Kanab. That site was presumed extirpated after a 1990 survey.
- **Participating cooperators:** Agencies, tribes, and NGOs (AGFD, BLM, BIA, CUPCA, GCMRC, NPS, NAU, [[Phoenix Zoo]], USBR, USFWS, UDWR, WAPA) contributed to the plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**
- *Three Lakes* (Utah): spring-fed ponds and wet meadow, about 0.8 km long and roughly 2 ha, dominated by cattail and sedge.
- *Vaseys Paradise* (Arizona): a cool dolomitic spring fed from the Redwall limestone. Habitat is about 850–900 m² (suitable habitat), dominated by crimson monkeyflower, non-native watercress and native watersedge. Habitat increased about 40% after Glen Canyon Dam began moderating flows in 1963.
- *Indian Gardens and Minus 9 Mile* (Arizona): cottonwood-canopied and sedge-dominated wetlands fed by seeps or springs.

**Life history (Vaseys Paradise, captive and field data)**
- Lifespan of 12–15 months; reproductive maturity at about 11.5 mm; growth about 0.1 mm/day.
- Egg masses average 12 eggs; hatching takes about 27 days with about half of eggs hatching.
- Watercress-reared snails grew faster (0.11 vs 0.092 mm/day on monkeyflower) and laid more eggs per mass (mean 14.19 vs 9.7).
- Intrinsic rate of increase (r) was above 1 on watercress and below 1 on monkeyflower.
- Winter dormancy runs roughly October to March. Estimated mortality over dormancy is 25–80%.

**Population estimates**
- *Three Lakes*: about 100,000 snails in June 1990; 180,000–288,000 in the habitat above the upper pond in June–August 1998.
- *Vaseys Paradise*: 18,476 (March 1995) to 104,004 (September 1995); later standardized estimates 7,286–29,630 in spring.

**Threats**
- *Three Lakes*: dewatering, habitat modification, commercial development and livestock grazing by the private landowner, plus silt burial (1994).
- *Vaseys Paradise*: scour and inundation from high Colorado River flows. A 45,000 cfs experimental flow in 1996 scoured about 14% of habitat. Also recreation, flash floods, and bighorn sheep grazing.
- *Indian Gardens*: trampling, flash floods, and landscape maintenance.
- *Southern Utah sites*: development, livestock grazing, dewatering and road expansion. Grazed sites had lower densities.

**Parasites and predators**
- *Leucochloridium* trematode infection in mature (>10 mm) snails has been recorded since 1995 at 1–10% prevalence. It was also found at Three Lakes.
- Deer mice (*Peromyscus*) were observed eating marked snails. American robins and other birds have been noted but not confirmed as predators.

## Key Findings

**Conservation goals and tasks (16 task groups)**
- **Monitoring (Tasks 3–5):** bi-annual monitoring at Vaseys Paradise and Three Lakes; annual monitoring of Niobrara sites and south-central Utah; regional protocol development; annual distribution maps.
- **Flow and habitat modeling (Task 3.3):** develop a model of Vaseys Paradise habitat and snail response to high flows; high-zone surveys above 100,000 cfs.
- **Grazing research (Task 5.3):** test livestock grazing thresholds in south-central Utah.
- **Taxonomy and genetics (Task 6):** captive breeding tests for interbreeding, molecular marker baselines, expanded genetic sampling, and combined morphological study. Results to be published in peer-reviewed journals.
- **Conservation strategies (Task 7):** site strategies for Vaseys Paradise, translocated populations and Three Lakes; State Conservation Agreements for non-listed *Oxyloma* in Arizona and Utah.
- **Translocation criteria (Task 8):** the step-down criteria require at least 30% overwintering survival and then reproduction assessment; success is defined as densities and recruitment similar to the parent population for about 3 years.
- **Delisting criteria (Task 10):** refine downlisting and delisting criteria, which the 1995 plan did not define adequately.
- **Protection and land (Tasks 17.1–17.2):** acquire private land and water rights, including refuges, conservation easements, or TNC preserves. Acquisition depends on landowner willingness.
- **Outreach and data (Tasks 13–15):** public education, a centralized research database, and cooperator training.

**Translocation results**
- In September 1998, 450 snails were moved from Vaseys Paradise to three Grand Canyon sites (150 each); a second 450 followed in July 1999.
- Only Upper Elves Chasm has shown recruitment, overwinter survival and increasing density.
- The Phoenix Zoo refugium (established 1997–99) appears to have failed after September 2000, possibly from low initial stock and high temperatures.

**Sampling error**
- Subsampling of watercress habitat overestimated Vaseys Paradise densities by 473% in summer and 489% in autumn. At the Phoenix Zoo refugium it overestimated by 223%.
- Sorensen (2001) showed that these errors raise the probability of apparent extinction in population models.

**Taxonomy**
- Miller et al. (2000) found genetically distinct and unique populations using AFLP on 110 base pairs.
- Stevens et al. (2000) found the Vaseys Paradise population genetically distinct, but morphologically indistinguishable from Three Lakes. Results are provisional.
- The "Kanab ambersnail" name is kept until taxonomy is resolved.

**Review panel and stakeholder comments**
- The 1999 expert panel (Noss et al.) recommended more genetic and habitat studies, no further translocations, flows up to 45,000 cfs, minimal monitoring at Vaseys Paradise, and revision of the Recovery Plan.
- Reviewers from BOR, USFWS, WAPA and NPS asked for clearer scope (focusing on the Kanab ambersnail), a responsibility table for implementation, clearer task justifications, and data on private-land protections. The Bureau of Reclamation also raised whether the appendices were still needed.

**Funding and implementation**
- The implementation schedule lists 37 tasks with priorities, durations, responsible parties and annual costs (roughly $2,000 to $100,000 per task). Costs are not binding on any cooperator.
- Funding sources include [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Adaptive Management funds through the [[Upper Colorado Region Bureau of Reclamation]], CUPCA, WAPA, USFWS Section 6, Partners for Fish and Wildlife, the [[Arizona Heritage Fund]], NFWF, NSF and the Grand Canyon Association.
