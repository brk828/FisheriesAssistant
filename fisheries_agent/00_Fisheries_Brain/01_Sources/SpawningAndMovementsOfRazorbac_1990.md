# Spawning and Movements of Razorback Sucker in the Green River Basin

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered_species

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a monotypic catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults in the [[Green River Basin]] averaged 507 mm TL for males (range 405–576 mm, n=141) and 547 mm TL for females (range 485–608 mm, n=45). Males averaged 1,370 g (range 900–2,000 g) and females 1,686 g (range 1,030–2,250 g). Sex ratio was approximately 2.5 males to 1 female based on 307 breeding captures (60% males, 24% females, 16% immature tuberculated fish). Individual males remained in breeding condition for up to 28 days, while individual females were ripe for only 5–8 days (range 2–8 days). Ripe fish were captured over a 4–5 week period annually (mid-April to early June). Populations consist of old individuals with minimal or nonexistent recruitment. Green River fish are smaller than reservoir populations in the lower Colorado River. Age and maturity data indicate stocks are composed of aging cohorts with no widespread successful recruitment to adult stage.

**Habitat Associations**: Razorback suckers were widely distributed in low-gradient, flatwater sections of the [[Green River]] (RK 396.8–552) and lower 84.8 km of the [[Yampa River]]. Adults were relatively solitary outside spawning season. Two primary spawning aggregation areas were identified: (1) Jensen site (mouth of [[Ashley Creek]] to lower boundary of [[Dinosaur National Monument]], RK 476.8–504; 91% of breeding captures, n=182) and (2) Yampa site (lower 5 km of Yampa River; 7% of breeding captures, n=14). Spawning occurred on submerged shoreline riffles with cobble, gravel, and sand substrates at average depth 0.67 m and average water velocity 0.64 m/s. Specific spawning microhabitats included: Yampa River left channel at mouth (RK 0.8) and left shoreline above Echo Park Campground (RK 0.16); Green River right shoreline and channel below Green River Campground (RK 491.2–500.8). Some mature fish (18% of ripe captures, n=56) were captured in low-velocity backwater and eddy habitats in flooded bottoms of lower Ashley Creek, [[Stewart Lake Drain]], [[Old Charley Wash]], and lower [[Duchesne River]]—areas with sand-silt and vegetated substrates that may serve as resting-feeding areas during breeding season. The reach between mouth of Duchesne River and lower Yampa River supports the majority of extant razorback suckers in the Green River basin.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Eighty percent of 14 radiotagged and 68 recaptured razorback suckers were sedentary (20 local and 46 long-term contacts), with 91% (n=60) remaining in the Jensen site during observation periods. Migratory fish (n=16; 7 local and 9 long-term) exhibited movements ranging 52.8–190.4 km. Two long-distance movement patterns were identified: (1) between lower Yampa River and Jensen site (n=4, average one-way movement 49.6 km, range 30.4–60.8 km), and (2) between Jensen site and Old Charley Wash/Duchesne River (n=12, average one-way movement 94.4 km, range 54–106 km). One radiotagged fish moved round-trip 156 km between Jensen site and Old Charley Wash (May 4–August 2, 1988). Four fish exhibited round-trip movements during spawning season: one between Jensen and Yampa sites, three between Jensen and Old Charley Wash (averaging 177.6 km). All long-distance movements (n=16) were associated with movement to spawning grounds and occurred during/following spawning season; 10 migratory fish were captured ripe (8 males, 2 females). Some razorback suckers exhibited spawning site fidelity to specific riffles across years (n=31 recaptured in same spawning reach in different years; 5 recaptured in three different years). One ripe male was recaptured at the exact location in lower Yampa River (RK 0.16) in 1988 where it had been captured ripe in 1981, having overwintered in Jensen area and bypassed that spawning site to migrate 52.8 km. Six fish (5 males, 1 female) were recaptured at different spawning sites within and between years.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. discobolus*) in breeding condition were captured on the same cobble-gravel-sand riffles as spawning razorback suckers. At Yampa site, flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were 9× more numerous than razorback sucker. At Jensen site, flannelmouth sucker was 2× as abundant as razorback sucker and 4× as abundant as bluehead sucker. Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were ripe for longer periods (45 days and 42 days, respectively) than razorback sucker, with spawning of all three species overlapping mid-April to early June in 1989 at both sites. In 1989, 17 suspected flannelmouth × razorback sucker hybrids in breeding condition (14 ripe males, 3 ripe females) were captured in Yampa Canyon (RK 0–72) and upper Green River (RK 478.4–499.2). Hybrids were intermediate in morphology (skull, mouth, nape characteristics). Most upstream hybrid capture occurred in Yampa Canyon approximately 67.2 km upstream from Green River confluence. Numerous historical observations of suspected intermediates suggest incidence of hybridization and introgression may be increasing, potentially affected by abundance of flannelmouth suckers, paucity of razorback suckers, and temporal-spatial overlap in spawning.

**Environmental Drivers**: Razorback suckers spawned during increasing and highest spring flows. Water temperatures averaged approximately 14°C (Yampa site: mean 13.8°C, range 10.5–16°C; Jensen site: mean 14.1°C, range 9–17°C). Spawning temperatures were less than the 20°C noted for optimal hatch, suggesting current flow and temperature regimens altered by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure may adversely affect hatchability. Spawning with increasing flows indicates need for cleaned and loosened substrate. Abrasions on ventral caudal peduncle of ripe females suggest vigorous substrate sweeping in preparation for egg deposition. Interrelationship of high spring flows, warming temperatures, and other factors considered important for successful reproduction. Repeated collections of "running ripe" females and visual observations confirm spawning in riverine habitats with cobble-gravel-sand substrates.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: The razorback sucker, once so abundant it was harvested commercially, is now threatened with extinction throughout its range. Natural populations occur only in Lakes Mohave and Havasu on lower Colorado River, a 15-mile reach of upper Colorado River near Grand Junction, sporadically in lower San Juan River, and in Green and Yampa rivers of Utah and Colorado. The Green River basin population represents the largest extant riverine population, though rare. Stocks are composed of old individuals with minimal or nonexistent recruitment. Between 1981 and 1989, 55% (n=307) of 560 adult captures were in breeding condition, but lack of widespread recruitment indicates reproductive failure. The low number of reproducing adults is considered limiting to species survival. Population decline emphasizes need for immediate recovery measures.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: A continuing threat of extinction remains due to: (1) paucity of spawning adults, (2) hybridization with other suckers (particularly flannelmouth sucker), (3) lack of widespread successful recruitment to adult stage, and (4) habitat loss. Habitat alteration and loss include curtailment of spring flooding, loss of historic flow and temperature regimens, and loss of inundated bottomlands for nursery habitat. Current flow and temperature regimens altered by Flaming Gorge Dam closure may adversely affect hatchability (spawning occurs at 9–17°C vs. 20°C optimal hatch temperature). Predation by non-native fishes is implicated in recruitment failure. Stream blockage would interrupt spawning migrations (30.4–105.6 km documented) and is implicated as factor in decline where species has been lost. Loss of seasonally flooded habitats due to impoundment, curtailment of fish access, and control of spring flooding may adversely affect both reproduction and recruitment. Effects of natural and induced hybridization on decline are not well understood and need further study. Incidence of hybridization may be increasing due to abundance of flannelmouth suckers, paucity of razorback suckers, and temporal-spatial overlap in spawning of multiple sucker species.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Recovery efforts should address: (1) restoration of spring flooding and historic flow/temperature regimens, (2) protection and restoration of inundated bottomlands for nursery habitat, (3) maintenance of unobstructed stream passage for spawning migrations, (4) investigation and mitigation of hybridization incidence and consequences, and (5) control of non-native fish predation. Flooded lowlands (Old Charley Wash, Stewart Lake Drain) and flooded tributary mouths (Duchesne River, Ashley Creek) are important for recovery due to annual movements of some fish into these areas and should be protected. Spawning site fidelity to specific riffles indicates need to protect identified spawning microhabitats. Some historic spawning sites may now be lost; fish may be seeking habitats that no longer exist. Immediate measures toward recovery are needed given decline toward extinction. Caution is needed when evaluating spawning behavior of long-lived fish in altered riverine environments, particularly in turbid systems where ripe fish may be recaptured at widely different locations due to migratory behavior.
