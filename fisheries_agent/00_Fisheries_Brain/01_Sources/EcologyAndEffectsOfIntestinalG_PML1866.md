# Ecology and Effects of Intestinal Gas Accumulation in a Pupfish (Genus Cyprinodon)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This short paper describes an undescribed [[Pupfish]] of the genus [[Cyprinodon]] from the Bolsón de [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] in Coahuila, México. The fish suffers from intestinal gas accumulation during summer, linked to gas produced by dense [[Blue-Green Algae]] in its sediment-rich foraging habitat. Affected fish become buoyant, lose equilibrium, and show abnormal swimming, and some die. The authors describe the behavioural sequence of the condition, observations of natural and laboratory cases, possible predators, and a working hypothesis of gas supersaturation of the water.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** Two undescribed pupfish species occur in the Bolsón de Cuatro Ciénegas. The focal species is designated *Cyprinodon* sp. A for future reference. The OCR text is partly garbled, so the exact designation should be checked against the original.
- **Diet:** In its habitat, which lacks invertebrates and is harsh in summer, the fish feeds on digestible components of the sediment and eats it voraciously. Experimental stocks in outdoor pools at Tempe, Arizona, behaved similarly.
- **Gas production:** Under summer insolation, gases accumulate in algae-laden sediments of ponds and streams through intra- and extracellular formation of gaseous metabolic products. Feeding fish ingest gas along with solids.
- **Normal behaviour:** The fish usually lives in water less than 20 cm deep. During daylight, when not feeding or breeding, it moves aimlessly with a meandering locomotion (after Barlow, 1961). Its fins are held against the body and it swims parallel to the substrate, propelled by caudal beats. Every 20–30 cm it extends its fins, stops, and then changes direction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pathology and sequence of affliction**

1. **Early stage:** The intestine becomes inflated with ingested and digestive gases. The abdomen distends, and the body rises caudal-first at about 30° from horizontal. The fish swims with exaggerated caudal strokes and makes sudden bursts to stay beneath the surface.
2. **Middle stage:** Equilibrium is progressively lost. The fish dives into the soft bottom at irregular depths and angles. Some dives resemble the "chafing" behaviour described for other pupfish (Baerends and Baerends van Roon, 1950; Raney et al., 1953), in which the fish strikes the bottom and rubs its opercles or sides against it.
3. **Late stage:** After extended activity the fish seems to tire, and surface flotation occurs. Afflicted fish may remain at the surface, hang head-down in the sediment with only the caudal fin exposed, or lie on their sides. Most recover after a few minutes, when the distension is relieved by flatulence. The fish then resumes swimming and feeding normally.

**Mortality and causes**

- Mortality does not appear to be extremely high, despite the apparent trauma. The authors suggest death may result from intestinal rupture, as indicated by dissection of affected fish.
- Other possible causes mentioned include heat or desiccation, and fungal or other infection of the epidermis.

**Predators and population effects**

- Piscivorous birds such as the [[Great Blue Heron]] (*Ardea herodias*) were observed near affected pools. Other fish-eating birds, turtles, and some mammals may also prey on incapacitated fish.
- Sexes show no obvious difference in susceptibility, but juvenile fish are seldom affected.
- Symptoms were most obvious at midday, after periods of intense algal activity.

**Other species and gas-supersaturation hypothesis**

- Large-scale affliction had not been recorded in other southwestern *Cyprinodon* species. However, a few individuals of a second species (*C. variegatus*, per the OCR text) were seen swimming erratically in a pond near Tempe, Arizona, in August 1965, and others were observed floating in laboratory pools there in 1966.
- The authors propose that gas supersaturation of the water, which was not measured, may cause supersaturation of the blood and tissues. This would be analogous to [[Gas Bubble Disease]], which is known in other fishes (e.g., Embody, 1934) and causes buoyancy disorders.

## Key Findings

- **Phenomenon:** Intestinal gas accumulation in *Cyprinodon* sp. A causes abdominal distension, loss of equilibrium, flotation, and sometimes death. It is linked to algal gas production in summer sediments.
- **Occurrence:** The condition was observed three times in nature and in laboratory stocks during the summers of 1964, 1965, and 1966.
- **Behaviour:** Affected fish show a predictable sequence of abnormal behaviour, moving from exaggerated swimming and bottom-diving to flotation, with recovery after gas release.
- **Vulnerability:** Adults are more affected than juveniles. Symptoms peak at midday, following intense algal activity. The number of affected individuals in one case ranged from 10 to an illegible upper count.
- **Mortality:** Death is not common relative to the number affected. Intestinal rupture is the proposed cause.
- **Proposed mechanism:** Gas supersaturation of the water, not directly measured, is the suspected trigger.
