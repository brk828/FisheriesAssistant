# Gila River Basin Native Fish Conservation Planning Process

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-fauna

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1994 conceptual outline, [[Ecosystem Conservation Planning]] Process for the [[Native Fish Fauna]] of the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico, promoted by [[The Nature Conservancy]] and [[American Rivers]]. It proposes a holistic, ecosystem-level alternative to species-by-species management. The framework has four steps: characterize biological systems, identify threats, develop conservation strategies, and define and monitor success. The attached cover letter from [[David L. Propst]], Endangered Species Biologist with the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], to [[Rick Johnson]] recommends starting on a smaller scale in the [[San Francisco River]] drainage and inventorying data sources early because the available data are likely to be fragmented and of uncertain quality.

## Background & Description

- **Status of the fauna:** The native fish fauna of the Gila River is generally in decline. Local abundance ranges from abundant in some streams to rare in others and absent in still others.
- **Nonnative fishes:** Their distribution and abundance are not spatially congruent with natives but follow the same general pattern of local abundance, rarity, or absence.
- **Rationale:** The authors argue that unraveling the complex relationships between fish distribution, fish assemblages, and environmental conditions requires an ecosystem approach. A species-by-species approach is considered ineffective for this purpose.
- **Partners:** Implementation is envisioned with federal and state agencies, universities, and non-governmental organizations holding legal or other interests in Gila River ecosystem conservation.
- **Cover letter (October 20, 1994):** Propst agrees the proposal is "certainly needed" but calls it "rather grand." He recommends beginning with a smaller pilot area so the team can learn what data exist and redesign the approach if necessary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The proposal treats the Gila basin as a system where biological patterns (species and species assemblages) are shaped by biotic and abiotic processes. It emphasizes that fish distribution depends on:

- **Physiography and hydrology:** Soils, geology, river pattern, valley morphology, elevation, channel slope, sinuosity, stream order, regulated versus non-regulated flow, average peak discharge, and bankfull discharge (1.5-year return interval).
- **Water balance:** Total water resources and the sources, sinks, and storage of water at the sub-basin scale, to identify water-quantity threats.
- **Reach and micro-habitat conditions:** Stream width, depth, flow velocity, substrate, aquatic vegetation, canopy cover, gradient, and adjacent land use.
- **Water chemistry:** Dissolved oxygen, turbidity, conductivity, pH, and temperature.

Anthropogenic stresses listed include altered hydrologic regime, [[Eutrophication]], sedimentation, changes to biological assemblages, and habitat degradation. Sources of stress include [[Surface Water Diversion]], [[Groundwater Pumping]], [[Dams]], introduction of nonnative species, septic tanks, structural bank alteration, sand and gravel mining, [[Livestock Grazing]], timber harvest, floodplain farming, and removal of riparian vegetation.

## Key Findings

The document contains a planning framework and a set of recommendations rather than empirical results. No sample sizes or quantitative fish data are reported. The main points are:

1. **Scale of effort:** Start with a smaller pilot area, the [[San Francisco River]] drainage, which is small but spans several political units. Expand after gaining experience with available data.
2. **Data inventory:** Identify all potential information sources early, including [[USGS Stream-Gauge Records]], state agriculture departments, and state engineer offices. For each source, define the data types expected, which will reveal information gaps and help assess data quality.
3. **Data quality risk:** Data are expected to be of uncertain quality, thoroughness, and completeness, and temporally and geographically variable. The proposal must address how to handle fragmented and "uninformative" data.
4. **Historic and current distribution:** An in-progress [[American Rivers]] and [[Arizona State University]] study is expected to map current native fish distribution, so that data reflect what is present rather than only what was historically present.
5. **Macro-scale analysis:** Classify native-fish areas into general stream-type categories using physiography, topography, and hydrology. The [[Rosgen Stream Classification]] (Rosgen 1994) is proposed for classifying stream type.
6. **Field sampling:** Conduct extensive basin-wide sampling of fish presence, absence, and abundance (native and nonnative) alongside physical and chemical habitat variables. Sampling sites will be chosen from the AR/ASU study, [[Fall Fish Count]] data, and local experts. Sampling frequency should capture seasonal and annual variation and be adjusted as results come in. Field sampling should run in parallel with the AR/ASU study where possible.
7. **Statistical analysis:** Define species assemblages with [[TWINSPAN]] classification and relate them to environmental conditions with [[Canonical Correspondence Analysis]] ordination.
8. **Threat analysis:** Identify threatened systems and stress sources based on local expertise and the statistical results, with emphasis on differences between native-dominated and nonnative-dominated habitats. Use the water budget to calibrate threats and set thresholds.
9. **Conservation strategies:** Prioritize high-priority stress sources for removal or neutralization. Strategies, including protection and restoration, should account for species distribution, ecological pattern and process, [[Conservation Genetics]], threat assessment, stream type, landownership or control patterns, and feasibility.
10. **Measuring success:** Define a "vision of success," such as species recovery goals or ecosystem health, and build a monitoring program. Suggested metrics include the [[Index of Biotic Integrity]] (Karr 1993) and the [[Index of Hydrologic Alteration]] (Richter et al. 1994).
11. **Cover letter conclusion:** Propst asks for early and clear identification of information sources and gaps before committing to the full basin-wide scope, and offers to answer questions by phone.
