# Review of Sucker Population Genetics Study in Northern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #conservation-genetics #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This correspondence from [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology (dated 20 May 1991) provides critical review of a population genetics project report by Moyle and Berg concerning endangered catostomid fishes in northern California. The letter, addressed to the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], includes an anonymous peer review by Dr. Thomas E. Dowling and additional commentary questioning interpretations of morphological variation and hybridization in western sucker species. The reviewers express concern about premature conclusions regarding hybrid status and alternative morphological states, drawing on comparative examples from [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Cui-ui]] populations.

## Background & Description

The reviewed report (FG-8143) describes protein electrophoretic analysis of suckers in genera [[Catostomus]], [[Chasmistes]], and [[Deltistes]], with emphasis on endangered species. The correspondence addresses a controversy similar to previous disputes between [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[California Department of Fish and Game]] regarding hybrid interpretations in western suckers.

Key taxa discussed include:
- [[Warner Sucker]] (Catostomus warnerensis) - showing size variation between lake and creek populations
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus) - from [[Lake Mohave]]
- [[Cui-ui]] (Chasmistes cujus) - from [[Pyramid Lake]]
- [[Tahoe Sucker]] (Catostomus tahoensis)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis)
- [[Shortnose Sucker]] (Chasmistes brevirostris) - [[Klamath River]]

The correspondence notes that western suckers are long-lived fishes inhabiting disjunct drainages with complex isolation and contact histories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Western North American suckers occupy "big-river" systems with unique biogeographic histories distinct from well-watered zones like the [[Mississippi River Valley]]. These populations experience:

- Long-term isolation in specialized habitats
- Periodic contact and gene exchange over millennia
- Differential selection pressures maintaining morphological differences
- Extreme longevity (40+ years documented in [[Razorback Sucker]])

Environmental factors affecting morphology include:
- Habitat size effects: "big habitats support big fish and little ones have little fish"
- Age-related changes: massive skull ossification and skeletal features in old fish
- Sexual dimorphism: fat accumulation in pre-spawning females
- Disease-related morphological changes
- Senescence effects in non-spawning individuals

The [[Lake Mohave]] [[Razorback Sucker]] population demonstrates how geriatric changes can mimic hybrid characteristics, with fish averaging 40+ years showing substantial morphological variation.

## Key Findings

**Critical Review Points:**

1. **Hybridization Estimation Flaws**: The assumption that shared alleles among distinct taxa indicate gene exchange is invalid. Taxa known to be reproductively isolated share alleles at polymorphic loci due to common ancestry or convergent evolution. Maximum hybridization estimates may be "much higher than actual values and may be very misleading."

2. **Lake Mohave Case Study**: Of 18 [[Razorback Sucker]] initially identified as hybrids, only one individual carried introgressed genes from [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]; others were pure razorbacks (Buth et al. 1987). Morphological variation resulted from age-related ossification, disease, and reproductive status rather than hybridization.

3. **Cui-ui Morphological Variation**: Young [[Cui-ui]] (1980s year classes) appeared different from older individuals and seemed to trend toward [[Tahoe Sucker]] morphology. However:
   - Meristic characteristics were identical between age classes
   - Genetic analysis by two independent laboratories (including Dr. Berg's and Dr. Don Buth's at UCLA) found no hybrids based on allozymes
   - Morphological studies remain incomplete despite paper title including term "polytypic"

4. **Invalid Assumptions in Original Report**:
   - Statement that hybrids are typically infertile or at fitness disadvantage contradicts numerous documented cases of viable, fertile hybrids
   - Claim that one migrant per generation overrides moderate selection is incorrect; this level prevents drift differentiation but does not override selectively favorable alleles
   - Examples of alternative morphologies (p. 20) presented "glibly" without adequate documentation

5. **Conservation Implications**: Emphasis on identifying "most pure" populations for recovery raises concerns:
   - Replacement of locally adapted populations showing introgression with "pure" stocks may eliminate fitness-advantageous alleles
   - Genetic markers estimate introgression levels but not its impact
   - Selectively advantageous alleles are maintained despite introgression
   - Hybridization may represent an alternative evolutionary mode providing variation for environmental response

6. **Phylogenetic Analysis Limitations**: The phenogram shows insufficient distinctiveness among taxa. Excluding [[Tahoe Sucker]] data, only three fixed differences exist (Ldh-B2, Ada, Gpi-3), with only one for [[Klamath River]] species, preventing phylogenetic resolution.

7. **mtDNA Preliminary Results**: Dr. Dowling examined mtDNA from [[Klamath River]] suckers (from gametes provided by USFWS) showing patterns similar to allozyme results.

8. **Recommendations**:
   - Avoid premature conclusions about hybrid status and morphological states
   - Consider ontogenetic, geriatric changes and sexual dimorphism as alternative explanations
   - Conduct genetic studies prior to hatchery stock production
   - Convene meeting of biologists working with "big-river" and "long-lived" western fishes
   - Recognize that these populations may share alleles while maintaining morphological distinctiveness through differential selection

The correspondence emphasizes that morphologically diverse fishes sharing alleles are "even more interesting and important scientifically" and warns against applying logic from other faunal regions to the unique disjunct drainages of western North America.
