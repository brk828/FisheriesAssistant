# Lower Colorado River Basin Native Fish Management Plan with Emphasis on Big-River Fishes

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #river-ecology

## Executive Summary

This draft management plan addresses the recovery of four federally endangered "big-river" fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Submitted by the Parker Fishery Resource Office to USFWS Region II, the plan emphasizes circumventing recruitment failure caused by predation from non-indigenous fishes as the primary recovery mechanism. The basin encompasses 650,000 km² across seven U.S. states plus Mexico, divided into five ecoregions for management purposes. Wild populations of squawfish are extirpated from the lower basin; bonytail persist only in Lakes Mohave and Havasu; humpback chub maintain a viable population in the [[Grand Canyon]] area (primarily [[Little Colorado River]]); razorback sucker's largest population occurs in [[Lake Mohave]]. The plan proposes a six-level hierarchical approach from preventing extinction (Level I) through achieving self-sustaining populations and delisting (Level VI), utilizing isolated natural and constructed habitats, hatchery propagation with strict genetic management, and eventual restoration of free-flowing river reaches.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] basin drains portions of seven states plus Mexico, with elevations ranging from below sea level in the [[Salton Sea]] basin to over 4000 m in Rocky Mountain headwaters. The lower basin consists of the mainstem Colorado and five major subbasins (Little Colorado, Paria, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila) with their tributaries in Arizona, California, and Nevada. Prior to impoundment, the mainstem featured slow-moving turbid reaches in valleys alternating with violent canyon rapids. Discharge reflected Rocky Mountain snowmelt—running high and cold in spring, then decreasing abruptly and warming markedly in summer.

Under these extreme conditions evolved some of the largest and most unusual freshwater fishes globally. The four "big-river" fishes share remarkable characteristics: all attain large sizes and great age; they form a coevolved assemblage with shared life-history patterns driven by predation dynamics. [[Colorado Squawfish]] developed as an apex predator with large head, raptorial pharyngeal teeth, and solitary ambush behavior. The three prey species evolved high reproductive potentials (extended breeding periods, numerous eggs), rapid growth rates, hydrodynamic body shapes, and falcate fins—adaptations to survive intense predation pressure.

All four species are federally listed as endangered with critical habitat designations pending. Declines are attributed to: (1) habitat loss and modification from large lower-basin impoundments that blocked historic movement corridors, released water too cold for native spawning requirements, and removed natural flow variance (torrential floods alternating with prolonged drought); and (2) negative interactions with non-indigenous species, particularly predation on eggs, fry, and juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lower basin is subdivided into five ecoregions based on fish distribution and watershed relations:

**Ecoregion I**: Mainstem Colorado from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Hoover Dam]], including Paria, Virgin, and Little Colorado river confluences. Contains 10 native fish species including all four big-river fishes.

**Ecoregion II**: Mainstem Colorado from Hoover Dam to U.S.-Mexican boundary; only tributary is Bill Williams River. Contains 10 native species including all four big-river fishes and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*).

**Ecoregion III**: Middle Colorado River system—Little Colorado River above Grand Falls and Virgin River. Contains 10 native species including [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) and [[Gila Trout]] (*O. gilae*).

**Ecoregion IV**: Gila River from Colorado River upstream. Contains 12 native species including [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitus*), and [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*).

**Ecoregion V**: Mexican drainages (Rios Yaqui, Sonoyta, Magdalena basins, and endorheic Willcox Playa). Contains 12 native species including [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) and [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*).

Artificial reservoirs, canals, and agency stocking programs provide constant sources of non-indigenous predators and competitors. As more non-indigenous taxa establish, fewer natives are found. Experimental data indicate all four big-river taxa share capability to survive to maturity if isolated in habitats free of other fishes during early life-history stages, demonstrating that recruitment failure—not adult survival—drives population declines.

## Key Findings

**Current Population Status**: Wild [[Colorado Squawfish]] are extirpated from the lower basin. [[Bonytail Chub]] are caught only in Lakes Mohave and Havasu. [[Humpback Chub]] persist in a viable population in Grand Canyon, mostly in the lowermost Little Colorado River. The largest known [[Razorback Sucker]] population is in Lake Mohave, with wild individuals sporadically captured in Grand Canyon and below Davis Dam. The apparent beginning of collapse of the Lake Mohave razorback stock raises immediate extinction concerns.

**Management Approach**: The plan focuses on six hierarchical levels: (I) Prevent extinction; (II) Perpetuate existing genetic variability; (III) Stabilize populations; (IV) Expand populations; (V) Achieve self-sustaining populations; (VI) Recovery and delisting. Bonytail enters at Level I requiring immediate extinction prevention, though genetic data exist allowing simultaneous stabilization efforts. Razorback sucker has Level I concerns but genetic data are available and stabilization/expansion efforts are ongoing.

**Preventing Extinction (Levels I-III)**: Building on experience from Lake Mohave razorback efforts, the plan proposes: (1) Identifying natural/seminatural habitats (>5.0 ha surface area) that are isolated from other waters, amenable to piscicide treatment, and suitable for holding wild-caught and cultured fish in protective custody; (2) Removing non-indigenous fishes and stocking endangered taxa within three years, beginning on National Wildlife Refuges and expanding to other public lands; (3) When natives reach sizes minimizing predation pressure (ca. 300 mm TL), using them for population augmentation, reintroductions elsewhere, and as buffers against catastrophic events. Ideally, fish will ultimately be released throughout the mainstem Colorado River.

**Hatchery Infrastructure**: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center currently holds broodstocks of all big-river fishes except humpback chub. Their propagation, rearing, and distribution capabilities are pivotal. Other facilities ([[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona Game and Fish Department's Bubbling Ponds hatchery, Colorado River Indian Tribe ponds) will come online for propagation efforts. Primary roles include: (1) Maintaining broodstocks to supply genetically representative progeny while serving as refugia against potential loss of wild stocks; (2) Producing sufficient quantities and qualities of fish to meet recovery requirements.

**Genetic Management (Level II)**: Strict protocols to maximize and conserve genetic variability include: (1) Maximizing numbers of individuals contributing equally to recruitment—for razorback sucker, collecting wild-caught larvae at different times during extended breeding season at various Lake Mohave locations exploiting phototactic response; (2) If wild larvae unavailable, basing artificial propagation on strict pedigree with measured gamete contributions from individuals with known genetic attributes, or statistical breeding regimens ensuring each broodstock allele is represented in progeny; (3) Broodstock management maximizing use of wild individuals, infusing wild fish with "domestic" (F1) broodfish to minimize inbreeding, and applying strict pedigree to conserve all F1 generation alleles.

**Population Expansion (Level IV)**: Development of new, larger habitats (>5 ha) isolated from other waters and dedicated for long-term management includes: (1) Planning and implementing within five years creation and stocking of large, seminatural, isolated habitats, initially on Refuges compatible with refuge management including waterfowl, riparian, and neotropical bird habitat enhancement; (2) Modifying habitats to encourage natural reproduction and self-sustaining populations (Level V) usable as sources for reintroduction and genetic management; (3) Expanding within 10 years to floodplain habitats on other federal lands and tributaries in Ecoregions I (including humpback chub) and IV (Gila River basin); (4) Taking advantage of reservoir drainage for maintenance or drought by stocking adults and larvae coincident with refilling to exploit absence of predators, rapid native growth potential, and great longevity; (5) Restoring free-flowing river reaches (Salt, Gila, Verde, Bill Williams rivers, mainstem Colorado) through habitat improvement, barriers to non-indigenous invasion, removal and control of non-indigenous species, and reestablishment of native fish communities.

**Habitat Maintenance**: Perpetual maintenance requires: (1) Periodic renovations to control non-indigenous fishes appearing in dedicated habitats, with repeated stockings and attendant genetic, population, and production planning; (2) Evaluating interactions between non-indigenous and native fishes, particularly large non-indigenous predators; (3) Periodic dredging, vegetation control, and other manipulations since natural processes creating oxbows, backwaters, and isolated habitats no longer exist due to river controls. Alternatively, habitats changing through natural succession will be incorporated into refuge management as open-water components of wider wetland and riparian communities.

**Program Coordination**: A Lower Basin Coordinator is necessary to form a Lower Basin Recovery Team and maintain order in developing site-specific management plans. The coordinator will work with representatives from numerous agencies (Appendix B lists federal agencies including Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service, National Park Service; state agencies; Indian tribes; academia; environmental groups). The Recovery Team will consist of biologists responsible for developing and revising recovery plans to fit lower basin needs and providing biological guidance. One Recovery Team will address all four species rather than individual teams per species. The coordinator must have quick access to the Regional Director and strong ties with endangered species personnel. Annual working sessions will explore biology of the four taxa, ascertain best information on brood stock acquisition and production, and consolidate information on population stabilization and perpetuation.

**Rationale for Approach**: This research-based effort centers on a large scientific literature (reviewed by Minckley 1973, Deacon and Minckley 1974, Minckley and Deacon 1992). The plan differs from existing upper basin recovery plans due to: (1) Substantially longer time frame for lower basin modifications resulting in development stabilization—most damsites already used, major alterations completed, few new projects anticipated; (2) Larger population sizes of bonytail and razorback sucker in lower basin than elsewhere; (3) Extensive site-specific research base allowing fact-based recovery actions with increased confidence of positive results. The plan emphasizes circumventing recruitment failure as the principal means for initiating recovery, justified by immediate availability of amenable habitats and concentration of personnel and expertise.

**Fish Health Monitoring**: Natural/seminatural habitats must be examined for health-related problems prior to stocking by examining non-indigenous fishes. Health of native fish in hatcheries and stocked habitats should be monitored annually at minimum and prior to any site-to-site transfers to maximize health and minimize disease transfer probabilities.

**Species Coverage**: The plan deals primarily with bonytail and razorback sucker, with expansion to humpback chub and Colorado squawfish largely deferred. Humpback chub in Grand Canyon is currently under Environmental Impact Statement production mandated by Secretary of Interior (July 1989) and Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1993 provisions, coupled with Conservation Measures required by Biological Opinion on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations (USFWS Region II, 13 October 1993). Colorado squawfish has been reintroduced as "experimental, nonessential" in Salt and Verde rivers; the plan recommends modifying designation to "experimental, essential" so those populations and habitats may be included in recovery planning. USFWS requested withdrawal (2 August 1993) of earlier Arizona Game and Fish Department proposal for experimental, non-essential designation in lower Colorado mainstem; actions for this species are limited to that area.
