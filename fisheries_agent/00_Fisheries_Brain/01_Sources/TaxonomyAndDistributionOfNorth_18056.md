# Taxonomy and Distribution of North American Esocids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 review by E. J. Crossman, published in the American Fisheries Society Special Publication 11, examines the taxonomy and distribution of the family [[Esocidae]] in North America. Four native species (Esox masquinongy, E. lucius, E. niger, E. americanus) and one exotic species (E. reicherti) are recognized. The native species include five named forms, intergrades, and natural hybrids. Taxonomic divisions within species remain under study. Post-Wisconsin natural distributions have been altered by human activity, including extensive introductions, and the paper traces changes in taxonomic concepts and proposes past and future distributions.

## Background & Description

**Classification.** The esocids form a single family and genus with five species. Their closest relatives, the mudminnows (Umbra, Novumbra, Dallia; five species), are grouped in the family [[Umbridae]]. Recent work (Rosen 1974) places Esocidae, Umbridae, and [[Lepidogalaxiidae]] within the Suborder Esocoidei of the Order Salmoniformes, a lower rank than the earlier Order Haplomi. Many features once used to separate esocoids are shared with other salmoniforms.

**Fossil history.** Esocid fossils are limited to the Holarctic: seven described Old World records (Oligocene to Pleistocene) and seven New World records (Miocene to Pleistocene). The origin (Europe or North America) and dispersal routes (transatlantic or [[Bering Land Bridge]]) remain disputed.

**Morphology and ecology.** Esocids are salmonid-like fishes of cool to warm waters, specialized for sprint swimming and sight predation on single, relatively large prey fish. Body shape, fin position, mouth size, skull bone extension, and tooth form reflect these adaptations.

**Pikes versus pickerels.** The two groups differ morphologically, biochemically, and genetically, yet reproductive isolation is incomplete and natural hybrids occur.
- Pikes (subgenus Esox): Muskellunge (E. masquinongy), Northern Pike (E. lucius), and Amur Pike (E. reicherti).
- Pickerels (subgenus Kenoza): Chain Pickerel (E. niger), and Redfin and Grass Pickerels (E. americanus, with two subspecies).

The groups are separated by counting submandibular pores and branchiostegal rays. The paper provides a dichotomous key, which distinguishes these species using pore counts, branchiostegal ray counts, lateral line scale counts, vertebrae counts, and color pattern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Introductions.** Esocids have been widely moved for sport fishing or to control "trash" fish. Early examples include E. lucius, E. masquinongy, and E. niger introduced to Atlas Mountain impoundments in Morocco (E. lucius in 1935; others in 1964), E. niger in Ohio (1935), and E. masquinongy and E. a. vermiculatus in California (early 1890s, apparently unsuccessful). A second wave beginning in the 1960s spread northern pike and muskellunge into New England, the Southeast, and the Rocky Mountain states, and promoted the muskellunge × northern pike hybrid.

**Competition and hybridization.** Where northern pike and [[Muskellunge]] co-occur, muskellunge populations usually decline. Hybridization is frequent; in one sample of 69 specimens from Vermillion Lake, Ontario, six were hybrids. Where chain pickerel and redfin pickerel are sympatric, hybridization is extensive. In small North Carolina lakes, chain pickerel outnumbered redfin pickerel 8:1, while the reverse held in streams.

**Habitat preferences.**
- Northern pike: small lakes, shallow vegetated areas of large lakes, marshes, and sloughs; the only esocid to colonize arctic environments.
- Muskellunge: slow meandering streams and quiet parts of large rivers; more river-associated than lake-associated.
- Chain pickerel: larger ponds and lakes, clean shallow vegetated shoals; tolerates temperatures to about 35 °C, pH levels near lethal for most fishes, and salinity up to 22‰.
- Redfin pickerel: small, quiet, vegetated waters, often acidic and dark.
- Grass pickerel: small quiet waters, usually neutral to basic and silted.

**Impacts on salmonids.** Chain pickerel introduced into trout waters is a serious concern. One account reported that a reclaimed pond stocked with brook trout became "worthless for trout fishing" within a few years (Barr 1963), while another (Kendall and Goldsborough 1908) suggested pickerel reduced trout where trout were already declining.

**Community associations.** All four native species typically occur in fish communities containing centrarchids, catostomids, ictalurids, percids, and cyprinids.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy:** Four native esocid species plus one exotic (E. reicherti) occur in North America. The family Esocidae falls within Suborder Esocoidei. Earlier features used to separate esocoids are shared with other salmoniforms.
- **Northern pike:** Treated as a single, highly variable, circumpolar species. Populations on the two continents show no genetic divergence (protein and chromosome comparisons). The regional names do not reflect real differences. A silvery mutant ("silver pike") occurs irregularly.
- **Muskellunge:** Former subspecies (E. m. masquinongy, E. m. ohioensis, E. m. immaculatus) are likely one variable species, with pattern probably determined by environment. Some supposed "species" such as E. amentus proved to be pike × muskellunge hybrids.
- **Chain pickerel:** Historically synonymized with E. niger under many names (31 common names recorded). A silvery color mutant has been reported only in New York.
- **Redfin and grass pickerels:** Divided into two subspecies that intergrade in the south (Georgia, Florida, Alabama, Mississippi). They differ slightly in snout length, profile, and cardiform scale counts.
- **Distribution changes:** Natural ranges have been reshaped by introductions and habitat change. Muskellunge may have once extended farther west, to the Mississippi River and possibly to Kansas and Oklahoma.
- **Management implications:** Muskellunge may fare better where sympatric with only one smaller pickerel species. Chain pickerel introductions pose a serious threat to salmonid waters.
- **Future work:** Morphometric studies of chain pickerel and further taxonomic analysis of muskellunge and pike populations were in progress at the time of writing.
