# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program: A Quarter-Century Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a law review-style analysis (reproduced in a 2014 email, with OCR artifacts) of the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] ("Recovery Program"), a cooperative effort to recover four endangered native fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. The text traces the program's origins in conflict over the [[Endangered Species Act]] and water development, reviews its recovery criteria and costs, and weighs competing assessments of its success. The author concludes that the four species have not increased significantly but have not gone extinct, so the program's success depends on how "success" is defined.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin drains water from seven states, is the largest watershed in the southwest, and covers roughly one-twelfth of the contiguous United States. Ten freshwater fish species inhabit the basin; four are listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The four fishes have lived in the basin for more than three million years and show the highest rate of extinction risk of any fishes in the nation. Only five populations of Humpback Chub and two of Colorado Pikeminnow exist in the basin. No wild Razorback Sucker populations are known in the basin, and no reproducing populations of Bonytail Chub are known anywhere in the world.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies multiple, interacting causes of decline:

- Streamflow regulation by dams and reservoirs
- Water depletions and habitat modification
- Pollution
- Non-native fishes and altered food webs
- Parasites and intentional poisoning

These ecological pressures are compounded by water rights established through the [[Colorado River Compact]], federal law, court decisions, contracts, and regulatory guidelines, collectively termed the "Law of the River."

Conflict arose after the National Environmental Policy Act and the Endangered Species Act were passed in 1969 and 1973. The [[Colorado River Water Conservation District]] challenged the listing of Colorado Pikeminnow and Humpback Chub, and water developers challenged instream flow determinations by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] ("FWS"). By 1994, FWS biological opinions found that nearly 100 water projects could jeopardize the endangered fishes. In response, the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee]] was formed in 1984, leading to a cooperative agreement in 1988.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and goals**
- The Recovery Program rests on five elements: instream flow identification and protection, habitat restoration, nonnative fish management, propagation and stocking, research and monitoring, and information and education.
- It involves thirteen federal agencies, three western states, water users, power users, and environmental groups.
- The preeminent goal is to downlist the four species to threatened and ultimately delist them, using criteria on population number, population size, and biological characteristics.
- Example: Humpback Chub downlisting requires six distinct populations maintained over five years and one core population over 2,100 adults in the upper and lower basins. Delisting requires an additional three years of maintained populations.
- In 2002, FWS predicted downlisting dates of 2007 (Humpback Chub) and 2006 (Colorado Pikeminnow), with delisting by 2010 and 2013. As of 2012, these were pushed to 2020 and 2018 respectively. Downlisting of Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub was projected for 2020, with delisting in 2023.
- The program was extended through September 30, 2023.

**Costs and funding**
- Fiscal years 1999 to 2013 expenditures totaled $293,157,700, an average of $11,726,308 per year.
- Projecting the average over the next ten years gives a total of about $614 million over 35 years in current dollars, or over $58 million per species over the period.
- Funding sources: U.S. Bureau of Reclamation and FWS about 54%, power companies 37%, states 14%, other entities 3%, and appropriators 1%.

**Assessments of success**
- The program reports that it provides ESA compliance for approximately 2,025 water projects depleting more than 3.7 million acre-feet per year, with no lawsuits filed on that compliance.
- [[John Loomis]] estimated $ 519 million (as printed in the text) in net cost savings over two decades compared with litigating ESA compliance.
- FWS has found "sufficient progress" every year, although the term is undefined in program agreements.
- Critics include [[Robert W. Adler]], who argued that restoration efforts "are not working," and former Colorado wildlife commissioner Rick Enstrom, who called the program a "boondoggle." Critics also objected to removal of non-native sport fish.

**Conclusion**
- Populations of the four species have not increased significantly over 25 years, but none have gone extinct.
- No species is likely to be delisted before the program's thirtieth anniversary.
- Success depends on the metric: survival versus cost-effectiveness. The author notes that a single environmental disaster could still devastate populations and prevent delisting.
