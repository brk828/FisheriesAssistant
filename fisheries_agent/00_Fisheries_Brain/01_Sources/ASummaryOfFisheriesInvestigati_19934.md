# Fort Gibson Reservoir Fisheries Investigations, 1952–1956

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This job completion report from the Oklahoma investigations project (F-6-R-1) compiles fisheries data from [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]] for calendar years 1952 through August 31, 1956. It combines a 1952 [[Pre-Impoundment Survey]], seven [[Creel Census]] reports run by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and the [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Department]], [[Rotenone Sampling]] of fish populations, a [[Fish Tagging]] program, and [[Age and Growth Analysis]] of seven principal species. The summary tracks how the fishery changed after the reservoir was filled. Angler catch rates stayed fairly steady, while [[White Crappie]] and [[White Bass]] dominated the harvest and [[Gizzard Shad]] became the most numerous species by 1956. The report was prepared as a baseline for continued studies under the succeeding project.

## Background & Description

The reservoir was studied under several overlapping projects. The pre-impoundment survey was made by the Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory in 1952. Federal aid project F-4-R ran from September 1953 to August 1956 and produced Job reports F4R1, F4R2 and F4R3. The creel census began on May 1, 1952. Its data were collected as part of routine ranger duties on nine Tulsa District reservoirs. The Oklahoma Game and Fish Department census used more systematic monthly area surveys, road blocks at access points, and traffic counters to estimate fisherman-days.

Only lengths were recorded in the Corps census. Weights were estimated from length-weight curves. Black bass were grouped together, as were crappies and sunfishes, so the separate-species data from the state reports had to be regrouped for comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impoundment history.** The Grand River was closed by [[Fort Gibson Dam]] in September 1949. The pool was held near 525 ft msl until fall 1952. It was then raised to power pool elevation (554 ft msl) in spring 1953 and held at an average of 552.4 ft msl afterward (Table 4, Figure 1). The rising water produced the main ecological change in the reservoir, and the report repeatedly links growth and population shifts to it.

**Fish movements.** Some early [[Channel Catfish]] and [[White Bass]] were thought to have come from [[Grand Lake]] by passing over the spillways during floods of 1950 and 1951. This may have influenced the growth data for older year-classes.

**Community change.** Over time, black bass, [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] declined as a share of the sampled fish. [[Carp]], [[Freshwater Drum]] and [[Gizzard Shad]] increased. The report calls the shad increase a possible overpopulation concern.

## Key Findings

**Creel census (1952–1956)**
- Seven creel reports covered 11,998 anglers interviewed. Yearly interview totals were 543 (1952), 1,658 (1953), 2,621 (1954), 2,326, 1,401, 1,531 and 1,918.
- Estimated fisherman-days totaled 1,848,000 over 46 months: a daily average of 1,320, a monthly average of 40,173, and an annual average of 482,076.
- Angler success (percent of anglers catching at least one fish) ranged from 66.2% (F4R2, 1954–55) to 80.4% (F4R3, 1955–56), with an overall average of 69.8%.
- Catch rates averaged 0.52 fish per fisherman-hour, 2.7 fish per fisherman-day and 1.7 pounds per fisherman-day. The average trip lasted 5.3 hours.
- Annual harvest averaged 76.4 fish per acre and 45.9 pounds per acre. Harvest peaked in F4R3 at 155.2 fish per acre and 74.6 pounds per acre.

**Species composition of the angler harvest (average of seven reports)**

| Species | Percent of numbers | Percent of weight |
|---|---|---|
| Crappies (white and black) | 36.5 | 24.1 |
| White bass | 28.5 | 26.7 |
| Channel catfish | 10.7 | 16.0 |
| Sunfishes (combined) | 9.5 | 2.6 |
| Black basses | 5.4 | 8.5 |
| Carp | 4.6 | 9.0 |
| Drum | 4.0 | 6.3 |
| Flathead catfish | 0.4 | 3.9 |

- Crappies rose to 56.5% of numbers and 38.0% of weight in 1955–56. In 1952 they made up only 3.33% of numbers.
- White bass were 51.47% of numbers in 1952 and fell to 15.9% in 1955–56, but they stayed second to crappies in most years.
- Black bass fell to fifth place in the average. The report notes that the 1952 bass population was small because few fish had reached legal size.

**Growth histories (5,660 scale and spine samples)**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: Growth improved strongly in the rising-water period. Ages I–III kept improving through 1953 and then leveled off. Older fish appeared to be harmed by impoundment, but few were sampled.
- [[White Crappie]] (1,862 specimens): Peak growth for ages I–II came in 1953, and for ages III–IV in 1954. All age groups then declined.
- [[Black Crappie]] (346 individuals, six year-classes): Growth was much faster during rising water, dropped sharply in 1954, and was improving again by 1955.
- [[Channel Catfish]] (1,450 individuals, 13 year-classes): Wide length variation occurred within year-classes. Growth rose during impoundment and then generally declined, with the largest gains in older age groups.
- [[White Bass]] (726 individuals): None were older than four years. Growth improved steadily after 1949. The 1949 year-class grew unusually fast, similar to fish from Grand Lake.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (314 fish, ten year-classes): Impoundment's benefit was unclear. Older fish gained, but young fish grew less well by 1955 than before impoundment.
- [[Freshwater Drum]] (nine year-classes): Growth rose sharply in 1953 and dropped in 1954. By 1955 it was much better than pre-impoundment, though below the 1953 peak.

**Rotenone population sampling**
- Samples were taken in October 1953, September 1955, and July–August 1956. Two coves were treated each year, covering 7, 6.5 and 9.5 acres.
- Total fish recovered rose from 2,001 (1953) to 5,636 (1955) and 10,613 (1956).
- Game fish fell from 38.6% of the sample (1953) to 17.1% (1955) and 13.6% (1956). Largemouth bass fell from 19.2% to 2.8%.
- [[Bluegill]] were the main panfish. They made up 32.0% of the 1955 sample.
- [[Gizzard Shad]] rose from 31.7% (1953) to 17.3% (1955) and 60.1% (1956). The report calls the large-adult shad increase alarming.
- Rough fish rose from 7.9% to 19.8% (1955), then 11.1% (1956). Drum were the most common rough fish in 1956 (761 fish), with strong reproduction in 1955 and 1956.

**Fish tagging**
- In year one, 4,882 fish of 23 species were tagged. 105 were recovered: 39 (0.8%) by sampling gear and 66 (1.4%) by anglers. The overall recapture rate was 2.2%.
- In year two, 1,937 fish of 23 species were tagged, with 17 returned (0.88%).
- The report found tag returns too few for reliable harvest rates. Mortality from capture was high, so the program ended after two years. Creel census data were judged more reliable.

**Management outcome**
- The report recommended removing all length limits. On April 12, 1956, the legal length restriction was lifted in all Oklahoma public waters.
- The report notes that several important species were omitted and will be covered in future analyses.
