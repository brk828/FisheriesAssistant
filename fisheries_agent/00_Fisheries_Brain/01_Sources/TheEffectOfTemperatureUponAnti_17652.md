# The Effect of Temperature upon Antibody Production in Cold-Blooded Vertebrates

#herpetology #immunology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

K. A. Bisset of the [[University of Birmingham]] reports laboratory experiments on how temperature controls antibody production in cold-blooded vertebrates. The work used [[Common Frog]] (*Rana temporaria*) immunised with a killed suspension of [[Pseudomonas Fluorescens]]. The central finding is that antibody acquisition and antibody appearance in the blood are separate processes, and that the second is strongly suppressed at low temperature. Frogs immunised at 20 °C produced high agglutinin titres, which fell rapidly when they were moved to 8 °C and recovered on return to 20 °C. Frogs immunised at 8 °C produced no detectable antibodies until they were transferred to warmer conditions. The paper also links these results to field observations on [[European Perch]] and [[Powan]] infected by water bacteria, and it proposes that seasonal temperature change may cycle immunity in fish.

## Background & Description

Bisset frames the study around the balance between bacteria and host in cold-blooded animals. Raising the temperature increases both the virulence of the microbe and the defensive activity of the host. Antibody production is inhibited below about 12 °C, but natural resistance is higher at low temperatures. The paper notes that earlier reports on temperature and bacterial disease in cold-blooded animals were contradictory. Examples include [[Red-Leg Disease]] in frogs, which was reported to be arrested at low temperatures, and infected caterpillars and locusts, which showed opposite responses.

**Preliminary observations.** The work began with perch and powan in natural conditions. Infection by ordinarily saprophytic water bacteria decreased in summer as water temperature rose and increased in autumn. Fish appeared to clear certain bacterial species at high temperature, yet were infected again by the end of winter. The author inferred that immunity acquired in warm months is lost when temperature falls.

**Methods.** Frogs were used for serology because they tolerate handling better than fish, and their immunological reactions were assumed to be identical to those of fish. Animals were immunised intramuscularly, usually over six alternate or successive days. Serum was collected after decapitation. Agglutination was read at a standard set of final dilutions (1:5, 1:10, 1:25, 1:50), with a strong reaction at the top dilution recorded as "1:50+".

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature threshold:** Antibody production is inhibited below about 12 °C, while natural resistance rises at low temperature.
- **Seasonal cycle in fish:** Infection of perch and powan by water bacteria falls in summer and rises in autumn. Immunity is presumed to be lost over winter.
- **Interpretation of earlier conflicting reports:** Bisset argues that the contradictions arise from the balance between an established host–parasite equilibrium and a temperature shift. If the bacteria are antigenically unfamiliar, infection progresses more readily at high temperature. If an equilibrium already exists, warming disturbs it, and the bacteria are usually eliminated unless their virulence increases sharply.
- **Immunity cycling:** Under natural conditions, immunity is presumed to be raised and lowered with seasonal temperature. It is not thought to require a long immunisation period to restore it.

## Key Findings

**Experimental series (agglutinin titres against *Pseudomonas fluorescens*):**

- **Expt. I (6 successive-day inoculations, 4 frogs per temperature group):** Frogs kept at 20 °C throughout gave 1:50+ titres. Frogs immunised at 8 °C and kept there gave nil. Frogs moved from 20 °C to 8 °C lost their titres (1:5 and nil). Frogs immunised at 8 °C and moved to 20 °C produced 1:10 and 1:25. Antibodies appeared without further immunisation after transfer to warmth.
- **Expt. II (2 or 4 inoculations at 8 °C, then 20 °C for a week):** One frog gave 1:5 and two gave nil in the first group. The second group gave 1:5, 1:5 and 1:10. Residual antigen is therefore unlikely to explain the antibodies.
- **Expt. III (3 frogs immunised at 8 °C, moved to 20 °C 48 h after the course):** Titres were 1:10, 1:25 and 1:25. Antibody titre was roughly proportional to the prior immunisation. No antibodies were detectable while the animals stayed cold.
- **Expt. IV (warm to cold transfer):** Nine frogs immunised at 20 °C and moved to 8 °C fell from 1:50+ to 1:25–1:5 within two days, then fell only slowly. Nine frogs kept at 20 °C maintained 1:50+ after 3, 7 and 14 days.
- **Expt. V (20 °C, 1 week at 8 °C, then 4 days at 20 °C):** All five frogs gave 1:50+. A repeat with three frogs gave identical results. Titres therefore recovered rapidly on return to warmth.
- **Expt. VI (liver extracts):** Livers from three frogs with 1:50+ serum and two frogs with no agglutination were tested at dilutions of 1:12, 1:24 and 1:50. No agglutination occurred in any group, so circulating antibodies could not be detected in tissue.

**Conclusions and proposals:**

1. Frogs immunised at 8 °C produce no serum agglutinins. When moved to 20 °C without further immunisation, they begin to produce them.
2. Frogs immunised at 20 °C produce high titres. Transfer to 8 °C reduces the titre sharply, and return to 20 °C restores it.
3. The author suggests that similar phenomena occur in natural fresh-water fish.
4. Acquiring the potential for antibody production is distinct from producing antibodies and having them appear in the circulation. In cold-blooded animals, the production stage is the more temperature-sensitive.
5. The techniques may be useful for wider immunological questions.
