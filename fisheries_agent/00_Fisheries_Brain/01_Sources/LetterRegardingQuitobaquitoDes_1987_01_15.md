# Letter Regarding Quitobaquito Desert Pupfish at Finley Tank, Audubon Research Ranch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biology #cyprinodontidae #desert-pupfish #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary
This 1987 letter from Dr. Mark R. Stromberg (Research Ranch Sanctuary) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service addresses a conservation dilemma involving the federally endangered [[Quitobaquito Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*, treated here under the broader [[Cyprinodon Macularius]] complex). A population of pupfish, originally derived from **Quitobaquito Spring** stock and introduced to **Finley Tank** approximately nine years prior (founded from ~40 individuals), had persisted successfully in an isolated desert tank on Audubon's Research Ranch Sanctuary. The USFWS requested removal ("execution") of this population, citing concerns over stock purity and risk of genetic contamination to nearby reintroduction efforts. The letter argues against removal, proposes genetic/morphological verification via electrophoretic analysis, and raises broader questions about the scientific and conservation value of small founder populations.

## Background & Description
- No direct morphometric data are reported in this letter; rather, it references **ongoing** analysis.
- **Dr. Jeff Mitton** (University of Colorado) and graduate student **Daphne Rainey** were conducting **morphological and electrophoretic analyses** on samples presumed to represent Quitobaquito-derived stock from Finley Tank.
- A request was made to collect **30 specimens** directly from **Quitobaquito Spring** (source population at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]) to serve as a genetic/morphological reference standard for comparison against the Finley Tank population.
- The central scientific question was whether Finley Tank pupfish are **genetically/morphologically identical** to true Quitobaquito stock, or represent a **contaminated/mixed stock** (possibly hybridized with *Cyprinodon macularius* proper during transfer/storage).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Finley Tank is a small, isolated artificial impoundment on the Audubon Research Ranch Sanctuary, described as difficult to locate and access, aiding protection from poaching/trespass.
- **Watershed context:** The catchment above Finley Tank is very small (**<5 acres**), and the tank uses only ~10% of its dam capacity even in extreme flood events (including a probable 100-year flood in 1983), rendering natural downstream contamination of the **San Pedro River watershed** effectively impossible.
- **Population founding:** The Finley Tank population was established roughly **9 years prior** (i.e., ~1978) from an estimated founding group of **~40 individuals**, sourced from Quitobaquito Spring stock.
- **Comparative reserve populations:** The letter references three known populations of the taxon: (1) native/source population at **Quitobaquito Spring**, (2) the reintroduced/reserve population at **Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument**, and (3) the putative reserve population at **Finley Tank**.
- **Alternative refugium discussed:** USFWS proposed an additional captive refuge at the **Dexter National Fish Hatchery**, which the author contrasts unfavorably against Finley Tank due to hatchery costs, disease risk, variable management, and theft vulnerability.
- **Risk factors identified:**
  1. Illegal translocation of Finley Tank fish into sensitive reintroduction sites near Organ Pipe.
  2. Unprecedented flood events potentially moving fish into the San Pedro drainage (assessed as very low risk given tank hydrology).

## Key Findings
- **Regulatory status:** The fish were classified as federally endangered under the USFWS Final Rule (Federal Register 1986, Vol. 51, No. 61: 10843), which also designated the Finley Tank population as a "valuable reserve population" for Organ Pipe populations — creating an internal policy contradiction with the simultaneous order for population removal.
- **Provenance uncertainty:** A 1979 report (B. Knyard, National Park Service) asserted the Finley Tank fish were pure Quitobaquito stock, but subsequent rumors suggested possible contamination during transfer/storage, motivating the need for genetic verification.
- **Conservation-genetics rationale:** The author argues that studying a stable population founded from ~40 individuals over ~9 years offers rare long-term empirical data on genetic outcomes of small founder populations — a topic of broad relevance to conservation biology given how often reintroduction programs must rely on small founding stocks.
- **Policy stance:** The author references his own prior published position (*Biological Conservation* 1986, 35:97-110) that (a) the subspecies concept is scientifically questionable, and (b) reintroductions should use source populations as geographically/genetically proximate as possible to the release site — a position he notes is broadly aligned with USFWS's own approach.
- **Proposed resolution:** Collection of up to 30 specimens from Quitobaquito Spring (verbally approved for state permitting by **Dean Hendrickson**, AZ Game and Fish) to allow Dr. Mitton's lab to conduct comparative morphological/electrophoretic analysis, the outcome of which would determine whether Finley Tank continues as a reserve population or is restocked/replaced with verified native stock.
- **Institutional stakeholders:** National Audubon Society (Research Ranch Sanctuary), U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Park Service (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument), University of Colorado (Mitton lab), and Arizona Game and Fish Department.
