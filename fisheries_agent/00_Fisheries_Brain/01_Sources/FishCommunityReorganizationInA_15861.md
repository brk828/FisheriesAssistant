# Fish Community Reorganization in a Perturbed Stream: The Virgin River, Utah-Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a research proposal submitted by [[Arizona State University]] (Department of Zoology and Center for Environmental Studies) to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Region II, for a five-year study of fish community recovery in the [[Virgin River]]. The study follows an accidental [[Rotenone]] (ichthyocide) kill in October 1988, which was intended to remove non-native [[Red Shiner]] from a 34-km reach but killed native fishes over more than 100 linear km. The proposal aims to document how the mainstream fish community reorganizes after catastrophe, using morphological and genetic (allozyme and mtDNA) data, and to derive recommendations for future management operations. Requested funds for the first two years total $135,927 (first-year total $67,713.05; second-year total $68,214.25).

## Background & Description

The Virgin River system supports six native fish taxa, several of which exist as two morphological "forms": one in the mainstream and one in tributaries or springs. Three species, [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]], show this pattern, and the taxonomic status of these forms is unresolved. Two species of special concern were involved in the event:

- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), federally listed as Endangered.
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), a Candidate for listing.

Other native species include [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), which is rare in the channel and most abundant in tributaries. Table 1 of the source lists the six native taxa with habitat notes; the source gives no further taxon data beyond these.

Historic collections date to the Wheeler Expedition (1871-74). Later work by [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1934-1959) and [[John E. Deacon]] and students (from the 1960s) produced a long-term quantitative monitoring database for woundfin, which the authors identify as a key resource. The river is arid and flashy, and the lower reaches are intermittent under natural conditions. Red shiners were established below the [[Narrows]] for at least 25 years and expanded upstream after an irrigation reservoir on Quail Creek stabilized flows in the early 1980s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**The ichthyocide event.** Remedial rotenone treatment in September-October 1988 was designed to be detoxified at a barrier below the Washington Fields Diversion (WFD). The toxicant travelled farther and faster than expected, and detoxification began late (reportedly about 24 hours after planned). Fish died at least as far as Mesquite, Nevada, and possibly farther (the meeting summary suggests somewhere between Mesquite and Riverside). No quantitative data on mortality were available at the time of writing (24 October 1988).

**Salvage and sampling.** Woundfin and chub were salvaged before the treatment, but no other native species were salvaged. Baseline sampling in late October and early November 1988 found native fishes in small numbers, with reproductive population sizes sharply reduced.

**Recolonization pathways.** Repopulation of the 34-km reach could come from three sources: (1) survivors in situ; (2) downstream dispersal from above WFD; and (3) recolonization from tributaries or irrigation diversions unaffected by rotenone. Dispersal from above WFD was expected mainly as larval drift after 1989 reproduction. The identity of fishes above and below WFD had not been established.

**Management concerns.** The authors note that native fish communities are tightly integrated, so loss of species could alter population structure and recovery. Genetic bottlenecking and replacement by poorly adapted tributary forms are also possible. A "worst case" scenario is an explosive increase in red shiners, which could block native recovery.

**Contextual data from the meeting summary (3 November 1988).** Approximately 5,600 woundfin were salvaged in August (1,600 to Dexter National Fish Hatchery, 4,000 returned to the Virgin at Littlefield, and about 1,200 Gila held above WFD). The summary lists a 21-person participant roster from state, federal and university agencies.

## Key Findings

**Research objectives** (as stated in the proposal):
- Assess impacts of the 1988 event on the fish community, including the abundance of "forms," genetic diversity and geographic range.
- Track re-establishment of the community through morphological and genetic data.
- Test whether tributary forms have hybridized with or invaded the mainstream, and whether bilateral asymmetry indicates developmental instability.
- Compare current collections with historic ones to assess long-term variation.

**Sampling design:**
- One-time samples of six tributaries: Beaver Dam Wash, Santa Clara River, La Verkin Creek, Ash Creek, North Fork Virgin River and Middle Fork Virgin River. These must be collected before the 1989 reproductive season (scheduled for November 1988).
- Target of 30 to 50 individuals per species per locale, for statistical reliability.
- Mainstream sampling to track each year class for at least two generation-times per species (2 to 5 years).

**Methods:**
- *Morphometrics:* automated image-based measurement on microcomputers, with analysis of image data on an IBM 3090 supercomputer. Analyses include principal components, sheared PCA, cluster and multiple discriminant analyses.
- *Allozymes:* protein electrophoresis, which is cheaper but has limited diagnostic power at the population level.
- *mtDNA:* restriction endonuclease analysis of maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA, which the authors state evolves 5 to 10 times faster than single-copy nuclear DNA. Four-base enzymes give higher resolution for population-level variation.

**Timeline and personnel:**
- Five-year formal study, with decreasing effort in years 3 to 5. Genetic monitoring is recommended for a further five years. The source states that generation times range from 1 to 2 years for minnows and 2 to 3 years for suckers and chub, so equilibrium for long-lived species could take a decade.
- Four co-investigators: [[W. L. Minckley]] (PI), [[Michael E. Douglas]], [[Thomas E. Dowling]] and [[Paul C. Marsh]].
- Two graduate students in years 1-2, with a third added in years 3-5.

**Budget (as stated):**
- Year 1: direct costs $45,445.00; indirect (49%) $22,268.05; total $67,713.05.
- Year 2: direct costs $45,175.00; indirect (51%) $23,039.25; total $68,214.25.
- No capital equipment is requested. [[Arizona State University]] will provide field and laboratory equipment and the supercomputer.

**Key research questions:**
- Is the river a single genetic unit, or a series of discrete units?
- Will fish recover through repopulation by survivors or through recolonization from tributaries?
- Do hatchery and refugium stocks (woundfin at Dexter; chub in a pond below Quail Creek) carry more genetic variation than the extant wild stocks?

**Recommendations from the attached statement:** immediate sampling of the mainstream and tributaries, sampling of survivors to assess the genetics of parents of the next generation, detailed documentation of the operation, and creation of backup (hatchery) populations of woundfin and Gila before the second treatment.
