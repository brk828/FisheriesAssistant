# Connecticut Fish Faunal Documentation: Rare Marine Records, Upper Thames Estuary Seasonality, and an Over-Wintering Mosquitofish Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae #cyclopteridae #syngnathidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled report (Jacobson, 1980, Northeast Utilities Environmental Laboratory) assembles four studies on the fishes of Connecticut. The core paper, by [[Paul Perlmutter]] and [[Charles Steinmetz]], documents fish collections made near the [[Millstone Point Nuclear Power Station]] in Waterford from May 1969 to December 1979, adding 17 species not previously reported from [[Long Island Sound]]. Further papers cover seasonal use of the upper [[Thames Estuary]] by fishes, a localized over-wintering population of [[Mosquitofish]] in New Haven, and additional freshwater records for the state. The work is primarily an inventory of species occurrence, distribution and seasonality.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a historical account of Connecticut ichthyology. The first catalogue was compiled by Rev. J. H. Linsley in 1844. Later state publications by [[Thomson et al. 1978]] (Saltwater Fishes of Connecticut, Bulletin 105) and [[Whitworth et al. 1968]] (Freshwater Fishes of Connecticut, Bulletin 101) form the baseline lists. Thomson et al. (1978) listed 119 marine species, including the white catfish. The compilation notes that Connecticut's fish fauna has been studied through state and federal funding, and that several independent studies have yielded a common result: the species list for the region is a function of collecting effort.

The Millstone study drew on adult, juvenile and larval specimens. Collection methods included impingement on power station intake screens (36% of adults and juveniles), beach seines (41%), otter trawls (21%), gill nets (2%), and plankton tows (all larvae). About 80% of species collected are deposited at the Millstone Environmental Laboratory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Millstone Point area.** The study area covered six miles of shoreline near the power station. The [[Millstone Bight]] is the focal sampling area.

**Long Island Sound as a mixing zone.** The authors describe the Sound as a "mixing bowl" for fishes. A year-round resident population tolerates both high summer and cold winter temperatures. The [[Gulf Stream]] brings warm-water surface pathways for southern and tropical species in summer, while cold-water northern species arrive in winter under the influence of the [[Labrador Current]]. Summer visitors predominate.

**Upper Thames Estuary.** The upper 5 km of the estuary lies 3.5 to 20 km downstream of the Quinebaug-Shetucket and Yantic River dams, which act as complete barriers to upstream fish movement. Salinities were rarely below 10 ppt below 5 m, and annual water temperatures ranged from 0 to 28 °C. Freshwater conditions between the estuary and the dams were often unsuitable because of low oxygen, low flow, high temperature and pollutants.

**Beaver Ponds, New Haven.** The Campus Pond (about 2.6 ha) and Campus Lagoon (about 32.2 ha) are connected by an underground pipeline and drain to the [[West River]]. The Beaver Pond (about 6.4 ha) is adjacent. The Campus Pond receives heated effluent (about 10 °C) from the college power plant, raising its temperature 2-5 °C above surrounding waters.

## Key Findings

### 1. Seventeen new marine species for Connecticut (Perlmutter & Steinmetz)

- A total of 406,532 adult and juvenile fishes and 450,156 larval fishes were collected, representing 54 families, 96 genera and 103 species.
- Of 119 species listed by Thomson et al. (1978), 86 were documented by this collection.
- Fourteen of the 17 new species were collected as adults and/or juveniles. Three, including the American plaice, were known only as larvae.
- All species except the radiated shanny (*Ulvaria subbifurcata*) have ranges that include Connecticut coastal waters.

**Key conclusion:** Most new species are considered strays. The seasnail is the exception. It was reported as rare south of Cape Cod, but was collected regularly at Millstone and is considered common on the Connecticut coast. Its secretive habit probably kept it undetected.

### 2. Range comparisons

- Several southerly species were previously listed as rare or uncommon for [[New Jersey]] and [[Long Island]] coasts (Alperin and Shaefer 1965; Milstein and Thomas 1976). The cornetfish, permit, flying gurnard, blue runner, short bigeye and bigeye scad were noted in this way. Millstone results confirmed these species were very rare to uncommon in Connecticut.
- The radiated shanny extends its range southward, to Newfoundland through Cape Cod, based on 1,821 larvae collected in the present study.

### 3. Upper Thames Estuary seasonality (Whitworth & Marsh)

- Study conducted October 1972 to November 1973, using gill nets set at two-week intervals, and seines (June-September 1973, three sites). Data from the previous four years were also summarised.
- Seasons were defined as winter (January-March), spring (April-June), summer (July-September) and fall (October-December).
- Saltwater species were most numerous in spring and summer, and fewest in winter.
- Freshwater species were more numerous in spring and summer than in fall and winter. Freshwater species found in only one season occurred only in spring and summer.
- Freshwater species made up over 94% of individuals in gill nets and over 61% of those taken by seine. Authors attribute this to the dams, which prevent upstream movement of marine fishes, and note that freshwater fishes had access to a limited freshwater habitat.
- Table 3 and 4 show the species percentages in each season (the percentage of freshwater species that utilized the estuary in spring was 91%; the value for salt water was 33%).

### 4. Over-wintering mosquitofish in Connecticut (Steinmetz & Boehm)

- An accidental find on October 12, 1973, of a large [[Gambusia affinis]] population in the Campus Pond, New Haven. This was north of the 40° N native boundary and beyond the New Jersey limit cited for surviving populations.
- No successful introductions had previously been reported for Connecticut.
- During February 1976 to February 1977, 243 fish were captured. Captures were restricted to the Beaver Ponds, with no sightings in 19 West River sites.
- Mosquitofish survive over winter only in the Campus Pond, where heated water keeps temperatures 2-5 °C above ambient. The pond never completely froze in 1976 or 1977, while the Campus Lagoon and Beaver Pond were fully ice-covered in 1977.
- Populations in the cooler ponds are replenished each spring by migration from the Campus Pond. No evidence was found of hibernation in the mud.
- Mosquitofish had survived at least six winters at the time of writing and are considered fairly cold-resistant. The authors suggest the population may become a source for introductions elsewhere in Connecticut.

### 5. Additional freshwater and adventitious records (Whitworth, Minta & Orciari)

- Introduced freshwater species include the bowfin (*Amia calva*), stocked as a sport fish but not reproducing, and yellow bullhead, numerous in Candlewood Lake since 1976.
- Channel catfish is more widespread in the Connecticut River than previously reported.
- Central mudminnow was recorded occasionally on the intake screens of the Connecticut Yankee plant since 1976.
- Adventitious marine visitors to the Connecticut River include silver perch, spot, northern kingfish and lookdown.
- Gizzard shad, possibly moving up the coast from mid-Atlantic stocks, was recorded in the Connecticut River.
