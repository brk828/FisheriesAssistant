# Nutritive Value of Buffalo Fish

## Executive Summary
This 1962 study by Marinus C. Kik (University of Arkansas) investigates the nutritional value of protein derived from the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), a pond-reared catostomid fish of growing importance to [[Southern United States Aquaculture|fish farming in the southern United States]]. Using albino rat feeding trials over a ten-week period, the paper demonstrates that buffalo fish protein — whether used as a sole protein source or as a supplement to cereal grains (rice, corn, barley, wheat) — significantly improves growth rates and [[Protein Efficiency Ratio (PER)|protein efficiency ratios (P.E.R.)]]. The work also reports a full amino acid profile of buffalo fish flesh, identifying it as a lysine-, threonine-, tryptophan-, and methionine-rich protein source capable of correcting deficiencies common in cereal-based diets.

## Background & Description
While this is primarily a nutritional/biochemical study rather than a morphological one, the paper provides key biometric and compositional details of the study specimens:

- **Species**: Bigmouth Buffalo, *[[Ictiobus Cyprinellus]]* (Valenciennes)
- **Source**: Pond-reared specimens from Gumwood Fish Farm (England, Arkansas) and the Fish Farmers Cooperative Association (Dumas, Arkansas)
- **Age**: Three growing seasons old
- **Average weight**: 3–4 lb
- **Preparation**: Dressed to remove bones, fat, and inedible material; only human-consumption-grade portions retained, frozen, then dried and defatted with petroleum ether to produce a fish meal
- **Protein content (fat-free basis)**: 79.1% (reported elsewhere as 79.01%)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] belongs to the family [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), a group of freshwater fishes native to North America.
- The specimens used in this study were **pond-reared** under aquaculture conditions rather than wild-caught, reflecting the species' growing role in **fish farming operations in the rice-growing regions of Arkansas** and the broader southern United States.
- The species is associated with the use of **reservoirs and ponds in rice-farming areas** as dual-purpose aquaculture systems (per cited reference: Green & Mullins, 1959, *Use of Reservoirs for Production of Fish in the Rice Areas of Arkansas*).
- Ecologically, buffalo fish are benthic omnivores typical of warm, slow-moving freshwater systems, though this paper does not detail feeding ecology, instead focusing on their downstream value as a human/animal food protein source.
- The study situates buffalo fish within a broader food-systems context, linking [[Freshwater Aquaculture]] practices to human nutrition and livestock feed formulation, paralleling the roles of other studied fish protein sources such as [[Haddock]], [[Herring]], and [[Dilis Fish Flour]] (anchovy-based flour from the Philippines).

## Key Findings

### Growth and Protein Efficiency Trials
- Buffalo fish protein, added at 1%, 3%, or 5% levels (either as a **replacement** for cereal protein or an **addition** to cereal rations), consistently and significantly increased rat growth and [[Protein Efficiency Ratio (PER)|P.E.R.]] across nearly all tested grains:
  - **Whole rice**: +45.5% gain, +16.5% P.E.R. (1% replacement)
  - **Milled rice**: +67.3% gain, +67.4% P.E.R. (3% addition, pair-fed design)
  - **Whole rice flour**: up to +50.9% gain
  - **Milled rice flour**: up to +95.4% gain, +53.2% P.E.R.
  - **Whole yellow corn**: +199.0% gain, +94.5% P.E.R. (3% addition)
  - **Milled yellow corn**: +824.0% gain (3% addition) — largest relative response observed
  - **Milled white corn**: +757.7% gain, +485.0% P.E.R. (1% addition) — greatest overall response among tested cereals
  - **Whole barley**: up to +51.1% gain
  - **Whole wheat**: up to +127.1% gain
  - **Milled (enriched) wheat flour**: up to +93.6% gain, +68.5% P.E.R.
- **Milled white corn and whole/milled yellow corn** showed the greatest overall benefit from buffalo fish supplementation, consistent with corn proteins' known deficiency in lysine and tryptophan.
- Buffalo fish alone (9% protein level) supported an average gain of 177.5 g and a P.E.R. of 2.14 — higher than whole egg, though somewhat lower than whole milk, nonfat dry milk solids, or lactalbumin.
- A true **supplementary protein relationship** was demonstrated between buffalo fish and milled rice: the combination produced greater gains than either protein source fed alone (pair-fed Ration 7 vs. Rations 4 and 20).
- Combining buffalo fish with **L-lysine and DL-threonine** produced the largest measured response in the entire study: milled white corn + 0.5% L-lysine + 0.2% DL-threonine yielded a **1902% increase in weight gain** and 865% increase in P.E.R. relative to control.

### Biological Value / Nitrogen Metabolism (Mitchell Method)
| Ration | Biologic Value (%) | True Digestibility (%) | Net Protein Utilization (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole rice (control) | 88.1 | 85.7 | 75.5 |
| Whole rice + 3% buffalo fish | 91.7 | 93.6 | 85.8 (+13.6%*) |
| Milled rice (control) | 72.7 | 58.8 | 64.1 |
| Milled rice + 3% buffalo fish | 89.5 | 92.9 | 83.1 (+29.7%*) |
| Buffalo fish alone (9% protein) | 78.0 | 97.7 | 76.2 |

*Statistically significant, P = 0.05

- Buffalo fish supplementation significantly improved **net protein utilization** in both whole and milled rice diets, explaining the observed growth advantages via reduced nitrogen losses and more efficient protein utilization.

### Broader Implications
- Buffalo fish protein is proposed as a **valuable low-cost supplement** for cereal-based diets, particularly in regions where both rice cultivation and pond aquaculture co-occur (e.g., Arkansas).
- Findings align with prior research on other fish protein supplements (haddock, herring, Dilis fish flour) confirming that fish proteins broadly complement cereal-grain deficiencies in [[Lysine]], [[Threonine]], and [[Tryptophan]].
- The study reinforces the potential of underutilized regional aquaculture species — like the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] — as sustainable protein sources for both human diets and animal feed formulations.
