# Geochemical Interpretation of Water Analyses from Tennessee Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1944 paper, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (reprinted 1947), examines how the total alkalinity, carbonate alkalinity, and pH of unpolluted Tennessee streams relate to the [[Geological Formations]] forming their beds. Using 371 water analyses from an equal number of localities, the author recognizes sixteen geological formations as individually effective in determining stream water character. The work was done during routine [[Biological Survey]] work and was supported by the Tennessee Division of Game and Fish. The central conclusion is that stream chemistry can be predicted from the dominant bedrock of a small, single-formation drainage, a relationship the author considers unusual in published fisheries surveys.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier geochemical work, including [[Palmer 1911]] on the geochemical interpretation of water analyses, and [[Powers]] studies (1930, 1939, 1941) of low-alkalinity "mountain water" in East Tennessee and of [[Norris Lake]] water. The concept central to the paper is "alkali reserve": soluble carbonate from bedrock reacts with free carbon dioxide to form bicarbonate, so the characteristics of a geological formation are reflected in total alkalinity, carbonate and bicarbonate levels, and pH.

Methods were titration-based. Total alkalinity was measured with 0.025 N H₂SO₄ using methyl orange indicator. Carbonate alkalinity was measured with N/44 NaOH using phenolphthalein indicator. Results are in parts per million (p.p.m.), with average pH reported per region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Soft-water formations:** Crystalline Archaean rocks, the Pre-Cambrian [[Unicoi Formation]] of the Great Smokies, and sandstone-shale units (Chilhowie, Conasauga-Watauga, Pennsylvanian Lee and Jellico-Briceville) yield low alkalinity, typically 0 to about 23 p.p.m., and acidic to near-neutral pH.
- **Hard-water formations:** The [[Knox Dolomite]] (140.0 p.p.m. average alkalinity, pH 7.85), the Ordovician (average alkalinity 9.00 to 104.0 p.p.m., pH about 7.8 to 7.9 in the reported regions), and the Mississippian formations (up to 114.2 p.p.m.) produce alkaline, "hard-water" streams.
- **Dilution in large rivers:** The [[Cumberland River]] headwaters flow from insoluble rock, and its mainstem alkalinity (about 65 p.p.m.) is lowered by dilution, while adjacent tributaries in the [[Nashville Basin]] reach 150 p.p.m. or more.
- **Sequatchie River:** A stream cutting into the Ordovician and Knox Dolomite within Pennsylvanian plateau rocks shows more than 100 p.p.m. total alkalinity, about ten times the adjacent soft plateau waters.
- **Comparison with other states:** Studies in New Hampshire, Illinois, Wisconsin, and Michigan encountered more uniform bedrock and narrower chemical ranges, and Minnesota's [[Lake Superior]] North Shore showed moderate, uniform alkalinity.
- **Local modifiers:** Pollution, liming of adjacent soils, and local limestone outcrops can override the bedrock signal in small streams.

## Key Findings

- Total alkalinity of unpolluted Tennessee streams is generally related to the geology of stream beds and soil provinces.
- Sixteen geological formations were identified as individually effective in determining stream water character, based on 371 localities.
- Chemistry can be predicted from a geological map: survey members could estimate stream chemistry from the predominant bedrock of a drainage.
- The relationship is clearest in small streams flowing within a single stratum; large rivers are diluted.
- The study provides a tabulated correlation (Table 1) of average total alkalinity, carbonate alkalinity, pH, and station counts for each formation. Station counts range from 5 (Pleistocene loess) to 56 (Mississippian Tullahoma-Ft. Payne), and the counts for the two Ordovician-related and Knox rows are 33 and 51, respectively.
- Ten or more recognizable bands of acid, neutral, or alkaline water run east to west across the state (Figure 1).
- Nearly all 34 limestone-producing counties in Tennessee lie within the range of highly alkaline streams (Whitlatch, 1941).
- The author recommends confirming these correlations with complete chemical analyses of rocks from each formation.
