# Effects of Agriculture on Stream Fauna in Central Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This EPA Environmental Research Laboratory project summary, based on work from 1978 through 1980, examines how increasing agricultural land use alters the [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] and fish communities of small streams in [[Central Indiana]]. Fourteen stream segments spanned a range from almost no agricultural influence to intense agriculture, including drainage from [[Animal Feed Lots]]. The study finds that agricultural stress produces a staged response: biomass may rise first, then fish and insect communities shift abruptly toward tolerant taxa. The [[Riparian Buffer Zone]] emerges as a key protective feature.

## Background & Description

The effects of diffuse agricultural activity on water quality are usually assumed but rarely measured. The study was part of the Indiana Heartland [[Model Implementation Program]], a joint effort of the [[Environmental Protection Agency]] and the U.S. Department of Agriculture to demonstrate the benefits of [[Best Management Practices]]. The biological component, carried out by DePauw University, monitored four attributes:

- benthic macroinvertebrates
- fish populations
- riparian vegetation patterns
- stream morphology and habitat

Sampling stations were placed near the bases of six subtributaries of [[Eagle Creek]] (60%–75% cropland), near a fixed monitoring station on [[Finley Branch]], and at the bases and midpoints of the north and south forks of [[Stotts Creek]] (53%–58% cropland) and the lower main stem. [[Rattlesnake Creek]], a largely forested watershed, served as a control in case suitable unaffected tributaries were absent from the study streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community structure and habitat.** Habitat structure was fairly uniform among stations and correlated poorly with community parameters. Community metrics were examined against habitat structure, [[Horton Stream Order]] (no correlation), and mean station depth. In 1978, mean depth correlated positively with [[Standing Crop]].

**Stream morphology.** Sediment input causes aggradation, increasing channel width and decreasing depth. Mean pool depth was negatively correlated with percent cropland in subwatersheds (r = -.912). Pools flanked by woods were roughly 50% deeper than pools next to altered land (residential, meadow, or cultivated). Sub-watersheds ranged from 25 to 75 km² in drainage area.

**Buffer function.** Streams were consistently deeper where altered land was separated from the channel by natural grass or tree strips. The authors conclude the buffer is especially important for small streams, though the results were not clear-cut and buffer width was not controlled.

**Water quality pollutants.** Nonpoint-source inorganic sediment tended to reduce macroinvertebrate density, except for [[Chironomidae]], without greatly changing composition. Point-source pollution altered taxonomic composition. Affected sites were dominated by Chironomidae, [[Oligochaeta]], and Nematoda. Pastured cattle with stream access produced a similar pattern.

## Key Findings

**Fish communities**

- Fish were sampled twice yearly by [[Electric Seine]]. Analyses included [[Shannon Diversity Index]], evenness, [[Trophic Structure]] grouping, and [[Cluster Analysis]].
- Rattlesnake Creek standing crop was 73 kg/ha at the upper station and 173 kg/ha downstream. Shannon diversities at both stations ranged from 1.8 to 2.5.
- Rattlesnake Creek trophic structure was over 25% piscivores (mostly [[Centrarchidae]]), 40%–60% insectivores, and 25%–30% herbivores, omnivores, and detritivores.
- Agricultural sites had lower diversity and smaller fish, mainly because minnows dominated.
- Sites influenced by swine feed lots were dominated by detritivores, omnivores, and herbivores.
- Spearman's correlation between cropland and piscivore plus insectivore percentage was -.714 for Eagle Creek and -.90 for Stotts Creek.

**Benthic macroinvertebrates**

- Insects dominated the benthos. The best-ranked sites on Eagle, Stotts, and Rattlesnake Creeks, with 10–20 insect families each, served as benchmarks.
- Chironomidae were major components in May and June at all sites. They declined in July and August at "good" sites but stayed dense at "poor" sites.
- Benthic diversity correlated negatively with percent cropland in Eagle Creek subwatersheds (r = -.771).
- After chironomid decline at good sites, [[Hydropsychidae]] (Trichoptera) became dominant, with contributions from Baetidae, Elmidae, and Simuliidae.
- Detectable benthic recovery followed the end of bridge building and bulldozing, but not in areas with chronic problems.

**Synthesis of agricultural stages (Table 2 and Conclusions)**

1. **Initial stage:** Biomass of fish and macroinvertebrates may increase without major compositional change.
2. **Intermediate stage:** Chironomids become dominant among insects, and the fish community stays dominated by piscivores and insectivores, possibly with greater biomass.
3. **Heavy stage:** A sudden, pronounced shift occurs as bass, sunfish, and some insectivores are lost and omnivores, detritivores, and herbivores take over. Standing crop changes little, but non-chironomid insect families become much reduced.
4. **Additional point-source stress** (such as feed lots or landfills): causes further losses of macroinvertebrate diversity and density and lower fish standing crops.

**Methodological conclusions**

- Fish and macroinvertebrates gave parallel evaluations, but combining both was more informative than either alone.
- Standing crop alone was of limited value for chronic effects. The best fish indicator was the proportion of piscivores and insectivores combined with biomass.
- Biotic response was non-linear: incremental water-quality gains did not always produce visible community improvement until a critical threshold was crossed.

**Recommendations**

- Identify and regulate point sources, including animal feed lots and landfills, as a first priority.
- Study the cost-benefit of fencing livestock out of streams, a common Midwestern problem.
- Preserve or establish permanent riparian forests as buffers. Research the minimum effective width for different streams, since this zone is disappearing rapidly.
- Reduce erosion and confine soil, nutrients, herbicides, and pesticides to tilled fields through improved tillage and best management practices.
- Review existing state fish and wildlife files and literature, conduct new studies on key streams, and rank stream systems for protection or restoration.
