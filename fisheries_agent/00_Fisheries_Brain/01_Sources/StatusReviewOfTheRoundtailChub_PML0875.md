# Status Review of the Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*) in the Verde River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report (Nongame Branch, Technical Report 114, April 1997) reviews the status, distribution and threats facing the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) across the [[Verde River Basin]]. The review was produced under a cost-share agreement with the [[Prescott National Forest]] and combined literature, museum records, agency databases and 1996 field surveys. Because of widespread taxonomic confusion within the genus [[Gila]], many museum labels were treated as *G. robusta* when collected from reaches known to hold roundtail chubs or phenotypic intermediates. Populations are rated on a five-category qualitative scale. Roundtail chubs remain present in the upper Verde River, Fossil Creek, and several tributaries, but are uncommon or absent in the lower basin. The principal threats are nonnative predatory and competing fishes, water diversions and depletion, livestock grazing, and groundwater pumping linked to human population growth.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and nomenclature.** The roundtail chub is a member of the family [[Cyprinidae]]. It is also known as Verde chub, Verde roach, Colorado River chub, and *charalito atetado* or *charalito redondo* in Mexico. Its taxonomy is complicated by hybridization and morphological intermediacy with [[Gila Chub]] (*G. intermedia*) and with the "grahami" phenotype. DeMarais (1995) did not support "grahami" as a valid taxon and recommended referring those populations to *G. robusta*; this review follows that recommendation while noting earlier names.

**Distribution.** The species occurs in tributaries and mainstem rivers of the upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]], from Wyoming south through Colorado, Utah, Nevada, New Mexico and Arizona into Mexico. In Arizona it is a Species of Special Concern, and the AGFD designated it a game fish in 1995 with a 330 mm (13 in) minimum size limit and a one-fish daily take limit. Populations in the San Juan and Upper Gila drainages in New Mexico are declining, and it is extirpated from the Zuni and San Francisco drainages.

**Morphology.** Roundtail chubs have thick but not chubby, deep, compressed bodies. The dorsum is dusky to greenish olive-gray, with silvery ventral surfaces. Adults typically measure 250–350 mm (10–14 in), with a reported maximum of 430 mm (17 in). The largest angler-caught fish, from the lower Salt River in March 1994, measured 469.9 mm (18.5 in) and weighed 1782.62 g (3.93 lb). Key diagnostic traits include lateral-line scales usually above 80 (range 78–105 across sources), nine dorsal and pelvic fin-rays, a head length to caudal peduncle depth ratio usually above 3.0, and a rare nuchal hump.

**Life history.** Vanicek and Kramer (1969) provided one of the most comprehensive life-history accounts. Breeding occurs in spring and early summer, typically in pool and pool-riffle habitats. Females mature at about 150–180 mm and two to three years of age, and fecundity ranges from 600 to 45,125 eggs (Bestgen 1985). Growth is fastest in the first year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Roundtail chubs occupy cool to warm mid-elevation streams (1325–2000 m), with maximum water temperatures of 20–26.5 °C; temperature may limit distribution (Bestgen and Propst 1989). Adults favour deep pools, eddies and quiet water beneath cut banks, boulders, logs or overhanging vegetation, and avoid swift water. Juveniles use shallower backwaters, shoreline areas and macrophyte beds.

**Diet.** The species is an opportunistic omnivore, with diet dominated by aquatic and terrestrial insects (chironomids, mayflies, caddisflies), diatoms and filamentous algae. Diet diversity increases with body size. No fish remains were found in Fossil Creek chub stomachs (Neve 1976).

**Ecological interactions.** Native fish communities in the Verde River have declined, with seven native fishes remaining in the upper basin, representing 58% of the original native fauna (Hendrickson 1993). Nonnative species include [[Red Shiner]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Green Sunfish]]. Parasites are also significant. The copepod [[Lernaea]] infected razorback suckers and desert suckers heavily in the 1960s, and the abundance of Lernaea has been linked to native fish declines.

**Land use and hydrology.** Land ownership is mostly federal (Prescott, Coconino and Tonto National Forests), with private holdings in the Verde Valley. Agriculture, grazing, mining, recreation (including off-road vehicles) and water diversions affect the basin. Upper Verde flows near Paulden range from about 0.7 to 3.7 m³/s, and discharge into [[Horseshoe Reservoir]] in 1995 totalled 750,500 acre-feet. Water withdrawals and groundwater pumping are significant concerns.

## Key Findings

**Survey and status outcomes** (ratings from the report's five categories; "Unknown" indicates insufficient data):

- **Stable-Secure:** Deadman Creek (especially the South Fork), where roundtail chubs were common in 1991 and 1993 surveys, with no nonnative fishes established.
- **Stable-Threatened:** Upper Verde River (headwaters to Perkinsville), Verde River (Perkinsville to TAPCO), Verde River (TAPCO to Beasley Flat), and Fossil Creek. In the upper Verde, 1996 surveys found roundtail chubs remained present in large numbers.
- **Unstable-Threatened:** Beaver Creek, West Clear Creek, Verde River (Beasley Flat to Childs) and Verde River (Childs to Sheep Bridge). In the Childs to Sheep Bridge reach, chubs were recorded at only 0.1% relative abundance in 1996 and were absent from five of nine surveys.
- **Unknown:** Oak Creek, Dry Beaver Creek, East Verde River, Webber Creek, Verde River (Sheep Bridge to Horseshoe Dam), and Verde River (Horseshoe Dam to Salt River confluence). No verified records exist in the last two reaches.

**Key field data:**
- The May 1996 Dead Horse Ranch to Beasley Flat survey totalled N = 1708 fish; the Beasley Flat to Childs survey (May 1996) totalled N = 1984; the Childs to Sheep Bridge survey (June 1996) totalled N = 2383 fish, of which 131 were native.
- In the Perkinsville to Sycamore Creek reach (October 1995), 13 fish species were taken (n = 2060), with roundtail chubs the third most abundant at 12%.
- In the TAPCO to Tuzigoot survey (October 1995 to August 1996), 4342 fish were sampled, of which 88.5% were native.
- Fossil Creek roundtail chubs were caught in all three years of AGFD surveys (1994–1996), and smallmouth bass were established at least 13 km upstream of the Verde confluence.

**Population statements:**
- Roundtail chub populations in the upper Verde River and Fossil Creek were considered large, with multiple life stages present in Fossil Creek during 1989–1990 (Barrett and Maughan 1995).
- Gila chub (*G. intermedia*) populations were recorded from Big Chino Wash, Red Tank Draw, Spring Creek, Walker Creek and Williamson Valley Wash, and Gila chubs were not recorded in the Verde mainstem.

**Principal threats identified:**
- Predation and competition from [[Smallmouth Bass]], flathead catfish, channel catfish and [[Red Shiner]].
- Water diversions and depletion, with the lowest recorded Camp Verde flow at 0.2 m³/s (7 cfs) in July 1996.
- Livestock grazing, which in 20 of 21 reviewed studies negatively impacted riparian habitats and fish populations (Platts 1991).
- Projected population growth: the Verde Valley is projected to reach 98,620 people by 2030, increasing the risk of groundwater pumping.

**Management recommendations:**
- Establish and maintain minimum flows, particularly through the Camp Verde reaches.
- Restrict transport and use of live baitfishes, including crayfish.
- Implement actions to control nonnative fishes, and construct a fish barrier in lower Fossil Creek to prevent smallmouth bass immigration.
- Conduct intensive surveys to define distribution and abundance, particularly in tributaries and reaches with unknown status.
- Protect *G. robusta*, *G. intermedia* and intermediate stocks as components of the genetic variation within the Gila complex.
