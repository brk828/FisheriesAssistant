# Environmental Assessment: Proposed Endangered Fish Growout Facilities at Cibola National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Environmental Assessment (September 1993) evaluates a proposal by [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]], the [[Parker Fishery Resource Office]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] to build growout ponds for rearing endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] fingerlings before release into the lower [[Colorado River]]. The preferred action (Alternative A) converts an isolated ~2-acre backwater, the High Levee Pond, into a growout facility after removing competing fishes by netting, electrofishing and [[Rotenone]] treatment, and adds up to two dredged ponds of under 2 acres at undetermined sites. Alternative B would release hatchery-reared fish directly into the river, and Alternative C (No Action) would forgo reintroduction. The assessment concludes that no significant adverse impacts are expected and that the project is not likely to adversely affect listed species, a conclusion concurred with by the Arizona Ecological Services Office.

## Background & Description

**Historical context.** The lower Colorado River once supported several native big-river fishes, including the Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub, adapted to a seasonally variable, flood-pulsed system. Native fish declined sharply from the early 1900s and especially after [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]] closed in the late 1930s. Of nine primary native species, all but one have been extirpated from the mainstream or are extremely rare. The present fish fauna is almost entirely introduced, and many introduced species are predatory on larval fish.

**Status in Arizona.** Of 33 native Arizona fish species, one is extinct, 20 are federally listed as threatened or endangered, and 10 await review for listing.

**Project purpose.** The project aims to rear fingerling Razorback Suckers and Bonytail Chub to a size large enough for reintroduction, allowing fish to mature in a relatively natural setting with minimal predation. Long-lived species (over 30 years) could contribute to a self-reproducing population, which the document describes as critical for recovery.

**Alternatives considered:**
- **Alternative A (Proposed Action):** Develop the High Levee Pond and up to two dredged ponds as growout facilities.
- **Alternative B:** Release larger hatchery fish (about 12 inches) directly into the river from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]], without on-site growout.
- **Alternative C (No Action):** No reintroduction; the Refuge remains without historically native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Habitats include wetlands, marshes, oxbow backwaters, [[Cibola Lake]] (lentic) and the Colorado River and drains (lotic). Waters are generally within the range suitable for fishes, slightly basic and well buffered (Welsh and Maughan 1993). Dissolved oxygen was high at all sites, and occasional fish kills result from localized oxygen depletion. Selenium showed only a weak link to diversity and abundance; a strong inverse relationship with catch per unit effort was indicated, suggesting selenium is one of several limiting factors. No current impact on diversity was found, but impacts could arise if selenium inputs increase.

**Vegetation.** The High Levee Pond supports possibly spiny naiad, water milfoil, hornwort, bulrush, cattails, pondweed, sedges and stonewort, with mesquite and [[Salt Cedar]] on the banks. Proposed new pond sites are likely in salt cedar-dominated areas, a non-native, undesirable vegetation type on the Refuge.

**Fish and wildlife.** Twenty-one fish species have been recorded from Parker Dam to the Refuge (Ohmart et al. 1988). Species collected near the project include [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Black Crappie]]. Mammals, reptiles, birds and waterfowl also occur in the vicinity.

**Special status species.** The Razorback Sucker has nearly disappeared from the lower Colorado River, with the largest population in Lake Mohave. The Bonytail Chub persists sparsely in Lake Mohave and occasionally in Lake Havasu. Species that could occur nearby include the endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Brown Pelican]], and the candidate [[Western Least Bittern]]. The High Levee Pond was surveyed for Yuma Clapper Rails with negative results.

**Cultural and recreational resources.** Archaeological sites on the Refuge include rock art, geoglyphs, cairns and trails, with no long-term habitation sites found. Recreation near the site is limited mainly to bait-fish angling, with fishing and hunting in the Old River Channel and fishing and boating in the Dry Cut.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed Action (Alternative A):** Remove non-target fishes by netting and electrofishing, then apply rotenone (possibly twice, one month apart) to the High Levee Pond. After at least two weeks, stock Razorback Suckers and Bonytail Chub (2–6 inches) from Dexter. Monitor physico-chemical factors and growth monthly, and PIT-tag and release fish at 12 inches into the main channel.
- **Predator control:** Rotenone must be reapplied before each future stocking because Bonytail Chub are predators; unrecovered endangered fish are considered "expendable" under the plan.
- **Alternative B drawbacks:** Hatchery rearing is costly, fish grow poorly, residence times are longer, and hatchery rearing does not allow adaptation to natural conditions, possibly reducing post-release survival.
- **Environmental effects of Alternative A:** Temporary water quality effects from rotenone are expected to neutralize quickly. Vegetation clearing is necessary but aquatic vegetation is expected to recover. Wildlife impacts are minor and temporary. Recreation loss is minimal given the small facility size (<2 acres).
- **Predation trade-off:** Predation on stocked fish may select individuals better adapted to natural conditions, providing overall benefit to the species.
- **Cumulative impacts:** No significant adverse cumulative impacts are expected.
- **Consultation:** The Arizona Ecological Services Office concurred (September 1, 1993; consultation #2-21-93-I-417) that the project is not likely to adversely affect any endangered species.
- **No Action (Alternative C):** Would leave the extinction risk for both endangered species unchanged, with continued decline if any remaining fish persist.
- **Next steps:** Site-specific clearances for listed species are required before constructing new ponds; public notification and education displays are planned.
