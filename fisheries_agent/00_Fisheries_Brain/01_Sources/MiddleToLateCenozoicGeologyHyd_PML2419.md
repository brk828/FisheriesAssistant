# Middle to Late Cenozoic Geology, Hydrography, and Fish Evolution in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper combines geological and biological evidence to reconstruct the Cenozoic drainage history of the American Southwest. It argues that the [[Colorado River]] system was assembled through a sequence of tectonic and hydrologic events, and that fish faunas record this history. Closed basins formed between the [[Cordilleran Orogen]] and the rising [[Rocky Mountains]] and held lake fishes of Atlantic affinity. Oligocene to Miocene [[Basin and Range Province]] extension later fragmented the landscape and created conduits for fish dispersal from both the Pacific and Atlantic sides. The arrival of the modern Colorado River at about 5 Ma was the largest Cenozoic hydrographic change in the region and opened a new dispersal route. Mitochondrial DNA and fossil evidence indicate that late Miocene runoff from the [[Colorado Plateau]] did not flow down the ancestral [[Rio Grande]] or [[Platte River]], but did exchange fishes with the [[Snake River]].

## Background & Description

The paper is a contribution to the GSA volume on the Cenozoic drainage history of the Southwestern Great Basin and Lower Colorado River region. It is organized around four stages of regional geologic history:

1. Late Jurassic and Cretaceous magmatism and faulting created a mountain belt along the southwestern edge of North America.
2. Laramide orogeny (Late Cretaceous to early Tertiary) uplifted the [[Rocky Mountains]] as deformation migrated eastward.
3. Oligocene to Miocene extension and volcanism fragmented the western mountains, producing numerous closed basins.
4. Integration of drainages in the late Cenozoic, culminating in the arrival of the [[Colorado River]] at about 5 Ma.

The authors use fossil fish from the [[Bidahochi Formation]] (middle Miocene to earliest Pliocene, eastern Arizona), [[Lake Bonneville]]-related deposits, and modern fish genetics, particularly mitochondrial DNA sequence divergence, to test competing drainage hypotheses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake-based faunas:** Eocene closed basins between the Rockies and the western mountain belt held lake fishes of Atlantic affinity. Fish fossils from these basins show no connection to the Pacific Ocean.
- **Pacific-affinity dispersal:** Miocene and Pliocene fossils from the northern [[Great Basin]] record fish influx from adjacent areas outside the basin. Late Miocene fishes related to the [[Snake River]] and [[Sacramento River]] show faunal exchange across major drainages.
- **Fish size and river size:** Large fishes requiring large rivers were involved in the Miocene exchanges. The authors note this as surprising, because it implies that the transfers involved major drainages and not only small headwater streams.
- **Habitat signals:** Fossil fishes from the upper Miocene part of the Bidahochi Formation on the Colorado Plateau reflect a habitat of large, swift-moving waters.
- **Tectonic control of dispersal:** Headwater captures by streams cut by active faults, including the [[San Andreas Fault]] and related faults in the Los Angeles area and Salton Trough, transferred fishes between drainages.
- **Lower Colorado River lakes:** Lake highstands along the lower Colorado River are recorded by the Mohave, Blythe, Las Vegas, and Hualapai lakes. The southern two contain the [[Bouse Formation]] (ca. 5 Ma), whose marl and marine fossils contrast with the limestone and gypsum of the northern lakes.

## Key Findings

**Drainage evolution**

- The Cenozoic transfer of the upper Colorado River catchment from Atlantic to Pacific basins began with Eocene closed basins between the Rocky Mountains and the western mountain belt.
- Eocene to early Miocene sandstones and clastic sediments near the southwestern edge of the Colorado Plateau record generally northeastward stream flow toward the Baca Basin and probably the Claron Basin.
- The Colorado River arrived abruptly at about 5 Ma in the Basin and Range Province, filled formerly closed basins, spilled over basin divides, reached the [[Gulf of California]], and carved the lower Colorado River valley.
- The [[Gila River]] underwent a similar sequence of basin filling and spilling and reached the Gulf of California at about the same time.
- Overflow of a lake that filled the Little Colorado River valley is considered likely to have spilled over the [[Kaibab Uplift]] to initiate incision of the [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors state that such a lake could not have reached Telegraph Flat (elevation about 1,675 m, 5,500 ft, about 40 km east of Kanab, Utah), because its outflow would have incised a different channel.

**Fish evolution and dispersal**

- Fish from the Miocene Colorado River system are mostly of Atlantic affinity in the closed-basin faunas, and modern faunal differences, including mitochondrial DNA differences, indicate that late Miocene runoff from the Colorado Plateau did not flow down the ancestral Rio Grande or Platte River.
- Significant faunal exchange occurred with the Snake River, as shown by modern fishes, late Miocene fishes in the Bidahochi Formation and Snake River Plain, and recent genera in the Sacramento River drainage.
- A lack of zircon sand grains in pre-Pliocene Snake River Plain sediments indicates that Colorado Plateau runoff did not enter the western Snake River until the Pliocene. Miocene fish transfer therefore likely occurred through a stream that carried water from the upper ancestral Snake River to the upper Colorado Plateau drainage. The paper proposes that this may have been caused by uplift associated with late Miocene magmatism along the Snake-Yellowstone hotspot track.
- Speciation and DNA evidence indicate a long period of evolution dating back to at least the late Miocene in the greater [[Lake Mead]] area, or possibly in the ancestral Bill Williams-Verde-Gila drainages of Arizona. Fishes then migrated into the Lake Mead area after the Colorado River arrived and entered the river after ca. 5 Ma.
- Rainbow trout dispersed from coastal areas to the mountains of central Arizona and southwestern New Mexico, and cutthroat trout migrated down the upper Colorado River to the Virgin River.
- Dace and suckers from ancestral habitats, possibly in the Muddy Creek (Lake Mead) area, reached the [[Los Angeles Basin]] by stream capture. The Santa Ana mountain sucker and Los Angeles speckled dace probably reached the basin by this route, through headwater captures associated with fault movement.

**Conclusions**

- Adaptation and speciation within the separate Miocene parts of the Colorado River system produced separate faunas. These faunas mix only to the extent that upper-basin fishes moved downstream through the Grand Canyon.
- Aquatic conditions created by the 5 Ma integration of the Colorado River still offer opportunities and challenges for fish dispersal into environments that remain uncolonized.
