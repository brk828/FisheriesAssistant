# Recovery Plan for the Razorback Sucker, Xyrauchen texanus

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses the alarming decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a monotypic genus endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. Authored by Dr. Windell Minckley ([[Arizona State University]]) and Mr. Gail C. Kebetich ([[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]]), the plan documents the species' historical range from Wyoming and Colorado south to northwest Mexico, its current critically reduced distribution, and proposes a comprehensive 18-year recovery program (1973-1990). The razorback sucker has declined precipitously due to habitat modification, channelization, dam construction, flow regime changes, and competition from introduced species. The plan outlines objectives for population surveys, life history research, artificial propagation, habitat restoration, and public education to prevent extinction and restore viable populations.

## Background & Description

### Morphology and Identification

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a distinctive catostomid characterized by:
- Elongated head and laterally compressed, triangular body cross-section anterior to dorsal fin
- Sharp-edged dorsal keel behind the occiput, supported by greatly-produced neural spines
- Lateral-line scales: 68-87, deeply embedded in thick, leathery skin, often obscured by epidermis
- Long dorsal fin with 13-16 (usually 14-15) rays
- Inferior mouth that can project forward to nearly terminal position, surrounded by papillose, fleshy lips
- Slender, numerous gill rakers: 44-50 on first gill arch, with "fuzzy" appearance due to fine filaments
- Adults reach nearly 1 meter length and over 5 kilograms weight

### Coloration

Adults display olivaceous to brownish-black dorsum, lighter below (yellowish or creamy-white). Brown or brownish-black lateral stripe, which may become pink or reddened in breeding males. Breeding males show extreme coloration: essentially black back and upper sides, orange lateral stripe, yellowed belly and ventral fins. Sexual dimorphism pronounced during breeding: males develop large nuptial tubercles on anal fin and caudal peduncle; females show swollen, fleshy, sometimes tube-like urogenital region.

### Larval Characteristics

Young *Xyrauchen* are difficult to distinguish from other larval [[Catostomidae]], except by lip structure, number of dorsal fin-rays (shared or exceeded only by [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], *Catostomus latipinnis*), and melanophore distribution. The size at which the elevated pre-dorsal area develops is unknown.

### Taxonomic Note

The common name "razorback sucker" is preferred over "humpback sucker" because the structure is sharp-edged and laterally compressed (not smoothly rounded like humps in [[Gila cypha]] and [[Gila elegans]]). "Razorback" was the term used by older fishermen and is a literal translation of the generic name.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Historical Distribution (Pre-1880s)

The razorback sucker ranged throughout the [[Colorado River]] basin from headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado south to near the river's mouth in northwest Mexico. While specimens are lacking from certain tributaries ([[San Juan River]] in New Mexico, [[Virgin River]] in Nevada-Arizona-Utah, upper [[Gila River]] in New Mexico), other fishes with similar habitat requirements ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]] *Ptychocheilus lucius*, [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] *Pantosteus discobolus*) occurred there, suggesting razorback presence. Some streams (e.g., [[Little Colorado River]], uppermost [[Salt River]] in Arizona) may have been excluded by barrier falls ([[Grand Falls]], [[Quartzite Falls]]).

### Habitat Preferences

Evidence indicates razorback suckers preferred quiet backwaters and sloughs rather than swift mainstream currents, at least for much of the year. In the lower basin, pool or backwater habitats along the mainstream Colorado and [[Gila River]] and major tributaries supported largest populations. Fish also moved into canals in the Phoenix area, becoming trapped and taken in large numbers for food (domestic swine, human consumption) and fertilizer.

### Cultural and Commercial Significance

Aboriginal peoples widely used razorback suckers as food throughout the species' range, based on archaeological occurrences. In the 1880s, the fish was abundant enough in the [[San Pedro River]], southern Arizona, to support a commercial fishery with sales in Tombstone. Some populations sustained commercial fishing until the late 1940s ([[Saguaro Lake]], Maricopa County, Arizona).

### Causes of Decline

Decline seemingly coincides with modifications of the Colorado River and tributaries beginning in the 1900s:

1. **Channelization**: Markedly reduced available backwater habitat
2. **Arroyo cutting** (late 1800s): Produced similar habitat reduction in major tributaries
3. **Progressive impoundment**: Dams on most tributaries and mainstream created physical changes in flow regime, temperatures, turbidities, and habitat types
4. **Introduced species**: Mostly from [[Mississippi River]] valley, increased exponentially from ~1930 to present, contributing to competition and predation
5. **Low stream flow**: Decreased precipitation may have contributed to early extirpation stages

### Current Distribution (1963-1973)

California and Utah consider the species "rare"; protected by law in California. Current status:

- **Lower basin** ([[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Sea of Cortez]]):
  - [[Lake Mead]]: Increasingly scarce
  - [[Lake Mohave]]: Not uncommon
  - [[Lake Havasu]]: Rare
  - Davis Dam to International Boundary: Essentially absent, only large individuals
  - Mexican segment: No records for past 20-25 years

- **Gila basin**: Last collection of young-of-the-year in 1950 near [[Willow Beach]]. Persisted until at least 1954 in [[Verde River]] system, perhaps in [[Salt River]] reservoirs, but now extinct in entire Gila basin.

- **Upper basin**: Appears to maintain populations near confluence of [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Utah.

### Hybridization Concerns

Incidence of hybrids between razorback and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] increasing at [[Dinosaur National Monument]] and below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], indicating habitat disturbance, rarity of one or both parental species, or other ecological disruption factors.

### Reproductive Biology

**Spawning behavior**: Adults apparently undertook upstream migrations to spawn. Ripe fish taken from late winter ([[Lake Mohave]] in February) to early summer (below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in May-early June). Young abundant in [[Lake Mead]] in June 1959.

In Colorado River impoundments, spawning occurs along shorelines or in bays. One female tended by 2-12 males; group moves in circles <2 meters diameter, randomly spiraling over bottom. Males appear to "herd" female by nudging with heads and predorsal keels against her urogenital region. Female settles to bottom with male closely pressed to each side; all vibrate bodies laterally, terminating in "convulsive" finale when gametes presumably emitted. Three fish move forward and upward, leaving cloud of silt and sand marking spawning site. Females spawn repeatedly with numerous males.

**Eggs**: Reportedly transparent and adhesive, attaching to substrate upon deposition.

### Feeding Ecology

Foods in lower Colorado River reservoirs likely differ from pre-modification diet:

- **Young** (based on 15 individuals): Zooplankton and minute planktonic algae (diatoms)
- **Adults**: Algae and dipteran larvae reported from lower river; specimens from [[Lake Mohave]] had intestines entirely filled with planktonic crustaceans in May

**Feeding behavior**: Observed under floodlights at night in ~6 meters depth. Razorbacks moved in school of ~35 individuals with mouths projecting forward, "bouncing" up-and-down pattern produced by slow, alternating caudal fin sweeps. Pectoral fins held stiffly extended (presumably producing plane effect); little side-to-side head movement, perhaps due to keel-like "hump" acting as stabilizer. Number, length, and "fuzziness" of gill rakers provide indirect evidence of filter-feeding mode.

### Data Gaps

No data available on: growth rates, sex ratios, survivorship, age at sexual maturity, maximum age, life expectancy, or most other life history aspects.

### Captive Maintenance

Six adults held successfully for five months in small raceway (3 × 0.3 × 0.3 m) at [[Arizona State University]] in 1965. Fish were easily handled, fed readily, no problems with parasites or disease. Within days responded to feeding taps, remained docile even when removed for blood withdrawal (cardiac puncture) for hemoglobin and serum protein studies.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Plan Objectives

**Overall Goal**: Restore razorback sucker to suitable portions of native range, recognizing impossibility of system-wide restoration due to extensive Colorado River modifications. Multiple-use concept emphasizing:
- Species preservation
- Sporting value (bow hunting, spear fishing)
- Food value and potential commercial commodity
- Public education about native fish conservation

### Specific Objectives (52 detailed objectives outlined)

**Survey and Assessment**:
1. Determine present range and population levels in lower Colorado River through intensive survey
2. Determine present range and population levels in upper basin (Wyoming, Colorado data needed)
3. Standardize survey methods and divide river into segments
4. Collect data on morphological variability in razorback sucker populations
5. Collect museum specimen data for analysis

**Life History Research** (Objectives 2-31):
- Determine all life cycle stage requirements
- Establish spawning season, sites, temperatures, substrate requirements
- Determine effects of fluctuating water levels, turbidity, predators, competitors on spawning
- Quantify sex ratios, minimum viable population sizes
- Determine growth rates, age at sexual maturity, maximum age, life expectancy
- Establish feeding requirements and optimal food types for all life stages
- Determine seasonal migration patterns and movement directions

**Habitat Assessment** (Objectives 32-44):
- Determine pristine Colorado River conditions from old literature
- Identify suitable remaining habitat areas
- Use computer simulation to model unaltered habitat
- Determine optimal water chemistry (dissolved gases, salts) for all life stages
- Assess effects of disease and parasites
- Monitor water-oriented projects impacting razorback habitat

**Artificial Propagation** (Objectives 45-52):
- Develop standardized capture techniques for broodstock
- Determine hatchery requirements under artificial conditions
- Establish optimal conditions for egg incubation, fry rearing, juvenile growth
- Determine handling techniques for specimens
- Assess value as commercial, sport, scientific, and educational fish
- Develop public information brochure on life history and values

**Reintroduction and Monitoring**:
- Release young into suitable habitat beginning Fiscal Year 1975
- Conduct surveillance to determine survival of artificially-reared fish
- Continue monitoring through 1990

**Public Education**:
- Conduct public relations campaign through news releases, magazines, TV, radio
- Construct display center at [[Phoenix Zoo]] (planned 1974-1975)
- Maintain broodstock for 15 years for scientific and display purposes
- Prepare and distribute literature on razorback sucker

### Implementation Timeline (1973-1990)

**Fiscal Year 1973** (March-June): Set up recovery team, draft plan, begin Lower Colorado River survey, locate hatchery facilities, begin public relations. **Budget: $10,600**

**Fiscal Year 1974** (July 1973-June 1974): Continue survey, contract universities for life history studies, begin artificial propagation, begin computer simulation, search for suitable habitat, monitor water projects, collect morphological data, begin literature review. **Budget: $82,000**

**Fiscal Year 1975** (July 1974-June 1975): Finish survey, continue life history and propagation, conclude computer simulation, begin releasing young, continue monitoring, complete morphological study, begin Phoenix Zoo construction. **Budget: $154,000**

**Fiscal Year 1976** (July 1975-June 1976): Compile survey report, continue incomplete life history portions, continue propagation and releases, begin survival surveillance, continue monitoring and public relations. **Budget: $152,000**

**Fiscal Year 1977** (July 1976-June 1977): Continue life history studies, propagation, releases, surveillance, monitoring, public relations, and zoo display. **Budget: $152,000**

**Fiscal Year 1978** (July 1977-June 1978): Conclude major life history portions and publish, continue propagation, releases, surveillance, monitoring, public relations, zoo display. **Budget: $152,000**

**Fiscal Years 1979-1990** (Extended Management Program): Conclude life history studies and publish, continue releases if necessary, continue surveillance and monitoring, maintain low-level public relations, continue zoo display. **Budget: $25,000 annually**

**Total Estimated Cost (1973-1978)**: $692,600

### Critical Concerns

1. **Recruitment failure**: Despite spawning records in Lakes Mead and Mohave, no young fish collected. Last young-of-the-year collection from lower basin: 1950 near [[Willow Beach]].

2. **Hybridization**: Increasing incidence of razorback × flannelmouth sucker hybrids suggests ecological disruption.

3. **Complete extirpation from Gila basin**: Species now extinct throughout former Gila River range.

4. **Uncertain upper basin status**: Information needed from Wyoming and Colorado populations.

5. **Knowledge gaps**: Life history essentially unknown; data from artificial habitats (reservoirs) may not reflect pristine conditions.

6. **Habitat loss**: Channelization, impoundment, and flow modification have eliminated most suitable backwater and slough habitats.

7. **Introduced species**: Exponential increase in non-native fishes since 1930 likely contributing to decline through competition and predation.

### Management Implications

The plan recognizes that complete range restoration is impossible given extensive Colorado River modifications. Focus is on:
- Identifying and protecting remaining suitable habitat segments
- Establishing captive breeding program
- Reintroducing fish to historically occupied or potentially suitable areas
- Long-term monitoring (through 1990) to assess recovery success
- Public education to build support for native fish conservation
- Potential development of sport and commercial fisheries once populations stabilized

This represents one of the earliest comprehensive recovery plans for a threatened freshwater fish species in North America, establishing protocols for survey, research, propagation, and reintroduction that would influence subsequent endangered species programs.
