# Age Estimation for Razorback Sucker (Pisces: Catostomidae) from Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Michael S. McCarthy]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University, 1987) assesses which bony structures of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) give reliable age estimates, and uses the best method to examine the age structure and recruitment of the Lake Mohave population. Seven structures were tested. Only sectioned otoliths gave readable marks in older fish. Lake Mohave fish were found to be long-lived, with a maximum apparent age of 44 years, and the authors report that no detectable recruitment has occurred since about 1958 (or 1954 at the latest). They conclude that extirpation may be expected by about the year 2000 unless this trend is reversed.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The razorback sucker is a large [[Catostomidae]] fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was historically abundant and an important food fish for Native Americans and early settlers.
- **Historical range:** From the Green River, Wyoming, south to the Colorado River delta in northwestern Mexico.
- **Current status:** Large adults persist in the upper Colorado and Green rivers, but records of young fish are sporadic. The largest remaining population is in [[Lake Mohave]], with occasional specimens from [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Size:** Lake Mohave females average about 50 cm and males about 45 cm standard length (SL). The largest recorded specimens are a 57.6 cm SL female and a 4.99 kg male.
- **Feeding:** The species has a protrusible mouth and closely spaced gill rakers, indicating facultative planktivory. Adults in Lake Mohave feed mostly on zooplankton.

Background context: the Lake Mohave stock is thought to have originated before or during the construction and closure of [[Davis Dam]] (1942–1951) and the filling of the reservoir (1953–1954).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir effects:** Razorback suckers colonized mainstream lower Colorado impoundments in large numbers, but disappeared from [[Gila River]] reservoirs in the 1950s, about 40 years after dam closure. They are becoming rare in newer reservoirs of similar or greater age.
- **Life history in reservoir:** Annual spawning and larval production occur, but young disappear before reaching about 12 cm total length, so no recruitment has been documented.
- **Health:** Blindness, tumors and other maladies were noted in Lake Mohave specimens, suggesting a population of long-lived individuals.
- **Limnology:** Lake Mohave is a monomictic reservoir. The authors suggest that seasonal temperature variation there should drive otolith annulus formation, as it likely did in the pristine Colorado River.
- **Growth enhancement:** Rapid early growth in the reservoir may reflect increased availability of planktonic food.
- **Historical records:** Archaeological remains from [[Catclaw Cave]] and a vertebra from a Sun Peak site suggest that very large fish once existed. Estimates from local testimony of fish over 30 inches (about 60 cm SL) are considered suspect.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sample**
- A total of 88 wild-caught fish were examined, plus hatchery-produced fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH), the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AZGFD) and the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CADFG).
- Seven structures were compared: opercular, cleithral and branchiostegal bones, vertebral centra, pectoral fin rays, scales and sagittal otoliths.
- Standard length was back-calculated from otolith radius, which was highly correlated with SL.

**Structure evaluation**
- Only **sectioned otoliths** gave clear, readable annuli in older wild fish. Of 74 fish examined, marks were readily interpretable in many, though some sections were unreadable (about 12.3% lost during preparation, 13.4% sectioned through the wrong region, and 10.2% had irregular margins).
- **Scales** were unreliable. Some lacked visible annuli, and multiple scales from one fish gave widely varying estimates.
- **Opercles, cleithra, branchiostegal rays, vertebrae and fin rays** showed dense ossification and crowded margins, which led to underestimation of age in large fish.
- In hatchery fish of known age, marks were evident on all seven structures, which allowed calibration of the methods.

**Age and longevity**
- Readable sagittae from wild fish showed 23 or more distinct marks (24 if the last mark was counted as an annulus).
- The greatest apparent age based on unquestioned continuous annuli was **44 years**.
- Lake Mohave specimens were estimated at 21 to 44 years old at capture in 1981–1983. Those fish were thought to have hatched between about 1937 and 1958.
- Earlier reports of longevity were noted, including a 21-year-old male from the upper Colorado River and an estimated 47 cm SL fish with 17 annuli.

**Growth**
- First-year growth averaged about 26% of SL at capture. About 50% of SL was reached by the fifth year.
- Males and females grew at similar early rates, though later growth differed. The authors compare this with growth in hatchery stocks.

**Recruitment and population status**
- Recruitment is apparently absent: all individuals captured in recent years were large adults, and there is no detectable recruitment since about 1958.
- The authors note that the pattern parallels extirpation observed in Gila River reservoirs about 40 years after closure, as also documented by Minckley (1983).
- **Prediction:** Unless the trend is reversed, population extirpation may be expected by the year 2000.

**Conservation implications**
- The authors recommend that otolith sectioning be used for future age studies of this endangered species.
- They note that the older, long-lived Lake Mohave stock is at risk without new recruitment.
