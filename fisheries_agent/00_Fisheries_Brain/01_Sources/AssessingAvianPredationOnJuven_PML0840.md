# Assessing Avian Predation on Juvenile Salmonids Using Passive Integrated Transponder Tag Recoveries and Mark-Recapture Methods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies predation on juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Steelhead]] by piscivorous seabirds breeding on [[Año Nuevo Island]], a 10-ha island off central California. Researchers recovered [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags deposited by predators and applied a [[POPAN Model]] (open-population [[Jolly-Seber Model]] variant) to correct raw tag counts for tags lost between sampling events. The corrected minimum predation estimate was 362 tagged fish, and minimum predation rates by watershed ranged from 0.9% (Scott Creek) to 4.6% (Waddell Creek). Most detected tags came from estuary-reared steelhead, implying that [[Western Gull]] predation may disproportionately affect the adult steelhead population.

## Background & Description

Coho salmon and steelhead in central California are listed under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Population declines are attributed to overharvest, migration obstruction by hydropower facilities, habitat degradation, and hatchery propagation, but the role of avian predation in these coastal systems had not been quantified. Most published work on avian predation concerns large river systems, notably the [[Columbia River Estuary]], where [[Caspian Tern]] colonies consume substantial numbers of smolts.

PIT tags allow only minimum estimates of predation for several reasons: some tags are carried away from colonies, some are destroyed during digestion, some lose function after deposition, and not all tags on a colony are detected. The authors addressed the last two uncertainties by applying mark-recapture statistics, since tags were uniquely numbered, not removed, and repeatedly detected on the island.

Breeding and roosting birds on the island included western gulls, Brandt's cormorants, brown pelicans, and California and Steller sea lions. Western gull and Brandt's cormorant populations had increased in recent decades; by 2007 an estimated 2,196 western gulls and 3,660 Brandt's cormorants were present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study site:** Año Nuevo Island lies 1.6 km off Año Nuevo Point. Surveys focused on elevated portions of the island scannable for PIT tags.
- **Source watersheds:** Tags were deployed in six watersheds: Waddell, Scott, San Lorenzo, Soquel, and Aptos creeks (Santa Cruz County) and Gazos Creek (San Mateo County). Distances from the island ranged from 5.5 km (Waddell) to 41.0 km (Aptos).
- **Hydrology:** Dry-summer sandbars close estuary mouths, forming freshwater lagoons. Steelhead using these lagoons follow a life history with larger, lagoon-reared second-migration smolts (FL >150 mm).
- **Predator-prey timing:** Juvenile salmonid out-migration overlaps only partly with seabird presence on the island, and salmonids are available to predators only part of the year.
- **Prey size:** Brown pelicans and gulls consume small schooling fish; sea lions were the only predators using elevated island areas where large, adult-sized salmonids could be eaten.

## Key Findings

**Tag deployment and recovery**
- 34,485 PIT tags were deployed in coho salmon and steelhead across six watersheds.
- Between autumn 2006 and autumn 2010, 316 unique tags were detected on Año Nuevo Island. Identity was determined for 312 of them.
- Juvenile steelhead made up 88% of identified tags (273 of 312). Only 11% (35 tags) came from juvenile coho salmon.
- Tags from Scott Creek numbered 226, 2.5 times the combined total from all other watersheds (89).
- No tags from Aptos Creek were detected on the island.

**Mark-recapture correction**
- The best-fitting [[POPAN Model]] (survival and capture probability constant, time-varying entry probability; AIC weight 0.81) estimated survival at 0.8578, capture probability at 0.6436, and a superpopulation of 233.29.
- Gross population size (N*) was 242 tags (SE 9.8), and the 196 tags detected through spring 2009 were corrected upward accordingly.
- Adding 120 tags detected from autumn 2009 through autumn 2010 gave a corrected minimum deposition estimate of 362 tags.
- The goodness-of-fit test indicated lack of fit, attributed to overdispersion or heterogeneity; a variance inflation factor of 3.6 was applied.

**Predation rates**
- Corrected minimum predation by western gulls breeding on the island was 0.92% of tagged salmonids.
- Watershed-specific minimum predation rates were 0.9% (Scott Creek), 2.9% (Gazos Creek), and 4.6% (Waddell Creek).
- Predation was estimated to be higher for watersheds nearer the island, which the authors attribute to gulls using the island mainly for breeding and roosting.

**Source of predation**
- Tags from lagoon-reared steelhead made up 70% (93 of 133) of wild-origin Scott Creek steelhead detected on the island.
- Lagoon-reared steelhead comprised about 95.5% of returning adults but only about 4.5% of spring out-migrants, according to earlier work cited by the authors.
- Western gulls were the principal predators; sea lions were not considered responsible for most deposited tags. Fecal analysis of sea lions from 2007 onward recovered no PIT tags.

**Comparison with Columbia River**
- Caspian terns on Rice Island consumed an estimated 4.4% of smolts entering the estuary in 1998. A bioenergetics approach estimated 13% for the same colony.
- Caspian terns at Crescent Island were estimated at 7.4% of upper Columbia salmonids, and birds at Potholes Reservoir at 0.03–3.9%.
- The authors attribute the lower coastal rate to the generalist diet of western gulls, the smaller salmonid abundance and shorter availability period in coastal watersheds, and the much larger scale of the Columbia River system.

**Conclusions**
- Predation on juvenile salmonids by western gulls at Año Nuevo Island is a previously undocumented factor that may limit steelhead and coho recovery in central California.
- The estimate is a minimum, since tags deposited elsewhere, in-river mortality, and tag loss not captured by the model are excluded.
- The authors recommend further study, including bioenergetics modeling, to refine estimates.
