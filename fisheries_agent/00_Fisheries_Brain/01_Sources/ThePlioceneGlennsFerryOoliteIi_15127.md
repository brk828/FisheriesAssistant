# Pliocene Glenns Ferry Oolite-II: Sedimentology of Oolitic Lacustrine Terrace Deposits

#sedimentology #limnology #oolite #rift-basin

## Executive Summary

This sedimentological study describes the [[Shoofly Oolite]], a nearshore oolitic unit at the base of the Pliocene [[Glenns Ferry Formation]] along the southwestern margin of [[Lake Idaho]], in the [[Snake River Plain]] rift system. The authors interpret the deposit as a set of wave-built lacustrine benches comparable to the terraces described by Gilbert (1885, 1890) for [[Lake Bonneville]], with the key difference that the sand grains were coated with calcium carbonate. The unit records alternating constructional phases (bench progradation) and destructional phases (lake-level lowering and subaqueous erosion), reflecting strongly fluctuating water depths. The authors attribute this instability mainly to tectonic subsidence and lake-level change, which they suggest may be typical of rift-valley lacustrine systems.

## Background & Description

**Regional setting.** The study area lies in the western [[Snake River Plain]], one of the largest rift systems in North America. Rifting began in the Miocene and continued into post-Miocene time. A Late Cenozoic fill of fluvial-lacustrine clastic and basaltic rocks is thickest in the faulted, downwarped western plain and lies unconformably on older Tertiary volcanics. The shoreface and nearshore facies studied were deposited during a major transgressive phase of this rift-valley lake.

**Stratigraphy.** The basal Glenns Ferry Formation consists of thick oolitic carbonate, occasionally underlain by thin, well-sorted, coarse, friable sand. The oolite (informally named the Shoofly oolite) crops out in a northwest-southeast belt 45 km long and attains thicknesses of more than 35 m. It lies unconformably on offshore, deeper-water lake deposits of the Miocene [[Chalk Hills Formation]] and is conformably overlain by offshore lacustrine silts and volcanic ashes.

**Depositional evidence.** The sands were interpreted as beach and shoreface deposits on the basis of abundant molluscs, disarticulated and rounded fish bones, wood debris, and clasts of underlying lake silts. The oolite as a whole is progradational and palimpsest, containing sands deposited during short periods of progradation within an overall transgressive sequence.

**Study sequences.** The most complete sequences are exposed at [[Castle Creek]], a tributary of the Snake River about 10 km south of Oreana, Idaho. Each complete sequence has two units:
- A lower progradational unit of thinly coated ooids with steep [[Foreset Bedding]], overlain by low-angle topset beds.
- An upper transgressive unit of massive, burrowed, thickly coated oolite.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lake's sedimentary record is interpreted as highly sensitive to water depth, lake level, and sediment supply. Key environmental controls described in the paper include:

- **Lake level and subsidence.** Water depth changes were attributed to absolute lake-level change combined with differential basin subsidence and/or compaction of underlying silts. Tectonic movement is considered the probable driver of this instability.
- **Wave and wind processes.** Rapid progradation probably occurred during higher-energy storm events. Landward ripple migration on bench surfaces is attributed to onshore wind-generated currents during lower-energy periods.
- **Littoral winnowing.** The absence of silt and clay beneath progradational sequences suggests that fines were either winnowed by littoral processes or never delivered to the system.
- **Biological activity.** Infaunal burrowing in the massive units records periodic colonization during transgressions. Lacustrine fauna (fish and molluscs) and vertical burrows support a lacustrine rather than eolian origin.
- **Chemistry.** Ooid formation depended on calcium carbonate precipitation on grains that remained agitated on the bench platform under low sediment influx. The paper notes that these freshwater ooids have few direct analogs and that the Shoofly oolite is the largest lacustrine oolite reported in the stratigraphic record.

## Key Findings

**1. Sedimentary structures and geometry**
- Foreset beds dip basinward to the northeast at an average of 26° (s.d. = 5°, n = 53); foreset directions are consistent across all known localities.
- Individual foreset beds range from 5 to 20 cm thick.
- Progradational units attain thicknesses of 18 m and thicken basinward, mainly through increased foreset height.
- Low-angle topset beds dip up to 10° near the foreset transition and flatten upsection.
- Dish structures and slump structures occur in foresets, with dish structures restricted to the foreset toe.

**2. Grading patterns and sediment transport**
- Foresets show reversed grading (coarsening upward) in proximal positions and normal grading (fining upward) at the distal toe.
- The authors attribute this to grain flow on the upper slope (grain-grain interaction) and fluidized sediment flow on the lower slope.

**3. Ooid composition**
- Foreset ooids are thinly coated: carbonate makes up 8–23% of sediment by weight, and cortical thickness is 0.01–0.02 mm. Carbonate content is uniform within individual foreset beds.
- Massive units are thickly coated: carbonate ranges from 32% to 97% by weight (mean 66%, s.d. = 20%, n = 22), with cortical thicknesses of 0.05–0.6 mm.
- Cortical density is given as 2.3 g/cm³.
- In massive units, cortical thickness and carbonate increase upsection while nucleus size decreases.

**4. Massive transgressive units**
- These are horizontal, uniform in thickness (averaging about 1 m), and intensely burrowed.
- Burrows measure 4–8 cm long and 1–2 cm wide, are stained with iron oxides, and are filled with larger ooids from the overlying massive unit.

**5. Tectonic tilting and stabilization**
- Sequence 1 massive unit dips basinward at 2.5°, sequence 2 at 1°, and sequence 3 is horizontal.
- The authors interpret this as evidence of progressive subsidence and then stabilization of the lake floor toward the end of deposition.

**6. Depositional history at Castle Creek (Figure 8 water-depth reconstruction)**
- Each cycle comprises: (1) decreasing water depth during progradation; (2) lake-level lowering and subaqueous erosion; (3) increasing water depth and formation of massive palimpsest oolite.
- Second progradational unit formed at water depths of about 18 m, with depths reducing to a few meters during bench building.
- The third bench sequence consists of a foreset wedge up to 3 m thick overlain by up to 8 massive oolitic units. The foresets were likely originally thicker, implying erosion of several meters.
- Ooid formation ceased as the shoreline migrated landward, and widespread deep-water lake silts and ashes of the upper Glenns Ferry Formation accumulated.

**7. Conclusions**
- The Shoofly oolite is the largest lacustrine oolite yet reported in the stratigraphic record. Its geometry, structures, and setting resemble Gilbert-type wave-built terraces, but the deposit lacks bottomset beds and its topsets are bench deposits rather than fluvial units, so the authors reject a deltaic origin.
- The unit records rapidly fluctuating water depths in the nearshore transgressive phase of Pliocene Lake Idaho.
- The authors propose the Shoofly oolite as a model for cyclic deposition of wave-built terraces and benches in rift-valley lakes.
