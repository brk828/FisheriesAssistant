## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by Hynes & Roberts examined the biological effects of synthetic detergent (ABS) pollution in the River Lee, Hertfordshire, a river almost entirely composed of sewage effluent from Luton (and, to a lesser extent, Harpenden). Between 1950 and 1961, detergent concentrations in the effluent rose from ~1 ppm to 3–4 ppm (1958), then fell to ~2 ppm (1960) following the "Luton Experiment," which introduced biodegradable ("soft") ABS detergents. Despite a roughly threefold rise and subsequent partial decline in detergent concentration, **no clear, unambiguous biological changes in the invertebrate fauna, fish populations, or general pollution zonation (the [[Cladophora-Asellus Zone]]) could be attributed directly to detergent levels**; observed faunal shifts were better explained by variable sewage-treatment performance and an industrial copper cyanide spill (1953). However, controlled laboratory experiments demonstrated that ABS detergents — even after passage through sewage treatment — are **acutely toxic to submerged aquatic macrophytes** (e.g., [[Ranunculus pseudofluitans]], [[Potamogeton pectinatus]], [[Potamogeton densus]]) at concentrations as low as 1–2.5 ppm, suggesting detergents likely suppress macrophyte diversity in the river, in contrast to the comparatively low direct toxicity to fish reported in prior literature at equivalent post-treatment concentrations.

## Background & Description

This paper is not primarily morphological, but the following fish-relevant notes are recorded:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]] (Salmo gairdnerii)**: Referenced from prior toxicological literature (Roberts 1954; Herbert et al. 1957) as a bioassay species for ABS toxicity; no morphological description given, used purely as a sensitive indicator organism for detergent toxicity trials (not caught in the River Lee survey itself).
- **[[Three-Spined Stickleback]] (Gasterosteus sp.)**: Present in the river fauna at low-oxygen, organically enriched stations; noted as sensitive to both pollution (eliminated during the 1953 copper cyanide spill) and to high-flow "spate" conditions (swept away in late 1960), indicating limited swimming/shelter-seeking capacity in a shallow, featureless gravel-bed river.
- **[[Eurasian Minnow]] (Phoxinus phoxinus)**: Fewer than ten specimens collected across the whole survey period; first appeared at sampling stations only in 1959–1960, coincident with declining detergent concentrations — interpreted as a sign of modest ecological recovery. No morphological detail provided.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: A chalk-stream tributary (River Lee) whose natural spring-fed flow has been depleted by groundwater abstraction; for ~16 miles below Luton the channel is fed almost entirely by treated sewage effluent, creating a de facto effluent-dominated lotic ecosystem.
- **Substrate**: Flint gravel with variable silt cover; hard-bedded reaches unsuitable for rooted macrophytes such as *Potamogeton pectinatus*, independent of detergent effects.
- **Pollution zonation**: The river existed largely within the **[[Cladophora-Asellus Zone]]** of organic pollution (per Hynes' 1960 pollution-biology classification), with a variable short upstream **[[Chironomus Zone]]** whose extent contracted over the study period as effluent quality improved.
- **Indicator taxa structure**:
  - Pollution-tolerant dominants: *Chironomus*, *Asellus*, *Cladophora glomerata* (filamentous alga).
  - Moderately sensitive recovering taxa: *Gammarus pulex*, *Baetis* spp. (mayflies), *Hydropsyche* (caddisfly), Hydracarina (water mites), *Simulium* (blackfly larvae), *Helmis* (riffle beetle).
  - Fish (sticklebacks, minnows) functioned as top-tier sensitive indicators, appearing/disappearing in tandem with more sensitive invertebrates and reflecting both water quality and hydrological disturbance (spates).
- **Trophic/microbial base**: A persistent diatom biofilm (*Navicula*, *Synedra*) covered the streambed at all detergent concentrations tested, indicating high detergent tolerance in some primary producers even where macrophytes failed.
- **Detergent fate**: ABS detergents (resistant "hard" alkyl chains vs. more biodegradable "soft" chains) persisted through sewage treatment at concentrations sufficient to remain phytotoxic, even though prior work (Herbert et al. 1957) suggested treated effluent detergent was not seriously toxic to fish.

## Key Findings

1. **No detectable direct correlation between detergent concentration and fish or invertebrate community structure** across the 1950–1961 study period, despite an approximate threefold rise (1950–58) and subsequent ~46% decline (1958–60) in ABS concentration in the effluent (from ~1 ppm to 3.5–4 ppm, back down to ~2 ppm).
2. Biological changes that did occur (e.g., loss of *Gammarus*, *Asellus*, *Tanytarsus*, and fish following the 1953 event) were attributable to a **copper cyanide industrial discharge**, not detergent trends.
3. **Sticklebacks were eliminated in 1953** (cyanide spill) but had **returned to Lemsford by 1954**; they and **minnows re-appeared/increased at multiple stations by 1958–1959** as sewage treatment performance and detergent levels both improved — but sticklebacks **disappeared again from all but Lemsford by April 1961**, likely due to high-flow ("spate") conditions in late 1960 rather than detergent chemistry, since the river offers little physical shelter from swift currents.
4. Historical toxicology cited: **[[Rainbow Trout]] exposed to 3.0 ppm ABS detergent (retail packet formulation) suffered 50% mortality within 12 weeks** (Herbert et al. 1957), with toxicity exacerbated by low dissolved oxygen — however, detergent toxicity to fish was reported to **decrease substantially after passage through sewage treatment**.
5. In sharp contrast, **detergents remained highly phytotoxic to aquatic macrophytes even after full sewage treatment**: *Ranunculus pseudofluitans* was severely damaged by treated effluent containing only 2–3 ppm detergent, and by concentrations as low as 1 ppm derived from effluent foam.
6. Controlled dish experiments showed dose-dependent phytotoxicity thresholds:
   - *Cladophora glomerata*: died at 10–25 ppm within 3 weeks; unhealthy at 5 ppm; unaffected at ≤2.5 ppm.
   - *Eurhynchium riparioides/rusciforme*: unaffected ≤5 ppm; degraded at ≥10 ppm.
   - *Ranunculus pseudofluitans* and *Potamogeton pectinatus*: seriously affected at 2.5 ppm and by realistic treated-effluent concentrations (2–3 ppm).
   - *Potamogeton densus*: adversely affected at 2.5–10 ppm.
   - Non-ionic detergent **Lissapol had no phytotoxic effect up to 50 ppm**, implicating the anionic (ABS) surfactant chemistry specifically, rather than surfactancy in general, as the toxic mechanism.
7. **Micro-organism/diatom communities were comparatively resistant**: only the highest tested concentration (50 ppm) eliminated diatoms, rotifers, and protozoa in laboratory cultures; the natural river retained a diatom biofilm (*Navicula*, *Synedra*) throughout the study regardless of detergent level.
8. The overall pollution classification of the river remained fixed in the **Cladophora-Asellus Zone** throughout 1950–1961; reduction of detergent from ~3–4 ppm to ~2 ppm (the achievable limit of the "Luton Experiment" biodegradable-detergent program) produced only marginal shifts in faunal sensitivity and did not fundamentally alter the pollution status of the river.
9. The authors conclude that **detergent effects on fish and invertebrate fauna in effluent-dominated rivers are likely secondary to and masked by other water-quality parameters** (organic loading, B.O.D., oxygen, industrial toxicants), whereas **detergents exert a disproportionately strong and directly toxic effect on submerged flowering plants and mosses**, plausibly explaining long-term compositional differences in aquatic vegetation between the River Lee and comparable unpolluted chalk streams in the region.
