# Alewife, Rainbow Smelt and Native Fishes in Lake Michigan: Competition or Predation?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Coregonidae #Clupeidae #Osmeridae

## Executive Summary

Crowder (1980), published in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* (Vol. 5, No. 3, pp. 225–233), examines why several native fish species in [[Lake Michigan]] declined sharply during the increases of [[Rainbow Smelt]] (1930s) and [[Alewife]] (1950s–1960s). The paper proposes that predation by these two exotic planktivores on pelagic eggs and larvae, rather than competition alone, was a probable contributor to recruitment failure. The argument rests on reproductive guild theory ([[Balon Reproductive Guild Classification]]), spawning depth overlap, egg size relative to zooplankton, and comparisons with other Great Lakes. The author explicitly labels the predation hypothesis as largely untestable for extinct species and offers it as a testable alternative to competition.

## Background & Description

- **Study type:** Synthetic review and hypothesis paper; no new primary field dataset is reported beyond cited sources.
- **Focal taxa:** Native pelagophils and litho-pelagophils (e.g., [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Lake Herring]], [[Bloater]], [[Kiyi]], multiple [[Cisco]] species) and species of concern such as [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Deepwater Sculpin]].
- **Exotic invaders:** Rainbow smelt was planted in Crystal Lake, Michigan, in 1912 and spread rapidly in the 1930s. Alewife was first recorded in northern Lake Michigan in 1949 and increased exponentially in the late 1950s, plausibly due to a shortage of piscivores.
- **Framework:** Fish are classified by [[Reproductive Guild]] (Balon 1975). Pelagophils have non-adhesive, dispersed, near-neutral or buoyant eggs; litho-pelagophils deposit eggs on rocky bottoms where they are often buoyant and suspended by currents.
- **Table 1** summarizes spawning habit, egg diameter, and abundance trend for roughly 25 Lake Michigan species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical shifts.** Between 1930 and 1970, several native species declined to rarity or local extinction. Burbot fluctuated and were linked to [[Sea Lamprey]] abundance. Competing hypotheses included size-selective fishing, size-selective lamprey predation, competition for zooplankton, and predation by alewife or smelt.

**Egg and larval availability.** Pelagic eggs of pelagophils and litho-pelagophils are large relative to zooplankton. Emerald shiner eggs measure 3.00–3.25 mm; most rare pelagophil eggs are 2 mm or more, roughly twice the average size of the largest cladoceran in 1954.

**Zooplankton restructuring.** Between 1954 and 1966, mean zooplankton size declined sharply as alewife increased. Large zooplankters (1.13–5 mm) became extremely rare, while small species (0.43–0.93 mm) became more abundant. In 1966, only one calanoid copepod (*Limnocalanus macrurus*) exceeded 2 mm, and all cladocerans averaged under 1 mm. Zooplankton recovered toward 1954 composition after a 1967 alewife die-off.

**Depth and timing overlap.** Spawning depths and seasons were compared with adult alewife and smelt distributions (Figure 2):
- Alewife overlap the spawning habitat of shortnose cisco, emerald shiner, longjaw cisco, shortjaw cisco, and bloater, and possibly deepwater cisco, kiyi, and blackfin cisco (alewife found at ≥130 m in winter).
- Smelt remain in water generally shallower than 60 m and overlap with several cisco species and lake herring larvae, which share shallow winter habitat.

**Predator-diet evidence.** Direct observations are sparse. Alewife and smelt consume eggs, and both feed on young fish. Smelt consumed 3–11% of lake herring larvae in western Lake Superior (Selgeby et al. 1978). Rapid digestion (smelt larvae unrecognizable within an hour in alewife) means stomach surveys likely underestimate predation.

**Experimental evidence from Poland.** In Zawisza & Backiel (1970), fish and invertebrates both contributed to egg loss of *Coregonus albula*. Losses were significantly higher in open cages than closed cages (p < 0.05), implying fish had a greater effect. The author notes that buoyant eggs may have floated away, confounding the result.

## Key Findings

1. **Rarity pattern is non-random.** Nearly all native species that became rare or locally extinct are pelagophils or litho-pelagophils, with large, pelagic, or semi-buoyant eggs/larvae.

2. **Predation hypothesis favored for several species.** Crowder proposes that predation on early life stages probably played an important role in the final decline of [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Longjaw Cisco]], [[Kiyi]], [[Shortnose Cisco]], [[Shortjaw Cisco]], and [[Lake Herring]], and caused fluctuations in [[Bloater]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Deepwater Sculpin]].

3. **Emerald shiner most affected.** Predation on large pelagic eggs by alewife and smelt is judged the greatest mortality effect for this species, which declined first in northern Lake Michigan, consistent with alewife spread.

4. **Overfishing and lamprey as primary drivers for some species.** Deepwater cisco and blackfin cisco were rare before smelt or alewife became prominent, so predation is considered unlikely to have caused their decline. Size-selective fishing and lamprey predation are the main factors for longjaw, kiyi, shortnose, and shortjaw ciscoes, with alewife possibly decisive in their final decline.

5. **Bloater decline linked to alewife.** Bloaters declined to near extinction and recruitment approached zero at peak alewife abundance. Recovery followed alewife reduction after the 1967 crash and predation by stocked salmonids. Competition with alewife is judged less likely.

6. **Competition not excluded.** The competition hypothesis cannot be fully tested with available data. Declining recruitment is not a necessary consequence of adult competition, and smaller zooplankton (larval food) actually increased.

7. **Lake herring tied to smelt dynamics.** Lake herring produced a strong year class during a 1942–43 smelt die-off and declined as smelt increased. Christie (1974) also found lake herring declines more closely tied to smelt than alewife.

8. **Yellow perch and deepwater sculpin.** Yellow perch decline in the early to mid-1960s spread northward to southward, matching the alewife pattern. Eggs are encased in gelatinous sheaths and are unlikely prey, so larval predation or food competition is proposed. Deepwater sculpin recruitment failed during decline, and larvae are pelagic and unprotected after hatching.

9. **Slimy sculpin unaffected.** Slimy sculpin, which spawn under rocks with adhesive eggs guarded by males, did not decline and are unlikely to suffer substantial early-life predation.

10. **Rehabilitation implication.** Restoring pelagophil and litho-pelagophil stocks requires limiting planktivore intensity; combined size-selective adult mortality and egg/larval predation may depress recruitment below critical levels.

11. **Proposed test.** Predation can be assessed by examining promptly preserved alewife and smelt stomachs in zones overlapping dense egg or larval concentrations, combined with estimates of prey density. The author notes this makes the predation hypothesis "an interesting and testable alternative to competition."
