# Comments on the USFWS Preliminary Draft Lower Colorado River Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment package, dated March 30, 1995, from three state agencies: the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]], the [[Colorado River Board of California]], and the [[Colorado River Commission of Nevada]]. It responds to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) preliminary draft *Lower Colorado River Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus* for four endangered "big river" fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].

The agencies support the prospectus as a constructive starting point. They ask for clearer interim measures, explicit priorities, stronger attention to non-native predators, a more regional planning scope, and a realistic approach to water use. They reject the idea that the Lower Colorado River can be returned to natural hydrographs or pre-dam conditions.

## Background & Description

The prospectus addresses four endangered native fishes of the Colorado River system. Two are cyprinids (squawfish, bonytail, humpback chub) and one is a catostomid (razorback sucker). The comments are grouped by page, paragraph and sentence, and many concern the scope and wording of the draft.

Key background points from the letter:

- **Basin division:** The agencies state that the division of the Colorado River Basin derives from the 1922 Colorado River Compact, which sets the dividing point at Lee Ferry, below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. They ask that the prospectus use Glen Canyon Dam as the dividing point for native fisheries management.
- **Reintroduced squawfish:** Reintroduced populations in the Salt and Verde rivers are described as possibly small and fragmentary, and are derived from an Upper Basin gene pool.
- **Relevant programs:** The agencies cite the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]], the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]], the [[Native Fish Work Group]], the [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program]], and the [[Lower Division States/Tribes Endangered Species Steering Committee]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The agencies identify several factors that shape recovery feasibility:

- **Non-native predators:** Non-indigenous fishes are a central concern. The agencies argue that aggressive non-native fish management is a major element of any recovery program, and that recovery cannot succeed until non-native predation is addressed.
- **Habitat alteration:** The Lower Basin ecoregion is highly altered. The agencies state that restoring "natural habitats" and "dispersal corridors" may not be feasible, and they dispute that dams have produced long, dry reaches.
- **Free-flowing reaches:** The USFWS notes that the Upper Basin has a substantially greater proportion of free-flowing river. The agencies agree and note that free-flowing reaches are fewer and less extensive in the Lower Basin.
- **Lacustrine habitat:** Bonytail and razorback suckers have persisted for several decades in Lower Basin reservoirs, though without successful recruitment. The agencies suggest reservoirs may serve as stop-gap refugia for adult fish.
- **Water supply:** The Lower Division States are using nearly all of their 7.5 million acre-foot mainstream allocation. The agencies say no unallocated apportionment is readily available for fish flows.
- **Genetic vulnerability:** Bonytail are extremely rare, with all current progeny possibly stemming from as few as a dozen adults. Humpback chub occur in appreciable numbers in a limited area near the Little Colorado River confluence and could be vulnerable to catastrophe.

## Key Findings

**Goals and objectives**
- Establish self-perpetuating populations first, then stabilize and maintain them. Supplemental maintenance or augmentation (e.g., stocking) may be needed if self-perpetuation is not attainable.
- Add an objective for management and control of non-indigenous fishes.
- Clarify Objective 4 (natural habitats and dispersal corridors), which conflicts with the prospectus's description of a stable water and hydropower base.
- Fund and complete the Level I through VI objectives (Figure 1) in sequence, using adaptive management.
- Revise the stated purpose, which implies return to pre-dam conditions. The agencies call this unacceptable.

**Squawfish designation**
- The agencies oppose changing reintroduced squawfish from "experimental non-essential" to "experimental essential" status. They argue the non-essential designation does not preclude recovery and delisting.
- They ask whether the USFWS intends to expand critical habitat for squawfish in the Lower Basin, and request that any such intent be stated in the prospectus.

**Coordination and governance**
- The USFWS should expressly coordinate the prospectus with the Lower Colorado River EcoRegion Plan and the Steering Committee process.
- Each of the six named state agencies should be represented on the "Management Team."
- Study protocols should be evaluated by state, federal, academic and private experts before implementation, not only by the USFWS.

**Genetics and propagation**
- The genetic management plan should cover all four species, not only the razorback sucker.
- The USFWS should expand captive propagation. Options include retrofitting existing hatcheries, reopening closed facilities such as [[Niland Hatchery]], and building facilities on Tribal lands.

**Land and water use**
- The prospectus should consider Tribal, Bureau of Land Management and state lands, not only USFWS refuges.
- Specific habitat projects should be identified.
- Water-based strategies should be evaluated against alternatives that do not depend on additional releases.
- The agencies consider draining major reservoirs on the Salt, Verde or Gila rivers virtually nil, and dispute the claimed potential for free-flowing restoration in those systems.

**Data**
- The agencies support a centralized, standardized database. They recommend that it be built with the Arizona, California and Nevada wildlife agencies and with academic and private partners.
