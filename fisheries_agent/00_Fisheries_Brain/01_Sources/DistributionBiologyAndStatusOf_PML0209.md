# Distribution, Biology and Status of the Roundtail Chub, Gila robusta, in the Gila River Basin, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 Colorado State University master's thesis by Kevin R. Bestgen assesses the status and biology of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta grahami*) in the [[Gila River Basin]] of New Mexico. Fieldwork ran from December 1982 to December 1984. The study combined historic museum and agency records with new collections to document distribution, abundance, systematics, [[Habitat Preferences]], [[Food Habits]], [[Reproduction]], [[Age and Growth]] and interactions with introduced fishes. The species has declined sharply in the lower Gila River and is presumed extirpated from the [[San Francisco River]]. The main proximate threat identified is predation by introduced piscivores, especially [[Smallmouth Bass]], while habitat degradation from arroyo cutting, grazing and channelization is the underlying driver.

## Background & Description

The roundtail chub belongs to the *Gila robusta* complex, a group of western cyprinids with 15 recognized species and many subspecies (Robins et al. 1980). The complex is restricted to the Colorado River drainage and includes four subspecies. *G. r. grahami* is described as nearly a Gila River endemic, occurring in small to medium streams in Arizona and the Gila River in New Mexico. Its taxonomy is unsettled: the chubs of the Gila basin were under study by Bruce DeMarais at Arizona State University, and chubs from Hardin Cienega Creek may belong to *G. intermedia*.

Little was known about the species in New Mexico before this study. Earlier life history work came from two Arizona streams (Barber and Minckley 1966; Neve 1976; Schreiber and Minckley 1978). The species is listed as rare in both Arizona and New Mexico.

Morphometric and meristic data were collected from five geographic subgroups: Hardin Cienega Creek, the San Francisco River, the Gila headwaters, the mainstream Gila below [[Mogollon Creek]], and [[Turkey Creek]]. Results were variable, and the author drew no firm taxonomic conclusions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Gila River rises on the southern rim of the Mogollon Plateau and flows about 177 km through New Mexico, draining roughly 8,296 km². Elevations range from 3,320 m at Mogollon Baldy to 1,158 m where the river leaves the state. The San Francisco River enters New Mexico at 2,266 m, flows about 150 km, and drains about 4,500 km². Headwater reaches lie within the Gila National Forest and Gila Wilderness, while downstream valleys are largely private cropland and pasture.

**Climate and hydrology.** Climate varies with elevation. Annual precipitation ranges from about 22 cm near Glenwood to more than 75 cm in the Mogollon Mountains, and most of the study area receives 30–40 cm. Flow is highly variable. Irrigation diversions often dry the Redrock and Virden reaches, and the author notes that the river had not flowed year-round within living memory until 1983–1984. Water temperatures range from near 0 °C to 32 °C.

**Land-use history.** Arroyo cutting began around 1890, drawing down water tables, lowering streamflow, and draining cienegas. The author attributes this mainly to overgrazing that began around 1880, with drought and climate as contributing factors. Mangas and Duck Creeks now lie more than 10 m below their former floodplains.

**Turkey Creek.** This tributary, located about 13 km northeast of Cliff, is the only population that could withstand heavy sampling. It holds perennial flow for about 19 km, deep bedrock pools over 3 m deep, and a dense canopy of Arizona sycamore and alder. A barrier falls about 5 km above the confluence isolates the upper population from predatory fishes. Hot springs (about 74 °C) enter upstream of the falls.

**Associated species.** Twenty-two fish species were captured. Native species include [[Longfin Dace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Speckled Dace]], plus two suckers. Introduced predators include [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Bluegill]]. The Gila trout was not collected, although it occurs in nearby headwaters.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- 34 collections were made at 155 sites, yielding 69,340 fishes. Roundtail chubs were captured in 52 collections (15%) at 29 localities, totaling 857 individuals.
- Of these, 590 (69%) came from Turkey Creek, 138 (16%) from the Middle Fork of the Gila, and 129 (15%) from the mainstream Gila and the East and West Forks.
- Chub occurrence in collections below Mogollon Creek fell from 71% (historic to 1960) to 17% in the present study. Above Mogollon Creek, occurrence fell from 30% to 54% and then recovered to 45% after adjustment.
- Recent Gila mainstream collections averaged fewer than 3 chubs per positive collection. Historic collections yielded up to 71 fish (Koster, 1949).
- Chubs have not been collected from the San Francisco River since 1948 and are presumed extirpated there. Koster's 1948 collection was the last.
- The species appears to have declined by about 20% in range since Koster's distribution records, with the most downstream record near the Arizona border.

**Habitat**
- Adults were found in deep, slow pools near banks with overhead cover. Young fish used shallower, slower water near shore.
- After the October 1983 flood, young chubs were found in riffles away from banks, showing some flexibility in habitat use.

**Food habits**
- A total of 199 stomachs were examined. Chubs were mainly insectivorous (dipterans, mayflies, caddisflies), with algae, gastropods, crayfish and fish also eaten.
- Chubs ate fewer food groups than syntopic trout (13 trout stomachs), which also consumed terrestrial invertebrates. The author suggests chubs are more selective than previously reported.
- Gut length to standard length ratio for 55 fish averaged 0.99:1.0 (range 0.76–1.33), indicating a carnivorous diet.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurs in late spring to early summer. Temperature appears to be the main cue, with ripe Gila fish observed at about 17–22 °C.
- Turkey Creek females matured at a smaller size and younger age than Gila River females. The average reproducing Turkey Creek female was 3.2 years old, 152 mm TL, and produced 5,449 eggs. The average East Fork female was 5.2 years old, 306 mm TL, and produced 33,440 eggs.
- The author interprets earlier maturity in small habitats as a strategy to offset early mortality.

**Age and growth**
- Scales were the only reliable ageing structure. The back-calculation equation was TL = 28.1 + 3.51(SR).
- Turkey Creek fish reached a maximum age of V+, two years less than Gila River fish, which reached VII+. Turkey Creek fish grew more slowly at all ages.
- The author notes that scale-based ages may underestimate maximum age in long-lived fish, drawing on comparisons with [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]].

**Predation and threats**
- Smallmouth bass have reduced or eliminated chub populations in at least two localities. At the Turkey Creek "keyhole" barrier falls, bass are present below the falls and chubs are absent.
- Predation by introduced piscivores is considered the primary cause of historic chub declines. Habitat loss from grazing, channelization and arroyo cutting is a secondary cause.

**Conservation and management**
- Turkey Creek and the Middle Fork are the only populations considered plentiful, though their persistence is described as tenuous.
- A proposed dam on the Gila near the Middle Box would inundate about 20% of current chub habitat and would allow predators to access the river. The author concludes that the species will persist only if the river's natural attributes are preserved.
