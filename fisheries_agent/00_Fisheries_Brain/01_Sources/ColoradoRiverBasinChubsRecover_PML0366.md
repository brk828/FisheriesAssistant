# Colorado River Basin Chubs Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan, prepared by the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] (NMDGF) in September 2006, addresses three state-endangered chubs of the genus *Gila* in New Mexico: [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Gila Chub]] (*G. intermedia*), and [[Headwater Chub]] (*G. nigra*). The plan was developed under the [[New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act]] (WCA) and serves as New Mexico's strategy under the [[Three Species Rangewide Conservation Agreement]].

All three species have declined sharply. Roundtail chub occupy only about 45% of their historical Colorado River Basin range, and fewer than 50 have been collected from the San Juan mainstem since the early 1990s. Gila chub persist in New Mexico only in [[Turkey Creek]], and headwater chub are declining in the forks of the Gila River. The principal threats identified are [[Nonnative Fish Species]] (predation and competition), altered flow regimes from dam operations, habitat loss from watershed degradation and wildfire ash flows, and low population numbers that leave the chubs vulnerable to stochastic events.

The management goal is to make populations of all three chubs secure and self-sustaining across their historical New Mexico ranges. Objectives are downlisting to threatened by 2015 and delisting by 2025, supported by eight management issues, each with specific strategies, and a prioritized implementation schedule.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The three chubs belong to the minnow family Cyprinidae. Taxonomy of *Gila* has been debated. Roundtail and Gila chubs have been recognized as valid species for over 20 years. Headwater chub was formerly called *G. grahami*, but under nomenclature rules it was replaced by *G. nigra* (Cope and Yarrow 1875) in 2000. The three species are closely related, morphologically similar, and genetically similar. Headwater chub is morphologically intermediate between the other two and may share ancestry with both.

**Morphology.**
- *Roundtail chub:* streamlined, fusiform body; adults mottled dark gray to olive dorsally; 80–85 lateral line scales; typically 250–350 mm TL as adults, exceptionally over 400 mm.
- *Gila chub:* chunkier, more robust body; typically reaches about 150 mm, with females possibly exceeding 200 mm; 75 lateral line scales.
- *Headwater chub:* more streamlined than Gila chub; 73–83 lateral line scales; adults reach 250–400 mm TL based on field observations.

All three develop orange-red breeding coloration at fin bases and breeding tubercles, with males more intense.

**Distribution.** Roundtail chub historically ranged from Wyoming and Colorado south through the Colorado basin to Sonora and Sinaloa, Mexico. Gila chub were restricted to the Gila River basin in Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora. Headwater chub are also restricted to the Gila basin in Arizona and New Mexico. Historically, no two of the three species were collected from the same site.

**Life history.**
- *Food habits:* All three are omnivorous. Roundtail chub eat mainly aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, with plants, detritus, and fish as secondary items. Gila chub show an ontogenetic shift from debris and plants to invertebrates and small fish.
- *Reproduction:* Spawning occurs in spring and early summer, over clean gravel (roundtail) or vegetation and root wads (Gila chub). Roundtail fecundity ranges from 1,000–4,300 eggs in 100–269 mm females (Fossil Creek) to 14,163–45,124 eggs in 299–368 mm females (Gila River). Eggs are adhesive and demersal. Roundtail chub mature at 2–5 years; Gila chub typically mature in their second year.
- *Growth and longevity:* Roundtail chub grow about 70 mm in year one and about 50 mm per year thereafter until about age 4. The largest individuals are usually 7–10 years old, though they have been recorded to 20.
- *Movement:* Roundtail chub may make substantial spawning migrations. In the Navajo River, the average distance moved between contacts was 650 m and the maximum exceeded 13 km. Gila chub are most active at crepuscular periods.
- *Parasites:* Chubs appear highly susceptible to parasites. Roundtail chub have been infected by several parasites, including *Lernaea* copepods, and parasite infection has been linked to reduced condition in the Verde River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Roundtail chub favour deep (>1 m) complex pools with cover such as boulders and woody debris, intermixed with riffles, runs, and eddies. Gila chub occupy deep pools in small streams and cienegas near vegetated undercut banks and woody debris. Headwater chub use similar pools and runs near cover, in water up to 26.5 °C and velocities below 20 cm/s, at elevations of 1,325–2,000 m. No documented case shows Gila and headwater chub in the same habitat.

**San Juan River.** Flow was regulated by [[Navajo Dam]] (built 1958–1963), which stabilized flows and raised water temperatures. Sediment loads rose during livestock overgrazing in the late 1800s and early 1900s, then fell after nonnative tamarisk and Russian olive established. The river is subject to selenium, nutrient, pathogen, and temperature impairments; 136 river miles are listed as "Not Supporting" designated uses. Reoperation of the dam since 1992 aims to mimic a natural flow regime through the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]].

**Zuni River.** Logging and overgrazing caused severe watershed degradation, and reservoirs now trap sediment and alter channel form. Habitat of adequate quality is currently lacking, and flow is intermittent.

**Gila River.** The Gila is the only free-flowing river in New Mexico, with no major dams on the mainstem. Its main pressures are livestock grazing, bank instability, diversions (including two lowhead dams on the San Francisco River), and wildfire ash flows in the West, Middle, and East forks. Historical cienegas have been lost to groundwater pumping and grazing. Water quality is better than most New Mexico waters, but nutrients, siltation, and high temperatures are problems.

**Associated species.** Native associates include [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Loach Minnow]]. Widespread nonnative species include channel catfish, red shiner, common carp, smallmouth bass, and green sunfish. Channel catfish are considered particularly detrimental in the San Juan basin.

**Human context.** San Juan County grew 24.2% between 1990 and 2000; McKinley County grew 23% to 74,798 people. Human population in the Gila basin remains low overall. Public Law 108-451 (2004) allows New Mexico to develop up to 14,000 acre-feet per year from the Gila River, which raises concern for native fish.

## Key Findings

- **Roundtail chub** occupies only about 45% of its historical Colorado River Basin range (Bezzerides and Bestgen 2002).
- **San Juan River:** roundtail chub is absent from the mainstem between Navajo Dam and the Animas confluence. Fewer than 50 were collected from the mainstem after the early 1990s. A remnant population was rediscovered in the Navajo River in 2001, after no record since 1976.
- **Zuni River:** roundtail chub presumed extirpated in New Mexico, with no collection since 1853 (Zuni River records vary across the text; the type specimen was collected in 1851).
- **Gila River:** roundtail chub has not been found in the San Francisco River since 1948, and is rare or absent in the Gila mainstem.
- **Gila chub:** the only extant New Mexico population is in Turkey Creek above barrier waterfalls. It is the largest and only multi-age-class population known in the state, but abundance was greatly reduced by wildfire ash flows. It was listed as Endangered by New Mexico in 1978; critical habitat in New Mexico is limited to Turkey Creek.
- **Headwater chub:** still regularly found in the forks of the Gila River, but declining, with absence of young fish in the Middle Fork. Approved for state listing as Endangered in November 2006.
- **Federal status:** Gila chub was listed as Endangered with Critical Habitat in November 2005. Headwater chub was listed as a Candidate species in April 2006. Roundtail chub was found "not warranted" for listing in April 2006.
- **Main decline drivers:** nonnative species (catfish, bass), flow regulation, habitat loss, and wildfire ash flows. Nonnative predators showed very limited spatial overlap with chubs, yet the decline is still attributed to their presence.
- **Goal and objectives:** secure, self-sustaining populations across historical New Mexico range; downlisting to threatened by 2015 and delisting by 2025. Genetic and demographic assessment is due in the San Juan basin by 2008 and the Gila basin by 2010.
- **Management issues (8):** distribution information gaps, limited populations, habitat loss, angling and public use (including a proposed ban on taking headwater chub), nonnative dominance, information gaps, fragmented management, and related coordination needs. Each has specific strategies.
- **Economic outlook:** conservation benefits are real but hard to quantify. Recovery actions are voluntary, so direct economic effects cannot be predicted precisely. Willingness-to-pay studies show public values for similar species (for example, a mean of $265 per household per year for nine fish in six Four Corners rivers, Loomis 1998).
