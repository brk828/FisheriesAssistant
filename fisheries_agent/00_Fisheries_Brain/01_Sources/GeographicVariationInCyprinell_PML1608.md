# Geographic Variation in Cyprinella lutrensis (Pisces: Cyprinidae) in the United States, with Notes on Cyprinella lepida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 study by [[Matthews]] (published in *Copeia*) examines geographic variation in the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) across its native United States range. Using 1,024 individuals from 105 localities, the author combined meristic counts, morphometric measurements, multivariate analyses and spatial autocorrelation tests, with additional observations of nuptial coloration. Variation is mostly local rather than directional, and the author argues that the Nueces-Frio form, [[Cyprinella lepida]], is distinct enough to warrant recognition as a full species. A possible northeastern subspecies, *C. l. forbesi*, is also flagged for further study.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), a widespread cyprinid of the central United States. Its nominal subspecies were unresolved in the literature, including *C. l. lutrensis*, *C. l. suavis*, *C. l. forbesi* and *C. l. blairi*.
- **Related taxon:** [[Cyprinella lepida]] (Nueces, Frio and upper Guadalupe rivers, Texas), historically treated as a species, then as a form of *C. lutrensis*, and recently re-elevated as a species.
- **Taxonomic history:** Earlier workers used [[Subspecies Classification]] schemes based on coastal versus prairie forms. Mexican variation was studied by Contreras-B. (1975), and Wooten (1983) studied genetic and morphological variation in Oklahoma and Texas.
- **Methods:** Specimens were drawn from museum collections and the author's 1978 field collections (43 of the 105 localities). Counts and measurements followed [[Hubbs and Ortenburger Methods]], with 9 meristic counts and 19 measurements retained. Analyses used [[Principal Components Analysis]], [[Sheared Principal Components Analysis]], [[Mantel Test]] and [[Spatial Autocorrelation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drainages:** Specimens were grouped into 18 major drainage areas, plus Illinois-upper Mississippi-Missouri (Drainages 1-3), Great Plains (4-9), and Gulf coastal drainages (10-18).
- **Habitat associations:** Shape differences were linked to flow. Populations from zero or slow-flowing streams (N = 19) differed significantly from those in moderate or fast current (N = 24) (F = 8.276, P < 0.01). Upland and lowland populations showed no significant shape difference, and stream width showed no significant effect.
- **Coloration and hybridization:** In Kansas and Missouri, three populations had yellow fins, and a Chikaskia River sample showed intermediate individuals. Hybridization with a native species was considered but not confirmed.
- **Disturbance:** The author noted that the Frio and Guadalupe rivers had been affected by disturbance, which may have contributed to hybridization in the past.
- **Temperature:** The author suggested that developmental temperature differences may explain the regional pattern in meristic variability.

## Key Findings

- **Sample size:** 1,024 *C. lutrensis* from 105 locations, with 10 individuals from most localities.
- **Meristics:** All nine meristic characters differed significantly across the range. Regional patterns were weak and often local. Spatial autocorrelation was significant and positive up to about 400 km, and significant and negative at 800-1300 km.
- **Morphometrics:** All proportional measurements differed among drainages (ANOVA, P < 0.001). Great Plains fish had greater mean body depth than any coastal drainage. Head depth and eye size were variable among coastal drainages.
- **Shape (H scores):** H scores differed significantly among 18 drainages (F = 5.42, P < 0.001). No significant relationship with latitude or longitude, and Mantel tests showed no simple geographic pattern.
- **Meristic variation:** Mean coefficient of variation exceeded 5% in 42 populations and 6% in 16 populations. Northern populations were the least variable.
- **Groups:** The author identified a Great Plains group, a coastal group with low scale counts, and an intermediate group, while noting that the groupings were heuristic.
- **Color:** Male *C. lepida* of the Frio-Nueces had bright yellow fins, orange scales with purple posterior borders, and a distinctive pattern unlike any *C. lutrensis* in the US.
- **Taxonomic conclusions:**
  - *C. lepida* of the Nueces-Frio is "distinct from all C. lutrensis groups in the United States" and warrants full species status. The author noted that this had been recognized by Mayden (in press) on morphological grounds and by Gold (pers. comm.) on biochemical evidence.
  - The coastal form apparently corresponds to *C. l. suavis*, but formal delimitation needs further study.
  - A northeastern subspecies, *C. l. forbesi*, might be definable from Illinois to the Great Plains, pending a regional study.
  - *C. l. blairi* from the Big Bend region may be extinct. No specimens were found in 1978, and its status relative to *C. l. lutrensis* remains unresolved.
- **Conclusion:** Geographic variation in *C. lutrensis* is largely local. Subspecies and trinomial questions need targeted regional studies before formal taxonomic changes.
