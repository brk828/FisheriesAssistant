# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Fossils from the Anza-Borrego Desert and the Ancestral Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This San Bernardino County Museum Association publication, prepared for the 1993 Desert Research Symposium, reports fossil [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*) remains from [[Anza-Borrego Desert State Park]] in Southern California. The authors, Stewart and Roeder, present the first documented fossil evidence that a major fluvial tributary system of the [[Ancestral Colorado River]] once flowed into the [[Borrego Badlands]] during part of the [[Pleistocene]]. A nearly complete, foot-long skeleton from the [[San Felipe Hills]] (specimen SBCM A768.1) is provisionally assigned to the [[Pliocene]], and six [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]] (LACM) specimens from the [[Ocotillo Formation]] extend the record of this large catostomid.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is one of the largest [[Catostomidae]] (sucker family) in North America, reaching up to about 1 m in length. It is endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage, where its historic range was limited to the main stem and major tributaries such as the Gila and Green rivers. The fish was an important food source for Native Americans along the Colorado River. Because of flow manipulation on the lower Colorado River, it is now listed as endangered in California (McGinnis, 1984).

Fossil freshwater fishes are rare in the ancestral Colorado River drainage, and the authors note that the only prior published fossil record of the genus, a Pleistocene specimen from the Colorado Desert west of the Salton Sea (Minckley et al., 1986), was based on a specimen identified by co-author Roeder.

The San Felipe Hills specimen, SBCM A768.1, was collected by Ruth Coyle and donated to the San Bernardino County Museum. It consists of a nearly complete skeleton preserved in an oval arenitic sandstone concretion. Paul Remeika (Anza-Borrego Desert State Park) identified the concretion lithology as Colorado River-derived sediment of the [[Diablo Formation]] rather than the lacustrine [[Borrego Formation]]. Remeika (pers. comm.) assigned the specimen to the early Pliocene. The specimen is being studied by Dr. Kenneth Lobalet (California State University, Bakersfield) and Mark Roeder.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper places the fossils within a changing drainage history:

- **Diablo Formation (Pliocene):** Colorado River-derived sandstones deposited along the western side of the [[Salton Trough]]. Remeika (1991) and Remeika and Lindsay (1992) proposed the informal "Diablo Formation" to separate these deposits from the [[Palm Spring Formation]], which is restricted in outcrop to the Vallecito Badlands of Anza-Borrego Desert State Park.
- **Ocotillo Formation (Pleistocene):** Composed of locally derived sediment that probably does not represent Colorado River sediment per se. The authors interpret it as deposition by a sizeable Colorado River tributary system, based on the large size of the fossil Xyrauchen. Estimated age is approximately 0.3 to 1.25 Ma (Remeika and Jefferson, this volume), mostly within the [[Irvingtonian Nalma]] age.
- **Age constraints:** The Irvingtonian/Rancholabrean [[North American Land Mammal Ages]] boundary is poorly dated, with estimates ranging from 0.24 to 0.55 Ma (Lundelius et al., 1987). A volcanic ash in the upper Inspiration Wash Member is correlated with the [[Bishop Tuff]], whose most reliable K-Ar and average zircon fission-track ages are each about 0.74 Ma (Izett et al., 1988). A potassium-argon date for this ash was pending. Some LACM localities lie above this horizon and some below.

Paleontological context and the authors' taxonomic arguments depend on the [[Weberian Apparatus]] anatomy described below.

## Key Findings

**1. Expanded geographic and temporal range.** The authors document the first fossil evidence that a major tributary of the ancestral Colorado River flowed into the Borrego Badlands during part of the Pleistocene, based on Ocotillo Formation fossils. Xyrauchen is also present in the lower Pliocene Diablo Formation around the San Felipe Hills.

**2. Early Pliocene specimen.** SBCM A768.1 is a foot-long, nearly complete skeleton that Remeika assigned to the early Pliocene. If confirmed, it would extend the known range of the genus back to early Pliocene times. Because the specimen is nearly complete, the authors state it may show whether it represents an earlier species or only extends the range of *X. texanus*.

**3. Lineage age.** Remeika's age assignment indicates that the large-river-adapted lineage of *Xyrauchen* existed more than three million years ago.

**4. LACM Pleistocene material.** Six specimens in the LACM collections from the Ocotillo Formation are assigned to *X. texanus*:

- All specimens come from large individuals, well over 50 cm standard length, compared with LACM 43611.
- Elements include neural complexes of the Weberian apparatus (five specimens from four Borrego Badlands localities), a modified pleural rib fragment from a fifth locality, and other fragments such as a quadrate, operculum, centrum, and pectoral fin rays.
- Specimens are from the Inspiration Wash and Los Playas members of the Ocotillo Formation.

**5. Diagnostic anatomy.** The neural complex of the Weberian apparatus is the most readily identified element. Compared with the related genus *Catostomus*, *Xyrauchen* differs in:

- The third vertebra's neural spine does not contribute to the posterior margin of the neural complex.
- The posterior border of the neural complex in *Catostomus* is very acute and directed dorso-posteriorly; in *Xyrauchen* the supraneural sits directly above vertebrae one through four.
- The fourth vertebra's neural arch leans slightly forward to reach the neural complex, and the fifth vertebra's arch extends considerably forward to make contact.
- A large supraneural is attached to the posterior side of the neural complex, and it bears a smaller supraneural on its own posterior side.

These elements form the skeletal basis of the conspicuous hump that gives the fish its common name.

**6. Conclusion.** The Ocotillo and Diablo fossils show that a large sucker, likely requiring substantial river habitat, was present in the Colorado River system over a long period. The authors argue that this supports a sizeable tributary system in the Borrego Badlands area during the Pleistocene and that the Diablo specimen may reach back into the Pliocene.
