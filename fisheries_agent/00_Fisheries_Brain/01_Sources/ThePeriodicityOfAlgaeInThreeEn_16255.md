# The Periodicity of μ-Algae in Three English Lakes

#phycology #limnology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[J. W. G. Lund]] (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside) reports fortnightly estimates of the standing crop of μ-algae (algae ≤15 μm along every axis, the [[Nannoplankton]] fraction) from 1955 to 1959 in [[Windermere]] (north and south basins), [[Blelham Tarn]] and [[Esthwaite Water]]. The data, drawn mainly from 1956–1959, show a consistent annual pattern: a winter minimum, a spring maximum and irregular summer fluctuations. The paper also compares μ-algae with larger algae by cell number and dry weight. Lund concludes that μ-algae are probably less important as primary producers than their numbers suggest, though this remains untested because grazing losses are unknown. The appended discussion includes critiques from [[Ropes]], [[Foge]], [[Tomas]], [[Sugiura]], [[Marcater]] and [[Reynolds]], which raise issues of turnover rates, growth rates, filtration error and nutrient response.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definition.** Lund defines μ-algae as all algae 15 μm or less along every axis. He also includes *Ankistrodesmus falcatus* vars. *acicularis* and *spirilliformis* (cells up to 30 μm long, less than 2.5 μm broad) and *Rhizosolenia eriensis* (up to about 100 μm long, 10 μm broad in girdle view). Although *Rhizosolenia* is large, its walls are delicate and its protoplast is small, so it is treated as nannoplankton.

**Methods.** Samples were taken from the 0–5 m water column at central, deep-water stations with a plastic tube. Sedimentation followed the inverted microscope method (Lund, Kipling and Le Cren, 1958). Each litre sample was fixed in the field with 20 ml of iodine reagent. Dry weights were estimated for natural populations (N), washed laboratory cultures (L) and silica content (Si). Carbon and dry-weight figures appear in Table 1 (μg per million cells, or per colony/filament for F-type estimates).

**Lakes studied.** The lakes are in the English Lake District, with long-term limnological records summarised by [[Pearsall]], [[Mackereth]], [[Macan]] and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal framework.** Total μ-algal numbers reach their yearly minimum between December and February and their maximum by March or April. A more or less marked decline follows between April and June. From June to September numbers fluctuate irregularly, rarely falling below 1000 cells per ml. From September to February numbers decline, except in Blelham (and occasionally Esthwaite), where a November or December increase can occur.

**Taxonomic groups.**
- **Green algae (Chlorophyceae):** mainly coccoid forms and *Stichococcus* spp. They dominate the early winter maximum in Blelham and show a large spring maximum in all lakes, followed by a decline in April and May.
- **Diatoms (Bacillariophyceae):** abundant from February to April or May. Only Windermere shows a clear seasonal cycle, dominated by [[Cyclotella Glomerata]] with minor *C. comensis*. In Blelham, *Rhizosolenia* predominates.
- **Cryptophytes and chrysophytes:** both decline from August to January or February. Their main abundance is usually in early to late summer. Cryptophytes are almost entirely [[Rhodomonas Minuta]] var. *nannoplanktonica*. Chrysophytes are largely a [[Chrysochromulina]] sp., which can exceed 10,000 cells per ml and is among the rarest algae in midwinter.
- **Absent or rare groups:** minute Myxophyceae (cyanobacteria) are absent, and Peridineae are very rare.

**Lake comparisons.** The north basin of Windermere has the smallest crops and is the least productive. Blelham and Esthwaite have the greatest maxima, but Blelham has the larger average population. Esthwaite has the largest population of larger forms and is judged the most productive lake. Its productivity is partly underestimated in Table 2 because estimates for several large taxa (*Anabaena*, *Uroglena*, *Dinobryon*, *Chrysochromulina*, *Coelosphaerium*, *Diplocystis*, *Ceratium*) are missing.

**Size and weight.** Cell-number comparisons do not account for size. Lund assigned 100 cells per individual to Myxophyceae, which he acknowledges is arbitrary. Dry weights differ substantially:
- Green μ-algae: 3–20 μg per million cells
- Larger algae: 30–5000 μg per million cells
- Myxophyceae: 6–20 mg per million cells (excluding *Anabaena*)

Average weight per cell of larger forms is roughly ten times that of μ-algae. Lund therefore argues that the crops of larger forms are usually greater than those of μ-algae, even though μ-algae are more numerous.

## Key Findings

1. **Annual cycle.** Total μ-algae peak in spring (March–April) and reach their minimum in winter (December–February), with irregular summer fluctuations.
2. **Taxonomic succession.** Green algae peak in spring, diatoms in February–April/May, and cryptophytes and chrysophytes in summer. Windermere's diatom cycle is dominated by *Cyclotella glomerata*.
3. **Lake productivity ranking.** Esthwaite is the most productive lake, based on the large population of larger forms. The north basin of Windermere is the least productive.
4. **Biomass versus numbers.** Larger algae often exceed μ-algae in crop weight, and in autumn and early winter larger forms often predominate. Lund suggests μ-algae are less important as primary producers than their numbers imply, but notes that this cannot be confirmed until grazing losses are known.
5. **Largest crops (Table 2).** The largest single-crop dry weights were 14.60 g/m² for *Cyclotella glomerata* (Windermere South Basin, 1956), with *Asterionella formosa* and *Melosira italica* prominent in other lakes and years.
6. **Oligotrophy hypothesis.** Lund's impression is that the more oligotrophic a lake, the more important μ-algae become relative to larger forms.

**Discussion points raised in the appended debate:**
- **Turnover and production.** Ropes argued that small algae should have higher turnover rates and therefore greater production. Lund agreed that the question is unresolved and that his data cannot yet decide it.
- **Growth rates.** Foge reported a laboratory generation time of about 24 hours for a single μ-alga strain. Lund replied that natural rates would be slower and that *Asterionella* can grow nearly twice as fast under comparable laboratory conditions.
- **Nutrient response.** Tomas reported rapid green nanoplankton blooms in Lake Baldegg (Baldeggersee) after nitrate addition, rising from 8,000 to 56,000 cells between 2 and 16 September 1958 with little grazing loss. Lund agreed that nutrient addition typically favours these forms.
- **Filtration error.** Sugiura asked about filter losses. Lund noted that at least 90% of chlorelloid forms could pass Whatman 541 paper if the filter is not clogged.
- **Pigment evidence.** Marcater found a higher proportion of chlorophyll *a* in the fraction passing coarse filters, which he linked to higher μ-algal productivity.
- **Protozoan feeding.** Reynolds supported Lund's view that protozoa feed more on bacteria than on μ-algae, based on a long-term study of *Urceolaria*.
