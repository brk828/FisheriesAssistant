# Cutthroat and Rainbow Trouts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from Robert Rush Miller's *Occasional Papers* (No. 529, "Cutthroat and Rainbow Trouts") addresses three linked problems: the nomenclature of the Kamloops trout, the taxonomic status of the Piute and San Gorgonio trouts within the [[Cutthroat Trout]] complex, and the conservation status of several cutthroat subspecies. Miller resolves a priority dispute between [[Oncorhynchus Kamloops]] and *Salmo kamloops* on the basis of a dating stamp, argues that several trouts are derived from the [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], and reports that most historic cutthroat forms are extinct or presumed extinct. ## Background & Description

The excerpt concerns trouts of the genus *Salmo* and *Oncorhynchus*, traditionally grouped under [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. The main taxonomic subjects are:

- **Oncorhynchus kamloops** (Kamloops trout), described by [[Jordan]] in 1892.
- **Salmo clarkii henshawi** (Lahontan cutthroat), the ancestor of the [[Piute Trout]] (*S. c. seleniris*).
- **Salmo evermanni** ([[San Gorgonio Trout]]), whose original description records hyoid teeth.
- **Golden trouts**, whose number of species remains unresolved.

Morphological features discussed include the presence or absence of [[Hyoid Teeth]], spotting pattern, and the red cutthroat mark. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

Miller states that several cutthroat subspecies have disappeared from their historic ranges:

- ***S. c. henshawi*** (Lahontan Basin, California, Nevada and Oregon): virtually extinct, per [[Miller and Alcorn]] (1946).
- ***S. c. utah*** (Bonneville Basin, Utah and Idaho): believed extinct.
- ***S. c. stomias*** (originally described from the [[Platte River]]): not taken in recent years.
- ***S. c. macdonaldi*** (Twin Lakes, Colorado, Arkansas River basin): extinct for many years.
- ***S. c. seleniris*** and ***S. c. erromogenes*** (Crab Creek, Washington): believed to have disappeared, per [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948).

Miller also notes intergradation in the wild. Specimens from [[Silver King Creek]], below Llewellyn Falls (UMMZ No. 157655), range from almost unspotted to well spotted, so the two forms intergrade in one of the most striking features thought to characterize *seleniris*.

## Key Findings

1. **Priority of Oncorhynchus kamloops over Salmo kamloops.** Jordan submitted a manuscript naming the fish *Oncorhynchus kamloops*. He later changed the Forest and Stream paper to match the California State Board of Fish Commissioners proofs, but the Forest and Stream issue (dated November 10, 1892) was printed unchanged. The Forest and Stream date is the actual publication date. [[Taxonomic Priority]] is decided by the "DEC 5 1892" dating stamp found in the University of California copy of the Biennial Report for 1891–1892. Miller accepts this as the only evidence of publication date and considers *Oncorhynchus kamloops* to have priority. [[W. I. Follett]] concurred.

2. **Nomen nudum.** The name *Salmo kamloops* first appeared as a *nomen nudum* (Jordan, 1892a: 405) in a popular account by "G. B.C.", which preceded Jordan's formal description.

3. **Piute trout origin.** Snyder (1933, 1934) demonstrated that the Piute trout was derived from the Lahontan cutthroat, although he did not mention hyoid teeth in *S. c. seleniris*.

4. **Status of Salmo evermanni.** The original description states hyoid teeth are present. Miller verified this on the two paratypes at the United States National Museum. Croker (letter of December 8, 1949) reported no red cutthroat mark on two specimens (6 to 7 inches long) caught on June 23, 1935. The specimens were uniform dark brown with pronounced black spots. Because *evermanni* agrees with the cutthroat series in all other characters, Miller finds no basis for considering it a rainbow derivative, contrary to Jordan and Grinnell (1908), Evermann and Bryant (1919), Snyder (1940), and Shapovalov (1941). He suggests it may be a subspecies of *S. clarkii*, though its distinctive life colors may justify full species status.

5. **Golden trouts.** The golden trouts may represent only one or two species (Curtis, 1935a, b). Miller states that critical studies in their native habitat are still needed. Until those studies are published, he declines to choose between competing taxonomic solutions.

6. **Conservation.** Most historic cutthroat forms are extinct, virtually extinct, or believed to have disappeared. Only the taxonomic status of the surviving forms remains uncertain.
