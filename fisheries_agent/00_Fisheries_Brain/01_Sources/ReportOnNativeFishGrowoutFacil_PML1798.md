# Native Fish Growout Facilities at Imperial and Cibola National Wildlife Refuges, 1993–1995

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (November 1999, Parker Fishery Resources Office) documents the establishment and performance of native fish growout ponds on the lower Colorado River, focusing on [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] between 1993 and 1995. The program targets the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], whose wild populations have collapsed largely because of predation on larvae and juveniles by introduced fishes. Growout ponds were used to rear hatchery-produced fish to larger sizes (>250 mm) before release, with the Imperial "Farm Fish Pond" ultimately failing and the Cibola "High Levee Pond" proving effective.

## Background & Description

Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and Bonytail (*Gila elegans*) are native to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and are federally listed as endangered with designated Critical Habitat. Both were once abundant; the Razorback Sucker supported a commercial fishery in southern Arizona, while the Bonytail, though abundant and easily caught, was considered a poor food fish because of its many bones. Today, only two wild Razorback Sucker populations remain (upper Green River and [[Lake Mohave]], the latter estimated at 85,000 in 1989 with roughly 25,000 remaining), and wild Bonytail are nearly extinct, with fewer than 100 captured in Lake Mohave since 1981.

Declines are attributed to habitat degradation, water development, and introduced fishes. Construction of dams such as [[Hoover Dam]], Imperial Dam, and [[Parker Dam]] created impoundments favoring exotic species. Recruitment failure, driven by predation on larvae by channel catfish, common carp, and red shiner, is considered the leading cause of decline. No juveniles of either species had been observed in the wild since the mid-1950s before augmentation, and the lower basin Razorback Sucker population was projected to become extinct near the turn of the century without intervention.

Broodstock came from Lake Mohave and were reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Earlier stocking of fry and fingerlings into the Verde and Salt Rivers and lower river backwaters, including California Department of Fish and Game efforts, failed to reverse declines, indicating that larger fish were needed. In 1990 the [[Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group]] was formed and produced guidelines for native fish facilities along the lower river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lower Colorado River is a highly altered system of dams, channelized reaches, and armored shorelines. The Imperial Division (about 60 km reach) retains backwaters, lakes, and marshes, though sedimentation since the closure of Imperial Dam in 1938 has isolated many upper backwaters. Its largest and deepest backwaters lie in the lower third of the division. The Cibola Division (about 30 km reach) has been heavily modified by channelization and riprap, with nearly 100% of both shorelines armored and largely sandy substrate. Cibola NWR was created as a mitigation refuge for these effects.

Vegetation at Imperial's Farm Fish Pond included sago pondweed, spiny naiad, cattails, tamarisk, and giant cane, with waterfowl and wading birds common. Summer pondweed and naiad growth filled the pond, impeding netting and contributing to low dissolved oxygen. At the High Levee Pond, vegetation was dominated by cattails with mesquite, catclaw, arrowweed, and salt cedar. Crayfish activity later caused predation problems at the High Levee Pond.

Water quality was monitored with a Hydrolab. At the Farm Fish Pond, water temperatures ranged from 10–26 °C in winter to 33–35 °C in early fall, and dissolved oxygen fell near anoxic levels by September. At the High Levee Pond, temperatures ranged from 11–31 °C, dissolved oxygen remained adequate through summer (low readings occurred only where depth exceeded 2.0 m), and pH ranged from about 6.9 to 8.8. Specific conductivity generally ranged from 0.4 to 1.7 mS/cm.

## Key Findings

- **Growout protocol:** Ponds were isolated by berms, barrier nets, or golf course ponds, treated with the piscicide [[Rotenone]], stocked with 4–6 inch fingerlings after two weeks, monitored, and netted after 8–10 months. Fish over 250 mm received [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] before release.
- **Recaptures:** In 1996, growout-pond fish were recaptured in Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu, and the old Colorado River channel at Pretty Water on Cibola NWR, confirming the program's viability. The authors note more rearing sites are needed for a significant population impact.
- **Farm Fish Pond (Imperial NWR), failed:** Established January 1995 (60 ft nylon barrier net, 15.7 acre-feet maximum volume, 4–8 surface acres). About 5,000 Razorback Sucker (mean TL 65 mm) were stocked on 20 January 1995, with 4,000 more in November 1995. Only 60 fish were captured in monitoring, with none large enough for PIT tagging except three. Breaches from high spring water, low dissolved oxygen, lack of cover, and predation by waterfowl and wading birds caused failure. A fish die-off occurred on 6 September 1995 with threadfin shad and one Razorback Sucker observed. Monitoring was halted, and the pond was abandoned.
- **High Levee Pond (Cibola NWR), effective:** Established October 1993 (about 4.9 surface acres, 20.7 acre-feet maximum), rotenoned in October 1993, and stocked with Razorback Sucker and Bonytail from Dexter NFH. Over 5,000 Bonytail were stocked, and about 14,000 Razorback Sucker, of which about 600 were PIT-tagged. Mean condition factor for Razorback Sucker was 1.08 (range 0.86–2.28). Crayfish predation halted netting from May to September 1996, but the pond was judged an effective growout facility, and spawning activity with tubercles and milt was observed in winter 1996.
- **Growth estimates:** Average growth rates were used to predict when fish would reach 250 mm. Trammel nets, biased toward larger fish, were the only capture method early on, so the estimates were treated as gross approximations. Bonytail growth could not be determined because age classes could not be distinguished.
- **Conclusions and recommendations:** The authors state that rearing facilities work. Pond sites should be isolated and checked for water quality, predator presence, and vegetation; stocking rates should be derived from hatchery data, prior pond experience, and biomass removed during rotenone treatment; and agencies should share methods to expand successful approaches. Emphasis should be on releasing large numbers of fish into the Colorado River system.
- **Data availability:** All data on the Imperial and Cibola growout facilities are held at the Parker Fishery Resources Office.
