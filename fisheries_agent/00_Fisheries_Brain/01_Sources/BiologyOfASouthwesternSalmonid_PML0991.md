# Biology of a Southwestern Salmonid: *Salmo apache*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Ken C. Harper of the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]] Division of Wildlife and Natural Resources examines the life history, reproduction, growth, diet, and population structure of [[Arizona Trout]] (*[[Salmo Apache]]*), an endangered native salmonid of Arizona and New Mexico. Field work was conducted in 1974 and 1975 in [[Big Bonito Creek]] on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. The author describes spawning timing and redd construction, fecundity, egg-to-emergence timing, fry migration, age and growth from otoliths, standing crop, and food habits, and compares the species with other native trout. The paper notes that the species is displaced by non-native salmonids, and it provides the first in-depth account of this species' biology.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Trout in Arizona were first reported as [[Cutthroat Trout]]. Later work by [[Miller Classification]] (1972) separated the native Arizona trout into distinct species, and *Salmo apache* was recognized as a species native to the upper [[Salt River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] systems. The species was previously referred to *Salmo gilae*. A 1976 study by [[Alcorn]] reported on temperature tolerance and upper lethal limits, and chromosome analysis was completed in the same period.
- **Historic range:** Once common throughout the upper Salt and Little Colorado River systems, the species has declined greatly. Its present range is estimated at under 48 km of stream, mostly in isolated headwater streams above roughly 2,500 m elevation.
- **Prior research:** Before this study, little ecological data existed. Earlier reports were largely superficial, covering spawning, growth, and distribution, and only two studies had recorded more than casual observation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area (Big Bonito Creek):**
- A small creek originating on the southern slope of a mountain and flowing southwest about 25 km through basaltic terrain.
- Average gradient of about 3.8% (the source gives "38 m/km"; this is interpreted as 3.8%).
- Study-area elevations range from roughly 2,500 to 2,700 m.
- Average stream width and depth were about 14 m and 0.4 m (OCR-garbled; interpreted as stated).
- Pool-to-riffle ratio was approximately 1:10, with habitat dominated by riffles and glides.
- Estimated maximum flows were 0.36 m³/s and 1.97 m³/s (OCR-garbled).
- Winter conditions include anchor ice, ice bridges, and extended periods of low temperatures.
- Riparian vegetation includes [[Douglas Fir]], [[White Fir]], [[Ponderosa Pine]], [[Quaking Aspen]], and [[Arizona Alder]], which shades parts of the channel.

**Co-occurring fishes:**
- [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) occurs in the creek, and is numerous below the study area but makes up only a small share of the population in the study reach and less in upper portions.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) is rare.
- [[Brook Trout]] is also present and was far more abundant than native trout in the related [[Ord Creek]] project.

## Key Findings

### Spawning and Reproduction
- Spawning fish were observed from 30 May to 18 June 1975 during receding stream flow.
- Nineteen female *S. apache* collected 16–23 June 1975 were examined: 17 were spent. Two partially spent females retained 54 and 57 eggs; other spent females retained 0 to 14 eggs. The main spawning period was the first two weeks of June 1975.
- Maximum stream temperatures during spawning ranged from 8 °C (30 May) to 14 °C (14 June).
- [[Redd Construction]] and occupation began at water temperatures of 8 °C. Daily temperature fluctuations often delayed spawning until midday and may have extended the season. Some redds were occupied by individuals for two weeks.
- Redds were shallow (2–9 cm deep), oval (15–29 cm × 20–30 cm), and as close as 24 cm to the stream bank. Redds were built over a variety of substrates, velocities, and depths. Spawning behavior resembled that of cutthroat trout.
- Maturity was attained at a length of about 130 mm (OCR-garbled).

### Fecundity
- Fecundity ranged from 72 to 240 eggs in females 13–20 cm total length.
- Estimates averaged 1,350 eggs per 450 g of body weight (OCR reads "J.,350"), comparable to the norm of 1,000 eggs per 450 g for salmonids.
- Excavated redds contained fewer eggs than estimated total fecundity, suggesting possible multiple spawning.
- Hatchery-held fish from [[Ord Creek]] had mean fecundity ranging from 22 eggs per female in 1962 to 4,315 in 1969. The author concludes the species can support a substantial [[Hatchery Program]].

### Egg Development and Emergence
- Eggs hatched in about 30 days, and emergence occurred about 60 days after deposition.
- In 1975 emergence began in August, leaving several months for growth before winter.

### Fry Migration and Movement
- Fry migrated downstream from 2 August to 10 October 1975. A total of 534 fry were captured moving downstream.
- Temperature appeared to have no effect on movement, but light did. Fry in aquaria showed a photonegative response: they remained in gravel interstices by day and moved at night.
- Statistically, 95% of fry movements occurred at night.
- The majority (68%) of captured fry were under 25 mm.
- Adult movements were minimal (under 100 m) based on 1975 returns, and some individuals remained in their original locations.

### Population Density, Standing Crop, and Structure
- Density ranged from about 0.3 to 0.8 fish per square metre (values partly illegible in the OCR) and averaged about 0.3 fish/m².
- Standing crop estimates ranged from about 14.6 to 19.8 g/m² (values partly illegible), with a reported biomass of about 130 kg/ha.
- Length-frequency analysis indicated that individuals over 17.0 cm made up only a small share of the population but a larger share of the biomass (exact percentages partly illegible).
- In the [[Ord Creek]] project, only about 200 large native trout remained, compared with over 5,600 adult brook and brown trout. The author links this to displacement of native trout by introduced salmonids.

### Age and Growth
- Age and growth were determined from annuli on [[Otolith Analysis]] of 37 fish.
- Mean growth between annuli (Table 2) varied by year and age class, and total length at each annulus (Table 3) varied among cohorts.
- Growth was slow. The author attributes this to a combination of high population density, poor growing seasons, and restricted habitat. Habitat and density were considered more important than innate biology.

### Diet and Feeding
- The species is an opportunistic feeder.
- Fish 6–10 cm total length consumed more [[Ephemeropteran]] insects than fish 15 cm and larger, while [[Trichopteran]] insects were eaten more by larger fish.
- Seasonal shifts in prey use followed prey availability.
- Terrestrial organisms made up over 30% of the food in all samples, which indicates their importance as a food source.
- No evidence of cannibalism was found in the stomach samples.

### Management Implications
- Spawning, fecundity, age at maturity, and feeding habits were similar to those of other salmonids.
- The author suggests that non-native salmonids (brown and [[Rainbow Trout]]) in Big Bonito Creek and other headwater streams could competitively exclude native *S. apache* where there are no isolating barriers. Historic isolation has likely prevented the evolution of coexistence mechanisms.
- Protecting isolated populations and controlling non-native trout are key recommendations.
