# Pond Fish and Fishing in Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #warmwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Illinois Department of Conservation bulletin, written by chief fishery biologist A.C. Lopinot, is a practical guide to stocking, managing, fishing, and protecting farm ponds. Its central argument is that good pond fishing is a managed crop, not a byproduct of building a dam. The recommended warmwater package is [[Largemouth Bass]] as predator, with [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Channel Catfish]] as prey and sport species. The text also identifies species that tend to ruin pond fisheries, describes the seasonal oxygen dynamics that drive fish kills, and sets out harvest, stocking, fertilization, rotenone-renovation, and commercial options such as [[Fee Fishing Areas]], food-fish culture, and [[Bait Minnow Production]].

## Background & Description

The bulletin states that Illinois had 62,627 ponds in 1965, totaling 38,455 acres of water, with about 1,200 new ponds built each year. Most were not managed for fishing. The [[Illinois Department of Conservation]] classifies all impoundments under six acres as ponds.

The species treated in detail are:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]**: sight-feeding predator; spawns at about two years old in May–June; average adult 1–2 lb and 12–15 in; life expectancy about 8–10 years.
- **[[Bluegill]]**: small panfish that nests in colonies; matures at one year; average catch 1/4–1/3 lb and over six inches; life span about four to six years.
- **[[Redear Sunfish]]**: shellcracker that feeds on snails from deeper water; matures at one year; grows faster than bluegill.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: bottom feeder, mostly active at night; matures at 13–16 in; maximum over 25 lb.

The text covers unwanted species in separate sections: [[Crappie]], [[Bullheads]], [[Carp]] with buffalo and suckers, and [[Green Sunfish]]. Northern pike, walleye, and trout are noted as seldom successful in Illinois ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond design and water quality.** A pond should be at least one acre when full. The Department will not provide fish for ponds under ¼ acre. Seven to nine feet of depth should cover one-quarter of the pond, ideally 10–15 feet at maximum depth, and shoreline water should be about four feet deep. The recommended watershed is 10 to 20 acres per surface acre of water. Pollution, livestock access, and erosion are all to be avoided, because silt fills basins and muddy water prevents bass from seeing prey.

**Thermal stratification and oxygen.** Illinois ponds turn over in spring and fall, mixing and reoxygenating the water. In summer, the warm upper layer (2–10 feet thick) holds fish, while cold deep water is often oxygen-poor. In winter, decay can deplete deep oxygen under snow-covered ice.

**Food chain.** Dissolved nutrients from runoff and dust feed algae, which support microscopic animals, then crustaceans and insect larvae, then small fish and crayfish, and finally predator fish. Shallow water produces more food than deep water because light penetrates to the bottom.

**Major fish kill causes.** The bulletin lists winter kill, summer kill from plant die-off (usually near sunrise), summer kill from high temperature, organic pollution, insecticides, natural mortality, explosions, and industrial or mining wastes.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and population management**

- Only warmwater species are suitable. The recommended stock is fingerlings: [[Largemouth Bass]] at 100 per surface acre; [[Bluegill]] at 100–400 per acre, depending on fertility; or a 30% [[Redear Sunfish]] and 70% bluegill mix; [[Channel Catfish]] at 50–100 per acre unless fed.
- Largemouth bass must be stocked as predators with any panfish. Adult substitution is discouraged because it quickly overpopulates the pond.
- Wild fish should be removed before stocking. Channel catfish rarely reproduce successfully when bass and bluegill are present, so they need periodic restocking.
- The [[Fish Stocking]] minimum standards require that the pond be free of fish, have at least ¼ acre of surface area, and reach 7–9 feet depth (7 ft southern, 8 ft central, 9 ft northern Illinois) in a quarter of its area. The owner must also fence livestock, avoid pollution and barnyard drainage, and avoid fee-based angling. Applications received before August 1 are stocked the same year.

**Harvest balance**

- An average Illinois pond supports about four times as many pounds of bluegill as bass.
- For each pound of bass harvested, three to seven pounds of bluegill should be taken.
- Anglers should return bass under 10–12 inches. Excess harvest of small bass in the first two years is described as one of the most common causes of pond failure.

**Carrying capacity**

- Largemouth bass: 10–25 lb per acre on forest-watershed ponds, 25–50 lb on light-colored soils, and 50–125 lb on black soils.
- Bluegill: 100–200 lb per acre on light-colored soils.
- Greater fertility raises [[Carrying Capacity]].

**Diagnosing pond condition**

- The [[Swingle Seine Test]] (late June–July, ponds stocked two years or longer) and the angler's catch both classify the population. The text considers the angler's catch the more reliable method.
- Overcrowded bluegill (for example, many 3–5 inch fish and no bass recruitment) calls for trapping, seining, or [[Drawdown]].

**Water level control and spawning beds**

- Drawing a pond to half its surface area in early September, held for at least three months, concentrates small fish for predators and boosts growth. It requires enough bass to be effective, a pond of at least one acre, and a watershed of 15–25 acres per acre of water with a drain of four inches or more.
- Bluegill beds can be destroyed by raking or trampling.

**Fertilization**

- Fertilization has generally failed in Illinois because it causes nuisance plant growth, increases the risk of kills, is expensive, and produces plankton blooms. It may help new ponds with clay basins or infertile watersheds.

**Rotenone and draining**

- Five percent emulsifiable [[Rotenone]] is the recommended toxicant at 2 ppm (0.656 gallon per acre-foot). It must be applied under [[Illinois Department of Conservation]] supervision, at 70°F or above, typically in late summer or early fall, with a permit from the Division of Fisheries.
- Draining is the first choice where possible; restocking should follow within a month or two, or the next spring.

**Water quality and pests**

- [[Muddy Water]] can be treated with gypsum (525 lb per acre-foot), green hay, straw, or agricultural lime.
- [[Aquatic Weed Control]] is most effective with herbicides applied in spring, and must follow label directions. Algae may be treated with copper sulfate.
- Muskrats and crayfish can damage dams. Turtles mainly eat vegetation and can be trapped.

**Angling**

- Largemouth bass are best in spring and early summer, using lures, with artificial baits often outperforming live bait.
- Bluegill are most easily caught on spawning beds. Redear are taken on deep-water bait.
- Channel catfish are caught mainly at night, with still fishing as the best method.

**Special cases**

- Cold-water species (trout, walleye, northern pike, muskellunge) need oxygen at all depths, cool water, and large lakes with specialized spawning sloughs. These conditions are extremely rare in Illinois.
- [[Fee Fishing Areas]] require a state license and sites near populated areas of at least 50,000 people. Fees run from $0.50 to $3.00 per day.
- Commercial [[Channel Catfish]] production at 2,000 fingerlings per acre, fed about 3% of body weight daily, can yield 1,500–2,000 lb live weight per acre in one year with at least 90% survival. The bulletin cites a net profit of $100 to over $135 per acre.
- Bait minnow species described include the [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Bluntnose Minnow]], each with specific habitat, spawning, and stocking data.
- [[Frog Farming]] is not considered proven in Illinois because the growing season is short and frogs take three to four years to mature.

**Safety and facts**

- The Soil Conservation Service recommends life rings with 100-foot rope, warning signs, and proper boat loading.
- Safe ice thickness for 24-hour periods is 1.75–6 inches for a single person at 0–30°F, rising to 3.75–12 inches for crowds at 40–50°F.
- Illinois has 212 fish species in 74 genera and 29 families, of which 177 species in 69 genera and 27 families were positively identified during the previous five years.
