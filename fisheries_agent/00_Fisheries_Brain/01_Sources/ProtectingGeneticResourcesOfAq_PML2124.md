# Protecting Genetic Resources of Aquatic Organisms: Elimination of Stock Transfers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document is an [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) position statement, written by [[David P. Philipp]], [[John M. Epifanio]], and [[Martin L. Jennings]] of the Center for Aquatic Ecology, Illinois Natural History Survey. It argues that stocking and introduction programs that move individuals between genetically distinct [[Stock]]s threaten the genetic integrity of native aquatic organisms. The authors call on federal, state, tribal, provincial, and private stakeholders to eliminate stock transfers, end organism "trading systems," tighten private-sector rules, define stock criteria, create species-level working groups and genetic reserves, and run public education programs.

The cover sheet is a Virginia Tech Department of Fisheries and Wildlife Sciences letter. Most of its handwritten and OCR-degraded text is illegible, so its content cannot be reliably summarized beyond its institutional origin.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definitions.** The statement distinguishes three types of introduction:
1. [[Non-Native Introduction]]: a species placed where it did not naturally occur.
2. [[Stock Transfer]]: fish from one genetically distinct stock placed into waters inhabited by another stock of the same species.
3. [[Genetically Compatible Introduction]]: cultured fish from a stock returned to waters inhabited by that same stock.

A stock is defined as a randomly breeding group with spatial, temporal, or behavioral integrity from other such groups. The authors note that a single water body can hold several stocks, and one stock can span several water bodies.

**Genetic resources.** Genetic variation has two components:
- [[Intra-Stock Variability]]: variation among individuals within a stock, which is the raw material for natural selection. Small effective population size raises the risk of [[Inbreeding]] and [[Genetic Drift]].
- [[Inter-Stock Variability]]: variation among stocks, which reflects local adaptation.

The authors cite Allendorf and Ryman (1987) on evidence that ignoring genetic principles reduces the variability of hatchery stocks.

**Outbreeding depression.** Stock transfers can break down [[Coadapted Gene Complexes]] built up in separate environments. Interbreeding can then produce offspring less fit for local conditions, a pattern known as [[Outbreeding Depression]]. Restoring fitness may take many generations, and lost complexes may be irretrievable.

**Stock Concept of Management.** Under the [[Stock Concept of Management]], the stock, not the whole species, is the operational unit of management. Methods for distinguishing stocks include [[Protein Electrophoresis]], cytogenetic analysis, and DNA sequence divergence estimates. The authors stress that no consensus on stock criteria exists yet, but protective action should not wait for it.

**Three stocking scenarios.** The statement describes practices that produce harmful transfers:
- Single hatcheries supplying distant geographic regions, often with donor broodstock from other water bodies.
- "Trading" of excess production between agencies, exchanging species or stocks to meet quotas.
- Private hatcheries selling fish to an unregulated clientele, which may release them or let them escape into wild waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Local environments differ in physical, chemical, and biological conditions, so each stock adapts to its own conditions through [[Natural Selection]]. Gene flow must be restricted for stocks to diverge, and transfers remove the isolating mechanisms that maintain this divergence. The result can be [[Hybridization]] between genetically differentiated groups. Fish species generally occur as related but distinct stocks rather than as single panmictic units.

The statement references several species in its literature base, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Chinook Salmon]], as examples of stocked or studied organisms. The document itself is a policy statement, not a field study, and reports no new site-level data.

## Key Findings

- Stock transfers threaten the genetic integrity and evolutionary potential of native aquatic organisms, which the authors view as at risk and in need of long-term protection.
- Stock is the operational unit of management concern. Protective action should not wait for a final, consensus definition of stock.
- Intra-stock and inter-stock variability both matter for long-term species survival.
- Outbreeding depression and loss of coadapted gene complexes are the main risks of mixing stocks.
- Existing AFS guidance (Kohler and Courtenay 1985) on introductions of aquatic species does not address the genetic effects of stock transfers.

**Recommended course of action** (addressed to federal, state, tribal, provincial, and private stakeholders):
1. Review current culture and enhancement practices and eliminate stock transfers within each jurisdiction.
2. Eliminate "trading systems" that exchange species or stocks to meet production quotas.
3. Develop stricter regulations for production, sale, and transport of aquatic organisms by the private sector, with enforcement.
4. Work toward consensus on stock-defining criteria and build genetic variation databases for native sport and non-game species.
5. Form species-level working groups to propose stock boundaries, identify [[Genetic Reserves]] protected from contamination, and set ecologically and genetically based management goals.
6. Run a public education program to build awareness and acceptance of these strategies.

**Reference base.** The literature cited is about three dozen entries, drawn largely from fisheries genetics, population genetics, and stock-concept literature (e.g., Wright 1978; Waples 1991a; Hindar et al. 1991; Philipp 1991).
