# Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Studies: 5,000 cfs Low Flow Test and Program Update (December 1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This December 1989 newsletter from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program reports on a four-day steady 5,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) flow release from [[Glen Canyon Dam]], conducted October 6-9, 1989, within a broader October 4-17 study period. Over 40 researchers examined beaches, trout spawning bars, water quality, hydrology, recreation, channel geomorphology, and archaeology. The update also covers the appointment of [[Steve Robinson]] to direct the [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS) for the dam, a 24-month schedule for a Final EIS by December 1991, and a series of river research trips and meetings. The authors describe the flow test as a technical and coordination success, while noting that four days was probably too short for beaches and sediments to reach equilibrium.

## Background & Description

The GCES program is the research arm supporting the EIS on the impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on downstream resources in the [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] and [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Secretary of the Interior Lujan directed the EIS on July 27, 1989. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] leads the EIS, and the [[National Park Service]], [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] contribute field studies.

The steady-flow test was planned under an integrated research plan developed by GCES staff and Senior Scientist [[Duncan Patten]]. The planned release averaged 5,004 cfs during the four-day steady period, within a range of 4,835 to 5,166 cfs.

Steve Robinson, previously Deputy Director and then Acting Director of the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, was named Director of Colorado River Storage Project Studies and Initiatives in Reclamation's Upper Colorado Region. He reported to the Salt Lake City office on August 28, 1989. His background includes Nevada state government posts and a master's degree in Public Administration from California State University at San Jose.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River below the dam is a regulated tailwater system. Fluctuating flows from hydropower operations affect sand beaches, sediment, and trout spawning habitat. The newsletter identifies several resource concerns:

- **Beaches:** Sand beaches within the daily fluctuation zone change with flow. Fine red-brown silt was found at low elevations on most surveyed beaches. Boat mooring and boat traffic were linked to rapid cutbank formation and sand redistribution.
- **Trout spawning:** Two spawning bars in the [[Glen Canyon]] reach were surveyed for particle size and potential use. The report states that observable trout spawning areas "have been impacted by operations."
- **Water quality and aquatic plants:** Nutrient and water quality sampling examined the nutrient supply through the reach. The update states that the system could maintain steady flow in October "without excessive adverse effect to the [[Cladophora]], the water quality, or the fishery resource."
- **Archaeological resources:** Operations affect archaeological sites in the Grand Canyon corridor, including sites eroding from dunes within the historic high-water zone.
- **Recreation:** Boating access above [[Three Mile Bar]] depends on water level.

## Key Findings

**5,000 cfs steady flow test (October 1989)**
- Over 40 researchers took part, concentrated at Lee's Ferry with personnel throughout the Grand Canyon.
- The release averaged 5,004 cfs (range 4,835-5,166 cfs). Weather was clear, and Western Area Power Administration reported no major electrical distribution problems.
- Western identified the cost of providing the steady flows as $64,000.
- Initial indications suggest four days at 5,000 cfs were not enough for the beaches and sediment system to reach equilibrium.
- The river between the dam and [[Lee's Ferry]] flows more slowly at 5,000 cfs than expected, which the authors attribute mainly to the river's geomorphic shape and slope.

**Rhodamine dye time-of-travel study**
- About 27 gallons of Rhodamine dye were released at 7 p.m. on October 8, 1989, at the dam.
- The dye reached the first gage, one-quarter mile downstream, about one hour after insertion, and reached the [[Lee's Ferry]] gage around 11:30 a.m. on Monday. Travel speed was under one mile per hour.
- The dye was traced to the Grand Canyon gage, but at least two series of fluctuating flows had passed through the dye mass before it was recorded.
- No negative environmental effects were noted apart from red footprints at the dam.

**Beach dynamics**
- National Park Service and Northern Arizona University volunteers monitored beaches using temporary wells, sampling grids, and erosional indicators before, during, and after the 5,000 cfs release.
- The [[U.S. Geological Survey]] resurveyed 14 Grand Canyon beaches in September 1989: 11 previously surveyed in 1974, 1980, and 1985, and three surveyed for the first time.
- The team gathered information on how beaches respond to steady and fluctuating flows and how quickly water infiltrates and drains from sand.

**Radio telemetry pilot study (September 6-18, 1989)**
- Five researchers from Reclamation, the Fish and Wildlife Service, and Arizona Game and Fish tested radio telemetry at the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence.
- Radio tags were surgically implanted in [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Signal extinction was tested against depth, distance, and conductivity.
- The study assessed the feasibility of the method for objectives in the biological opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations. A trip report will follow final data analysis.

**Recreation and boating**
- Researchers observed boating accidents and access above Three Mile Bar.
- About 20 percent of boaters had problems getting above Three Mile Bar. Accident rates appeared to reflect boater experience, equipment, and knowledge of the channel.

**Gaging network**
- A new gage was established one-quarter mile below the dam. The original Glen Canyon Dam gage ran from October 22, 1964, to September 30, 1972.
- Gages at [[Lee's Ferry]] (River Mile 0), Grand Canyon (River Mile 87), National Canyon (River Mile 166), and Diamond Creek (River Mile 225) were re-established. They collect discharge, water quality, temperature, and suspended-sediment data through the end of GCES.

**Archaeological monitoring (October 11-28, 1989)**
- Twenty participants from the National Park Service, Reclamation, and the U.S. Geological Survey, plus volunteers, assessed dam impacts on archaeological sites.
- Arroyo cutting in 1983-1984 exposed Anasazi remains dated to roughly A.D. 800-1200. Several new sites were found eroding from dunes above river level.
- The work is governed by several federal laws, including the NPS Organic Act (1916) and the [[National Historic Preservation Act]] (1966, amended 1980).

**Channel characteristics**
- A qualitative assessment and aerial photo verification were made for the 16-mile channel between Glen Canyon Dam and Lee's Ferry. Additional cross-section measurements were taken.

**Photography and video**
- Aerial photography covered the river from Glen Canyon Dam to Pierce Ferry at Lake Mead, plus the lower Little Colorado River. Helicopter video covered the Glen Canyon reach, Little Colorado River, and lower Paria River.

**Program schedule and planning**
- A Final EIS is due by December 1991, which requires GCES research to follow a tight schedule.
- At the November 14-15, 1989, Technical Team meeting in Tempe, Arizona (over 40 attendees), the group determined that responses to the draft research program were due by December 8, 1989. A follow-up meeting was held December 12, 1989.
- A core integration team will be set up under Dr. Patten. A GIS working group meets January 17, 1990, in Denver. The next full Technical Team meeting is March 8-9, 1990, in Phoenix.
- The summary report on the 5,000 cfs test is still being prepared.

**Congressional briefing trip (August 10-15, 1989)**
- Twenty-eight participants from Congressional, state, federal, water, power, recreation, environmental, and scientific groups took a river trip covering about 100 river miles, from Grand Canyon Village to River Mile 186 (Bar X Ranch).
