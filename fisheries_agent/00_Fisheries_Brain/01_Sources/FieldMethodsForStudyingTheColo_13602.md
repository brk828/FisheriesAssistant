# Field Methods for Studying Colorado River Fishes in Grand Canyon National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods evaluation, conducted October 1990 to November 1991, tested field techniques for studying the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other fishes in the whitewater reaches of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The work formed part of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), which assessed the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on native fishes. The authors evaluated small maneuverable [[Research Boats]], modified sampling gears, and [[Radiotelemetry]]. They concluded that these methods are effective in whitewater environments, although they require skilled boat handlers and experienced biologists.

## Background & Description

Whitewater canyon reaches of the Colorado River basin were among the least intensively sampled areas in the basin because they are remote, hard to access, and difficult to sample with conventional gear. Recent surveys in National Park Service and Bureau of Land Management canyons found populations of endemic, federally listed endangered fishes, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The largest humpback chub population was reported from Grand Canyon.

The basin is difficult to study for several reasons:
- Access points are few and sometimes hundreds of kilometers apart.
- Seasonal flows vary widely. Spring snowmelt raised flows 10- to 20-fold; the Colorado River rose from 200 to 2,000 m³/s in 1986.
- Daily fluctuations below dams such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and Glen Canyon Dam make travel hard, alter habitat, and can strand gear.
- Turbidity and conductivity affect electrofishing efficiency and radio signal range.
- Swift, turbulent rapids limit travel and sampling opportunities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study covered 275 km of the Colorado River from Kwagunt Rapid (RK 91) to Diamond Creek (RK 366). Sampling used a stratified random design. The area was divided into three reaches (Kwagunt Rapid to Red Canyon; Red Canyon to Havasu Creek; Havasu Creek to Diamond Creek). Strata followed the geomorphic classification of [[Schmidt and Graf 1988]], which gave an approximately even distribution of sampling effort across habitat types.

Key habitat features included eddies, pools, slow runs, tributary inflows, side channels, backwaters, pocket waters, and shallow shorelines. Filamentous algae (*Cladophora glomerata*) accumulated on nets and reduced their effectiveness. Water conductivity during electrofishing ranged from 832 to 1,103 µmhos/cm.

## Key Findings

**Research boats**
- Two inflatable boats were used: the SU-16 (4.9 m), for electrofishing, and the SH-170 (5.2 m), for netting and radiotracking. Both had 40-hp outboard motors.
- The boats increased access to upstream and downstream sites from base camps, allowing replicate sampling and relocation of radio-tagged fish.
- They required skilled handlers. Large rapids were negotiated by backing upstream or by running downstream, depending on the rapid.

**Electrofishing**
- A pulsed-DC system (200–250 V, 8–15 A) with spherical electrodes was used to reduce spinal injury. Electrodes were swapped hourly to clean the cathode.
- Electrofishing had the highest catch rate for humpback chub, at 12.57 fish/10 h.
- It captured 86% of young-of-year (241 of 281), 78% of juveniles (60 of 77), and 6% of adults (39 of 606). Overall it took 340 of 964 humpback chub (35%).
- Of 9,063 fish captured in total, 5,643 (62%) were taken by electrofishing. Bruises or spinal deformities were seen on 73 (1.3%), all in trout. One adult humpback chub died after extended narcosis.

**Nets, traps and seines**
- Trammel nets with 3.8 cm mesh captured 271 adult chub (45% of adults), the highest adult catch of the 11 gears. Trammel nets with 2.5 cm mesh had a catch rate of 6.57 and captured 134 chub. Only one chub died from a trammel net.
- Gill nets with 2.5 cm mesh (catch rate 6.76) and experimental gill nets (6.36) performed about as well as trammel nets. Gill nets with 5.1 cm mesh caught only 2% of chub and were judged ineffective.
- Minnow traps caught 40 young-of-year and 3 juvenile chub (catch rate 0.68 fish/100 h), with no known injuries.
- Hoop nets and seines were ineffective for humpback chub in the mainstem. No chub were taken in seines.

**Radiotelemetry**
- Fifty-three adult humpback chub were radio-tagged from October 1990 to November 1991. Of these, 6 carried 9 g transmitters and 47 carried 11 g transmitters.
- Transmitter weight was limited to 2% of body weight. The 11 g transmitters were tracked for 56–147 days (mean 99 days).
- Tracking of fish that moved into the [[Little Colorado River]] was limited by high water conductance.
- Transmitter range was up to 30 m at a maximum depth of 4.5 m, and up to 1,200 m at 1 m depth.
- Only 1 of 53 tagged fish died after surgery and initial tracking.
- Signal data showed chub spawning ascents into the Little Colorado River, supported by lower mainstem catches.

**Conclusions**
- The methods are effective for whitewater ichthyofaunal surveys. They need continued refinement and experienced personnel.
- Electrofishing is considered the most reliable method for pre-adult humpback chub, and trammel and gill nets with 2.5–3.8 cm mesh for adults. The authors accepted the observed electrofishing injury rate as a justified cost.
