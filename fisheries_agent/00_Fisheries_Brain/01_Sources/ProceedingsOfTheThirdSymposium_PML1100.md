# Identification of Larval Sunfishes from Southern Louisiana (Centrarchidae, Elassomatidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the Third Symposium on Larval Fish (Bowling Green, Kentucky, February 1979) and authored by John V. Conner of the [[Louisiana State University]] School of Forestry and Wildlife Management, reviews the state of the art for identifying wild-caught larval sunfishes from southern Louisiana. Although many taxa are described in the literature, reliable identification, sometimes even to genus, remains difficult. The study examined about a thousand larval and early juvenile specimens, comparing meristic and morphometric characters across five genera and several informal "types." The author concludes that species-level identification is largely impossible at present, that morphological variation is extensive and partly environmental, and that future work should emphasize comparative, quantitative approaches.

## Background & Description

Sunfishes (family [[Centrarchidae]]) are a major component of southern Louisiana freshwater fish faunas. Of the 16 native sunfish taxa in the region, nine have been described throughout most of their larval development, three are illustrated only as juveniles, and four remain undescribed in all immature phases. All four undescribed taxa belong to the genus [[Lepomis]], and two of them (*L. humilis* and a second species) are largely confined to the central and south-central United States.

The study drew on:
- Roughly 1,000 wild-caught larvae and early juveniles from riverine, floodplain swamp, backwater, and lake habitats, chiefly in the lower Mississippi Drainage.
- Lab-reared series of four *Lepomis* species (longear, redbreast, bluegill, and redear sunfish) provided by the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] larval fish laboratory.
- Specimens fixed in 10 percent formalin and later transferred to 3–5 percent buffered formalin, and deposited in the LSU Fisheries Collections.

Measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm with an ocular micrometer, and total length (TL) was used throughout. Myomere counts followed [[Siefert 1969]], and fin-ray counts followed Hubbs and Lagler (1964). Developmental phases were terminology-based on Snyder (1976), subdivided into subphases such as protolarvae (P), early mesolarvae (EMS), and late mesolarvae through early juveniles (LMS/J). Illustrations were made from camera-lucida tracings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several factors complicate identification:

- **Extended spawning and temperature exposure:** Some taxa (especially *Lepomis*) spawn through most of spring and summer, so eggs and larvae face wide variation in water quality, notably temperature. Substantial morphological variation is expected (Barlow 1961).
- **Hybridization:** Sunfishes, especially *Lepomis*, hybridize readily (Hubbs 1955). Several hybrid combinations have been recorded as adults in southern Louisiana, so hybrid larvae may occur.
- **Turbid versus clear water:** Material from turbid water is less pigmented than lab-reared or clear-water specimens, on which most keys are based.
- **Sampling bias:** The author's collections were concentrated in non-pelagic habitats, which yield taxa and stages rare in open-water plankton. Conventional plankton sampling may not reflect adult abundance (see Key Findings).
- **Seasonal window:** Sunfish larvae were encountered from March through October.

## Key Findings

### Diagnostic Characters by Genus

- **Myomeres:** [[Elassoma]] protolarvae hatch with no more than 26 total myomeres (27–36 in the study material), whereas most [[Percidae]] darters have at least 40. Among centrarchids, the Lepomis and Micropterus group generally has 14–18 postanal myomeres, while [[Pomoxis]] and [[Centrarchus]] usually have 19 or more (typically 20+) postanal myomeres before complete caudal fins form.
- **Preanal length (% TL):** Protolarvae of *Elassoma* and *Pomoxis* usually have preanal lengths below 41 percent of TL, while *Lepomis* and *Micropterus* lie well above 41 percent. For later phases, preanal length is more reliable.
- **Gas bladder position:** In [[Lepomis]], the gas bladder terminates well before the anus. In *Pomoxis* and *Centrarchus*, it extends to or beyond the anus. In very small crappies (under 5.5 mm TL), the bladder fails to reach the anus.
- **Gut architecture:** [[Micropterus]] larvae have a thick, massively coiled gut, with a robust head and relatively large eye, which gives a superficial resemblance to Lepomis gut characters but differs in overall size and pigmentation at comparable stages.
- **Pigmentation:** The supra-anal melanophore typical of *Lepomis* (per Anjard 1974 and Hardy 1978) was present in only some taxa and was more prevalent in clear-water specimens.
- **Eye size (*Pomoxis* vs. *Centrarchus*):** In specimens under 12.0 mm TL, eye diameter exceeds snout length in *Centrarchus*. Dorsal spine counts also separate these genera at 12 mm and above.

### Genera and Taxa Recognized

- Five genera were identified in the collections: *Pomoxis*, *Centrarchus*, *Lepomis* (including *Chaenobryttus*), *Micropterus*, and *Elassoma*.
- Larvae of the local [[Ambloplites]] (rock bass) were not in the collections, but the author expects them to resemble *Ambloplites rupestris* as illustrated by Hogue et al. (1976).
- *Centrarchus* (flier) larvae were not captured in plankton tows despite repeated sampling. The few specimens available came from dipnetting in shallow littoral vegetation.

### Informal Lepomis Types

The author recognized several *Lepomis* types, which the study treats as hypothetical:

- **Green sunfish type:** Probably includes [[Green Sunfish]] (*L. cyanellus*) and [[Warmouth]] (*L. gulosus*). Characterized by short preanal length, deeper heads, the lowest modal preanal myomere counts among *Lepomis*, and extensive head and trunk pigment.
- **Longear type:** Probably *L. megalotis*, which appears first as early mesolarvae without a free-swimming protolarval phase. Longear larvae were extremely rare in collections, suggesting that they stay nest-bound or near shore.
- **Bluegill types ("B"):** Most likely *L. humilis* and possibly *L. macrochirus* ([[Bluegill]]). The "B" type is the most frequently collected *Lepomis* in plankton samples in the study area, regardless of water body type.
- **"A" type:** Intermediate between bluegill and green sunfish types, with the closest resemblance to TVA lab-reared *L. gulosus* or *L. punctatus* (spotted sunfish). Its identity remains uncertain.
- **Redear type:** Tentatively linked to [[Redear Sunfish]] (*L. microlophus*).

### Abundance and Sampling

- Relative abundance of larvae in plankton samples did not necessarily reflect adult densities. Adult [[Micropterus]] (largemouth and spotted bass) rank among the top five centrarchids by catch and mark-recapture estimates, yet their larvae are poorly represented in plankton. Longear and dollar sunfish are common as adults but no longear-type larvae were found.
- The study contrasts these findings with Dorr et al. (1976), who found close agreement between larval and adult species composition in Lake Michigan.

### Lab-Reared Versus Wild Specimens

- Lab-reared larvae were consistently larger and more robust than wild-caught fish at comparable pre-juvenile stages. They were also much more heavily pigmented, including clear-water specimens.
- The author suggests that the captive-reared differences may reflect genetic stock, ideal conditions, and lack of food competition, and that field sampling may be selective for weaker individuals.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- Morphological variation is extreme both within and between taxa, and the study area's material is limited geographically. Many traditional diagnostic characters are therefore environmentally plastic, and typological approaches in the descriptive literature may have reduced reliability.
- The author recommends greater emphasis on comparative and quantitative methods, with similar studies in other regions to facilitate general comparative compilations.
- For the present, larval sunfishes will largely have to be identified to genus or as "types," with species identifications remaining largely impossible.
