# Annotated Bibliography for Aquatic Resource Management of the Upper Colorado River Ecosystem

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Resource Publication (No. 135, 1980), compiled by Richard S. Wydoski, Kim Gilbert, Karl Seethaler, Charles W. McAda, and Joy A. Wydoski at the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]], is an annotated bibliography of 1,109 published and readily available unpublished references on the Upper Colorado River Basin. It was written to support management decisions as water development (for municipalities, irrigated agriculture, and [[Energy Development]]) expands across the basin. The compilation focuses on fish and macroinvertebrates, but also includes references from archaeology, economics, geology, hydrology, law, and history that bear on the river environment. Each entry is annotated and tagged with key words, and a key word index is provided.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The original aim was to compile information on the distribution, relative abundance, and ecology of fish and macroinvertebrates. Because the topic overlaps many disciplines, the authors broadened coverage. Annual series (such as U.S. Geological Survey water supply records) were cited only by their latest volume plus the series start date. The authors acknowledge that the list is not exhaustive.

**Study system.** The Colorado River flows through 2,317 km (1,440 miles) of arid land. The basin produces about 7.4 hectare-meters (60 acre-feet) of water per 2.6 km² (1 square mile), less than any other major U.S. river. It serves about 15 million people. The urban population was projected to rise about 250% between 1960 and 2000.

**Focal species.** The abstract identifies four endemic fishes considered rare and possibly threatened with extinction: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki pleuriticus*) is also treated as a rare native salmonid.

**Key word system.** Each reference carries subject tags (for example ABUNDANCE, ALTERATION, FISH-rare, MIGRATION, RIVER-Colorado). Notable definitions include:
- AGE-GROWTH: age, growth, and length-weight relations of fish.
- ALTERATION: man-made change to terrestrial or aquatic ecosystems.
- FISH-rare: species few in number that may be threatened, endangered, or unique.
- MIGRATION: directed, usually seasonal, movement into or from an area; MOVEMENT is a smaller change in position.

**Acknowledgments.** Support came from U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Contract No. 14-16-0008-1140 and the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Principal threats identified across the bibliography:**
- **Water development and impoundment:** Reservoirs such as [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (Lake Powell), and Granby Dam alter flows, temperatures, and habitat. Entries describe reservoir pre- and post-impoundment surveys.
- **Introduced species:** Exotic fishes are cited as a major reason for native decline. Deacon and colleagues (1964) documented local extirpation effects in southern Nevada. Holden and Stalnaker (1975) identified introduced species as outnumbering natives 19 to 10 in middle and upper basin collections.
- **Salinity and mineral pollution:** Salinity is treated extensively, including the 1975 Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Forum standards and mine and uranium mill tailings effects.
- **Energy development:** Oil shale (Piceance and Uinta basins), coal, and the associated water demand are major themes. Davis and Wood (1974) noted that energy industries must compete with other water users.
- **Grazing and watershed alteration:** Lusby and colleagues found runoff from grazed watersheds exceeded ungrazed runoff by about 30% in western Colorado salt-desert shrub areas.
- **Channelization and rotenone treatment:** Dredging and rotenone eradication before Flaming Gorge filling nearly eliminated invertebrates and fish in parts of the Green River (Dexter 1965; Binns 1965).

**Habitat details noted in the bibliography.** Kendall Warm Springs, Wyoming, is the sole habitat of the Kendall Warm Springs dace (*Rhinichthys osculus thermalis*), a 300 m creek with adult lengths of 23-54 mm (mean 39 mm). Dissolved oxygen ranged from 0.55 mg/l at the spring source to supersaturation downstream.

## Key Findings

The compilation supports several management conclusions, drawn from the annotated entries:

1. **Rare endemic fish status.** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) reported that the four endemic species (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, and humpback sucker) reproduce in the lower Yampa River, Desolation Canyon of the middle Green River, and the lower Green River in Canyonlands National Park. Habitat alteration by high dams and exotic introductions are cited as the main causes of native decline.
2. **Yampa system importance.** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) found the Yampa system "appeared important to reproduction and preservation of Colorado squawfish." Twenty-two species were collected there, including four rare or endangered forms.
3. **Squawfish occurrence.** Carlson et al. (1979) collected 18 Colorado squawfish from the Yampa and White Rivers (July 1975 to September 1978). Lanigan et al. (1979) captured or observed 13 adults in the lower 12 miles (19 km) of the White River in 1978, with no juveniles or larvae found.
4. **Cutthroat trout status.** Binns (1977) reported that the Colorado River cutthroat trout is the only native trout of the upper basin. Twelve streams and two lakes held pure or essentially pure stocks, and 22 more waters had slight to moderate hybridization. Stream habitat often held average daily flows below 0.14 cm/s. Binns judged the status "sensitive," with threatened or endangered listing likely if losses continue.
5. **Species decline counts.** Deacon (1968) reported that 55 taxa west of the Rocky Mountains were extinct, rare, endangered, or of unknown status.
6. **Salinity projections.** Hill (1965) estimated that Colorado River water carries about 0.9 ton of salt per acre-foot. Average inflow to Lake Havasu (11,821,000 acre-feet/year, 1941-1960) may fall to 8,500,000 acre-feet in the future and possibly to 7,500,000 acre-feet during the last 20 years of the century.
7. **Sediment load.** Howard (1947) reported that the annual suspended sediment load at Grand Canyon (1925-1941) ranged from 50,080,000 to 480,000,000 tons, with a mean of 200,200,000 tons.
8. **Mining effects on invertebrates.** Canton and Ward (1978) found aquatic insects made up over 90% of the Trout Creek, Colorado fauna, with caddisflies dominant. Water quality declined mainly during spring runoff.
9. **Recovery planning.** The Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan (1978) includes a 5-year budget for restoring the species to non-endangered status. A draft humpback chub recovery plan (1979) outlines research and management needs and a budget.
10. **Regional policy gaps.** Bowden (1975) concluded that energy plans for the Colorado and Missouri rivers would "seriously deplete the water supply; alter the quality of the water, land, and air; and increase the human population." Bishop and Porcella (1980) provided an overview of environmental change in the basin and research needs.

**Scope note.** The document is a bibliography rather than a primary study, so it reports no new field data of its own. The figures above are quoted from the cited references as annotated in the compilation.
