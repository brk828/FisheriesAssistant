# Bear Lake Fishery: Fish, Plankton, Water Chemistry and Creel Census

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 bulletin, produced jointly by the [[Utah State Department of Fish and Game]], the [[Idaho Department of Fish and Game]] and the Wildlife Management Department of Utah State Agricultural College, reports a multi-year study of [[Bear Lake]], a large, deep, naturally alkaline lake straddling the Utah-Idaho border. The study covers limnology (temperature, turbidity, chemistry, zinc question), the biology of plants, bottom fauna and plankton, the abundance and life histories of the fish community, and a [[Creel Census]] of angler effort and success from 1953 to 1955. The authors conclude that the lake is low in productivity, that most stocked trout die or are never harvested, and that management should remain liberal, with a focus on lake trout, cutthroat trout and the abundant but underused [[Bonneville Whitefish]].

## Background & Description

**Physical setting.** Bear Lake occupies the southern end of a high mountain valley formed by faulting. It is just under 20 miles long and 4 to 8 miles wide, with a surface area of just under 110 square miles, a 48-mile shoreline and a maximum elevation of 5923.6 ft. The lake was once far larger, filling a valley about 50 miles long, and its old shorelines are still visible. Its maximum recorded depth is 208 feet, with more than half the lake deeper than 100 feet. The basin is separated from [[Mud Lake]] and [[Dingle Swamp]] by a natural beach bar at the north end.

**Water management.** The [[Bear River Diversion System]] (Telluride Power Company works from 1907; Utah Power and Light Company from 1912) diverts Bear River water into the lake and can pump water back out. The lake can be drawn down about 21.65 ft, but annual fluctuation is typically 3 to 4 ft. The average fluctuation from 1917 to 1955 was just over 3.5 ft.

**Study history.** Earlier investigators include [[Kemmerer et al. 1923]], Snyder (1919), Tanner (1936), Hazzard (1935) and [[Perry 1943]]. The Dingell-Johnson project began field work on 1 September 1951, the first such project in the United States. Federal aid field work ended 31 December 1955.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and zinc.** Kemmerer et al. (1912 sample) reported 0.65 ppm zinc and suggested that zinc limited productivity. Current analyses (35 determinations, three collections) found a maximum of 0.076 ppm and a lake average of 0.020 ppm across 14 determinations, so the authors conclude zinc is not a limiting factor. The lake contains more magnesium than calcium, which may be a limiting condition that was not investigated. Methyl orange alkalinity fell from 586 ppm (1912) to 294 to 313 ppm (1952 to 1955), consistent with dilution by Bear River water. pH ranged from 8.4 to 8.6. Dissolved oxygen was abundant at all depths, with a lowest value of 5.9 ppm at 210 ft.

**Temperature and ice.** Surface temperatures rarely exceed 70°F, with a high of 73°F recorded in 1952. A thermocline forms in late June and persists into November. Bottom water below 150 ft seldom exceeds 42°F. The lake has frozen completely in 26 of the last 33 winters. In 1952-53 and 1953-54 it failed to freeze, cooling to an isothermal 35.5°F.

**Turbidity and bottom types.** Open-water turbidity ranged from 1 to 5 ppm silica equivalent. Secchi visibility in 1952 reached 15 ft. Rocky littoral area is less than 0.001 percent of the bottom. Sand dominates to about 25 ft, grading to silt marl below about 75 ft, where the marl is 58 percent CaCO3.

**Plants.** Emergent vegetation is scarce (cattail and bulrush along the northwest and west shores). The main submerged plant is a thin-leaved [[Pondweed]] (Potamogeton). The authors attribute limited plant growth mainly to wave exposure and fluctuating water levels, not to zinc.

**Plankton and bottom fauna.** Phytoplankton are present in very high numbers (Ankistrodesmus falcatus exceeded 2 million cells per liter in several samples, with a maximum of about 4.5 million per liter), but productivity per unit volume is low. The main zooplankters are the copepod *Epischura* and the rotifer *Conochilus*, with maximum densities of 4 *Epischura* and 11.5 *Conochilus* colonies per liter. Midge larvae reach about 500 per square yard in sand-silt-marl, oligochaetes up to 3,000 per square yard in fine silt marl, and ostracods are abundant. The authors note that the lake is many times less productive than fertile regional waters such as [[Henry's Lake]], Fish Lake and Panguitch Lake, but not extremely unproductive compared with [[Lake Pend Oreille]].

**Molluscs.** Live snails and clams are absent, but abundant shells (the snail *Carinifex newberryi* and the fingernail clam *Sphaerium mormonicum*) indicate a former higher lake level. Shells date the peak to about 10,000 years ago and a dry period to about 5,000 years ago.

## Key Findings

**Fish community and abundance**
- The most numerous fish are the [[Bonneville Cisco]] and an undescribed [[Sculpin]] (*Cottus* sp.). Together they make up about half the fish in the lake.
- The [[Utah Sucker]] is about 20 percent of the population but contributes more total poundage than the cisco or sculpin.
- Three other whitefish (Bear Lake, Bonneville and mountain whitefish) follow in abundance. The Utah chub (about 4 percent) and carp (about 3 percent) rank next.
- All three sought trout (lake, cutthroat, rainbow) together represent no more than about 3 percent of the population.
- The total fish population in 1952-53 was greater than in 1938-42. Catch rates were 1.843 and 0.706 fish per 100-foot gill-net hour, t = 4.35 (95 percent significance).

**Species-specific findings**
- *Cisco* feed almost entirely on zooplankton (more than 95 percent Diaptomus in 1930 stomach samples), spawn in late December and January, and seldom exceed 7 inches.
- *Lake trout* concentrate at 50 to 100 ft. Natural spawning appears to be limited by silt on rubble at the North and South Eden deltas. Few stocked fish reach the creel. Only one lake trout smaller than 20 inches was taken.
- *Cutthroat trout* are a hybrid mix of cutthroat and rainbow lineage, considered ecologically cutthroat. They grow faster and larger than rainbow trout. Stocking of about 2.1 million cutthroat (1939-1954, plus later plantings) produced a catch of only about 1,200 fish per year.
- *Rainbow trout* return poorly. Fewer than 5 percent of planted fish reached the creel, and fish under 10 inches showed high mortality within weeks. The best return for any plant was about 20 percent (one 1955 plant) to 35 percent (one 1952 plant of 11-12 inch fish, a rough estimate).
- *Utah sucker* juveniles are absent from the lake and occur in Mud Lake. Most suckers at Mud Lake were parasitised by the body-cavity tapeworm *Liguala intestinalis*.
- *Carp* are borderline habitat, grow slowly (4-year-old fish about 11 inches vs. about 20 inches in Bear River Bird Refuge), and most lake carp are spawned in Mud Lake.
- *Yellow perch* sustain an intermittent fishery near the north-end inlets; the fishery collapsed after 1952.
- *Kokanee* were introduced 1933 to 1938, rarely exceed 8 inches and have achieved little reproduction.

**Creel census (1953 to 1955)**
- Estimated angler effort declined from 12,000 fisherman days (1953) to 10,000 (1954) and 9,000 (1955).
- Overall success was 0.33, 0.26 and 0.18 fish per hour in 1953, 1954 and 1955.
- Highest success: whitefish and yellow perch (about 0.53 fish per hour in the 1953-54 peak). Lake trout were the hardest to catch, requiring about 33 hours per fish.
- Total 1953 expenditure was $109,000 ($1.50 per surface acre), compared with $82.00 at Navajo Lake and $283.00 at Panguitch Lake. Average spending was $9.09 per fishing day.
- About 70 percent of anglers live in Cache, Weber and Rich counties, Utah. Almost all remaining anglers are from Bear Lake County, Idaho.
- Less than 20 percent of fishing pressure occurs June to September, because summer is the poorest fishing period.
- Shore anglers using spinning tackle caught about 15 percent more game fish than other gear, and about twice as many cutthroat and lake trout.

**Management recommendations**
- Keep regulations on time, gear and creel liberal. Only a small percentage of any species is harvested, so closures should be minimised.
- Continue lake trout stocking within economic limits. Stock fish at least 7 inches, preferably 10 inches, and scatter them from boats or planes.
- Stock 10-inch or larger rainbow in June to improve the summer fishery. Stocking small rainbow is judged uneconomical.
- Recognise that cutthroat planting appears uneconomic. Shore fishermen would not notice a large increase in density.
- Encourage greater use of [[Bonneville Whitefish]], which is abundant and shows no evidence of depletion.
- Consider aspen-pole habitat cribs and creation of rubble spawning areas for lake trout, both of which were judged useful but expensive.
- Monitor carp and sucker populations. If high water years allowed these fish to overwhelm the game fish, commercial removal may be required, though the problem was not critical in 1957.

**Explanation of low productivity**
- The authors attribute Bear Lake's low angler success to its great mean depth (about 100 ft), short shoreline (48 miles for about 110 square miles), infertility and cold water, which together give low productivity. They cite Rawson (1955) on depth and shoreline as strong influences on productivity.
