# Instream Flows to Assist the Recovery of Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report by Jack A. Stanford, prepared for the [[Instream Flow Subcommittee]] of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], reviews the ecological science behind instream flow recommendations for four endangered big-river fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin. The four species are [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. River regulation by dams and diversions has truncated peak flows and increased baseflow variability, degrading the low-velocity habitats these fishes need. The author finds that the Fish and Wildlife Service flow recommendations were inconsistently derived, and he recommends interim flows that re-establish seasonality, paired with monitoring, research, peer review and adaptive management. The review concludes that current ecological knowledge is sufficient to justify endorsing interim flows.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Four endemic fishes listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Bonytail chub is nearly extirpated. Razorback sucker and humpback chub are rare. Squawfish is the most abundant but restricted by dams.
- **Historical range:** The fishes were abundant in the potamon (warm, downstream) reaches of the basin during settlement, circa 1870s–1950s.
- **Reproducing humpback chub populations** occur only in five canyon segments: Black Rocks and Westwater Canyons (Colorado), Gray and Desolation Canyons (Green), and Yampa Canyon.
- **Causes of decline:** Habitat loss from reservoirs, reduced and regulated flows, predation by introduced species, and selenium from irrigation return flows.
- **Scope:** The study treats the ecosystem as the whole Upper Colorado Basin, bounded downstream by [[Lake Powell]]. The author used a literature review, discussions with researchers, and an expert panel of [[Edmund D. Andrews]], [[William J. Matthews]] and [[James V. Ward]].
- **Key concepts:** The [[Rhithron-Potamon Transition]] zone, the [[Varial Zone]] (shoreline inundated and dewatered by flow), and [[Serial Discontinuity]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and habitat.** Squawfish spawn on cobble chute channels that form and are cleaned by spring runoff. Larvae drift downstream into backwater nursery habitats. Razorback sucker are most often captured in flooded gravel pits and wetlands connected to the channel. Backwaters are often 5–10°C warmer than the channel, which benefits spawning condition. Naturally functioning backwaters also provide food: zooplankton, macrophyte-associated invertebrates and detritus.

**Regulation effects.** Reservoir releases have reduced spring peaks and raised baseflows. Hydropower peaking adds short-term fluctuation. Sediment retention reduced channel area on the Green River by 6–7% (Andrews 1986) to 10% (Lyons and Pucherelli 1992). Riparian vegetation has encroached on the regulated channel. Non-native species such as [[Tamarisk]], [[Reed Canary Grass]] and [[Russian Olive]] dominate regulated margins.

**Ecological discontinuity.** On the [[Gunnison River]], the [[Aspinall Units]] shifted the rhithron-potamon transition downstream 40–50 miles. Bankfull discharge of 11,000 cfs, which occurred every 3.2 years before regulation, is now expected only once in 40 years (Elliott and Parker 1992). Hypolimnial releases cooled the river by nearly 10°C in summer at the North Fork confluence.

**Non-native predators.** Red shiners, fathead minnows, walleye, northern pike, channel catfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass and green sunfish prey on or compete with native fishes. Lake Mohave razorback sucker recruitment failed because of predation on larvae and early juveniles by non-native minnows and sunfish.

**Food webs.** Algae and zoobenthos are most productive on cobble substrates in the upper potamon. Downstream, silt and clay deposits limit zoobenthos. Peaking flows interrupt insect emergence and flush the varial zone. Weisberg et al. (1990) recorded a tenfold increase in zoobenthos standing crop after peaking was eliminated.

## Key Findings

**Population status and recruitment**
- Bonytail chub appear extirpated in the upper basin.
- Adult squawfish recruitment appears to occur almost every year. Recruitment of adult humpback chub and razorback sucker has not been demonstrated.
- Razorback sucker in the Green–Yampa system likely never exceeded about 1,000 fish in the last two decades. In 1989, Lanigan and Tyus estimated 232 adults above Desolation Canyon.
- In 1993, 67 razorback sucker were collected from Etter Pond, a gravel pit last connected to the river during the 1984 flood. Most were about nine years old.

**Flow–recruitment relationships**
- Squawfish young-of-year production is highest at intermediate (near-average) spring peaks and lower in very high or very low flow years.
- Spawning temperature for squawfish is 20–22°C. Razorback sucker spawning occurs at 5–10°C below that range, and the optimal range for reproduction is 20–22°C.

**Central tradeoff**
- Very high flows are needed occasionally to form and maintain the habitats fishes depend on, but they can reduce reproductive success.

**Critical uncertainties identified**
1. Flow seasonality and its correlates may not be the limiting factor for native fishes.
2. Water volume may be insufficient for recovery, given the high value of trout fisheries and Colorado Compact entitlements.
3. Channel and floodplain morphology is not a simple flow–area relationship.
4. The tradeoff between larval drift and the need for high flows to connect channels and wetlands is unresolved.
5. Whether food webs in backwaters can re-establish, given hydropower operating windows.
6. Whether the fishes can expand range given the downstream shift of the transition zone.
7. Non-native interactions may limit recovery regardless of flow.

**Critique of Fish and Wildlife Service methods**
- The [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) was used on the Colorado River but its output was discarded on the Green and Yampa Rivers. The author judges the method unsuitable for the potamon reaches of the upper basin. Weighted usable area was maximized at very low flows that were exceeded most of the time in the historical record.
- Yampa River recommendations assumed a historical baseline of 68,000 acre-feet depletion. The author estimates current depletions at about 10,000 cfs and argues monthly means hide important daily variation.
- Green River recommendations gave a peak-to-baseflow ratio of about 12, compared with about 40 under predam flows. Baseflow fluctuation was set at ±12.5%, while the North Fork Flathead River (unregulated) varies about 5% per day at baseflow.
- Colorado River recommendations were the most data-based. The 15-Mile Reach flows included peaks of 30,000–40,000 cfs and baseflows of roughly 700–1,200 cfs.

**Recommendations (ecosystem approach)**
- **Interim flows:** Immediately implement interim flows that re-establish seasonality. Peaks should approach preregulation amplitude and frequency. Summer and winter baseflows should be stable, with daily changes likely no more than about 5% per day.
- **Common water understanding:** Establish a shared understanding of water availability in each subbasin, linked to the "Guru II" process.
- **Monitoring:** Improve the [[Standardized Monitoring Program]] by adding community ecology perspectives, quality control, and annual raw data reporting. Use [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] for mark–recapture.
- **Research diversification:** Select study reaches in alluvial segments, instrument them with temperature sensors and topographic surveys, and run controlled experiments, including a proposed factorial field experiment on backwater fluctuation.
- **Peer review:** Fund an independent panel of 3–6 ad hoc experts to review research proposals annually.
- **Adaptive management:** Build a management process able to change interim flows based on monitoring results.

**Conclusion.** The author judges that interim flows should be endorsed, that they should be monitored for at least about 10 years, and that success will be judged by long-term trends in the endangered fish populations.
