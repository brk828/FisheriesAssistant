# Predator Avoidance Training in Hatchery-Reared Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper tests whether hatchery-reared [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) can be trained to avoid predators before release. Fish were conditioned in a hatchery raceway to flee a moving electrified model of a [[Common Loon]] (*Gavia immer*). Trained and untrained lots were then released into [[Mykiss Lake]] in [[Algonquin Park]], Ontario, where a loon pair was present. Trained fish were recovered in greater numbers than untrained fish in both 1970 and 1972, suggesting that [[Predator Avoidance Training]] can improve the survival of stocked trout. The document is a research article, probably attributed to J. M. Fraser of the Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources (the byline is partly garbled by OCR).

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Brook Trout]] is widely stocked in Ontario waters. Hatchery-reared trout often survive poorly after release, and the paper cites evidence that stocked fish have low survival compared with wild fish.
- **Behavioural traits:** Hatchery fish tend to school, show little wariness, and prefer to remain in open water. The author suggests these traits leave them vulnerable to predators.
- **Predators:** Fish-eating birds and mammals are the most important predators of planted brook trout. The paper lists the [[Common Loon]], common merganser, great blue heron, osprey, otter and mink.
- **Rationale:** Earlier work reported that conditioned fish, such as chinook salmon trained to avoid electrified models, showed better avoidance behaviour. The author therefore tested whether a similar training approach could reduce predation after stocking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental setting**

- **Training site:** A hatchery raceway about 9 m × 1.2 m (partly garbled in the source), with 0.4 m of water.
- **Training apparatus:** A life-like plastic loon model was suspended from an overhead track. It moved through the water at 0.6 m/sec, slowing to 0.1 m/sec at the ends of the compartment. An electrified field surrounded the model's head.
- **Release lake:** [[Mykiss Lake]] is a wilderness lake of about 23 ha with a maximum depth of 12 m. It has no sport fishery and has been occupied by a loon pair since observation began in 1962.
- **Resident fish:** Besides the planted trout, the lake held creek chubs (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), common shiners (*Notropis cornutus*), blacknose dace (*Rhinichthys atratulus*) and a few white suckers (*Catostomus commersonii*). The paper argues that competition with these species was unlikely to explain the results.

**Context for release and recovery**

- Fish were released at a single shore location and were later recovered in a downstream stream, as well as by netting.
- The author notes that predator recognition by trout seems general rather than specific, so a broadly wary response may be more useful than learning one predator.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**

- **1970 lot:** Trained and untrained trout were stocked together. Training took place over 9 days.
- **1972 lot:** Training took place over 8 days, with a new cohort. Fish were finclipped for identification.
- **Exposure:** In 1970, trout received four daily one-hour periods of exposure, separated by intervals of 2–3 hours. In 1972, they received 4 hours of daily exposure in irregularly spaced periods of 20–60 minutes. The source text for these intervals is partly garbled.
- **Control:** An untrained control lot was kept in an adjacent compartment of the raceway.

**Stocking and survival**

- Estimated populations in Mykiss Lake, with 95% confidence limits, were 189 (177–201) for trained and 256 (241–267) for untrained trout in 1970. In 1972 they were 143 (130–156) for trained and 165 (150–180) for untrained trout.
- Population estimates used the Leslie method and a geometric mean regression of catch (Ricker 1973).
- Survival percentages are not clearly recoverable from the scan. The text suggests that survival was notably low in these hatchery trout.

**Behaviour and conclusions**

- Trained trout showed clear avoidance of the electrified model after a few days of training.
- Trained trout were recovered downstream in greater numbers than untrained trout in both years.
- The author concludes that [[Predator Avoidance Training]] can increase the survival of stocked brook trout. The effect is attributed to the fish learning a general wariness toward loon-like predators.
- The author notes that the low survival of hatchery fish may partly be due to their large size, which makes them more conspicuous to predators.
- The author also cites a large size difference between trained and untrained fish, and notes that the recovery of large trout (30–38 cm) in nets may reflect the release conditions.

**Limitations**

- Only two years and two lots were tested.
- Recovery data depend on netting and stream counts, which are not equally reliable.
- The lake had a single loon pair, so results may not apply to lakes with more predators.
