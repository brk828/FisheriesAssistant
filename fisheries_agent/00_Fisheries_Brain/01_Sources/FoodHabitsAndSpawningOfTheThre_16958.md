# Food Habits and Spawning of the Threadfin Shad in a Small Desert Impoundment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #dorosoma

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by Gerdes and McConnell, published in the *Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science*, examines the diet and spawning of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in [[Pena Blanca Lake]], a small fishing impoundment in southern Arizona. The authors had two aims: to measure the ratio of plant to animal matter in the diet as an index of trophic efficiency, and to assess possible dietary overlap with young [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Channel Catfish]] stocked in the lake.

Foreguts were examined from collections made in 1959–60. Amorphous organic debris and animal matter (mainly crustaceans) dominated the diet, while fresh plant material was minor. Spawning evidence from ovary volumes, egg surveys, and direct observation indicates a spawning peak in late spring to early summer, with no spawning recorded after early August.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*), widely introduced into large multipurpose reservoirs in California and Arizona as forage for warm-water game fish. Prior work by Kinsey and colleagues (1954, 1957, 1958) covered its introduction, and Haskell (1959) and R. V. Miller (1960) studied its food habits.
- **Study rationale:** The authors state this is the only reported effort to evaluate the threadfin shad's role in a small impoundment built solely for fishing.
- **Study lake:** Pena Blanca Lake was built under Federal Aid to Fisheries project FW-5-D and first filled in spring 1958. It was stocked with largemouth bass, black crappie, channel catfish, and threadfin shad.

**Methods (summary):**
- Collections were made with dip, cast, and seine nets (3/8 to 3/4 inch mesh). The text reports 10 collection days in 1959; Table 1 lists 11 dates from March 31 to January 23 (1959–60).
- Foreguts (esophagus and gizzard) from 10 fish of modal length were pooled per collection. Food items were counted on a modified haemocytometer at 100× magnification. Areas and counts were converted to volumes using thickness and volume factors (Table 2).
- Ovaries from four fish per collection (eight collections) were measured by water displacement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diet and trophic position.** Amorphous organic debris made up the largest share of the diet, and the authors suggest it was taken during bottom feeding and from plant beds disturbed by wave action. Animal matter was the most important living food. The authors compare their results with two other studies:

- [[Haskell 1959]] (Arizona reservoirs): organic debris constituted 40.6% of the organic volume ingested when sand is excluded.
- R. V. Miller (1960, Lake Chicot, Arkansas): animal and fresh plant material were taken in roughly equal proportions. Miller apparently excluded organic debris from his analysis.

The present study found animal matter considerably more important than fresh plant material. Miller's volume factors have a narrower range than those used here. When Miller's counts are expanded using the present factors, animal volume is about four times plant volume in the Lake Chicot diet.

**Competition with game fish.** Copepods and cladocerans can be important forage, so the authors consider possible competition with young game fish. Average standing crops of littoral and pelagial cladocerans occasionally exceeded 20 lb/acre (dry weight). The authors judge competition unlikely when crustaceans are abundant, but it could occur if shad selectively fed on crustaceans during periods of scarcity, when young game fish need them most.

**Winter feeding.** Both Haskell (1959) and Miller (1960) found little food in winter stomachs. At Pena Blanca Lake, foreguts from January 1960 contained only a few algal fragments, found after extensive searching.

**Spawning environment.** Eggs adhered to submerged vegetation such as *Myriophyllum*-type beds (given in the text as *Amacharis*) and filamentous algal mats near shore.

## Key Findings

### Food habits

- **Amorphous and unidentified material** dominated the diet: about 51% of relative numbers and 50–51% of relative volume (Table 3), and it occurred in all 10 pooled samples.
- **Animal matter** was about 48–49% of relative volume and about 6% of relative numbers. Copepods were the largest animal component (~31–32% of volume, present in 4 of 10 samples), followed by cladocerans (~9% of volume) and ostracods (~6–7%). Rotifers contributed ~1% of volume.
- **Fresh plant material** was minor, about 1% of volume, though it occurred in 9 of 10 samples.
- Low amorphous material in seston on shad collection days supports the bottom-feeding hypothesis. Shad also fed on suspended seston and decomposed aufwuchs stirred from *Myriophyllum*-type beds (*Amacharis*) by agitation, with the mouth open for 2–3 seconds in the centre of food clouds.
- The authors could not determine how much organic debris was digested and assimilated.

### Spawning

- Ovary volumes (mean of four fish):

| June 24 | 0.40 ml | Last sampling of 1958–59 parental stock |
| November 1 | 0.30 ml | Fish-of-the-year, mode 125 mm |
| January 23 | 0.30 ml | |

- Ovaries from the March 31 collection could not be measured by this method.
- The spawning peak was placed between May 26 and June 24, 1959.
- In 1961, the first eggs appeared on May 18 (surface water 68 °F). Fertile eggs were very abundant on June 17 (79 °F). Eggs declined through July and were scarce by August 1.
- Spawning was observed on at least three dates, always at sunrise or shortly after. Schools rushed along plant-bed margins, turned shoreward, and spawned for 5–10 seconds on the vegetation.
- No spawning was recorded after early August. Fall turnover had cooled the water to about 60 °F by November, although fish-of-the-year appeared mature.
- Kinsey et al. (1957) reported both spring and fall spawning within the same year. Brood pond observations suggested spring progeny may mature and spawn within five months.

### Laboratory rearing

- Eggs hatched in the laboratory in 1960 and 1961, but all fry died within about a week in containers up to 29 gallons.
- Fry survived 30 days in a 600-gallon concrete tank, but none exceeded 15 mm total length before the experiment ended.
- Egg and larval size and appearance closely resembled those reported for [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) by R. R. Miller (1960).
- Larvae stalked cladocerans and struck from an S-shaped posture, unlike captive adults.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- Animals were more important than fresh plant material in the Pena Blanca diet, which differs from Miller's Arkansas findings.
- Whether threadfin shad can grow on a plant-only diet remains untested. The authors recommend comparative growth trials of captive shad on vegetable and animal diets.
- Competition with young game fish is possible but unlikely under the observed crustacean abundance. Assessing it would require accounting for littoral semi-planktonic forms, which matter in shallow lakes where littoral area equals or exceeds pelagial area.
- The authors note a date discrepancy: the text gives January 24, 1960 for a winter collection, while Table 1 lists January 23.
