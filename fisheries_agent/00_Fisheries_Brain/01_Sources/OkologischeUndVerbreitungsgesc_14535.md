# Ecological and Biogeographical Studies on Limnic Groundwater Fauna from the Artificial Mesopsammal of the Helgoland Düne

#marine-biology #groundwater-fauna #parastenocarididae

## Executive Summary

This German-language paper by [[Siegfried Husmann]] (Hydrobiologische Anstalt der Max-Planck-Gesellschaft, Schlitz, 1962) examines interstitial animals living in artificially deposited sand on the [[Helgoland Düne]], a small islet beside [[Helgoland]] in the [[North Sea]]. The sand was dredged from the Loreley Bank and piled up in 1938 and 1943 to rebuild the island. Sampling in late July 1957 found that the sand pits were partly dry, yet they held a dense, living community of nematodes, oligochaetes and harpacticoid copepods. The copepods were groundwater-dwelling [[Parastenocaris]] species, which are normally found in cool, moist groundwater.

The author argues that the community reflects a freshwater groundwater lens beneath the islet, with oligohaline (low-salinity) tolerance, and that the Parastenocaris populations are archaic relict forms whose distribution was shaped by repeated North Sea transgressions and regressions over geological time.

## Background & Description

The study sits within [[Groundwater Biology]] and [[Interstitial Fauna]] research. Husmann notes that dredged sands can be colonized by natural groundwater fauna when groundwater moves from native aquifers into the pore systems of deposited material. He cites earlier work on beach-nourishment sand on the Weser at Bremen-Blumenthal and on filter sands in Ruhr-valley groundwater-recharge plants.

Study ponds (Teiche A, B and C) formed in sand pits dug into the reconstructed island surface. Bomb craters from 1945 and construction work created these pits, which filled when groundwater was high and dried in dry periods. Chloride measurements on 29 July 1957 gave 160 mg/l Cl for Teich B and 470 mg/l Cl for Teich C. Teich C's shore profile showed a dry, shell-rich coarse sand, a faulty mud layer at about 30 cm depth, and black, putrid sludge below. The dredged sediment itself came from the Loreley Bank, 4 km east of the Düne.

Methods followed the approach of [[Thienemann Sieve Method]]: a litre of coarse sand was washed, and the suspended residue was sieved. Samples were fixed with the Angelier mixture. Oligochaetes could not be reliably identified in fixed material and were excluded from the species analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Faunal composition.** The fauna comprised 14 nematode species and 2 harpacticoid copepod species. Two taxa were described as new to science, and the paper marks these in its species list. The copepods were:

- [[Parastenocaris Phyllura]] (Kiefer 1938)
- [[Parastenocaris Vicesima]] (Kiefer 1935)

Nematodes included [[Plectus Cirratus]], [[Mononchus Macrostoma]], [[Dorylaimus Obtusicaudatus]], [[Dorylaimus Balticus]] (a marine species) and [[Sporonchulus Schulzi]]. The fauna is described as interstitial and largely ponds-and-sand specific rather than purely groundwater.

**Abundance.** Ranked by mean relative abundance across all sites, the top species were Parastenocaris phyllura, Plectus cirratus, Parastenocaris vicesima and Mononchus macrostoma. Only about eight species stood out in density, and the author takes this as a sign of extreme, unusual site conditions. In one 1000 cc sample from Teich C, Parastenocaris phyllura numbered 385 and Parastenocaris vicesima 43.

**Desiccation and microclimate.** Although the sand was dry, the copepods were abundant and very active. Husmann proposes that moist air in sand pore spaces supplies oxygen much as cold groundwater does, because cool water holds more dissolved oxygen. Interstitial groundwater species can therefore survive drying to a certain degree. A separate note indicates that a planned study of tolerance limits under progressive drying had not yet been carried out.

**Salinity gradient.** The lack of marine fauna at Teiche B and C shows that a freshwater lens under the islet controls the pore-water chemistry. The marine nematode Dorylaimus balticus appeared at Teich A, closer to the North Sea shore, and Parastenocaris did not occur there. The author reads this as a salinity gradient along the coastal groundwater. Reported salinity tolerances were:

- Parastenocaris vicesima: 8–10‰ (Noodt 1956)
- Parastenocaris phyllura: 5–6‰ (Remane 1950)
- Parastenocaris fontinalis: about 4‰ (Noodt, personal communication)

**Relict status.** The author places Parastenocaris in a "Parastenocaris–Bathynella biocoenosis" with [[Bathynella Relationship]] taxa such as [[Phyllognathopus]], [[Epactophanes]], [[Nitocrella]] and [[Elaphoidella]]. These groups are blind, have limited dispersal ability, and are typical of archaic groundwater habitats. Their survival in extreme conditions is seen as evidence of ancient origin, and they are called "living fossils" and "phylogenetically conservative" forms. Parastenocaris does not survive complete desiccation, which rules out passive dispersal.

**Biogeographic history.** The paper traces the North Sea region's history of alternating land and sea since the Zechstein, including Tertiary marine phases and Pleistocene glaciations. Helgoland's last land phase ended with the Corbula transgression (about 5500 BCE), after a land period of more than 100,000 years in which fauna could recolonize from the continent. Parastenocaris is said to have spread widely across formerly glaciated areas, reaching southern Sweden and Finland. Helgoland's Parastenocaris are therefore treated as "geomorphological relicts" left behind when the sea flooded the last North Sea land. The paper also cites [[Niphargus Aquilex]] from fissure water on Helgoland as further evidence of groundwater spread.

## Key Findings

1. **Artificial sand supports natural groundwater fauna.** Dredged marine sand on the Düne was colonized by limnic interstitial organisms, showing that human-made deposits can carry groundwater communities from native aquifers.

2. **Desiccated sand still hosts active groundwater animals.** Parastenocaris were abundant and vigorous in dry sand, suggesting that humid pore air can act as an alternative to cold groundwater for oxygen supply.

3. **Community reflects a freshwater lens.** The absence of marine fauna at Teiche B and C, combined with oligohaline freshwater and moist-soil taxa, indicates that a subterranean freshwater lens dominates pore-water chemistry at the study sites.

4. **Salinity gradient toward the shore.** Marine taxa (Dorylaimus balticus) appear at Teich A, nearer the North Sea, while limnic Parastenocaris are absent there. This marks the edge of the freshwater groundwater lens.

5. **Two new species and a dominant copepod community.** Fourteen nematode species and two copepod species were identified, including two taxa new to science. Parastenocaris phyllura and P. vicesima were the most abundant copepods.

6. **Parastenocaris are archaic relicts.** The author argues that Parastenocaris and related Bathynella-group taxa persist from ancient times in the mesopsammal, and that Helgoland populations are geomorphological relicts of a wider distribution now reduced by marine transgression.

7. **Biogeographic model of recolonization.** Repeated transgressions and regressions during the Tertiary and Pleistocene, followed by the Weichsel glaciation and a land period of over 100,000 years, shaped the present distribution of North Sea interstitial fauna.

8. **Need for further study.** The author notes that comparative research on tolerance limits to progressive desiccation in groundwater-fed and drying sand systems was still planned.
