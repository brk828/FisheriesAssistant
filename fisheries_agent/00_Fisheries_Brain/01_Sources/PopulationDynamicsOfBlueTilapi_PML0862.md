# Population Dynamics of Blue Tilapia in Trinidad Lake, Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis aureus*, cited as *Tilapia aurea*) in [[Trinidad Lake]], a 303 ha cooling reservoir for a steam electric generating station in Texas. Monthly [[Gillnet Sampling]] ran from January 1973 to December 1975, and a [[Petersen Mark-Recapture]] estimate was made for December 1974. Fish were aged by [[Scale Aging]]. The authors estimated a population of 4,856,963 tilapia and a standing biomass of 799,978.2 kg (2,640.2 kg/ha) in December 1974. Annual survival from Age I to Age II was about 7 percent. Gillnet catches fell over the three years, and a cold-weather die-off in December 1975 to January 1976 eliminated the population, with no survivors found in 1976 or 1977. The authors estimated a potential harvest of 1,629,501 marketable fish (521,153 kg) and proposed weir harvesting in the discharge canal.

## Background & Description

Blue tilapia were introduced to the United States for vegetation control, fish culture and recreation. They became established in heated waters, where [[Thermal Pollution]] from power plants allows sub-tropical fish to survive winters that would otherwise be lethal. Rapid expansion had been documented in Florida lakes, including Lake Parker, where tilapia invaded in 1966 and reached dominance within six years. Few population dynamics data existed for U.S. waters at the time. Trinidad Lake tilapia were first noticed in the late 1960s and became established rapidly.

The study aimed to determine:
- Population size
- Growth rates
- Condition
- Mortality rates
- Potential yield for sport and commercial harvest

The work was supported by Texas A&M University (Project S-1896).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Trinidad Lake comprises three parts:
- Main body: 287 ha, average depth about 25 m
- Intake canal: about 6 ha, average depth 4 m
- Discharge canal: about 60 ha, average depth about 1 m

The lake is eutrophic, with a year-round phytoplankton bloom. Reported conditions (from [[Gleastine 1974]]) include:
- Phosphate of 0.5 mg/l
- pH usually above 9.0
- Alkalinity of about 200 ppm
- Dissolved oxygen near saturation from surface to bottom in most of the lake

**Sampling design.** Experimental multifilament gillnets were set monthly at 10 sites for two consecutive one-day periods. Five nets were 45.7 m long and 2.4 m deep, with square mesh from 25 to 89 mm. Five were 36.6 m long, with mesh from 63 to 102 mm.

**Temperature.** Blue tilapia are semi-tropical and need water above 10 °C to survive. Earlier studies reported lower thermal limits: [[McBay 1961]] found small fish did not tolerate 9 °C, while larger fish survived short periods at 3.5 °C. Other reported limits were 8.0 to 8.5 °C (Sarig 1970) and 12 °C (Avault et al. 1968). In Trinidad Lake, overwinter mortality occurred each year when discharge canal temperatures reached 10 °C. In December 1975 and January 1976, temperatures ranged from 6 to 9 °C, causing a massive die-off.

## Key Findings

### Catches and seasonality
- Gillnet catches of blue tilapia were low in winter, when fish moved out of the main body into the warmer discharge canal.
- Catches rose in March and April, when fish returned to the lake.
- Total annual gillnet catches declined over the study: 923 (1973), 586 (1974) and 362 (1975).
- The authors suggest the population may have exceeded carrying capacity early on, then moved toward a lower standing crop, possibly with stunting of young fish.

### Age and growth
- Scales from 918 fish were aged: 566 males and 347 females.
- Males ranged from 183 to 517 mm TL, aged 0 to III.
- Females ranged from 132 to 304 mm TL, aged 0 to II.
- Annuli formed mainly in March and April (1973, 1974) and April and May (1975).
- Length-frequency distributions corresponded closely to scale ages, supporting the validity of the method.
- Only 3 Age III fish were captured in three years.
- Back-calculated mean total lengths:
  - Males at annuli I, II and III: 197, 258 and 299 mm
  - Females at annuli I and II: 183 and 248 mm
- Males were significantly larger than females at each annulus.
- Lengths were lower than those reported by Gleastine (1974) for 1971–72. Gleastine's males reached 242, 318 and 346 mm at Ages I to III, and females 223 and 307 mm at Ages I and II.
- Mean growth increments:
  - Males: 197, 49 and 37 mm (Age III based on very few fish)
  - Females: 183 and 48 mm

### Condition
- Length-weight relationships were:
  - Males: log W = −4.8614 + 3.0501 log L
  - Females: log W = −4.9887 + 3.1061 log L
- Slopes were nearly 3.0 and did not differ significantly between sexes (F = 3.04), indicating little change in plumpness with length.
- Elevations did not differ significantly (F = 3.14), indicating no overall condition difference between sexes.

### Population and biomass (December 1974)
- Marking: 30,104 tilapia were marked by clipping the right pelvic fin (19 October to 17 November 1974).
- Recapture: 99,930 fish were captured in January to March 1975, of which 583 were marked recaptures.
- Age 0 fish below 160 mm: 1,378,517. Age 0 fish above 160 mm: about 1,742,298 (the table gives 1,742,293). Combined Age 0: 3,120,810.
- Age I: 1,591,823. Age II: 144,330.
- Total population: 4,856,963.
- Total biomass: 799,978.2 kg (2,640.2 kg/ha).
- Biomass by group:

| Group | Biomass (kg) | kg/ha |
|---|---|---|
| Age 0, 50–150 mm | 19,905.8 | 65.7 |
| Age 0, 160–219 mm | 226,323.9 | 746.9 |
| Age I, 200–299 mm | 486,507.8 | 1,605.6 |
| Age II, >270 mm | 67,240.7 | 221.9 |

### Survival
- Age I to II survival from the population estimate was 9 percent. Because recruitment varied, this was adjusted downward by 25 percent to an estimate of **7 percent**.
- Gillnet-based estimates (assuming equal vulnerability) were 15 percent for the 1972 year class and 25 percent for the 1973 year class. The authors attribute the high values to increased vulnerability of older fish to the nets.
- Age 0 to Age I survival was estimated at 51 percent, but this is subject to variation in recruitment.

### Mortality event
- Water temperatures of 6 to 9 °C in December 1975 to January 1976 caused a massive die-off.
- Seining, gillnetting and electrofishing in 1976 and 1977 found no survivors or progeny.

### Potential yield and maturity
- Fish reach marketable size (about 182 g) at roughly 220 mm TL.
- An estimated 1,629,501 fish weighing 521,153 kg were of marketable size.
- Most of these could be harvested with weirs at the discharge canal, where fish gather each winter.
- Age 0 females did not mature until October, after water had cooled below spawning temperatures. The authors concluded that spawning was by Age I or older fish.
- Of females caught in 1974–75, 85 percent were mature overall (148 of 175). Among 170–219 mm females, 75 percent were mature.
- The remaining adult stock of 1,965,538 fish (170–219 mm) was judged sufficient to sustain the population through multiple spawning. The authors also predicted that harvesting adults would improve young-fish growth and survival and lead to earlier female maturity.

### Management implications
- Harvest of the overwintering population in the discharge canal could be feasible, but the population's dependence on heated water makes it vulnerable to cold-weather die-offs.
- The authors describe the population as having declined by 1975, and the subsequent total loss in 1976–77 indicates that the population did not recover.
