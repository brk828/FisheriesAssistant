# Fish and Grazing Relationships in Southwestern National Forests

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes Chapter 8 of *Livestock Management in the American Southwest: Ecology, Society, and Economics* (Elsevier, 2000, edited by Roy Jemison and Carol Raish), authored by John N. Rinne of the USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Research Station. The chapter reviews what is known about the effects of [[Livestock Grazing]] on fishes and their [[Riparian Areas]] in the southwestern United States, with emphasis on [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]]. Rinne concludes that the relationship between grazing and fish is poorly demonstrated: most published work is non-peer-reviewed, salmonid-focused, and affected by weak study designs, while habitat, [[Nonnative Fish Introductions]], [[Flooding and Drought]] and [[Geology and Geomorphology]] can equal or exceed any grazing effect. The chapter calls for linked, replicated, peer-reviewed, cooperative research and monitoring.

The back matter of the volume (Front matter, copyright, contents, and the concluding chapter by Jemison and Raish) was also present in the source. The publisher and copyright boilerplate has been omitted from this note.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Livestock grazing has existed on southwestern landscapes for centuries. The [[Organic Act]] (1897), [[Taylor Grazing Act]] (1930), [[Multiple Use and Sustained Yield Act]] (1960), [[National Forest Management Act]] (1976) and [[National Forest Renewable Resources Research Act]] (1978) progressively addressed management and research on public lands, including grazing and fisheries.
- **Native fauna:** Fewer than 60 fish species are native to the southwestern United States, a low diversity compared with the eastern US. Most native species are federal- or state-listed.
- **Nonnative fauna:** Over 180 nonnative fish species have been introduced to Arizona waters, with nearly 50 becoming established (Figure 2).
- **Threatened species:** The [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) are listed; seven federally listed species were reviewed in Region 3 in 1997.
- **Scope:** The chapter addresses the state of knowledge, factors that complicate grazing–fish studies, research gaps, and monitoring needs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian and habitat linkage.** Water quantity and quality, in time and space, determine where fishes can survive. Riparian areas act as indicators of watershed condition. Grazing affects riparian vegetation, stream channel, streambanks and undercut banks, which in turn affect fish cover, shade and water temperature. Surface water was ranked the primary limiting factor for southwestern native fishes, with nonnative fishes second (Rinne 1994).

**Wider Region.** Elk, a wild ungulate, also affects riparian-stream areas and must be considered in any grazing evaluation. Lower-elevation desert streams are used by cypriniform fishes (minnows and suckers) whose responses may differ from salmonids.

**Land management responsibility.** The [[USDA Forest Service]] manages habitat, while state agencies such as the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] manage fish species, creating a need for interagency coordination.

## Key Findings

**1. The grazing–fish evidence base is weak.**
- Platts (1991) concluded grazing has an adverse impact on fishes and habitat in the west and southwest, but controversy remains because valid evaluations of grazing strategies for fishery productivity are lacking.
- Of 21 studies on grazing and riparian areas reviewed by Platts (1991), all suggested negative impacts to riparian habitat, but only 15 of the same 21 reported a negative effect on fishes (Platts 1992 as cited).
- In Rinne's review of about 200 references on riparian, fishes and grazing, roughly two-thirds addressed riparian components, and only a third included fish. Of 66 fish-related references, 19 were salmonid only, 13 non-salmonid, and 8 general fish opinion or review papers. 4 were data-based studies in the southwest.
- Of the 29 studies analysed in detail, 17 (59%) addressed only salmonids, one addressed only non-salmonids, and nine considered both.
- About 83% of the fish–grazing literature appeared in non-peer-reviewed publications.

**2. Study design flaws.**
- No study achieved the ideal paired-watershed design with pretreatment data.
- Replication in space was present in 24 of 29 studies (83%); temporal replication was present in about half (13 of 29), and only a second year of sampling was typically obtained.
- Quantitative data were available for only a minority: 6 studies contained only quantified data, 3 only qualitative, and 19 (70%) a combination.
- Only 10 of 29 studies contained statistical analysis.
- Platts (1978) recommended at least three years of replication to capture variability.

**3. Confounding factors (Rio de las Vacas, West Fork and Verde River case studies).**
- **Species:** Results differ depending on whether suckers, chubs, trout or total fish are examined. In the Rio de las Vacas, trout were greater in ungrazed reaches, while Rio Grande sucker and chub were greater in grazed reaches (Table 4). Inverse trout–cypriniform relationships are attributed partly to predation.
- **Temporal variation:** Multiple years of data show that single-year results can mislead, and that "snapshot" sampling is inadequate for policy.
- **Spatial variation:** Grazed and ungrazed reaches were often separated by over 4 km, with different habitat types.
- **Fishery management:** Stocking of trout (Rio de las Vacas, 1982–83) and of rainbow trout (Otter Creek, Nebraska) confounded grazing conclusions. Sport angling may affect fish density and population estimates in low-density native trout populations.
- **Nonnative predators:** In the Verde River, nonnative species, many predatory, dominated E-type channels with undercut banks; laboratory predation experiments showed smallmouth bass reduced red shiner prey by 40–100% in 24 hours (mean loss 80%) and yellow bullhead reduced prey by about 21% in 24 hours (mean loss) across 12 replicates.
- **Natural factors:** Geology, drought and flooding can overwhelm grazing effects.

**4. Geology, drought and flooding.**
- In the White Mountains, fine sediment content was nearly identical on Apache National Forest and Fort Apache Reservation streams (22.4% vs 22.6%), yet optimum Apache trout spawning substrate (4–32 mm) differed markedly, and was significantly correlated with fish density (P = 0.000) and biomass (P = 0.02). Reservation geology (alluvium and glaciated valley bottoms, with Pleistocene glaciation) favours spawning gravels over basaltic National Forest lands.
- On West Fork streams, drying in meadow reaches over 3 of 4 years, within a total ungulate exclosure, lowered fish populations relative to upstream permanent-water reaches.
- Native fish populations dominated nonnative ones in upper Verde River reaches following flood events (1994 and 1996 cited).

**5. Vegetation and habitat.**
- Vegetated reaches on the West Fork contained 10 to 100 times the fish numbers of non-vegetated reaches.
- Platts (1991) reported that riparian vegetation improves quickly after fencing, while stream morphology improves slowly and fish populations may or may not improve.
- Streambank vegetation and undercut banks provide cover for fish; shade moderates water temperature (Brown and Krygier 1970, Beschta 1997).

**6. Regional management priorities.**
- In a 1997 Region 3 evaluation, 50 of 74 projects (68%) with potential direct effects on four cypriniform fishes involved grazing allotments. This was based only on the presence of animals.
- The 1997 evaluation covered only four threatened fishes, whereas almost 12 times that many occur on National Forest lands, so impacts on native fishes may be greatly underestimated.
- Over three dozen native and nonnative fish species are monitored at some level in Region 3.

**7. Recommendations.**
- Link grazing effects on vegetation and streambanks to fish habitat and then to fish populations.
- Use replicated, quantitative, statistically analysed, peer-reviewed data collected over at least three years.
- Recognise and control confounding factors: species, stocking, angling, predators, hydrology and geology.
- Expand research on non-salmonid and lower-elevation species, which are poorly studied.
- Pursue cooperative, adaptive management, such as the 6-year West Fork Ungulate Study and the Upper Verde River Adaptive Management program, which involve the [[Forest Service]], permittees, the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]], AGFD and private interests.
- Combine research and monitoring, as demonstrated by collection of nearly 17,000 fish over five years in the upper Verde River.

**8. Concluding perspective (Jemison and Raish).** The volume calls for rigorous, collaborative, long-term studies, and for research on exotic species, threatened and endangered species, urban growth and ranch economics. Rinne's chapter is consistent with this, stating that the goal is not to show grazing has no effect but to avoid simplistic conclusions about a complex, ecosystemic relationship.
