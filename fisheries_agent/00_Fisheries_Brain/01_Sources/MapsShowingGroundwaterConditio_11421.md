# Depth to Water and Altitude of the Water Level, Quality of Water, and Hydrographs of Selected Wells in San Bernardino Valley Basin

#hydrogeology #groundwater #aquatic-habitat #endangered-fish

## Executive Summary

This hydrologic report documents groundwater conditions in the [[San Bernardino Valley]] basin, located in southeastern [[Arizona]] (Cochise County) and southwestern [[New Mexico]] (Hidalgo County), encompassing 387 square miles in Arizona. The basin contains the [[San Bernardino Ranch]] area and a 2,309-acre [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Refuge]] established for endangered fish species. The study characterizes depth to water, water-level altitudes, water quality, and hydrographs from selected wells measured during 1990-1992. [[Black Draw]], the primary stream, is ephemeral except near the refuge where springs and unregulated flowing wells create perennial aquatic habitat supporting a lush grassy oasis with cottonwoods, willows, small lakes, and springs. Groundwater occurs primarily in basin-fill sediments and interbedded basalt flows, with depths to water ranging from 24 to 398 feet below land surface. Water quality is generally suitable for domestic and stock purposes, though six samples exceeded secondary maximum contaminant levels for dissolved solids.

## Background & Description

The [[San Bernardino Valley]] basin is an elongated, gently-sloping valley situated between parallel mountain ranges: the [[Peloncillo Mountains]] to the east, the [[Perilla Mountains]] and [[Pedregosa Mountains]] to the west, and the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] to the north. The basin extends 21 miles from the southern end of the [[San Simon Valley]] sub-basin to the United States-Mexico international border, with an average topographic gradient of 49 feet per mile sloping southward. Land-surface altitudes range from 3,715 feet on the valley floor at the international border to 6,410 feet in the Pedregosa Mountains.

The basin was formed during extensional tectonics and vertical faulting from approximately 18 million years ago to present. High-angle normal faults along basin margins produced the north-south trending basin structure. Surrounding mountains consist primarily of Cretaceous and Tertiary andesitic and rhyolitic volcanics, with outcrops of Cretaceous and Paleozoic sedimentary rocks in the Perilla Mountains, southern Chiricahua Mountains, northern Pedregosa Mountains, and southeastern valley floor.

Over half the basin floor is covered by basalt flows from multiple volcanic episodes, with radiometric dates ranging from 3.3 million to 274,000 years old. Well logs reveal layering of at least four basalt flows separated by basin-fill sediments of sand and clay. Total basalt thickness decreases southward, from multiple flows in the north to an 8-foot interval in a 100-foot deep well (sec. 16, T.28S., R.30E.) to no basalt in wells 2 miles east and near the southern border.

The [[San Bernardino Ranch]] area, historically known as [[Slaughter Ranch]], consists of 300 acres with a museum. This lush grassy oasis features cottonwoods, willows, small lakes, and several springs, making it an important stopover since the late 1600s. The area was part of the 73,240-acre San Bernardino Land Grant awarded to Ignacio Perez in 1822 by the Mexican government. Adjacent to the ranch is a 2,309-acre [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Refuge]] established for endangered fish species.

The semiarid climate features mild winters and hot summers. Average daily maximum temperatures during June range from 95°F at [[Douglas]] to 91°F at [[Apache]]. Average daily minimum temperatures during December range from 26°F to 26°F. Average annual precipitation ranges from 13 inches (sec. 18, T.23S., R.31E.) to 18 inches at Apache. Nearly half the annual rainfall occurs during July and August from intense, short-duration thunderstorms.

Vegetation includes palo verde, mesquite, manzanita, creosote, bottlebrush, ocotillo, and warm season grasses such as grama. Pinyon, juniper, and oak trees grow in higher elevations of surrounding mountains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cattle ranching has been the primary industry since 1822. Approximately 300 acres was cultivated and irrigated in the early 1950s. In 1991, 100 to 200 acres just south of the international border was observed under irrigation from wells. The [[U.S. Geological Survey]] estimates 100 acre-feet of groundwater per year is pumped from the Arizona portion, all used for domestic and stock purposes.

[[Black Draw]], the primary stream, flows southward into Mexico where it becomes the [[Rio San Bernardino]]. The draw is ephemeral over its entire length except in the area of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Refuge where it is perennial due to springs and unregulated flowing wells. Tributaries include [[Mulberry Draw]], [[Silver Creek]], and [[Cottonwood Draw]].

Groundwater occurs in basin-fill sediments (semi-consolidated to poorly consolidated sand, silt, clay, and gravel) and interbedded basalt flows (lava flows, pyroclastic deposits, and some intercalated gravels). Each lava flow interbedded within basin fill has potential to act locally as an aquifer or confining layer depending on fracturing extent. These flows are likely hydraulically connected to some degree, creating a single aquifer system effect.

Groundwater also occurs in surrounding mountains within zones of fractured or weathered volcanics, isolated blocks of sedimentary rocks, and thin alluvial deposits overlying bedrock. Elevation differences suggest these zones are not hydraulically connected to the basin aquifer.

Infiltration of mountain-front runoff is the major groundwater recharge source. An unknown amount of inflow occurs from the San Simon Valley sub-basin to the north. Water-level altitude contours suggest groundwater flows from mountain fronts and the San Simon Valley sub-basin toward the basin center, then flows south.

Continued irrigation of farmland south of the border may result in lowering of the water surface in wells north of the border, potentially affecting the aquatic habitat supporting endangered fish species in the refuge.

## Key Findings

**Water Levels and Depths:**
- Depths to water measured in 1990-1991 ranged from 398 feet below land surface at the northern end to 24 feet below land surface in the southern end
- Depths to water in surrounding mountains ranged from 293 feet to 8 feet below land surface
- Flowing wells are present in the [[San Bernardino Ranch]] area and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Refuge near the international border
- Hydraulic head measured in February 1992 on four flowing wells ranged from 3 to 42 feet above land surface

**Water-Level Changes:**
- Water-level measurements from wells date to the early 1950s
- Hydrographs A through C (wells with more than three recorded water levels) show no trend in water levels over the past 40 years
- Hydrographs B and C show slight declines between 1968 and 1976 of 9 feet and 11 feet, respectively

**Water Quality:**
- Water samples from 31 wells were analyzed: 16 with detailed inorganic mineral and trace metal analyses, remainder analyzed for fluoride concentration and specific conductance only
- Fluoride concentrations ranged from 0.1 to 3.0 mg/L (maximum contaminant level = 4.0 mg/L)
- Specific-conductance values ranged from 300 to 1,800 microsiemens per centimeter at 25°C (µS/cm)
- Dissolved-solids concentrations approximated by multiplying specific conductance by 0.65
- Six water samples exceeded the secondary maximum contaminant level (SMCL) for dissolved solids (500 mg/L, approximately equivalent to 833 µS/cm)
- Two of six samples exceeding SMCL were from wells completed in Cretaceous sedimentary rocks of the Perilla Mountains
- Four samples exceeding SMCL were from wells in the southern basin portion near outcrops of Paleozoic sedimentary rocks (predominantly limestone)
- Concentrations of all other major ions analyzed were below maximum contaminant level (MCL) and SMCL levels
- Water temperatures ranged from 21°C to 32°C
- Groundwater is of suitable quality for domestic and stock purposes

**Hydrogeology:**
- Well log from 609-foot deep well (sec. 13, T.22S., R.30E.) revealed layering of at least four basalt flows separated by basin-fill sediments
- One well (sec. 2, T.24S., R.31E.) penetrated 5,679 feet of Paleozoic strata, starting in Permian limestone and bottoming in Cambrian quartzite
- A thin layer of alluvium covers basalt flows around southern and western basin margins
- Basin extends southward into Mexico, encompassing as much as 400 square miles within the Republic of Mexico
