# University of Michigan Museum of Zoology Fish Collection Records: Colorado River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #museum-collections #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Colorado-River-basin

## Executive Summary

This document comprises field collection records and museum catalog entries from the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] (UMMZ) spanning 1934-1978, documenting fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] basin in [[Coconino County]], [[Arizona]]. The records include handwritten field notes, collection data sheets, and computerized catalog summaries for thousands of specimens representing native and introduced species. Collections were made by prominent ichthyologists including [[Robert Rush Miller]], [[Carl Leland Hubbs]], and [[Gerald R. Smith]] at numerous localities throughout the [[Grand Canyon]] and tributary systems. The document emphasizes the unique endemic fish fauna of the Colorado River system and includes critical early records of rare species like [[Gila cypha]] (Humpback Chub).

## Background & Description

The collection records document systematic ichthyological surveys of the [[Colorado River]] drainage in Arizona, with particular focus on [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and its tributaries. Major collecting localities include the mainstem [[Colorado River]] at various river miles (RM) below [[Lee's Ferry]], and tributaries such as [[Little Colorado River]], [[Paria River]], [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Diamond Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], [[East Clear Creek]], and [[Oak Creek]].

The records span multiple decades of fieldwork, with significant collections made during 1942 (Hubbs), 1950-1975 (Miller), and 1968 (Grand Canyon expedition series GC68 and GRS68). Collection methods included seining (minnow seines, 6' nylon seines), electrofishing (60V), and gill netting. Specimens were preserved in alcohol (formalin initially, then ethanol) with some prepared as cleared-and-stained (C&S) or skeletal preparations.

The [[Catostomidae]] (suckers) are represented by three native species: [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (Flannelmouth Sucker), [[Catostomus discobolus]] (Bluehead Sucker), and [[Catostomus clarki]] (Desert Sucker). Native [[Cyprinidae]] include the endemic [[Gila cypha]] (Humpback Chub), [[Gila robusta]] (Roundtail Chub), [[Gila elegans]] (Bonytail), [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (Speckled Dace), [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (Little Colorado Spinedace), [[Lepidomeda mollispinis]] (Virgin Spinedace), [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] (Woundfin), and [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] (Colorado Pikeminnow). Introduced species documented include [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow), [[Notropis lutrensis]] (Red Shiner), [[Cyprinus carpio]] (Common Carp), [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (Channel Catfish), [[Ictalurus melas]] (Black Bullhead), [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (Green Sunfish), and [[Perca flavescens]] (Yellow Perch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River basin fish fauna represents one of the most distinctive ichthyofaunas in North America, with an entire endemic subfamily of minnows (Plagopterinae), 7 endemic genera, 41% generic endemism, and 76% species endemism—the highest for any major river system in North America. This exceptional endemism reflects long-term isolation and adaptation to harsh desert river conditions.

Habitat descriptions in the field notes document diverse aquatic environments: swift riffles, deep pools, backwaters, side canyons with plunge pools, and intermittent reaches. Substrate types included sand, gravel, cobble, bedrock, and mud. Water temperatures ranged from cold spring-fed tributaries to warm mainstem reaches. Several localities showed evidence of flash flooding and seasonal desiccation, particularly in tributary streams.

The records document ecological associations between species. [[Rhinichthys osculus]] was consistently the most abundant and widespread species, occurring in virtually all habitats from small headwater streams to the mainstem Colorado River. [[Catostomus discobolus]] was frequently collected in swift, rocky riffles, while [[Catostomus latipinnis]] occurred in both mainstem and larger tributary habitats. The rare [[Gila cypha]] was documented at several mainstem Colorado River localities, particularly near tributary confluences.

Introduced species were increasingly documented in later collections (1960s-1970s), indicating progressive invasion of non-native fishes. [[Notropis lutrensis]] and [[Pimephales promelas]] were found in backwaters and pools, often associated with fine substrates. Hybrid specimens were noted, including [[Catostomus discobolus]] × [[Catostomus latipinnis]] and [[Pimephales promelas]] × [[Notropis lutrensis]].

A handwritten note by R.R. Miller (page 10) provides critical historical context: in 1946 (Miller, Science 104:517-519), he described [[Gila cypha]] as the rarest fish in the Colorado River basin and urgently advocated for comprehensive ichthyological surveys of major western rivers, particularly the Colorado and Columbia. He emphasized the need for such surveys due to human impacts including pollution, habitat destruction, and introduction of exotic species. Despite the unique endemic fauna, his appeal went unheeded by the [[Smithsonian Institution]], which felt such research should be reserved for university scientists.

## Key Findings

**Species Diversity and Distribution**: The collections document 19 fish species from [[Coconino County]], Arizona, including 13 native species and 6 introduced species. [[Rhinichthys osculus]] was the most frequently collected species (appearing in 50+ collection events with thousands of specimens), followed by [[Catostomus discobolus]] and [[Catostomus latipinnis]].

**Rare and Endemic Species Documentation**: Critical voucher specimens of the federally endangered [[Gila cypha]] were collected at multiple localities including the mouth of [[Little Colorado River]] (RM 61.5), [[Vasey's Paradise]] (RM 32), and near [[Lava Cliff Rapids]] (RM 64.5). Specimens ranged from 43-211 mm SL. The endemic [[Lepidomeda vittata]] was documented from [[East Clear Creek]] with large series (up to 313 specimens in a single collection, UMMZ 181451).

**Meristic and Morphological Data**: Detailed counts of dorsal fin rays, lateral line scales, and other meristic characters were recorded for many specimens. For example, [[Catostomus latipinnis]] typically showed D. 11-13 (most commonly 12), while [[Catostomus discobolus]] showed D. 9-11 (most commonly 10-11). These data support taxonomic distinctions between species.

**Temporal Changes**: Comparison of collections from 1934-1942 versus 1960s-1970s reveals increasing presence of introduced species. Early collections (1930s-1940s) were dominated by native species, while later collections frequently included [[Notropis lutrensis]], [[Pimephales promelas]], and centrarchids.

**Hybridization**: Natural hybridization between [[Catostomus discobolus]] and [[Catostomus latipinnis]] was documented (UMMZ 179569), and artificial hybridization between introduced [[Pimephales promelas]] and [[Notropis lutrensis]] was noted (UMMZ 189220).

**Collection Effort**: The 1968 [[Grand Canyon]] expedition (GC68 and GRS68 series) represents the most intensive survey effort, with collections at river miles 32, 40-48, 52, 61.5, 64.5, 72.5, 87, 108.5, 122, 136, 143, 166.5, 198.5, 200.8, 202, 207, 208.6, and 217. This systematic survey documented species distributions throughout the canyon.

**Specimen Preservation**: Collections include 15,000+ alcohol-preserved specimens, 35+ skeletal preparations, and 10+ cleared-and-stained preparations housed at UMMZ. Large specimens were stored in tanks (Tank 14(5) series), while smaller specimens were preserved in jars and vials.

**Habitat Associations**: Field notes document specific microhabitat preferences: [[Notropis lutrensis]] "over sand especially in inlet," [[Rhinichthys osculus]] "on shallow sand and near rocks," and [[Catostomus discobolus]] "off riffles" when electroshocked.

**Conservation Context**: Miller's 1946 appeal for systematic surveys, documented in this collection, presciently identified threats to native aquatic biota from habitat alteration and exotic species introductions—threats that have since resulted in multiple endangered species listings for Colorado River fishes.
