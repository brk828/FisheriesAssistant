# California Department of Fish and Game Forage Fish Introduction Program for Warmwater Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Clupeidae #Cyprinidae #reservoir-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This October 1955 California Department of Fish and Game bulletin describes an active program to introduce four non-native forage fish species into California's warmwater reservoirs to support predatory gamefish populations. The program, led by Associate Fisheries Biologist J.B. Kimsey, addresses declining fishing quality in man-made reservoirs by introducing [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Plains Red Shiner]], and [[Golden Shiner]] as forage species. The document also reports the department's 1954-55 fiscal status and waterfowl hunting regulations.

## Background & Description

### Reservoir Fisheries Problem

California's expanding agricultural and industrial development has created numerous large dams and reservoirs at lower elevations. While these waters initially produce good fishing for [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Crappie]], and [[Catfish]] in the first 2-3 years after stocking, fishing quality typically declines over time. The primary cause is lack of suitable small forage fish for predatory gamefish.

### Forage Fish Requirements

The ideal forage fish must:
- Live and reproduce in lakes/reservoirs without requiring streams for spawning
- Not overpopulate and stunt (unlike [[Bluegill Sunfish]])
- Not grow too large too quickly (critical when young bass transition from insect/plankton diet to fish diet at approximately 3 inches length)
- Utilize the entire reservoir volume, not just shallow margins

### Major Limiting Factor

Under existing conditions, 90 percent of the average large reservoir functions as a desert for fish food production. Bass and panfish are restricted to shallow edge zones. Fluctuating water levels from power and irrigation withdrawals during summer and fall create mudflats and prevent development of productive weedy shoreline areas.

### Introduced Species Descriptions

**[[Threadfin Shad]]** (*Signalosa petenensis atchafalayae*):
- Flat, compressed, silvery fish with yellow-green tail and fins
- Seldom exceeds 6 inches in length
- Named for threadlike dorsal fin extension reaching nearly to tail
- Flown in from Tennessee in 1953
- Feeds on plankton throughout water column
- Spawns in open water
- Prolific but not to point of overpopulation
- Maximum length approximately 6 inches

**[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*):
- Dark olive above with brass tinges behind head and tan along sides
- Dusky crossbar in middle of dorsal fin
- Dark blotch on back just in front of dorsal fin
- Spawns on underside of flat objects
- Lays 200-500 eggs at a time
- Feeds on microscopic plants, small insects, and own larvae
- Maximum length 4 inches
- Imported from New Mexico in 1951 and 1953

**[[Plains Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*):
- Deep-bodied, robust minnow
- Dark back, silvery blue sides, red fins
- Seldom exceeds 4 inches length
- Prolific, spawns on sandy bottoms in shallow water
- Eggs hatch rapidly
- Tolerates muddy waters and warm temperatures
- Hardy, good bait fish
- Brought from Arizona in spring 1954

**[[Golden Shiner]]** (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*):
- Flat-bodied minnow with greenish back, golden sides, brass-colored belly
- Large scales with rich golden sheen in adults
- Popular Eastern bait and forage fish
- Prefers weedbeds
- Brought to Northern California in 1950 from San Diego County

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Reservoir Characteristics

[[Millerton Lake]] (Fresno County) exhibits typical fluctuating reservoir conditions with barren shorelines where water levels vary seasonally with power and irrigation needs. High water mark follows vegetation line.

### Stocking Locations

**[[Threadfin Shad]]**:
- [[San Vicente Reservoir]] (San Diego County) - experimental introduction, thriving
- [[Lake Havasu]] (Colorado River) - 46-mile-long reservoir backed by Parker Dam, part of tri-state fisheries management program
- [[Puddingstone Reservoir]] (Los Angeles County)
- Recently appeared in irrigation canals near Yuma, Arizona
- [[Salton Sea]] - apparently from Havasu plant

**[[Fathead Minnow]]**:
- [[Salt Springs Valley Reservoir]] (Calaveras County)
- Other waters (unspecified)

**[[Plains Red Shiner]]**:
- [[Colorado River]] - already established from Arizona stocking

**[[Golden Shiner]]**:
- [[Clear Lake]] (Lake County)
- [[Millerton Lake]] (Fresno County)
- Other waters (unspecified)

### Native Species Experiments

Experiments underway with native fishes of limited distribution:
- Freshwater smelt from lower [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]] transplanted to:
  - [[Don Pedro Reservoir]] (Tuolumne County)
  - [[Lake Clementine]] (Placer County)
  - [[Phoenix Lake]] (Marin County)
  - Success not yet determined

Freshwater shrimp from delta area introduced to [[Lake Clementine]] and [[Don Pedro Reservoir]] in 1954 to evaluate forage potential.

### Management Approach

All proposed introductions require [[Fish and Game Commission]] approval. New fish restricted to one or two waters from which they cannot readily spread until risk of becoming nuisance is eliminated. Concerns include:
- Fish with innocuous food habits in native range could become undesirable predators in California
- Risk of overpopulation without natural checks
- Potential to become serious nuisance

## Key Findings

### Forage Fish Program Status

The department's [[Warmwater Fisheries Project]] has transitioned from purely investigational phase to active program of controlled introductions with careful observation of results on native gamefish populations. Success will be measured directly in terms of better warmwater fishing throughout the state.

### Threadfin Shad Advantages

[[Threadfin Shad]] represents potentially transformative forage species because:
- Feeds on plankton, utilizing entire reservoir volume rather than just margins
- Puts whole reservoir into fish production instead of just margins
- Not affected by fluctuating water levels
- Spawns in open water
- Proven successful in large TVA reservoirs in native Tennessee
- Too delicate for bait use (preventing uncontrolled spread)

### Critical Bass Life Stage

Young bass face crucial transition point at approximately 3 inches length when changing from insect-and-plankton diet to diet of small fishes. Many existing forage species (sunfish, native minnows) grow too large too quickly, leaving adequate forage for adult bass but insufficient numbers of adults to utilize it.

### Waterfowl Regulations (1955)

- 72-day split season: October 15-November 18 and December 10-January 15
- Bag/possession limit: 7 ducks plus 3 additional sprig or widgeon (agricultural depredation control)
- Geese: 6 bird limit, no more than 3 dark species
- District 22 (Imperial County, eastern Riverside and San Bernardino Counties): only 1 Canada goose
- Coots: 25 birds
- Colorado River area: straight 80-day season (October 28-January 15) to coincide with Nevada and Arizona
- Shooting hours: one-half hour before sunrise to sunset (traditional noon opening eliminated)
- Maximum 1 hooded merganser and 1 wood duck in bag limit
- No Ross geese permitted
- Black sea brant season: December 10-February 10 (3 bird limit)
- Jacksnipe season: December 10-24 (8 bird limit)
- Federal duck stamp required for hunters 16 years and older

### Public Waterfowl Shooting Areas

14 public waterfowl shooting areas operated by department with advance registration system:
- Applications available at department offices and license agencies
- Permits selected by drawing
- Vacancies filled first come, first served at checking stations
- Shooting days: Wednesdays, Saturdays, Sundays, certain holidays, opening/closing days of split season
- $2 daily fee on all areas except [[Napa Marshes]] ($3 seasonal permit)
- New area: [[Mendota Waterfowl Management Area]] (Fresno County) open for first time
- Major function: attract birds away from agricultural crops
