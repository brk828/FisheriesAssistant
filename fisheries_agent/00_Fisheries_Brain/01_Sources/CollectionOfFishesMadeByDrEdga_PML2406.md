# Collection of fishes made by Dr. Edgar Mearns from rivers tributary to Gulf of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This systematic paper by [[John Otterbein Snyder]] (Stanford University) reports on a freshwater fish collection made by [[Edgar A. Mearns]], a major and surgeon of the United States Army who worked on the Mexican boundary survey between January 1892 and September 1894. The collection came from streams of the [[Colorado River]] system and from the Gulf of California drainages, including the [[Gila River]], [[Verde River]], [[Santa Cruz River]], [[Yaqui Basin]] tributaries and the Sonoyta and Altar rivers. Snyder reviews nomenclature and synonymy, compares meristic and proportional characters, revises the generic relationships of several sucker genera, and describes one new species, [[Notropis Mearnsi]]. The text also records field observations on color, diet and habitat.

## Background & Description

The Colorado drainage was described as particularly important because, although considerable work had been done on its fishes, many questions about their habits, relationships and distribution remained open. The author notes that the faunas of the Colorado, Altar and Yaqui systems were not yet well enough known to make firm statements about their relationships. He infers that the Colorado and Yaqui fishes were once connected through a channel that has since been cut off by the salt water of the Gulf of California. Two Colorado forms had reached the larger streams of southern California, where they are now represented by [[Notolepidomyzon Clarki]]-related populations (*Notolepidomyzon santa-anae*) and *Richardsonius orcutti*.

The collection was analysed species by species, with meristic counts (scales, fin rays, gill rakers), proportional measurements expressed as fractions of body length, cranial structure (especially the fontanelle), and fresh-colour notes taken by Mearns in the field.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fishes were collected across desert and montane streams. Notable localities include:

- **Verde River and Beaver Creek** near Fort Verde, Arizona.
- **Gila River** at Gila City, and **Colorado River** at Yuma.
- **Oak Creek**, a mountain stream in a canyon with walls more than 1,000 feet high, lined with Douglas spruce, willow, aspen and walnut. The canyon walls are limestone above and sandstone below.
- **Clear Creek**, about 20 miles above its confluence with the Verde.
- **San Bernardino River** near the international boundary, and **Sonoyta River**.
- **Santa Cruz River** near Tucson.

Field notes describe the fishes' feeding and behaviour. Suckers were caught in deep rocky holes by snaring or with a line of hooks dropped onto schools. The stomach of one [[Gila Robusta]] contained a frog, and [[Salmo Irideus]] stomachs held sand, pebbles, wasps, grasshoppers and grubs. The author records that the Colorado-system forms display flattened, depressed heads and dorsal humps in old age. Several species were noted as food fishes; Mearns reported excellent flesh in *Xyrauchen* and *Gila robusta*.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy and synonymy**
- *Catostomus gila* (Kirsch) is a synonym of [[Catostomus Insignis]]. Kirsch's material probably included three *C. insignis* and two *Notolepidomyzon clarki* specimens.
- [[Catostomus Sonorensis]] is provisionally identified from San Bernardino Creek. These suckers are more slender than the type and share scale and fin-ray counts with *C. insignis*. No comparable *C. insignis* were available.
- The genus *Pantosteus* contains two natural groups: forms with a thick cranium and closed frontal-parietal suture (renamed *Notolepidomyzon*), and forms with a thin cranium and open fontanelle (retained as *Pantosteus*). [[Catostomus]] differs in having a broad, rectangular fontanelle and lips without a horny sheath.
- Three names were previously used for Colorado *Pantosteus*. Snyder shows that *P. arizonae* and *P. clarki* are the same species, and that *P. delphinus* and *P. virescens* are regarded as synonymous. The Colorado system therefore holds only two species.
- In the Bonneville Basin, *Catostomus generosus* belongs in *Notolepidomyzon*, and *Minomus platyrhynchus* belongs in *Pantosteus*.
- The Lahontan species *Pantosteus lahontan* is closely related to *P. platyrhynchus*. The Rio Grande species *Catostomus plebeius* belongs in *Notolepidomyzon*.
- [[Richardsonius Gibbosus]] (*Gila gibbosa*) is synonymous with *Tigoma intermedia* and *Gila nigra*, based on examination of the types.

**Meristic and diagnostic data**
- *C. insignis* cotypes (4 specimens) have 54–58 scales in the lateral series, 11–12 rays in the dorsal fin, and 26–30 scales between occiput and dorsal fin. Mearns' specimens ranged from 54 to 62 lateral scales.
- *Notolepidomyzon clarki* from Beaver Creek have 62–70 lateral-line scales, and one specimen is 70 mm long with no fontanelle yet visible.
- Across *P. arizonae* and *C. clarki* cotypes, lateral scale counts ranged from 62 to 75.
- The type of [[Pantosteus Virescens]] (No. 16758, U.S.N.M.) measures 370 mm and is the largest *Pantosteus* the author had seen. Specimens from the Bonneville system reached 230–395 mm, the largest weighing 13 pounds.
- [[Xyrauchen Texanus]] has a high, narrow dorsal keel formed by an enlarged occipital crest and fused spines and interneurals. Its skull differs from *Catostomus* in fontanelle shape, so the author finds no close relationship between the two genera.

**Colorado system species**
- [[Gila Elegans]]: one 15-inch example, with a flattened head and nuchal hump. The narrow caudal peduncle, depressed head and dorsal hump are shared with other Colorado forms.
- [[Gila Robusta]]: large individuals develop an arched back and flattened, concave head. The author treats *G. seminuda* as synonymous. A 9.5-inch Verde specimen was described in life as "bony-tail" or "Verde trout."
- [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]]: recorded from the Colorado at Yuma and the Gila River.
- [[Richardsonius Purpureus]]: scale counts 55–60 in the lateral series, 15–16 above the lateral line, 8 dorsal and 8 anal rays. Recorded from the San Bernardino River.
- [[Agosia Chrysogaster]]: 10 specimens examined, with well-developed pharyngeal grinding teeth (4 per side). Breeding tubercles were present on males taken in January. Recorded from the Sonoyta River.
- [[Plagopterus Argentissimus]]: three specimens from the Gila River near Gila City, with rudimentary anterior dorsal scales and a papillose throat mass.
- [[Poecilia Occidentalis]]: pregnant females from the Santa Cruz River near Tucson, collected November 20, 1893. One 2.5-inch female carried 15 developing young.

**New species**
- [[Notropis Mearnsi]] is described as new. The type (No. 76163, U.S.N.M.) was collected from the San Bernardino River near boundary monument 77 by Mearns on October 6, 1893. Diagnostic features include a rounded, elongate body, large scales (40 in the lateral series), 4–5 teeth, a short alimentary canal, an almost complete lateral line, and no dark bars or spots. Five specimens were studied from the Yaqui Basin. The author suggests a relationship to a Rio Grande form but leaves the exact affinities unresolved.

**Introduced species**
- [[Salmo Irideus]]: a single 8.5-inch specimen from Oak Creek is considered a fish introduced from a California stream. Other Oak Creek trout, measuring up to 19.5 inches, were recorded. Scale rows in the lateral series were 140 in the type and 116–120 in the Clear Creek trout. The largest caught weighed slightly over 3 pounds. June was identified as the best fishing month.

**Future work**
- The author calls for further investigation of *Pantosteus platyrhynchus*, *P. jordani* and *P. columbianus*, and anticipates that undescribed forms may be found, particularly in the upper Columbia Basin, which may contain *Notolepidomyzon generosus*.
