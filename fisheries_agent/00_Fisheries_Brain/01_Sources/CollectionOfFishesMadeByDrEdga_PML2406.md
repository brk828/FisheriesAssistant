# Fishes of the Colorado River System and Adjacent Gulf of California Drainages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper reports on a collection of freshwater fishes made by [[Dr. Edgar A. Mearns]], US Army medical officer and naturalist, while in charge of the biological section of the Mexican Boundary Survey (1892–1894). The specimens came from the [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries, the [[Gila River]], [[Verde River]], [[Yaqui River]], [[Sonora River]] and several smaller streams, along with a few fish from the [[Gulf of California]] drainage. The author revises the taxonomy of several sucker genera, especially [[Catostomus]], [[Pantosteus]] and [[Notolepidomyzon]], using scale counts, fin-ray counts and skull structure. The paper also describes new or redescribed forms, most notably the [[Xyrauchen texanus]] group and the [[Gila]] minnows.

## Background & Description

Before this survey, the fishes of the region were poorly known, and several questions about their habits, relationships and distribution were still open. Mearns's collections were made from headwaters to the mouths of the main streams, and the paper reviews earlier descriptions by [[Kirsch]], [[Cope]], [[Girard]], [[Baird]] and others, many of which relied on very small or poorly localized samples.

Taxonomic work centers on:

- **Catostomidae (suckers):** [[Catostomus insignis]] and [[Catostomus sonoriensis]]; the [[Pantosteus]] and [[Notolepidomyzon]] complex, whose members differ in lip structure and the fontanelle (an opening in the skull roof).
- **Cyprinidae (minnows and chubs):** [[Gila elegans]], [[Gila robusta]], [[Gila seminuda]], [[Richardsonius]] species, [[Notropis mearnsi]] (described as new) and [[Plagopterus argentissimus]].
- **Salmonidae:** [[Salmo iridaeus]], a rainbow trout introduced from California and collected in Oak Creek.
- **Poeciliidae:** [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]], collected in the Santa Cruz River near Tucson.

Key morphological characters used throughout include lateral line scale counts, scale rows above and below the lateral line, predorsal scale counts, fin-ray counts, gill-raker numbers, and skull features such as the fontanelle and the presence or absence of a horny lip sheath.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper notes that the Colorado system fauna is closely linked to the geographic history of the region. The author argues that the distribution of the sucker genera follows the Bonneville Basin, where most of the genera are thought to have originated, and suggests that species such as Pantosteus platyrhynchus and Notolepidomyzon clarki may also occur in the Columbia and Lahontan systems. Several species are known only from restricted reaches; for example, Xyrauchen texanus is reported from the larger streams of the southern [[Colorado River]] basin but does not extend into the high mountain streams.

Habitat notes in the text include:

- Large sucker specimens (Xyrauchen) taken from deep holes among rocks, using a line of hooks.
- Gila robusta taken in the Verde and Gila rivers; the author records that it takes baited hooks and that its flesh is good eating when taken from cool water.
- Salmo iridaeus in Oak Creek, a cold canyon stream bordered by Douglas spruce, with stomach contents of small pebbles, wasps, grasshoppers and grubs.
- Plagopterus argentissimus, a spined minnow with rudimentary scales on the anterior dorsal body.

## Key Findings

- **Catostomus insignis:** Four specimens in the US National Museum were compared. Scale counts in the lateral series ranged from 54 to 58, with 11 or 12 rows between the lateral line and mid-back, 1 or 10 between the lateral line and the ventral base, and 11 or 12 dorsal rays. The author concludes that [[Catostomus insignis]] and [[Catostomus gilae]] are the same species, with *C. insignis* having priority.
- **Catostomus sonoriensis:** Specimens from San Bernardino Creek near the international boundary were identified as this species. They are slenderer than *C. insignis*, and their scale counts are similar.
- **Notolepidomyzon clarki and Pantosteus:** The genera are separated by lip structure (horny cutting edge in both, but a thick cranium and no fontanelle in Notolepidomyzon versus a thin cranium with a fontanelle in Pantosteus). The author recognizes Notolepidomyzon clarki from the Colorado system, Pantosteus platyrhynchus and Pantosteus jordani from the Colorado and Columbia systems, and says *P. argentissimus* (later P. delphinus) cannot be reliably separated from other forms with the available material.
- **Pantosteus platyrhynchus:** Scale counts were 62 to 75 in the lateral series, and specimens in the paper are described in detail with fontanelle variation by age.
- **Xyrauchen texanus:** The collection contains a large series showing a high, narrow dorsal hump formed by fused neural spines and interneurals. The skull and fin structure are closely related to *Catostomus* but distinct, and the species shows color variation.
- **Gila elegans, Gila robusta and Gila seminuda:** Described from the Gila and Colorado rivers, with notes on dorsal hump development, fontanelle shape and scalation differences between populations.
- **Richardsonius:** Specimens from the Rio Santa Cruz and Verde River, and a *Richardsonius gibbosus* type measured in the paper, with 40 lateral scales in some counts (see the measurement tables in the original).
- **Notropis mearnsi (new species):** Five specimens from the Yaqui Basin, named in honor of Mearns. The type is from the San Bernardino River near Mexican boundary monument 22, collected by Mearns on 6 October 1893. It has a deep, elongate body, large scales, 4–5 pharyngeal teeth and a almost complete lateral line.
- **Plagopterus argentissimus:** Three specimens from the Gila River near Gila City, with rudimentary scales on the anterior dorsal body and papillae on the throat.
- **Salmo iridaeus:** Specimens from Oak Creek with 116 to 120 lateral scales. The author records that this introduced rainbow trout lives in cold water and spawns in June.
- **Poeciliopsis occidentalis:** A specimen collected in the Santa Cruz River near Tucson in November, with embryos and eggs at various developmental stages.

[Overall, the paper concludes that several previously named species are synonyms, that the Colorado sucker fauna is less diverse than earlier authors assumed, and that the genera Notolepidomyzon and Pantosteus need further study across the Columbia and Lahontan systems.]
