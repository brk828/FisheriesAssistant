# Environmental Impacts of the Central Arizona Project: A Prospectus for Research

#environmental-impact #water-resources #reservoir-ecology #desert-ecology #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This 1971 prospectus from [[Arizona State University]]'s [[Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] outlines a comprehensive, multi-year research program to assess environmental impacts of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The CAP represents one of the most ambitious water transfer projects in U.S. history, diverting [[Colorado River]] water through a system of aqueducts, pumping plants, and reservoirs across central and southern Arizona. The proposed research encompasses aquatic, riparian, and terrestrial ecosystems from the [[Mojave Desert]] through the [[Sonoran Desert]] to potential [[Chihuahuan Desert]] connections. The program emphasizes baseline data collection, control site monitoring, construction-phase surveillance, and long-term operational assessment across diverse habitats ranging from warm desert to coniferous forest zones.

## Background & Description

The Central Arizona Project involves major infrastructure including: intake facilities at [[Lake Havasu]], multiple aqueducts (Granite Reef, Gila-Salt River, San Pedro), and several new reservoirs (Buttes, Orme, Charleston, Hooker). The prospectus recognizes that environmental studies must account for Arizona's highly variable climate, the project's vast geographic scope encompassing nearly all conceivable desert and semi-desert habitats, and the sequential implementation timeline requiring flexible research design.

The document proposes four basic research phases: (1) study of existing reservoirs and their environs; (2) research on proposed aqueduct routes and existing canal systems for predictive information; (3) investigation of reservoir sites including baseline surveys, construction monitoring, and post-impoundment surveillance; and (4) comprehensive analysis of the entire operational system. The research philosophy recognizes the CAP as a major geologic change creating aquatic and riparian dispersal corridors that will facilitate movement of [[Mojave Desert]], [[Sonoran Desert]], and [[Chihuahuan Desert]] biotic assemblages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Systems**: Research on [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Mead]] must address water movement patterns, nutrient and sediment loads, density currents, and biotic responses to physical-chemical components. Increased water transport through these reservoirs will modify thermal regimes and density currents affecting plankton blooms and fish distribution. Desert reservoirs show unique dependence on rainfall intensity and frequency for productivity pulses. Stabilization of [[Lake Havasu]] water levels for CAP intake will create new riparian and aquatic plant communities unavailable in fluctuating reservoirs.

The prospectus notes that fluctuating reservoirs support minimal riparian vegetation, with desert plants growing to water's edge. Stabilized water tables will trigger biological responses ranging from vegetation species composition changes to development of new communities available for vertebrate and invertebrate colonization. Flowing-water fauna in areas to be impounded (Buttes, Orme, Charleston, Hooker) will be replaced by quiet-water species.

**Terrestrial and Riparian Systems**: Construction and operation will create potential sites for riparian community establishment, desert vegetation succession, and increased recreational activities. Control areas must be established at least 18 months prior to construction in various habitats along aqueduct routes, permanently protected to differentiate CAP impacts from natural changes. Lead-time studies of existing canal systems (Phoenix metropolitan area, possibly Los Angeles aqueduct) will provide predictive information on aquatic vegetation growth, introduced species like the Asiatic clam *[[Corbicula]]*, sedimentation patterns, and microenvironmental changes.

**Water Quality Considerations**: The prospectus addresses entrainment and mortality of aquatic organisms in intake systems, effects of local storms on water quality and operations, and potential impacts from upstream developments (e.g., copper industry in northern Sonora, México affecting Charleston Reservoir water quality). Studies must examine dissolved oxygen, hydrogen sulfide levels, and other parameters affecting reservoir productivity and fisheries development.

## Key Findings

**Research Requirements and Timeline**: Minimum 18 months baseline data collection required before construction at any site to achieve acceptable credibility under unpredictable desert conditions. Control areas must be established early and monitored throughout program duration (potentially 15+ years based on Figure 1 timeline). Some control areas already under surveillance for 6+ years provide valuable lead-time information.

**Personnel and Budget**: Minimum personnel requirements include 6-8 post-doctoral research associates, 15-20 graduate research assistants, 1-2 computer programmers, 2-3 secretaries/keypunch operators, and 5-10 undergraduate technicians. Estimated budgets: FY 1971-72: $360,500 (including $250,000 capital equipment); subsequent 4-year periods averaging $370,000-$960,000 annually, with highest intensity during middle implementation years.

**Institutional Capabilities**: [[Arizona State University]] offers strategic location within CAP impact area, extensive computing facilities (transitioning to large-scale system by 1973), specialized laboratories (aquatic, terrestrial, radioisotope, electrophoretic), reference collections, and collaborative arrangements with [[University of Nevada Las Vegas]] for [[Lake Mead]] studies. The [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]] Forest Hydrology Laboratory on campus provides additional watershed and wildlife habitat research capacity.

**Predicted Ecological Changes**: The CAP will create continuous or oasis-like habitats along aqueducts serving as dispersal avenues for plant and animal life. Stable plant communities will provide habitat for vertebrate and invertebrate faunas not previously found in these arid environments. Major biotic changes anticipated in both aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, with counter-movement of species along the new water corridors, though downstream dispersal likely faster than upstream movement.

**Methodological Approaches**: Research will employ remote monitoring (aerial photography including high-resolution color-infrared and ektachrome imagery, climatological stations) combined with intensive field sampling on weekly to bi-monthly schedules. Computer simulations of density current activities based on salinities and thermal profiles will provide insight into reservoir dynamics. KWIC (Key-Word in Context) and KWOC (Key-Word out of Context) computer-based indexing systems will facilitate literature and data retrieval.

**Comparative Studies**: Six years of data from stabilized Salt River lakes (pump-storage installations) provide lead-time information on daily generating cycles, entrainment effects, and riparian/aquatic vegetation changes. Roosevelt Lake data offers comparison for Orme Reservoir predictions. San Carlos Reservoir studies inform Buttes Reservoir expectations, though water quality differences (low dissolved oxygen, high hydrogen sulfide) require specific investigation.

**Long-term Monitoring**: Full operational system assessment deemed imperative but difficult to estimate at proposed funding levels. Research data should enable projection of overall system impact, but ground-truth data collection essential to confirm projections. Deviations from projected impacts may require entirely new research areas to explain observed changes. The prospectus acknowledges that some impacts may be readily observable after a few years while more subtle changes may require long-term study to become evident.
