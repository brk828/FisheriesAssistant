# FY-1996 Native Fish Rearing Coordination Meeting: Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum from the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] records a coordination meeting held on December 5, 1995, between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR). The meeting set the FY-1996 plan for rearing [[Razorback Sucker]] (RBS) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (BTC), two endangered fishes now confined mainly to [[Lake Mohave]] on the Lower Colorado River. The plan focuses on collecting wild razorback sucker larvae, expanding hatchery capacity through solar-heated recirculating raceways, and funding ten equipment, facility, and staffing items. Its central constraint is that fish must reach at least 250 mm before release, which requires two growing seasons.

## Background & Description

The memorandum was sent by the hatchery manager to the Gila-Colorado (GM LCRE) office and attached personnel. Attendees included three FWS staff (George Divine, Jim Hanson, Pete Carboni) and four BOR staff (Bill Sinne, Tom Shrader, Tom Burke, Glen Gould). The meeting minutes are the hatchery's "Native Fish Rearing Needs" document, which outlines the station's history with native fish and its 1996 needs for facilities, equipment, supplies, and personnel.

The hatchery has held endangered species and tested rearing techniques since the early 1970s, but this work was mostly peripheral to major recovery efforts. Starting in 1994, FWS directed the station to help produce and rear razorback suckers and bonytail chubs as part of a federal effort to maintain genetically diverse populations.

Funding for 1996 was to come from a mix of FWS sources (Archery, Stewardship, and others), BOR accounts, and Interim Conservation Measures developed under the [[Lower Colorado River Multi Species Conservation Program]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water temperature is the main environmental variable in the document. Larval and juvenile razorback suckers need warm water, and the hatchery's shortfall is in heating and rearing space:

- **1994:** Electric water heaters in two tanks could reach 80° F. The system worked but was costly, with added electrical demand of $500 to $1,000 per month. Larvae were started on [[Brine Shrimp]] and then moved to trout diet, which produced few fish because of poor diet and inadequate rearing conditions after transfer.
- **1995:** Drains and valves were modified to limit warm-water loss, and an outdoor [[Recirculating Aquaculture System]] prototype was built with filtration, UV sterilization, biological filtration, and air injection. Water in the outdoor facilities held at 62 to 70° F through November. The team expected that solar refinements could keep at least 70° F through winter. A [[Solar Heating]] collector mat was installed to heat recirculating water.
- **Growing environment:** Wild larvae are being moved from Lake Mohave to [[Ephemeral Ponds]] near Lake Mohave, where growth was described as excellent, though many fish did not reach 250 mm before the season ended.

Other environmental threats were noted. Birds (particularly black-crowned night herons) were a significant predator, and many juvenile fish were lost each year. Power failure also posed a serious risk to the whole stock, since the station's standby generator dates from 1962.

## Key Findings

**Population goals and rearing strategy**

- The FY-1996 collection plan aims to at least double the number of razorback sucker larvae collected in 1995. Collection is planned for late January through April 1996, and the hatchery is prepared to receive up to 100,000 larvae.
- A minimum of 40,000 RBS larvae is expected at Willow Beach in 1996. Fish will be held roughly one year and should exceed 100 mm.
- Because many fish did not reach 250 mm in one season, the group concluded that two growing seasons are needed. In January 1997, ponds will be stocked to carrying capacity. More than 90% of fish are expected to exceed 250 mm before release to Lake Mohave in fall 1997.
- The cycle is to be repeated until the adult razorback sucker population reaches 50,000 fish.
- The working target is at least 30,000 RBS of 100 mm or larger per year. This goal depends on a [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]] agreement to use an old commercial hatchery for expanded growout ponds near the Lower Colorado mainstem. BOR asked that this agreement be finalized before funding for that project is discussed.

**Facility and hatchery status (1995 baseline)**

- About 7,500 wild razorback sucker larvae were collected from Lake Mohave by BOR, the Native Fish Work Group, and volunteers in 1995.
- Roughly 6,000 razorback sucker fingerlings from the 1995 wild collection were in the hatchery, measuring 60 to 200 mm.
- A planned conversion of four raceways to solar-heated recirculating systems was dropped when construction bids far exceeded estimates. Both agencies mutually agreed to cancel that contract, and new plans favor a simpler, cheaper heating method.
- The station has the physical capacity to convert up to eight more raceways (four pairs) to solar-heated recirculating systems without affecting trout production.

**Staffing, health, and coordination**

- BOR will hire a three-year native fish technician, and the hatchery will provide quarters under the Service/Bureau Cooperative Agreement.
- Fish health services will come from the [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]]. Dr. Gary Carmichael (FWS, Albuquerque) was asked to provide on-site consultation in February through April 1996.
- Glen Gould will coordinate with the National Park Service. Divine will check on the status of CRIT ponds.
- Participating offices included Fishery Resources in Albuquerque, Dexter NFH&TC, Arizona Fishery Resources Offices in Pinetop, Parker, and Flagstaff, and the Pinetop Fish Health Center.

**Agreements reached at the meeting**

- BOR will purchase the solar and recirculating equipment and set up an account so purchases can be tracked. Service and BOR personnel will install it. The group chose more expensive solar mat because it is versatile and easy to install, remove, and modify.
- Cryopreservation is to be pursued as a tool against extinction for both species. Divine will look for FWS funding.
- Specialized diets should not be cost-limited, since the cost of collecting eggs and larvae is high and food costs are small compared with the value of recovered fish.
