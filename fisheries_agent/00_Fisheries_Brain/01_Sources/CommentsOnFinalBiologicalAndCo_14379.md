# Comments on Lower Colorado River Biological Opinion for Endangered Species Recovery

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #ESA-consultation #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document comprises expert commentary from Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University) on the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's Biological Opinion (BO) for Lower Colorado River operations and maintenance. The correspondence, dated July 10, 1997, critiques the BO's approach to recovering endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), as well as the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*). Minckley argues that the BO lacks cohesive ecosystem-level integration, instead defaulting to single-species management scenarios that fail to coordinate recovery efforts. He emphasizes that adequate scientific knowledge for recovery already exists and that the primary barrier to native fish recruitment is non-native predator/competitor presence, not lack of research. The commentary includes detailed critiques of specific Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) provisions and advocates for holistic, integrated management approaches aligned with the developing Multi-Species Conservation Plan (MSCP).

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Colorado River]] system has been dramatically altered by dam construction and water management operations, creating a series of reservoirs ([[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]]) and regulated river reaches. Two native fish species—[[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]—are federally listed as endangered, along with the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], a riparian-dependent bird subspecies. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operates these facilities and must comply with Endangered Species Act consultation requirements.

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a long-lived catostomid (lifespan 40-50+ years) characterized by extremely high fecundity (~1800 eggs/cm standard length) but experiencing complete recruitment failure throughout its range. Small numbers of adults persist in [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Mead]], and scattered locations in the lower mainstem Colorado River. Age estimates from otolith analysis indicate some Lake Mohave fish were 26-44 years old in 1981-84, with many individuals still alive 13-17 years later (as of 1997).

The [[Bonytail Chub]] is similarly endangered, with populations reduced to remnant adults. Both species demonstrate successful reproduction (eggs and larvae documented) but fail to recruit juveniles to adult populations under current conditions dominated by non-native predatory and competitive fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The pre-modification [[Lower Colorado River]] likely featured numerous small aquatic habitats in various successional stages within an extensive floodplain, rather than a few large water bodies. The native fish fauna evolved in this dynamic, warm-water system with seasonal flooding and variable flows.

Current conditions are fundamentally altered: cold hypolimnetic releases from upstream dams, stabilized flows, loss of floodplain connectivity, and establishment of dense non-native fish assemblages. Minckley emphasizes a critical ecological principle: **"As long as habitat conditions improve for non-native fish, efforts to provide for recruitment of native fish are largely unproductive"** and **"the habitat values of backwaters to native fish are negated to a large degree by the subsequent invasion of non-native fish."**

Both [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] have demonstrated recruitment capability in predator-free environments (including simple mud-bottomed ponds), but recruitment has failed universally where non-native predators/competitors are present. This pattern holds across the species' ranges and represents the fundamental barrier to recovery.

Genetic analysis (Dowling et al. 1996a,b) reveals important population structure in [[Razorback Sucker]], with mitochondrial DNA variability indicating that Lake Mohave fish likely represent a single cohort from the 1950s filling period. Genetic considerations must guide any translocation or augmentation efforts, particularly for populations below Parker Dam.

The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] occupies specific successional stages of riparian vegetation, raising questions about long-term habitat protection strategies when suitable habitat represents a transient phase in ecological succession.

## Key Findings

**Critique of Management Approach:**
- The BO lacks synthesis and integration, reverting to single-species scenarios despite rhetoric about ecosystem management
- This approach will likely result in separate working groups and uncoordinated projects, repeating past failures
- Short-term actions under the BO should directly inform the 50-year [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP), but integration mechanisms are unclear
- Inequitable treatment exists between fish and bird species in terms of habitat protection requirements and compensation mandates

**Native Fish Recovery - Critical Issues:**

*Augmentation (Provision 1):*
- Genetic variability must be carefully managed, especially for razorback suckers used below Parker Dam
- Confusion exists regarding whether "50,000 fish" means stocking numbers or target population size
- Fish should be stocked at sizes exceeding predation range (>35 cm total length)

*Impoundments (Provision 3):*
- Strongly supports creating dedicated water bodies (600 acres initial target) for native fishes
- **Recommends multiple small water bodies (3-15 acres each) rather than few large ones** for several reasons:
  - Better reflects historical floodplain habitat structure
  - More manageable for non-native fish control
  - Can be serially managed to provide successional diversity
  - Less attractive to unauthorized stocking
  - Individual units can be drained/refilled as needed
- Non-native fish exclusion is far more critical than concerns about salts, pesticides, or herbicides
- Without non-native fish control, native fish may survive as adults but will not recruit

*Research Funding (Provision 4):*
- **"Scientific knowledge adequate for recovery and perpetuation of bonytail and razorback sucker and technology to accomplish those goals already exists"**
- Further study of "habitat preferences" and "predator/competitor management" is too vague and risks becoming a "money sink"
- Recommends instead: synthesize existing information, have specialists identify genuine knowledge gaps, then fund hypothesis-based studies only for identified gaps
- The species' longevity is the only reason they haven't already gone extinct

**Razorback Sucker Recruitment Analysis:**

*Lake Mead Population (Provision 10):*
- Provision 10 requires determining what conditions allowed successful recruitment in Lake Mead/Grand Canyon and duplicating them—**Minckley considers this provision "impossible to satisfy"**
- Multiple scenarios could explain current Lake Mead population:
  - Original cohort from 1930s reservoir filling (requiring 60+ year longevity)
  - Immigration from Grand Canyon before 1962-63 chilling by Lake Powell
  - One or more in-reservoir recruitment events
  - Any combination of above
- A reported 14-inch (small) razorback in Lake Mead suggests recent recruitment, but documentation is lacking

*Critical Prerequisites for Historical Analysis:*
1. Individual fish ages must be determined (requires sacrifice for otoliths—unacceptable for endangered species)
2. Detailed river/reservoir operations data must exist (it does not, especially at temporal/spatial scales needed)
3. Fish movement histories are unknown and currently unattainable

*Lake Mohave Context:*
- Present population likely resulted from 1946-1954 filling period (partial filling, drainage for modifications, final filling in 1954)
- Assumed high survival due to predator-free environment and/or rapid growth
- Genetic evidence (Dowling et al. 1996b) suggests large spawning population and high survival
- Population persisted due to longevity, began detectable decline after 1990

*Recruitment Definition and Reality:*
- Recruitment means addition of reproductive adults through offspring survival
- For population persistence, recruitment must balance mortality (1:1 ratio minimum)
- In long-lived, low-turnover species (40-50+ year lifespan), even 1:2 recruitment:death ratio could allow 90+ year persistence of declining population
- High fecundity (~1800 eggs/cm) means small survival increases could dramatically boost populations
- Despite reproduction documented at multiple sites, recruitment to juvenile stages essentially never occurs
- "It is almost inconceivable that all eggs and larvae would be consumed by predators...yet they apparently are, or they succumb to other mortality factors"

**Incidental Take and Monitoring:**

*Take Categories:*
- Category 1 (take that precludes recruitment) includes any non-native fish introduction/stocking that competes with or preys on native species—**"casts a dark light on Federal and Federal-supported sport fisheries projects in the LCR"**
- Inequity persists: fish larvae/juveniles not considered "individuals" while all flycatcher life stages receive that status

*Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs):*
- RPM 1 (Stranding): Quantifying spawning/nursery habitat exposure by water fluctuations "approaches science fiction" given fish rarity
- Both species recruit in almost any habitat when predators absent; habitat quantification is premature
- RPM 2 (Dams): Fish passage mortality occurs but is unavoidable; efforts better directed elsewhere
- RPM 3 (Entrainment): Evaluation of diversions premature given current population status and more critical limiting factors

**Ecosystem Restoration (Provision 14):**
- This is essentially the only ecosystem-level requirement in the BO
- Review should be conducted by independent, objective group external to agencies with vested interests
- Must evaluate "improbability of re-establishing 'self-sustaining populations' of bonytail chub and razorback sucker in the LCR channel under any circumstances in the face of great numbers of well established non-native predators/competitors"
- Natural river reaches could maintain breeding adults of high genetic variability even without recruitment
- Analysis should compare scenarios from maximum human water-use efficiency to various conservation-oriented operations
- Presupposes extensive future restoration "which may tend to negate an objective synthesis"

**Integration and Timing Concerns:**
- MSCP process to complete in ~3 years (by 2000)
- Reclamation must initiate implementation by May 15, 2001 (one year after MSCP completion)
- BO consultation period ends 2002
- **No apparent provisions for integrating Reclamation's BO compliance with MSCP development**
- Relationships between Reclamation actions and MSCP goals require clarification

**Literature and Knowledge Base:**
- Minckley et al. (1991) provides comprehensive management framework for razorback sucker recovery
- McCarthy & Minckley (1987) established age estimation techniques via otoliths
- Dowling et al. (1996a,b) documented genetic structure and hatchery stock implications
- Small fish (confirmed young, age 2-12 years) documented over 20 years from: seepage ponds, canals below Parker Dam, Lake Mohave, lower Little Colorado
- Low-level recruitment does occur sporadically, demonstrating biological capability under certain conditions
