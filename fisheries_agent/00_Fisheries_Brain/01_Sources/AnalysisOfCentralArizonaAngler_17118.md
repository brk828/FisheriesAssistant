# Analysis of Central Arizona Angler Opportunity and Benefits from Artificial Habitat in Saguaro Lake Using RIOFISH

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This completion report, prepared for the [[Tonto National Forest]] (U.S. Forest Service) by New Mexico State University, applies the adapted fishery planning model [[RIOFISH]] to [[Saguaro Lake]] near Phoenix, Arizona. The aim was to estimate how artificial habitat installed in the lake would change angler opportunity and benefits (consumer surplus) over a 10-year period under varying planning conditions. The model assumed artificial habitat concentrates fish and doubles their catchability.

Five hypotheses were tested across 21 scenario pairs (42 model runs, with and without management). Water supply, nutrient concentration, human population growth, a 25 percent reduction in largemouth bass mortality and [[Lake Pleasant]] management all influenced predicted outcomes, so all five null hypotheses were rejected. Benefits from the habitat were small, and under the most favorable conditions tested they would not exceed the project cost over 10 years. The report's central point is that angler use, constrained largely by parking, and fishery productivity matter more than the fish concentration effect alone.

## Background & Description

The Saguaro Lake Fish Habitat Improvement Project was initiated in 1989 by the Tonto National Forest, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and Anglers United. Artificial cover (plastic "trees" and refuges) was placed in littoral waters during a temporary drawdown. Only about 2 percent of the shoreline is road-accessible, so placing cover near fishable shore areas was intended to concentrate shore and boat anglers. The project planned to double the parking area, add fishing docks and build trails.

The original proposal estimated a project cost above $1,000,000 and an angler benefit:cost ratio above 8:1 over 10 years. Demand was projected to grow from about 350,000 to 500,000 anglers in the Phoenix area by 2000. Model results later showed benefits well below the original $8 million estimate.

Saguaro Lake is the smallest of the Phoenix-area recreational reservoirs in the chain on the Salt River, while [[Theodore Roosevelt Lake]] is the largest. Lakes in the substitute network include Lake Pleasant, Horseshoe, Bartlett, Theodore Roosevelt, Apache, Canyon, Saguaro and San Carlos reservoirs. Saguaro Lake's harvest is dominated by sunfish (*Lepomis* spp.). Saguaro Lake is one of the few lakes in the system where pump-storage operations cause daily drawdowns of 2 to 3 m.

Fish species noted in the report include largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), crappie, channel catfish, yellow bass, sunfish, bullhead, carp-buffalo and threadfin shad (forage). Smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*) and white bass (*Morone chrysops*) introductions had failed. Walleye and rainbow trout were stocked in other lakes but excluded from the model.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stratification and oxygen:** All Salt River reservoirs stratified before pump-storage began in the 1970s. Thermoclines sat at 6 to 7 m in the lower reservoirs and at 10 to 12 m in Theodore Roosevelt Lake. Hypolimnetic oxygen was depleted, and Saguaro Lake was documented as stratified and oxygen-depleted in 1989.
- **Light:** Euphotic depth (1 percent light) was about 14 to 16 m at Apache, 10 to 12 m in Roosevelt, and 6 to 8 m in Canyon and Saguaro lakes (Rinne 1973).
- **Nutrients and productivity:** Phosphorus was the key fertility driver. Modeled phosphorus varied with sedimentation rate; the lowest-sedimentation scenario, multiplied by 3, best matched observed outflow values. Lake Pleasant phosphorus averaged more than twice Saguaro Lake's.
- **Organic loading:** Allochthonous organic matter was assumed at 1.0 g/m³ in tributary inflows. Theodore Roosevelt Lake may receive loads exceeding 1000 g C/m²/yr in some years, while RIOFISH estimated Saguaro Lake production at under 200 g C/m²/yr.
- **Water supply:** Water years were classified as low (e.g., 1946–1951, 1956–1959), intermediate (e.g., 1944–1945, 1952–1954, 1960–1965) and high (1979–1988). Lake Pleasant storage was expected to rise to 1,600–4,000 ha after 1994.
- **Human use:** Saguaro Lake parking holds about 200 cars and fills early on summer weekends. Observed use was about 30,000 angler days in 1989, versus an RIOFISH prediction near 250,000 at $12 per visit. Lake Pleasant received roughly 400,000 anglers.

## Key Findings

- **Hypotheses rejected:** All five hypotheses (water supply, nutrients, human population, 25 percent lower largemouth bass mortality, Lake Pleasant management) were rejected. Each condition altered predicted opportunity and benefits.
- **Catchability and overexploitation:** When angler demand approached potential yield, doubling catchability intensified harvest on the reproductive stock, reduced recruitment and lowered long-term benefits. Doubling catchability is roughly equivalent to doubling fishing pressure.
- **Benefit range:** The 10-year benefit ranged from about $65,700 (lowest scenario) to $169,480 (highest, discounted at zero percent), a spread of about $103,700. Water supply alone gave a benefit:cost ratio of 0.08 to 0.17; intermediate water years with the most likely fertility gave about 0.13.
- **Parking constraint:** Parking capacity appeared to be the main limit on angler use. Benefits were restricted when fertility was high, while low fertility made parking limits protective of long-term benefit.
- **Fertility effects:** High-water years roughly doubled the benefit of management relative to low-water years. Fertility effects were greatest at intermediate water supply. RIOFISH suggested sustained catch-rate doubling needed about five times current fertility (about 0.15 mg/L phosphorus).
- **Mortality reduction:** A 25 percent cut in largemouth bass mortality changed benefits modestly: +$1,872 in high water, and about -$3,005 in medium and -$2,995 in low water relative to the no-change case. Compensatory effects in RIOFISH dampened the response.
- **Lake Pleasant:** Management at Lake Pleasant could shift Saguaro Lake's benefits by up to about 20 percent. High Lake Pleasant water after the 1995 dam filling was predicted to lower benefits to $11,680 from $12,815 (intermediate water).
- **Cost comparison:** Even with favorable assumptions, benefits were unlikely to cover the roughly $1,000,000 project cost. Stocking enough catchable fish to offset fishing mortality would cost about $1,000,000 per year (benefit:cost about 0.4).
- **Recommendations:** The report recommends identifying constraints on benefits (fertility, access, substitute sites), inventorying the fraction of sustained yield actually harvested across all sites, collecting comprehensive angler and stock data, managing the whole regional system rather than a single site, and recognizing that increasing catch rate where parking or substitutes are limiting is an uneconomical way to raise benefits.
- **Model limits:** RIOFISH does not model pump-storage daily fluctuations, parking-induced crowding dynamics, or the direct effect of artificial cover on catchability. The catchability multiplier of 2x was arbitrary. The report stresses that the direction and relative impacts of effects matter more than the precise dollar values.
