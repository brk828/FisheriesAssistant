# Climate Change and Stream Communities

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This chapter by Nancy B. Grimm, from *Biotic Interactions and Global Change* (1993, edited by Peter M. Kareiva, Joel G. Kingsolver, and Raymond B. Huey), argues that streams are transport systems that also transform and integrate materials, and that their response to [[Climate Change]] will depend on how hydrologic disturbance and [[Biotic Control]] interact. The author proposes that changes in hydrologic extremes and variability, more than changes in averages, will shift the balance between disturbance and biotic interactions in streams. Examples come mainly from [[Desert Streams]] studied by the author's laboratory, especially [[Sycamore Creek]] in Arizona. The chapter closes with three recommendations: expand scale, conduct comparative studies, and merge abiotic and biotic realms in conceptual models.

## Background & Description

Streams are described as linear, unidirectional systems with disturbances that are primarily hydrologic, lateral, longitudinal, and vertical connections to adjacent systems, short-lived organisms, and diverse communities. The chapter focuses on small to mid-sized lotic ecosystems and uses deserts as a model because the influence of the physical environment is more transparent there than in other biomes. Historically, stream ecology has centered on small temperate forest streams.

Figure 1 frames the system as two controlling regimes, thermal and hydrologic, plus ecological exchanges with the terrestrial environment. The hydrologic regime has three components: amount, timing, and variability. Together these make up the "disturbance regime."

The chapter also reviews a classification by [[Poff And Ward Classification]] (1989), which sorts continental U.S. streams by intermittency, flood frequency, and flood predictability. This scheme extends the earlier [[Harsh-Benign Hypothesis]] of Peckarsky (1983), which the author considers too simple because it does not allow biotic and abiotic controls to vary within one system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Sweeney et al. (1992) examined allozyme variation in six mayfly species along a latitudinal transect in eastern North America. Genetic variability was lowest at high latitudes, where warming is expected to be greatest, which suggests limited capacity to adapt. Warming also reduces oxygen solubility, which may make species with high oxygen requirements especially vulnerable. Shifts in a trout species' range could change invertebrate communities where the trout is a predator, and could also affect the fishery.

**Riparian zone.** Tree species may respond individually to temperature change. Shifts in riparian species composition can alter the quality of leaf litter entering streams, since leaf species differ in decomposition rate and food value for [[Aquatic Macroinvertebrates]]. Riparian loss would have far-reaching effects, because streams depend on trees for food, shade, and habitat structure.

**Human disturbance.** Riparian canopy removal, channelization, logging, flow regulation, and pollution may obscure climate effects. Predicting the relative importance of climate change requires a clear understanding of stream dynamics.

**Desert hydrology.** Arid-land streams are already intermittent, and small rainfall changes can produce large streamflow changes. Figure 6 (after Wigley and Jones, 1985) shows that runoff change in deserts is especially sensitive to precipitation change. At [[Sycamore Creek]], the stream is continuous in winter along a 25-km reach but has permanent flow at only six sites in summer.

## Key Findings

- **Extremes drive stream responses.** Changes in flood and drought extremes are expected to matter more than changes in means, because extremes define disturbance. Regional hydrologic change is hard to predict because of uncertain effects of warming on precipitation, vegetation, and soils.
- **Biotic and abiotic control shift over time and space.** At Sycamore Creek, the chapter describes temporal shifts between disturbance control and biotic control. Flash floods are followed by about 30 days of postflood recovery. Long interflood periods allow drying to become an important disturbance. Periods of disturbance and biotic control overlap broadly, so the two should not be viewed as mutually exclusive.
- **Wet and dry years at Sycamore Creek (Table 1).** Across a 29-year record, the five wettest years averaged 8.4 floods versus 1.8 in the five driest. Wet years averaged 128 days (35 percent of the year) in early to middle postflood succession. Dry years averaged 167 days (46 percent) in late succession and likely accelerated drying. Time under biotic control was about 48 percent of the year overall (mean 177 days), and the author suggests this proportion stays similar across very different climates.
- **Seasonality matters more than total runoff.** 1970 and 1988 had similar annual runoff but different spate numbers (2 vs. 9) and timing (fall vs. winter-spring). A single September 1970 flood accounted for 97 percent of that year's runoff.
- **Community structure is resilient to floods but not to drying.** Collective attributes such as biomass and density change sharply with flooding, yet diversity is seldom reduced and community structure is only slightly displaced. A fall shift in 1984–1986, with fewer mayflies and more *Physella* snails, was linked to prolonged drying. Drying can cause physiological stress and intensify biotic interactions, and it cuts off colonists from adult insects.
- **Predator-grazer-algae interactions.** In Briar Creek, Oklahoma, [[Largemouth Bass]] restricted to deep pools during low flow reduced densities of the grazing fish [[Campostoma Anomalum]]. Pools with bass were "green," while pools without bass had sparse algae. Spates reduce algal standing crops by more than 80 percent.
- **Nitrogen links algae and invertebrates.** Desert stream algae are often nitrogen limited. Early successional diatom communities reduce nitrogen levels, while late successional communities dominated by nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. In 12 successional sequences, invertebrate biomass crashed in six, which the author attributes to declining detrital food quality from nitrogen limitation.
- **Alternating controls in Rattlesnake Creek.** High winter flows reduce standing crops, blackflies recolonize scoured substrate but are displaced by hydropsychid caddisflies, and predators regulate lower trophic levels during low flow.
- **Seasonally biotic systems.** In the South Fork Eel River, [[Cladophora Glomerata]] turfs become infested with midge larvae in summer if fish that reduce midge predators are abundant. Snowmelt- and glacier-fed streams have predictable dry-season biotic phases that may be strongly affected by changes in seasonality.
- **Patch dynamics.** Streams are heterogeneous, and the chapter recommends identifying patches as a basis for prediction. [[Beavers]] show how species activity can reshape habitat distribution and fish productivity.
- **Species that matter.** Species matter when they have unique abilities, act as [[Keystone Species]], are the only representative of their kind, or create physical structure. Grazing fishes are cited as likely keystone species, with *Campostoma anomalum* described as a "strong interactor" with no substitutable species. Debris dams and beaver dams are examples of structure that controls stream retention and nutrient dynamics.
- **Recommendations.** The author calls for (1) expanding the scale of studies, including long-term descriptive work and [[Whole-System Experiments]]; (2) using comparative studies along aridity gradients and across historical records; and (3) merging abiotic and biotic realms in conceptual models, treating abiotic controls as alternative hypotheses and noting that abiotic factors often reflect biotic processes.
