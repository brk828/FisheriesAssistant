# Annotated List of the Fishes of the Lake Ontario Watershed

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This technical report by [[E. J. Crossman]] and [[Harry D. Van Meter]] (Great Lakes Fishery Commission Technical Report No. 36, June 1979) is an annotated checklist of the fishes of [[Lake Ontario]] and its watershed. It draws on published distribution records, [[Royal Ontario Museum]] collections, and survey data from the 1970s, most of it from the 1972–73 [[International Field Year for the Great Lakes]] (IFYGL).

The checklist covers 130 forms: 129 species plus the hybrid [[Splake]]. Of these, 20 have disappeared or are now extremely rare. Comparing lake-proper records with the 1920s survey, the authors find measurable losses of native biodiversity, with several species now extinct.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Fishes recorded at some time from about 1850 onward, representing 25 families and 61 genera.
- **Study area:** The Lake Ontario basin covers an estimated 56,000 sq km and includes the incoming [[Niagara River]] and many lakes and streams that drain into the lake before it discharges into the [[St. Lawrence River]].
- **Survey effort:** More than 260 survey trips were made to inshore and offshore sites and to north-shore tributaries. Participants included the New York Department of Environmental Conservation, Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources, Royal Ontario Museum, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Alfred University.
- **Nomenclature:** Common and scientific names follow [[Bailey et al. 1970]]. The list is alphabetical by common name in Table 1 and taxonomic in the annotated section.
- **Symbols:** Species marked with an asterisk (68) were collected in the lake proper in the 1970s. Species marked with a dagger (20) were formerly present but are now absent or extremely rare.
- **Date range:** The list started in 1972 and includes information to 1976.

The annotated section gives brief notes on distribution, abundance, habitat preference and biological or economic status for each species. Families are arranged in taxonomic order, from lampreys (Petromyzontidae) through sculpins (Cottidae). The bibliography contains 86 references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake-proper changes (1929 vs. 1972–75):**
- 64 species were reported in the lake in 1929 (Dymond et al. 1929).
- 51 of those were still present in 1972–73.
- 13 were not collected: [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[American Shad]], [[Mooneye]], [[Kiyi]], [[Shortnose Cisco]], [[Lake Trout]], blackchin shiner, blacknose shiner, sand shiner, brook stickleback, ninespine stickleback, [[Sauger]] and [[Blue Pike]].
- Four of these (American shad, kiyi, shortnose cisco, blue pike) are considered extinct in the lake since 1929.
- Lake sturgeon is scarce and probably no longer reproducing in the lake. Lake trout occurs only through recent introductions.
- Three minnows (blackchin, blacknose and sand shiner) were common to abundant in Burlington Bay in 1927 and were not found despite numerous seine collections in 1972–73.

**Species first recorded in the lake-proper survey (1972–75) but not reported in 1927:** 18 species, including coho and chinook salmon, rainbow and brown trout, splake, brook trout, [[Rainbow Smelt]], white perch, smallmouth bass, white crappie, rainbow darter and mottled sculpin. The authors attribute these to recent invasions (e.g., [[White Perch]]), direct introductions elsewhere (e.g., rainbow smelt), or introductions to the lake or its tributaries (e.g., [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Chinook Salmon]]). Some may have been present but not identified in 1929.

**Human impacts and management:**
- Overfishing, introductions and eutrophication have reshaped the salmonid community.
- Commercial fisheries for [[Cisco]] and [[Lake Whitefish]] have declined sharply. Cisco production reached a record 5 million pounds in 1889.
- [[Sea Lamprey]] is common and widespread; chemical treatment of larval nursery areas is under way.
- [[Alewife]] has suffered heavy mass mortalities since the 1890s and continues to die off in late winter and spring.
- Rainbow smelt was rare until the mid-1940s and then became abundant enough to support commercial and recreational fisheries.

## Key Findings

1. **Biodiversity:** The watershed hosts 130 forms (129 species and one hybrid), of which 20 are absent or extremely rare.
2. **Extinctions and losses:** Since 1929, at least three native species are lost from the lake, along with the introduced American shad. The authors state that nine more are "probably lost." [[Atlantic Salmon]] was lost before 1900.
3. **Recent arrivals:** Seventeen species and the splake were either unknown in the lake in 1929 or introduced since.
4. **Salmonid restoration:** Stocked [[Coho Salmon]] (since 1968) and [[Chinook Salmon]] (since 1969) now support sport fisheries. Atlantic salmon reintroductions from 1947–1970 failed, although restoration efforts continue. [[Kokanee]] introductions from 1950–1970 also failed.
5. **Cisco and whitefish decline:** Cisco, lake whitefish, bloater and round whitefish are rare, reduced or endangered. The bloater's last known record was in 1972.
6. **Taxonomic corrections:** Several early records were discounted. Redbelly dace records refer to northern redbelly dace (*Phoxinus eos*). Straw-colored minnow records refer to sand shiner. Early redhorse records before the 1930s are considered unreliable.
7. **Abundant and important species:** Emerald shiner is the most abundant minnow. Common shiner, spottail shiner, bluntnose minnow, [[White Sucker]], yellow perch and [[Smallmouth Bass]] are widespread. Johnny darter is the most abundant darter.
8. **Conservation priorities:** Authors call the losses serious even though the number is small, and they recommend continued monitoring of rare and endangered species.
