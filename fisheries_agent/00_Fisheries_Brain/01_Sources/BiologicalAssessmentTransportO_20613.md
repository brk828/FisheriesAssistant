# Biological Assessment: Transport of Nonnative Fishes into the Santa Cruz River Basin by the Central Arizona Project Aqueduct

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This March 1994 draft Biological Assessment, prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Arizona Projects Office), evaluates whether transporting nonnative fish through the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) aqueduct could harm federally listed fish and amphibians in the Santa Cruz River Basin of southern Arizona. The Bureau's finding is "no effect." The assessment was prepared as a follow-on to the [[Pima Lateral Feeder Canal]] consultation on the Gila River, and it reviews CAP water use, basin hydrology, listed species, the CAP fish community, establishment potential of target species, augmentation risk, physical barriers, and sources of nonnative fish such as stocking, aquaculture and the aquarium trade.

## Background & Description

**Origin of the consultation.** The [[Gila River Indian Community]] (GRIC) held rights to water in [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. To prevent a fish kill during drought, GRIC agreed to leave 30,000 acre-feet (af) in the reservoir in exchange for 30,000 af of CAP water delivered through the Pima Lateral Feeder Canal (PLFC). Section 7 clearance for the PLFC was conditioned on an electrical fish barrier to block nonnative fish moving into the Florence/Casa Grande Canal and the Gila River, and was granted for one year only.

**Prior analysis.** Reclamation contracted Dr. William J. Matter (University of Arizona) in July 1990. His February 1991 report examined five target species: striped bass, white bass, blue tilapia, rainbow smelt and grass carp. In May 1991 the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) issued a draft biological opinion finding that the Gila River delivery would jeopardize the loach minnow, spikedace, Gila topminnow and razorback sucker. Reclamation agreed to accept the opinion in March 1994.

**CAP system.** The 92.8-mile assessed aqueduct segment begins at the Pima Lateral turnout and ends near Tucson. Eight pumping plants lift water a total of 1,235 feet. Capacity ranges from 1,245 cubic feet per second (cfs) at the Brady Pumping Plant to 200 cfs at the Black Mountain pumping plant. Ten turnouts serve 26 CAP water users. All turnouts have trash racks with a maximum bar spacing of 2 inches, which excludes fish with a head or body width greater than 2 inches.

**Water use.** Agricultural water is applied mainly by flood irrigation, so tail water can reach major channels. Municipal and industrial (M&I) water is treated to drinking standards, which removes or kills fish, but it may be used for recharge. Tucson holds the largest M&I allocation (148,420 af/year) and is the only user with firm plans. Indian allocations (Table 3) are largely for agriculture, with settlement and recharge options under consideration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Santa Cruz River hydrology.** The river's watershed covers 13,790 square miles across Arizona and Mexico. Its headwaters are in the San Rafael Valley. Perennial flow below Nogales is maintained by effluent from the [[Nogales International Water Treatment Plant]] (NIWTP), which began discharging in 1951. Historically the river flowed perennially to a point about 11 miles north of Tubac. Gage data show that at the Continental station, 95 percent of the time flow is below 37 cfs, and 85 percent of the time there is no flow. The Pima Mine Road recharge site, the most southerly contemplated CAP discharge point, lies 11 miles downstream of that gage.

**Comparative flows.** Monthly flows on the gaged perennial segments of the Santa Cruz River and Sonoita Creek are lower than those on the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Aravaipa Creek]], which Matter used as analogs.

**Temperature.** The lower lethal temperature for tilapia is 6.0–6.5 °C (43–44 °F), with some mortality at 10 °C (50 °F). Winter normal minimum air temperatures at Tucson, Tumacacori and Nogales were compared with this threshold (Table 7). Air and water temperatures are assumed to correlate closely in these small, shallow streams.

**Threats to native fish.** The assessment attributes declines in native fish to habitat degradation, reduced flows from pumping and diversions, water-quality problems and effluent pollution. It identifies exotic species as the main concern, acting through predation, competition and habitat alteration.

**Listed and Category 2 species.** Gila topminnow and desert pupfish are federally endangered. Category 2 species (no legal ESA protection) include Gila chub, speckled dace, longfin dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, lowland leopard frog and Chiricahua leopard frog. Roundtail chub was also listed by FWS for the area.

**Gila topminnow.** Listed March 11, 1967. Only eight natural populations remain in Arizona, and most are in the Santa Cruz Basin. Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) are a major predator. As of 1991, three populations were free of mosquitofish: Cottonwood and Monkey Springs, and Cienega Creek. Populations at Santa Cruz River, Sonoita Creek and Redrock Canyon are highly threatened by mosquitofish. Downlisting criteria were met in 1985 and 1987, but many researchers consider downlisting premature.

**Cienega Creek.** This site holds the largest topminnow population in Arizona, with no exotic fish recorded. Its natural barriers are a 20-foot waterfall, a dry tank, a concrete dam and an earthen dam, plus several miles of ephemeral wash.

**Desert pupfish.** Listed March 31, 1986. No critical habitat lies in the Santa Cruz Basin. The only possible basin population is in Bog Hole Tank (Location 10), where unauthorized stocking has established green sunfish, largemouth bass, crappie and mosquitofish. Renovation scheduled for 1992 had not occurred by February 1994.

**Leopard frogs.** Both species have declined in Arizona since the late 1980s. Possible causes include predation by exotic fish and bullfrogs, loss of riparian habitat, and speculative acid-rain effects. Fish may eat frog larvae.

## Key Findings

- **Nonnative fish in the CAP aqueduct.** Table 4 lists native and nonnative species. All nonnative fish in the aqueduct also occur in the Santa Cruz Basin, except the five Matter target species (only two present in the aqueduct) and goldfish. Striped bass have been collected in the lower aqueduct, but only two were found downstream of Brady Pumping Plant, and their numbers remain low.
- **Escape routes are limited.** Escape from the aqueduct would require an exit to agricultural fields, a recharge basin, or overflow during a flood, followed by upstream travel of at least 30.2 miles. The probability of this approaches zero for small species such as mosquitofish.
- **Establishment is unlikely.** Matter concluded that tilapia were most likely to establish self-sustaining populations but were unlikely to cause significant harm to native fish. He concluded the other four species would not establish self-sustaining populations even with repeated introductions. Striped bass stocked in Oak Creek in 1970 (270 fish) failed to establish.
- **Augmentation is implausible.** Populations are limited by environmental factors such as food, cover, predation and disease. The assessment concludes that augmenting established nonnative fish, such as channel catfish, poses no plausible threat to listed species.
- **Physical barriers are effective.** Dry stream reaches and drop structures have kept Cienega Creek free of exotic fish. Patagonia Lake dam blocks upstream movement to Sonoita Creek sites 6a and 6b.
- **Nonnative sources are significant.** Arizona stocking records total 190,740,654 fish since 1933. FWS Albuquerque stocked 4,691,495 nonnative sportfish in Arizona in fiscal years 1991–93 (Table 9). Aquaculture (channel catfish, tilapia, hybrid striped bass) and aquarium dumping add further risk. Tribal lands are exempt from state aquaculture and stocking regulation.
- **Conclusion.** Reclamation concludes that operation of the CAP aqueduct will have "no effect" on endangered species in the Santa Cruz River Basin. The conclusion rests on dry-stream isolation (flood-only connection), low augmentation probability, and cold waters unsuitable for the target species. Reclamation will reinitiate consultation if new aquatic species are found in the aqueduct.
