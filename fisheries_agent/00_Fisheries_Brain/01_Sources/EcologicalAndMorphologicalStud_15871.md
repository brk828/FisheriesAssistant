# Ecological and Morphological Studies on Gammarid Amphipods (*Gammarus* spp.) in Spring-Fed Streams of Northern Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[Gerald A. Cole]] (Occasional Papers, [[C. C. Adams Center for Ecological Studies]], Western Michigan University) examines two coexisting amphipod crustaceans in Doe Run, a spring-fed stream in Meade County, Kentucky. The species are [[Gammarus Minus]], a widespread North American form, and [[Gammarus Bousfieldi]], apparently endemic to Doe Run. The authors compare habitat use, feeding, behavior and morphology across the mainstream and several tributaries. They find that the mainstream population of *G. minus* is markedly differentiated from the typical form found in [[Morgan's Creek]], and that tributary populations form clines linked to stream gradient and moss beds. The authors propose that this divergence may reflect character displacement, neoteny, or both, while acknowledging that the causes remain undetermined.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic context:** Earlier North American gammarid records were often confounded across several species under one name, which limited ecological work. European *Gammarus* research, reviewed by [[Hynes]] (1954, 1955), was cited as more taxonomically stable.
- **Species:** *G. minus* Say (1818) occurs in springs and streams of the northern Ohio River basin and south along the Appalachians. *G. propinquus* and *G. purfurescens* are treated as synonyms. *G. bousfieldi* Cole and Minckley (1961) had been recorded from no other locality at the time of writing. Both belong to subgenus *Rivulogammarus*.
- **Study area:** Doe Run rises as a spring near Ekron, Kentucky, and flows 9.7 stream miles to the [[Ohio River]]. It has a modal discharge of about 30 cfs, ranging from 10 to 600 cfs during 1959–1962. The region is mature karst developed on Mississippian limestone.
- **Study streams:** Doe Run mainstream (five stations), Seven-Springs Branch, Tributary III-i, and Morgan's Creek (a spring stream from Morgan's Cave, about 3,800 feet long).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** At Doe Run Station I, temperatures were 13–14 °C, dissolved oxygen 70–85% saturation, and bicarbonate alkalinity 200–260 mg/l CaCO3. Downstream, alkalinity fell as marl precipitated, pH rose, and oxygen approached 100% saturation.
- **Vegetation and substrate:** Upper Doe Run was dominated by the moss [[Fissidens Julianus]], along with *Vaucheria geminata*, *Potamogeton foliosus*, *Nitella flexilis* and *Myriophyllum heterophyllum*. Seven-Springs Branch had marl-cemented reaches and a Fissidens-dominated upper section. Morgan's Creek had rubble substrate and few large aquatic plants, partly due to human disturbance.
- **Thermal and flow regimes:** Tributary III-i sometimes reached 25 °C on hot summer days. Morgan's Creek discharge ranged from 0.1 to 0.5 cfs in summer and 0.6 to 1.2 cfs in winter and spring (1961–62). Major floods occurred in July 1960 and spring 1961.
- **Associated fauna:** The isopod [[Asellus Bivittatus]] lived in Fissidens beds and was sparse or absent downstream. A rich fish fauna, including the [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*), occurred in the mainstream, whereas tributary upper reaches lacked fish but held salamanders (*Desmognathus fuscus*).

## Key Findings

**1. Habitat partitioning.** *G. minus* (Doe Run form) was concentrated in dense Fissidens beds on swift mainstream riffles. Water velocities there were greater than 1 m/sec, yet dye remained in the mat for over half an hour, so the micro-habitat is semi-lentic. *G. bousfieldi* was common in quiet pools, on vegetation and on silt bottoms. Where it was absent, *G. minus* used both pools and riffles.

**2. Abundance.** Peak densities were 2,435 individuals per square foot in a Fissidens bed at the Seven-Springs source (August 1960), 1,783 per square foot in leaf packets from Morgan's Creek (October 1960), and 733 per square foot in gravel interstices at Morgan's Creek (August 1960). Populations of both species fluctuated with discharge, with the lowest numbers after major floods.

**3. Behavior.** *G. bousfieldi* cruised in open water, often near the surface, and burrowed into sand or silt. Males were larger and swam faster than females. *G. minus* darted into moss when disturbed and seldom swam more than two feet. The authors note that these swimming behaviors contradict the morphotype predictions of [[Bousfield]] (1958).

**4. Diet.** Both species were omnivorous. *G. minus* fed mainly on amorphous detritus, decomposed Fissidens fragments and isopod feces. Diatoms dominated its spring diet. *G. bousfieldi* ingested more particulate detritus, including watercress and leaf fragments, and more animal material (oligochaetes, Baetis larvae, chironomid fragments). One *G. bousfieldi* was seen eating a smaller amphipod.

**5. Morphological divergence.** Of 238 *G. minus* dissected, the Doe Run mainstream males differed from Morgan's Creek males in three traits:
- Coxal plate setation: Doe Run males averaged about 4.2 antero-ventral setae in adults (6.5 mm or longer), compared with 6.9 at Morgan's Creek. Doe Run plates were 95% single-setaed.
- Antennal peduncle setation: most Doe Run adults had two ventral setal groups on segment 2, compared with at least three in 74% of Morgan's Creek adults. The difference was significant at the 0.001 level.
- Calceoli: all 45 mainstream Doe Run males lacked calceoli, while most Morgan's Creek males had them.

**6. Three-point index.** Morgan's Creek typical males scored 3 and Doe Run males scored 0. Doe Run males were mostly one type. Intermediate forms occurred in certain tributary populations.

**7. Tributary clines.** Tributary III-i populations resembled Morgan's Creek at the source and Doe Run at the mouth, forming a gradient. Seven-Springs Branch showed a reversed cline. Stream gradient correlated with morphology, with steep-gradient populations closer to the typical form.

**8. Size.** The largest mainstream Doe Run male was 2.73 mm shorter than the largest Morgan's Creek male. Gravid Doe Run females were as small as 5.11 mm, versus 5.91 mm at Morgan's Creek. Doe Run males exceeded 11 mm, so simple size reduction does not explain the morphology.

**9. Proposed explanations.** The authors weigh three hypotheses for the Doe Run form: (a) character displacement or selection from congeneric competition with *G. bousfieldi*; (b) neoteny, with juvenile characters retained; and (c) genetic drift or speciation, which they consider less likely given the clinal evidence. For tributary populations they propose either survival of the original genotype, gene flow from the mainstream, or independent selection by moss beds or gradient. A historical Pleistocene hypothesis links the Doe Run form to Ohio River drainage changes.

**10. Conclusion.** The authors conclude that the morphological differences are likely genetic, arising from neotenous tendencies maintained by selection. They state that the selective agent (biotic competition, moss substrate, gradient, or another factor) remains undetermined.
