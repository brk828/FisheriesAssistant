# Influence of Pump Storage and Generation on Commercial Fisheries Potential of Reservoirs

#reservoir-ecology #hydroelectric-power #thermal-loading #desert-limnology

## Executive Summary

This completion report (1972-1975) documents the environmental impacts of pumped-storage hydroelectric generation on the lower Salt River reservoir chain in Central Arizona. The study focused on [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Saguaro Lake]], two desert reservoirs subjected to massive operational modifications including installation of pump-generation units at [[Mormon Flat Dam]] (1971) and [[Horse Mesa Dam]] (1972). Researchers tracked vertical temperature profiles, dissolved oxygen concentrations, and density currents to assess how reversible water flow affected thermal stratification and aquatic habitat. Results demonstrate substantial thermal loading of downstream reservoirs, disruption of previously stable thermal regimes, and fundamental alterations to water chemistry patterns. The study employed continuous monitoring from 1970-1975, capturing baseline conditions before modifications and documenting progressive changes as pumped-storage operations intensified, particularly during 1974 when 481,232 acre-feet were pumped through Mormon Flat Dam alone.

## Background & Description

The [[Salt River]] reservoir system in Central Arizona comprises a chain of on-river impoundments situated in Lower to Upper Sonoran Desert habitats at low elevations. The study focused on the four lowermost reservoirs: [[Roosevelt Lake]] (upstream), [[Apache Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]], and [[Saguaro Lake]] (downstream). These reservoirs serve multiple functions: water storage and delivery, hydroelectric generation, and recreational fisheries.

Between 1959 and 1975, the system underwent extensive modifications (Figure 2). Key changes included: drainage and refilling of the three lowermost reservoirs for outlet structure repairs (1959-1965); installation of a pump-storage generation unit at Mormon Flat Dam in May 1971 (increasing generation capacity by 82.5%); installation of a similar unit at Horse Mesa Dam by June 1972 (71.2% capacity increase); and various operational changes affecting water flow patterns.

[[Canyon Lake]] is impounded by Mormon Flat Dam and has a storage capacity of 57,905 acre-feet at full pool. It exhibits fjord-like morphometry with a narrow drowned river channel and lateral basins. [[Saguaro Lake]], immediately downstream, is impounded by Stewart Mountain Dam, which has approximately 50% of the back-pump capabilities of the upstream dams, creating hydraulic imbalances.

The pump-storage system operates by pumping water upstream during off-peak electrical demand periods and generating power during peak demand by releasing water downstream. This creates bidirectional flow patterns unprecedented in natural riverine systems. Prior to 1971, the reservoirs operated under unidirectional generation mode, with cool hypolimnetic water from [[Roosevelt Lake]] (typically <16°C, <1.0 mg/l dissolved oxygen) passing through successive reservoirs via density currents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Desert reservoirs exhibit extreme heterogeneity and unpredictability compared to temperate systems. The Salt River reservoirs are fed by high montane drainages, creating strong thermal stratification with remarkable vertical stability. Prior to pump-storage operations, these systems maintained well-defined density currents, with cool hypolimnetic water from upstream passing through the drowned river channels while warmer epilimnetic waters remained in lateral basins.

Dye studies in [[Canyon Lake]] (1970) revealed counter-clockwise surface water circulation in the lowermost basin, with no surface water entering the narrow canyon leading to Mormon Flat Dam. This pattern resulted from lateral friction of water passing through the drowned river channel. The studies demonstrated that lateral basins remained independent of the through-flow system, at least for warm surface waters during stratification periods.

[[Roosevelt Lake]] functions as a major sediment and nutrient trap for downstream reservoirs. During intense summer stratification (June-September), dissolved oxygen concentrations below 10-15 m depth fall below 1.0 mg/l. Water passing into lower reservoirs under generation mode carries this substantial oxygen deficit despite relative coolness (rarely >16°C). Prior to 1971, downstream reservoirs typically maintained >2.0 mg/l dissolved oxygen in bottom waters, reflecting reduced decomposer activity and mixing through successive penstocks.

The thermal regime prior to modifications showed the metalimnion (zone of maximum thermal change) centered approximately 5 m beneath the surface during maximum stratification. Surface temperatures reached slightly >28°C while hypolimnetic temperatures declined to <8°C. This stable pattern persisted with minor variations from 1970-1973.

Riparian and aquatic vegetation was essentially absent from reservoir shorelines prior to stabilization. By 1975, hundreds of meters of shoreline in [[Apache Lake]] supported cattail ([[Typha domingensis]]), giant reed ([[Phragmites communis]]), sedges (Cyperaceae), willow and cottonwood ([[Salix]] sp. and [[Populus]] sp.), plus submergent vegetation including [[Potamogeton]], [[Chara]], and [[Zannichellia]].

## Key Findings

**Thermal Regime Disruption (1974):**
The most dramatic changes occurred in 1974 under intensive pump-generation operations. In [[Canyon Lake]], the metalimnion center shifted from 4-5 m depth (1970-1973) to approximately 1 m beneath the surface. Maximum surface temperature decreased to 26°C, but minimum hypolimnetic temperature increased to >10°C (compared to <8°C in 1971). The lake stratified later, erratically, and achieved far less stability. Destratification occurred later in the year at temperatures 2-4°C higher than any previous study year.

**Pumpage Operations and Water Movement:**
Mormon Flat Dam pumped 481,232 acre-feet in 1974 over 259 days—equivalent to 8.3 times Canyon Lake's total storage capacity. Horse Mesa Dam pumped 613,499 acre-feet over 258 days—10.6 times Canyon Lake's capacity and 2.5 times Apache Lake's capacity. This created essentially continuous bidirectional water movement. In contrast, 1971 saw only 43 days of pumpage at Mormon Flat, 1972 had 100 days, and 1973 had 56 days. Operations returned to lower intensity in 1975 (74 days through September), with thermal patterns resembling 1972-1973.

**Direct Effects of Pumpage:**
During test pumpage on 7 June 1971, stratification was totally disrupted in the area above Mormon Flat Dam. Water pumped from [[Saguaro Lake]] (390 m downstream) moved upstream at surface velocities of 0.83-0.94 m/second. Surficial thermal isohets were forced up-lake for considerable distances. The pumped water initially surfaced in a boil, then dispersed into water of its own density within the lake. Metalimnic waters (not just epilimnetic) were ultimately brought into the forebay and injected into Canyon Lake, though warmer epilimnetic waters slowed their upstream movement after approximately three hours.

**Thermal Loading:**
Comparison of hypolimnetic temperatures reveals clear thermal loading. In 1974, the coolest water in Canyon Lake was slightly <18°C and in Saguaro Lake between 21-22°C. In 1973 and 1975, hypolimnetic temperatures were <16°C in Canyon Lake and <18°C in Saguaro Lake—representing 2-4°C warming in 1974. This thermal loading cannot be attributed to climatic factors; meteorological data analysis found no significant correlations between climate and the demonstrated water condition changes in 1974.

**Dissolved Oxygen Changes:**
The oxygen regime in both Canyon and Saguaro lakes decreased markedly in 1974 compared to previous years and 1975. A distinct water mass of decreased oxygen content appeared below the metalimnion (not extending to bottom) beginning in 1972, consistently present in August studies thereafter. This phenomenon may relate to increased generator capacity at Mormon Flat Dam and limited release capabilities at downstream Stewart Mountain Dam. The 1974 oxygen decrease appears too radical to result strictly from physical temperature effects, suggesting changes in biotic activities within the system.

**Density Current Patterns:**
Prior to high-intensity operations, well-defined density currents passed through the reservoirs. Water from Horse Mesa Dam generation moved through the drowned river channel of Canyon Lake while warmer waters remained in lateral basins, creating a "lens effect." With both Mormon Flat and Horse Mesa pump-generation units operating simultaneously, current measurements showed movement of the entire water mass upstream, with velocities and directions relating to drowned river channel configurations. Colliding water masses of different densities (from opposing pumpage and generation) caused massive disruption evident in 1974 thermal profiles.

**Biological Implications:**
Substantial fish kills occurred at the onset of pumpage, with numbers of moribund or dead fish declining as pumpage continued. However, trout tagged in Canyon Lake appeared in Apache Lake angler creels, indicating some relatively large fish survive upstream pumping. Plankton studies remained inconclusive regarding physical damage or mortality percentages. Zooplankton community species composition changed substantially during the study period, with progressive change continuing. Phytoplankton showed tremendous species diversity and sporadic bloom patterns, possibly explained by complex water movement patterns.

**Ecosystem Stabilization Effects:**
Stabilization of downstream reservoirs for pump-generation operations effected major environmental change. [[Apache Lake]], which had no true riparian or aquatic plants in 1973, by 1975 supported hundreds of meters of shoreline vegetation including emergent and submergent aquatic plants. This total transformation of littoral zones represents a fundamental ecosystem shift requiring ongoing study.

**System Complexity:**
The study revealed a dynamic system under continuous change toward a new and unknown state of ecosystem integration at all levels. Variance in measured parameters was extremely high, making strict interpretations difficult. The complexities indicated by water movement patterns demand ongoing investigation. Calculations of thermal regimen in gram calories per unit volume were in progress at report completion.
