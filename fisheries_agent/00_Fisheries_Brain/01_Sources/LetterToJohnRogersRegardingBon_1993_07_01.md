# Bonytail (Gila elegans) Capture, Handling, and Distribution Protocols

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary
This document is a formal letter from Arizona State University researchers (Paul C. Marsh and W.L. Minckley) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Regional Director, dated 01 July 1993. It does not present primary morphological or ecological data but instead addresses critical management, ethical, and jurisdictional concerns regarding the capture, handling, and post-capture mortality of wild adult [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a critically imperiled cyprinid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The letter documents a troubling outcome from 1993 field efforts: 14 wild adult bonytail were transferred to USFWS custody, but only 4 survived at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] shortly thereafter. The authors, citing nearly three decades of Colorado River fish research experience, request greater involvement in developing capture/handling protocols and raise concerns about unauthorized redistribution of specimens to third-party projects.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, body measurements, coloration) are presented in this source. The document is administrative/correspondence in nature. Relevant background context indicates that the specimens involved were **wild adult bonytail**, sourced from two distinct localities within [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona/Nevada), suggesting genetically or ecologically distinct subpopulations targeted for [[Brood Stock]] acquisition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Bonytail were sampled from [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the mainstem Colorado River spanning the Arizona-Nevada border — a critical remnant habitat for this species following historic riverine habitat loss.
- **Seasonal Capture Patterns**: Historical sampling effort was concentrated in **March**, but observations from **May 1988** (during unrelated sampling) suggested increased capture reliability for bonytail during **May**, leading to a shift in survey timing for five consecutive years (1988–1993) of cooperative capture expeditions.
- **Conservation Role**: Wild-caught adult bonytail from Lake Mohave have served as a primary source of genetic material for a captive **brood stock program**, coordinated with USFWS Region 2, as outlined in the official [[Bonytail Recovery Plan]] developed by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]].
- **Interagency Permitting**: Collection activities operated under multiple federal and state permit authorities, including the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Park Service]], [[Arizona Department of Game and Fish]], and [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]] — reflecting the complex multi-jurisdictional management structure surrounding this endangered species' habitat.

## Key Findings
- **High Post-Capture Mortality**: Of 14 wild adult bonytail transferred to USFWS custody in 1993, only 4 remained alive at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center by the time of writing — indicating significant issues in capture stress, handling, transport, or holding protocols.
- **Protocol Gaps Identified**: The high mortality event triggered internal USFWS correspondence, including:
  1. A memorandum from the Manager of [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] documenting handling conditions at that facility.
  2. A memorandum from Wayne Starnes ([[U.S. National Museum]]) proposing protocols for distribution of **tissues and carcasses** from deceased specimens.
- **Governance Request**: ASU researchers formally requested:
  1. Participation in discussions concerning capture/handling protocol development.
  2. Access to draft protocols and comparative capture/handling procedures used for other imperiled species at Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
- **Specimen Allocation Dispute**: A significant concern raised was the apparent **unauthorized reallocation** of bonytail specimens, tissues, and skeletal structures — collected at ASU's expense under specific permits — to third-party projects such as the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation's **"Gila Taxonomy Project"**, despite ASU having no contractual obligation or agreement to supply bonytail material for that specific initiative.
- **Historical Context**: This letter reflects a broader, decades-long collaborative (though sometimes contentious) relationship between academic researchers (ASU) and federal agencies (USFWS) in managing recovery efforts for both [[Bonytail]] and the sympatric [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Lower Colorado River Basin.
