# Evolution and Biogeography of the Springsnail Genus Tryonia

#malacology #freshwater-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This Smithsonian Scholarly Studies Program proposal requests **$25,966** over two years (Year 1: $14,640; Year 2: $11,326) to study the evolution and biogeography of [[Tryonia]], a genus of small freshwater snails ("[[Springsnails]]") in the family [[Hydrobiidae]]. The project is led by Robert Hershler ([[Smithsonian Institution]], National Museum of Natural History) with co-PI Margaret Mulvey ([[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]]).

The study combines morphological data (shell, operculum, radula, anatomy, ciliation) with allozyme data from starch gel electrophoresis to build a cladistic phylogeny. That phylogeny will then be used to generate and test biogeographic hypotheses using [[Vicariance Biogeography]], and compared with published histories of western drainages and with studies of western fishes. Anticipated products are a formal taxonomic revision of Tryonia and a synthetic review paper on its evolution and biogeography.

## Background & Description

Western North American springsnails are small (1–8 mm shell length), gill-breathing gastropods that brood their young and are obligate inhabitants of water. They disperse slowly, so most species are restricted to single springs or spring systems. Their distributions are therefore closely tied to regional drainage history, and their evolutionary histories are considered comparable to those of [[Western Fishes]].

Tryonia was chosen because it is the only large western hydrobiid genus that is well known in content and distribution. Its key features include:

- **21 described Recent species**, with two additional undescribed, locally endemic species from the northern Great Basin (Monitor Valley, Steptoe Valley).
- **16 of the 21 species** are restricted to single springs or spring systems.
- Distribution along a broad arc from the Pecos River west to the California coast and from the northern Great Basin into Mexico, plus two eastern species in peninsular Florida.
- Many species are often thermal and highly mineralized, indicating unusual physiological tolerance. The exception is *T. imitator*, which lives in brackish coastal waters.
- Female snails are ovoviviparous. *[[Tryonia Protea]]* is [[Parthenogenesis|parthenogenetic]], and several species show rarity and/or reduced male body size.
- Some species are "miniaturized," maturing at roughly one-third of typical adult size.
- No modern revision or classification exists. The genus was previously described as "often a series of monotypic species-groups with no evident mutual relationships," and its monophyly has not been corroborated.

The Principal Investigator has a strong publication record on this fauna. Prior work includes the 1990–1993 [[Great Basin Springsnail Survey]] (over 200 collections, most representing new records) and a 1991 revision and phylogenetic analysis of the genus [[Pyrgulopsis]]. The PI's CV lists more than 125 lots of anatomical material for most Tryonia species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage history and geology.** The proposal explicitly ties snail evolution to physical history, including accretion of terranes onto California and Nevada, development of the Gulf of California and its inland embayment, opening of the Rio Grande Rift, crustal extension, rotation of intracontinental subplates, mountain and basin formation, and development of lacustrine habitats. *Tryonia protea* has a disjunct distribution that has been interpreted as indicating a former connection between the [[Colorado River Drainage]] and the Bonneville Basin.

**Species flocks.** Clusters of closely related, locally endemic Tryonia occur, often syntopically, in [[Death Valley]] (four species) and [[Ash Meadows]] (three species). These clusters provide a test of whether invertebrates form [[Species Flocks]] comparable to those described in lacustrine fishes.

**Parallel fish studies.** The [[Cyprinodon Pupfishes]] of the western deserts share similar distributions and have developed local species flocks, making them a useful comparison for biogeographic congruence.

**Conservation status.** Tryonia are largely restricted to national parks, monuments, and wildlife refuges. One New Mexican species is listed as Endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USDI 1991a), and 13 western species are candidates for listing (USDI 1991b). The proposal notes that many species are as threatened as the fishes associated with them, and the genetic database is intended to support management programs.

**Excluded taxa.** Several taxa were deliberately excluded. *T. pazcuarensis* (near Mexico City) and an undescribed northern Mexican fauna (Chihuahua, Coahuila, Durango) were omitted because of permit problems in Mexico. *T. diaboli* may not be based on Recent material, *T. hertleini* probably went extinct after its type locality dried, and Hawaiian records are likely introductions.

## Key Findings

**Research objectives**

1. Reconstruct the evolutionary history of Tryonia using morphological (shell and anatomy) and allozyme data.
2. Use the phylogeny to generate and test biogeographic hypotheses explaining current distributions.
3. Compare results with similar studies of western fishes.
4. Test whether co-occurring Tryonia form spring-dwelling species flocks comparable to lacustrine fish flocks.
5. Study the evolution of parthenogenesis and miniaturization within the genus.
6. Produce a genetic database to support management of species that are largely confined to protected areas.

**Methods**

- **Fieldwork:** 53 collections of Tryonia for electrophoresis, plus six outgroup populations (*Aphaostracon*, *Littoridinops*, and *Pyrgophorus* from Florida), for a total of 59 populations. Widespread species (*T. imitator*, *T. protea*) will be sampled at 4–6 sites, and most others at 1–3 sites. Each population yields 40 adult snails, frozen in the field and stored at −70 °C.
- **Field schedule:** Four trips totaling 38 days (California, Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas, 21 days; California, 7 days; Florida, 10 days; plus a Death Valley trip funded separately).
- **Morphology:** Up to 175 potential characters from shell, anatomy, and reproductive biology, examined using [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]], dissection, and camera lucida illustration.
- **Allozymes:** Protein electrophoresis for 20 or more loci, with 40 individuals per population. *T. protea* is run on every gel as a reference.
- **Phylogenetic analysis:** Morphological and allozyme data are analyzed separately and combined. Methods include distance Wagner analysis, parsimony in PAUP 3.0 and HENNIG86, and character-state mapping in CLADOS. Outgroups root all analyses.
- **Biogeography:** Area cladograms are compared with the [[Cladistic Analysis|cladistic]] phylogeny, the drainage-history literature, and fish biogeography.

**Justification for allozymes:** Earlier hydrobiid phylogenies (Ponder and Clark 1990; Hershler In Press) were polytomous and poorly resolved, owing to high homoplasy and few synapomorphies. The proposal states that Tryonia morphological variation is limited, so biochemical data are needed. A recent California study found fixed allelic differences at 14 of 20 loci between two strongly differentiated species.

**Personnel and timeline**

- The PI devotes about 30% of his time over two years. He performs all fieldwork and morphological studies and prepares the taxonomic revision.
- The co-PI devotes 15% of her time over two years. She performs all electrophoresis at SREL and works with the PI on analysis and manuscripts.
- All fieldwork is planned for Year 1, with laboratory work completed by mid-Year 2. The final six months are for analysis and writing two main manuscripts.
