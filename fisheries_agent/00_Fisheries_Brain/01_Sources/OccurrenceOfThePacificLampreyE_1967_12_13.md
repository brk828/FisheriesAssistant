# Occurrence of the Pacific Lamprey, *Entosphenus tridentatus*, off Baja California and in Streams of Southern California

## Executive Summary
This 1967 paper by Carl L. Hubbs (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) documents a landmark southward range extension of the anadromous [[Pacific Lamprey]] (*Entosphenus tridentatus*) based on a juvenile specimen captured in a pelagic trawl off western Baja California, México — the southernmost oceanic record for the genus, species, and family [[Petromyzonidae]]. The paper also synthesizes historical and contemporary records of the species in southern California streams (Los Angeles Basin, Ventura Basin), concluding that the species had likely been extirpated from the Los Angeles Plain streams but persisted in the Ventura Basin (Sespe Creek, Santa Clara River, Santa Ynez River). Extensive taxonomic and nomenclatural analysis argues against a valid northern/southern subspecies split, supports separation of *Entosphenus* from *[[Lampetra]]*, and endorses generic status for *Tetrapleurodon*, *Lethenteron*, and *Entosphenus* proper.

## Background & Description
- **Type specimen (Baja California juvenile male, SIO 67-22-4):** 170 mm total length; captured in macrophthalmic (large-eye) juvenile stage.
- **Comparative morphometrics (Table 1)** across life stages/specimens (juvenile to adult, 170–575 mm TL) show:
  - Juveniles have proportionally shorter tails, smaller head/branchial regions, but disproportionately larger eyes (holdover from macrophthalmia stage) compared to adults.
  - Trunk region shows **negative allometry** in growth relative to abdomen size in juveniles — contrasting with positive allometric abdominal growth (linked to fecundity) seen in many other fishes, e.g., the dwarf pelagic shark *[[Euprotomicrus bispinatus]]*.
  - Dorsal fins in the sea-caught juvenile are lower and less contiguous than in adults.
- **Meristic counts:**
  - Trunk myomeres: 68–70 in four southern specimens examined (Baja California, Sespe Creek, Santa Ynez River), overlapping the "northern subspecies" range (68–74) proposed by Creaser and Hubbs (1922), refuting the validity of a low-count "southern subspecies" (*E. t. ciliatus*).
  - One Santa Ynez River adult had only 60 trunk myomeres — an outlier suggesting intraspecific/racial variability rather than clean geographic subspecies division.
  - Dental formula details recorded: supraoral cusps (3), infraoral cusps (5), anterior/posterior circumorals, bicuspid posterior circumorals, total circumoral teeth/cusps, transverse and longitudinal lamina cusp counts, marginal tooth counts.
  - Complete tooth/cusp counts obtainable only from the sea-caught juvenile and the Santa Ynez River maturing male; senile/necrotic Sespe Creek males had eroded or shed teeth precluding full counts.
- **Necrosis staging in spawned males:** Documented progressive post-spawning degeneration — from early necrosis (worn disc-tooth cusps, slight skin damage) to advanced necrosis (obliterated cusps, shed posterior marginals/circumorals, eroded laterals, massive skin damage) — consistent with semelparous death after spawning typical of lampreys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Life history:** Anadromous; ammocete (larval) stage in freshwater streams, followed by macrophthalmic juvenile transformation, seaward migration, parasitic marine feeding phase, and terminal spawning migration back into natal streams, followed by post-spawning death (senescence/necrosis).
- **Marine capture context:** The Baja California specimen was taken in a pelagic trawl (John N. Cobb) fishing for Pacific hake, *[[Merluccius productus]]*, at 28°58.6'N, 115°25.6'W — 29.5 nmi SW of Punta Canoas, in cold, upwelled water (surface temp 15.6°C, temp at 388 m tow depth 7.2°C), over approximately 460 fathom bottom depth. The lamprey is hypothesized to have hitchhiked on a hake host, since 158 hake were caught in the same haul alongside juvenile jack mackerel (*[[Trachurus symmetricus]]*) and small bathypelagic fishes — none considered likely hosts.
- **Host/parasitic relationships:** Documented as an ectoparasite feeding on host fish and marine mammal blood; records include lamprey remains in stomachs of sperm whales, fur seals, and harbor seals, and characteristic scarring on whales and fish from lamprey attachment (dentition imprint used for species identification of scars).
- **Trophic irony noted:** The lamprey preys on sperm whale blood, while sperm whales in turn prey on lampreys — a case of reciprocal predation.
- **Freshwater habitat (southern range):**
  - Historically recorded in Santa Ana River (Riverside), Los Angeles River, San Gabriel River — part of the interconnected "Santa Ana system of streams" on the Los Angeles Plain.
  - By 1962, intensive sampling by Scripps Institution found only introduced fishes in these streams — habitat degradation (dewatering) and invasive Eastern fish species implicated in likely local extirpation of native lamprey populations.
  - Persisted (as of early 1960s) in the Ventura Basin: Sespe Creek (tributary of Santa Clara River), with specimens collected in varying life stages (ammocete to spent adult), suggesting spawning in **late March–April**, with migration timed to brief high-water periods in winter when depleted streams reach the sea.
  - Additional adult record from the lagoon at the mouth of Santa Ynez River, Santa Barbara County (1941).
- **Sympatric/confounded species:** An undescribed nonparasitic species of *[[Lampetra]]* (fewer than 65 trunk myomeres) co-occurred in San Gabriel and Santa Ana rivers; many historical ammocete records attributed to *E. tridentatus* may actually represent this unnamed *Lampetra* species, confounding earlier subspecies designations.
- **Broader oceanic distribution:** At-sea records compiled range from Monterey Bay (southernmost known major stream-run region) northward through San Francisco Bay, San Pablo Bay, off Oregon, Puget Sound, Barkley Sound, Coal Harbour, Strait of Georgia, Queen Charlotte Islands, Skeena River mouth, the Aleutian area, and the Bering Sea (both U.S. and Soviet portions) — with predator stomach contents (sperm whale, fur seal, harbor seal) as an important record source.

## Key Findings
1. **Major range extension:** The Baja California specimen (170 mm juvenile, captured March 9, 1964) represents the southernmost oceanic record of *E. tridentatus* and of the family Petromyzonidae in the eastern Pacific, paralleling similar southward strays of other anadromous fishes (salmon, sturgeon, shad) attributed to cold upwelling conditions along northwestern Baja California.
2. **Origin uncertain but inferred:** The stray individual's origin cannot be pinpointed but is hypothesized to derive from streams tributary to Monterey Bay — the southernmost region of known massive spawning runs — with transport southward likely facilitated by hitchhiking on a host fish (probably Pacific hake).
3. **Local extirpation in Los Angeles Basin:** Native lamprey populations (*E. tridentatus* and an unnamed nonparasitic *Lampetra*) in Los Angeles, San Gabriel, and Santa Ana rivers had likely been extirpated by the early 1960s due to stream dewatering and introduction of competitive non-native fishes.
4. **Persistence in Ventura Basin:** *E. tridentatus* continued to inhabit Sespe Creek and related Santa Clara River drainage as of 1962, with evidence for a spring spawning period.
5. **Subspecies invalidated:** Trunk myomere counts from the newly examined southern specimens (68–70, with one outlier at 60) span and exceed the range previously used to define a "southern subspecies" (*E. t. ciliatus*), refuting the earlier north/south subspecies split proposed by Creaser and Hubbs (1922); apparent past low counts are attributed to methodological errors (miscounts on poorly preserved specimens) and to conflation with the unnamed *Lampetra* species.
6. **Nomenclatural resolution:** The correct authorship is designated as *Entosphenus tridentatus* (Gairdner in Richardson), per Article 50 of the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature, since Gairdner supplied the name and diagnostic description (three conspicuous maxillary teeth) even though Richardson (1836) formally published it.
7. **Generic taxonomy affirmed:** The genus *[[Entosphenus]]* is supported as distinct from *[[Lampetra]]* based on presence/absence of posterior circumoral teeth (a criterion previously questioned by Berg 1931 due to misinterpretation of incompletely transformed specimens). The paper endorses elevating *Tetrapleurodon*, *Lethenteron*, and *Entosphenus* (proper) — previously treated as subgenera by Creaser and Hubbs (1922) — to full generic rank.
8. **Undescribed taxa noted:** Two undescribed nonparasitic lamprey taxa are flagged for future taxonomic work: (a) a nonparasitic *Lampetra* species in the Los Angeles Basin streams, and (b) an unnamed nonparasitic derivative of *Entosphenus* in the Klamath and Sacramento river systems.
9. **Rarity of oceanic records:** Despite massive stream spawning runs throughout the North Pacific, documented at-sea captures/records of *E. tridentatus* remain sparse, with this study providing the first record south of Monterey Bay.
