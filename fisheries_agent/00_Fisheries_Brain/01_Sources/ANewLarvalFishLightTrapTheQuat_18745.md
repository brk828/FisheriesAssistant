# Quatrefoil Trap and Diver-Operated Underwater Pumping Device: Two Aquatic Sampling Gears

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fish #sampling-gear

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-part methods paper describing new aquatic sampling gear. The first part presents the [[Quatrefoil Trap]], a plexiglas larval fish light trap built to seven design criteria and validated in a [[Stream Larval Fish Habitat Partitioning Study]] in Kentucky. The second part describes a portable, manually operated [[Underwater Pumping Device]] used by two divers to collect eggs, invertebrates, periphyton and sediment in Great Lakes habitats. Both gears are designed to be cheaper, simpler and easier to transport than the alternatives they replace.

## Background & Description

### Quatrefoil Trap

Light trapping is known to attract many larval fish and other organisms, although the mechanisms remain poorly understood (Verheijen 1958; Faber 1981). [[Kindschi Et Al 1979]] showed that light trapping suits field conditions in the region and set seven design criteria: easy entry and difficult escape, large capture area relative to trap size, a broadly distributed light source, ruggedness, reliable electronics, portability, and economic feasibility.

The trap is built from plexiglas tube (7.62 cm O.D., 0.32 cm wall) and sheet, and must be machine-shop fabricated. Key specifications:

- Total weight with battery pack: 1.8 kg (4 lb)
- Height: 40.4 cm (storage) and 44.8 cm (with lamp)
- Top plate diameter 19 cm; bottom plate 24.3 cm
- Collection pan: stainless steel bowl, 24 cm diameter, with a 7 cm opening covered by 120 micron polyester mesh
- Light source: [[No. 1490 Miniature Bayonet Bulb]] (3.2 V, 0.16 A, 0.2 cp, rated life 3000 h), waterproofed with silicone sealant
- Light distribution: a spirally scribed plexiglas rod suspended at the trap centre
- Entry slot width: 0.15 cm, chosen from larval fish measurements to admit most larvae while excluding larger predators
- Power: two D-cell batteries in a weatherproofed freezer-bag pack

The trap can sit on the bottom or be suspended in the water column, provided the battery pack stays above the water surface.

### Underwater Pumping Device

Pumps have been used to collect zooplankton, alewife eggs, and lake trout eggs and fry (Evans 1981; Nigro and Ney 1982; Dorr et al. 1981; Stauffer 1981). Earlier systems needed large electric or gasoline pumps, mains power, and surface support crews, making them costly and labour-intensive. The device described here is compact, portable and hand-operated by two divers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trap was used in a [[Habitat Partitioning]] study of stream larval fishes, sampling low-flow and current habitats. Push seines sampled microhabitats immediately before trap deployment, while [[Ichthyoplankton Drift Nets]] sampled current areas. The pumping device targets benthic and epibenthic habitats, including reef substrates where lake trout spawn and alewife eggs settle. Target taxa include epibenthic algae, zooplankton, sediment and detritus, periphyton, invertebrates (fingernail clams, snails, Hydra, crayfish), fish eggs, and young-of-the-year sculpins (Cottus spp.). The authors note that suction intake is designed with large internal dimensions to minimise damage to fragile organisms and eggs. Sampling was conducted at about 10 m depth in eastern Lake Michigan.

## Key Findings

**Quatrefoil Trap**
- The trap was validated in a Kentucky stream study by [[Floyd 1983]]. It captured 6,057 larval fish of 28 species in total.
- Light traps caught 4,980 individuals of 25 species. Push seines caught 994 individuals of 20 species. Drift nets caught 83 specimens of 12 species.
- Equipment for eight units cost $205 (1982), an average of $25.60 per unit. Planning and tooling took 70 hours, and construction and installation took 39 hours. Labour cost was not valued because shop rates differ between institutions.
- Critical design points are the machining precision of the tube geometry, the stress relief cut on commercial plexiglas tubing, and the entry slot width. Other dimensions are not critical.
- A bulb up to 3.5 cp can replace the standard bulb without redesign, but that would mean more frequent battery changes or a heavier pack.

**Underwater Pumping Device**
- Two hand-operated diaphragm pumps in tandem give a flow of 60–120 L/min (about 15–30 gpm).
- The device weighs about 6.8 kg (15 lb) and costs about $280. Using cheaper diaphragm pumps could cut the cost to about $125.
- One diver can pump for 15–30 minutes while a second diver directs the intake and probes the substrate.
- The authors report the device is durable and easy to transport above or below water.
