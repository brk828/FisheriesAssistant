# Hiko White River Springfish Conservation Efforts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports a captive-breeding and reintroduction programme for the [[Crenichthys Baileyi Grandis]] (Hiko White River springfish), an endemic spring fish of south central Nevada. Wild fish from [[Crystal Springs]] were cultured in the laboratory and their progeny were released into [[Hiko Spring]], where the subspecies had been extirpated in 1967, and into [[Blue Link Spring]], a fishless refugium. The Hiko Spring restocking appears successful, but the authors warn that the introduced [[Cichlasoma Nigrofasciatum]] (convict cichlid) may create unsuitable conditions there, as it already has at Crystal Springs. The authors conclude that federal endangered status is justified and likely.

## Background & Description

The White River springfish is one of five subspecies of the genus *Crenichthys* found in spring pools and outflows of the pluvial [[White River Drainage]] in south central Nevada. A related species, [[Crenichthys Nevadae]], occurs in Railroad Valley. Historically, *C. b. grandis* was restricted to Crystal Springs and Hiko Spring in Lincoln County.

Two main threats are documented:

- **Hiko Spring:** The subspecies was extirpated in 1967 after the 1965 introduction of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and other non-native fishes. The bass apparently moved from a nearby reservoir through an irrigation canal. They disappeared from the spring in the early 1970s, and conversion of the canal to a pipe makes re-establishment via this route unlikely. [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Poecilia Mexicana]] also became established there in 1965.
- **Crystal Springs:** Illegal introduction of tropical aquarium fishes, including the convict cichlid and Poecilia mexicana, is positively correlated with the decline of the springfish. The cichlid was introduced in the mid to late 1970s.

Earlier work cited by the authors indicates that springfish populations are severely depressed where the convict cichlid is present. Gambusia and Poecilia have a less harmful effect alone than in combination with the cichlid. Hiko Spring was considered a good candidate for reintroduction because it lacked the convict cichlid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Egg and larval biology.** Eggs of *C. b. grandis* are about 1 mm in diameter, clear and adhesive. Comparative egg sizes reported are about 2 mm for *Crenichthys baileyi moapae* and 1.5 mm for *Crenichthys nevadae*. Egg size from Crystal Springs varies by ova class (Class I 1.40–1.58 mm; Class II 0.93–1.04 mm; Class III 0.51–0.53 mm). Newly hatched larvae stay near the surface in the first week, then move downward and feed along the bottom or throughout the water column by the end of the second week.

**Temperature tolerance.** In the outdoor [[Experimental Stream]], water temperature ranged from a low of 5 °C (air 8 °C, December 1984) to 35.0 °C (air 40.56 °C, 30 August 1984). The stream fell to about 10 °C on 23 November 1984 and stayed below 10 °C through at least February 1985. The authors state this species shows greater [[Cold Tolerance]] than previously shown for the genus. Nearly all fish captured in December 1984 were disoriented, probably from prolonged cold exposure, but many recovered in warmer laboratory aquaria.

**Water-quality sensitivity.** Earlier work on *C. b. moapae* indicates intolerance of water changes larger than 10% of total volume. Heavy mortality of larvae moved at about 5 mm total length, in one case 84.9%, suggests that stocking larger fish would give better results.

**Blue Link Spring refugium.** This spring-fed, fishless pool is in Mineral County. It arises from an artesian source (about 38 °C; flow about 19 gal/min), flows about 170 feet into a pond of about 0.1 acre, and then drains about 600 feet before disappearing into the substrate. Pond outflow is about 14 gal/min. A two-inch, 150-foot pipeline was installed by the [[Nevada Department Of Wildlife]] and the Bureau of Land Management to warm the pond. It raised the outflow to 37.5 °C, with average pond temperature near 24 °C on 28 January.

## Key Findings

**1. Captive propagation succeeded in the laboratory.**
- 20 wild fish were removed from Crystal Springs in October and November 1983: 12 males and 1 female on 14 October, and 3 males and 4 females on 4 November.
- Adults were held at 27 °C and fed commercial food twice daily. Spawning was induced by timed introduction of males.
- Two of four aquarium spawnings were followed closely:
  - Spawning 1: 39 eggs, 31 hatched, 27 larvae alive after 30 days (**87% survival**).
  - Spawning 2: 40 eggs, 33 larvae, 27 alive after 30 days (**82% survival**).
- Average time to hatching at 27 °C was 8 days, compared with 9 days for *C. nevadae* at 27 °C and 10 days for *C. b. moapae* at 25 °C.
- Stock fell from 20 to 10 fish by June 1984 because of mortality.

**2. The experimental stream produced successful reproduction.**
- Four males and three females were placed in the stream on 26 May 1984.
- Reproduction was first noted on 27 June 1984, and three size classes were present through late fall.
- 158 fish were moved to the laboratory in early winter. Live fish were last seen on 28 December 1984. Those captured that day included 11 dead fish; the live count is illegible in the scan. No fish were seen after that date.

**3. Hiko Spring restocking appears successful, but the convict cichlid is a risk.**
- 35 juveniles were released on 24 January 1984 and 40 more on 22 March 1984.
- Trapping on 22 March and 18 July 1984 did not detect the springfish. On 18 July, six convict cichlids (about 1.5% of the catch) were taken, apparently introduced between March and July 1984.
- On 11 February 1985, up to 14 springfish were seen at one time in one area, and one female was captured. The authors infer that the stocked fish reproduced. Convict cichlids had also reproduced.

**4. Blue Link Spring refugium stocking.**
- On 12 February 1985, 59 fish reared at UNLV were released into Blue Link Spring.
- About 157 fish reared in captivity are held in the laboratory for future release.
- Seven fish from Crystal Springs, removed in February 1984, were added to the laboratory stock.
- The authors state that this release, if successful, will establish the only population free of exotic fishes.

**5. Conservation conclusions and recommendations.**
- The reintroduction into Hiko Spring "has apparently succeeded," but the springfish and cichlid populations there are expanding together. The authors expect a response similar to that at Crystal Springs, where the springfish population remains quite low.
- Documenting population responses at Hiko Spring is a management priority.
- Classification as a federally endangered species "appears justified and is most likely in the near future." The subspecies was proposed for federal endangered status.
- The study was supported by the [[Nevada Department Of Wildlife]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
