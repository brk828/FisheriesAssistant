# Native Fish Physical Acclimation Study: Exercise and Predator Exposure of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Suckers (2006 Progress Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft administrative progress report, sent with a cover letter from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Denver Field Unit, documents the second year of a three-year study of how hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) respond to predators and to sustained current. Its aims are to test whether exercise and pre-stocking predator exposure improve short-term survival after stocking. The study was run with the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] at [[Achii Hanyo]], a satellite of [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

The main results were:
- Exercised fish survived [[Flathead Catfish]] predation better than controls (64 vs. 52 survivors from 100 fish per group; difference of 22%, p = 0.046).
- Exercised fish showed a non-significant 6.1% gain in [[Critical Swimming Performance]] (p = 0.171).
- Naive suckers did not avoid predators on first contact and were initially attracted to them. Avoidance appeared only after a predation event, which supports the view that it is a learned behavior.

Predator-prey trials were partly compromised by predator stress and equipment losses. The authors plan a third year, which will add [[Bonytail]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The razorback sucker is the focal native fish. Hatchery-raised individuals are released into the [[Colorado River]] system, but the report argues that current culturing practices leave them unprepared for predators. The bonytail is a planned secondary prey species for 2007.

**Predators.** Wild-caught [[Flathead Catfish]] were used as the main predator. Captured from the Colorado River, five individuals in the large tank averaged 680 mm total length (range 572–838 mm) and weighed 2.18–6.76 kg. Two catfish used in the avoidance tests averaged 605 mm and 665 mm in length. [[Largemouth Bass]] were proposed as predators for 2007.

**Experimental facilities.** Four 1.2 m × 1 m fiberglass rearing tanks had center dividers. Two had electric trolling motors (50 lb thrust) that produced velocities up to 25 cm/s, and two served as no-flow controls. Predator trials used circular tanks of 2 m, 4 m, and 7 m diameter. Water depth was held at 1 m.

**Rationale.** Telemetry work cited in the report (Mueller and Marsh 1998) shows stocked suckers disperse quickly and seek shelter, which can put naive fish in contact with predators. Earlier work (Marsh and Brooks 1989) recorded catfish removing an entire stocking of hundreds of young suckers in under four days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Behavioral ecology.** Hatchery suckers schooled along the tank bottom by day and dispersed through the water column at night. They were drawn to [[Shelter Use|predator shelters]] and to predators. The report links this to a general pattern: hatchery fish have the physiological means to defend themselves but lack learned recognition of predators. The authors cite related work showing that fright pheromones alone do not trigger avoidance; prey must associate predator odor with a traumatic event (Brown and Smith 1998; Brown and Godin 1999).

**Flow and exercise.** Current was set at 0.075 m/s and raised weekly in 0.025 m/s steps to 0.300 m/s over 10 weeks. Fish were exposed for 8 hours per weekday. Suckers in the treatment tanks swam freely, and no fatigue or stress was observed.

**Husbandry and stress.** Mishaps included corroded and failed wire screens, and a loss of aeration when a pipe coupling came loose. Roughly a quarter of the fish in tanks 3 and 4 were lost in the aeration failure. About 30 of the smallest fish died after gilling in poultry wire or being struck by the propeller.

## Key Findings

### 1. Predator Avoidance and Learning

- **Trial 1 (2-m tank, no shelters, 7 days):** Suckers co-existed with catfish without predation and used both sides of the tank about equally (46% with predators; n = 20 observations). No avoidance was seen.
- **Trial 2 (4-m tank, pre-predation, 7 days):** Suckers spent 52% of observations with predators (n = 16), again showing no avoidance.
- **Trial 2 (post-predation, 17 days):** After the first sucker was lost, prey shifted strongly toward the sanctuary. Sanctuary use was about twice that of the predator zone (64% vs. 36%; n = 50). An alternative figure gives 67% vs. 33% (n = 12).
- **Trial 3 (sanctuary reduced to one quarter of the tank):** Three suckers were eaten within four days. Suckers spent 63% of observations in the sanctuary (n = 12), which the authors describe as 6 times greater use than expected from area alone. A weighted-area analysis gave a preference of 88% for the sanctuary (n = 21).

The authors conclude that naive fish were not wary at first, and that avoidance was learned after exposure.

### 2. Exercise and Swimming Performance

- Treated fish averaged a critical flow velocity of 5.3 BL/s; the control average is not legible in the scan. The reported increase is 6.1% (p = 0.171, not significant). The authors note the median difference was 11% and that exercised fish were more uniform.
- Thirty pairs were tested in the flow chamber. Fish lengths ranged from 20 to 24 cm.
- Ucrit data (BL/s) are listed in ascending order for each group. Many fish, especially in the drafting "sweet spot" that was later screened off, maintained position without swimming. The chamber was modified to reduce this, which shortened the working section to about 80 cm.

### 3. Predator-Prey Survival

- Ten trials each used 20 suckers (10 treatment, 10 control), for 100 fish per group.
- In the trial summary, 64 treatment fish survived against 52 controls. This 22% difference was significant (p = 0.046) and is the report's main result.
- Survivors averaged larger than those eaten (p = 0.002), which suggests survival increased with body size. The text labels survivors as n = 84 and eaten as n = 116. The survivor counts (64 + 52 = 116) suggest these labels are swapped.
- Mean lengths of treatment and control fish were similar at the outset (214.3 mm vs. 214.8 mm; p = 0.842).
- Catfish fed about once every four days at first. Later they fed on alternate nights.
- Trial recapture took under an hour, and catfish were not disturbed by the routine.

### 4. Failed Predator Exposure

The planned pre-stocking exposure in the flow tanks failed. Moving the catfish caused stress, and they stopped feeding for more than two weeks. The 48-hour exposure gave no evidence of predation. This was an unanticipated design flaw. Exercise was still tested successfully, but the predator-exposure hypothesis remains untested.

### 5. Recommendations for 2007

- Move prey, not predators, so that predation is more likely.
- Use groups of at least 12 fish (instead of 10) to allow for losses.
- Test [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] for survival after exercise and pre-exposure. Test bonytail for swimming stamina and food conversion.
- Use [[Largemouth Bass]] as a second predator.
- Run exercise from mid-January through April, with trials from mid-March to late May.
- Requirements are at least 340 razorback suckers (100 control, 120 pre-exposed control, 120 exercised pre-exposed) and 400 bonytail (130 control, 120 control pre-exposed, 150 exercised pre-exposed, plus 60 for the flow chamber).
