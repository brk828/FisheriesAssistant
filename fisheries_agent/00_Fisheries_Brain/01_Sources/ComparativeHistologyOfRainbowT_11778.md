# Comparative Histology of Rainbow Trout and Channel Catfish in Intensive Static-Water Aquaculture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examined cellular-level (histological) effects of un-ionized ammonia (NH3) on [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]] reared in intensive [[Static-Water Aquaculture]] earthen ponds. Trout were sampled from nine ponds in the 1979–80 winter season and catfish from twelve ponds in summer 1981. Both species developed gill lesions characteristic of ammonia toxicity, while no kidney lesions were found in either species. Liver findings differed: trout with the most severe gill damage showed reduced glycogen vacuolation, whereas all catfish livers appeared well-fed. The authors conclude that NH3 fluctuations, driven by diel plankton activity, may impair respiration and that better water quality management could improve growth and disease resistance.

## Background & Description

The authors identify dissolved oxygen (DO) depletion and [[Un-Ionized Ammonia]] accumulation as the main constraints on aquacultural production. Static-water systems are common: in the United States they account for at least twice the production of [[Flowing-Water Aquaculture]], and the ratio is larger worldwide. Prior work on NH3 toxicity (e.g., Burrows 1964; Smart 1976; Thurston et al. 1978) was largely conducted under relatively constant DO and NH3 conditions, which do not reflect static ponds.

In static ponds, NH3 fluctuates strongly on a diel cycle because daily pH shifts result from [[Plankton Photosynthesis and Respiration]]. NH3 peaks coincide with near-maximum daily DO. The authors note that the role of NH3 in limiting static-water fish production had not been investigated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mechanism of toxicity:** Chronic NH3 toxicity is thought to involve respiratory impairment, and DO levels influence the effects of sublethal exposure (Downing and Merkins 1955; Merkins and Downing 1957).
- **Water chemistry in trout ponds:** Dissolved oxygen was held above 5 mg/L using 0.25-kW mechanical aerators, so that NH3 rather than DO would be the principal limiting factor. Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) ranged from 0.3 to 1.38 mg/L and was unrelated to stocking rate (P > 0.05).
- **Water chemistry in catfish ponds:** Average TAN did not exceed 1.0 mg/L; individual determinations reached at most 2.33 mg/L. Diurnal DO and pH were monitored on 24-hour cycles, and NH3 was calculated from pH.
- **Methods of NH3 calculation:** Total ammonia was measured by the [[Phenate Method]], and NH3 was derived using the equilibrium formula of Emerson et al. (1975) with ionization constants from Bates and Pinching (1949).

## Key Findings

**Rainbow Trout (winter 1979–80, nine 0.04-ha ponds):**
- Stocked 55 g fish on November 17, 1979, at 29,640–59,280 fish/ha to create a range of NH3 exposures; ponds were drained 120 days after stocking.
- Average NH3-N ranged from 2.3 to 8.4 µg/L; average daily maxima ranged from 8.3 to 48.7 µg/L.
- No gill damage was seen before January 19 (57 days after stocking); thereafter all ponds showed [[Gill Lesions]], mainly edema and aneurisms, with less common epithelial hyperplasia and lamellar fusion.
- Gill lesion severity did not correlate with NH3 concentration.
- Of 91 gill sections examined, only five contained *Trichodina*; no pathogenic bacteria were isolated.
- Fish with the most severe gill damage showed reduced [[Liver Glycogen Vacuolation]], similar to starved trout.
- No trunk kidney lesions were observed.

**Channel Catfish (summer 1981, twelve 0.04-ha ponds):**
- Stocked 10,000 fish of 14.5 g per hectare on March 1, 1981; fed 4% body weight daily; feed conversion assumed at 1.8; ponds drained 120 days after stocking.
- Minimum, average, and maximum daily NH3 averaged 4, 42, and 100 µg/L; average daily maxima ranged from 63 to 183 µg/L.
- Gill epithelial hyperplasia occurred in some fish on every sampling date, starting at lamellar bases and in severe cases filling lamellar troughs by about half; edema was less common.
- All catfish livers were highly glycogen-vacuolated, typical of well-fed fish.
- No liver or kidney lesions and no pathogenic bacteria were found.
- Six fish per pond were sampled on May 29, July 20, September 1, and October 6.

**Overall conclusions:**
- Gill damage occurred in both species despite low average exposure, indicating that fluctuating NH3 environments are harmful.
- Gill lesions likely impaired respiration, suggesting that improved water quality management could raise growth rates and reduce disease susceptibility.
- Trunk kidney histology was unaffected in both species, contrary to some earlier reports ([[Flis 1968]]; Larmoyeaux and Piper 1973; Thurston et al. 1978).
