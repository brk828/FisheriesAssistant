# Effects of Interim Flows from Glen Canyon Dam on Aquatic Resources of Lower Colorado River from Diamond Creek to Lake Mead - Quarterly Report No. 2

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #river-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This quarterly report documents the second field investigation (September 27 - October 9, 1992) of aquatic resources in the [[Colorado River]] from [[Diamond Creek]] (RM 226) to [[Lake Mead]] below [[Pierce Ferry]] (RM 286). The study was initiated by the [[Hualapai Wildlife Management Department]] with technical assistance from BIO/WEST, Inc. to monitor effects of interim flows from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on fish populations, habitat, and aquatic food resources. The 60-mile reach was divided into four longitudinal strata (A-D) representing different habitat complexes from steep canyon with swift runs to open alluvial delta and lake inflow.

Fourteen fish species representing eight families were captured using seven sampling gear types (electrofishing, gill nets, trammel nets, hoop nets, minnow traps, trot lines, seines). Only two species—[[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)—are native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]; the remaining 12 species are non-native. [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) dominated mainstem habitats, while red shiner and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) dominated tributaries. The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were not observed during this trip.

Water temperatures in the mainstem ranged from 14.5°C to 17.5°C, while [[Pierce Ferry]] temperatures reached 20.0°C to 23.0°C. Maximum river stage fluctuation of 42 cm was measured over 27.7 hours at [[Spencer Canyon]] (261.5 miles below Glen Canyon Dam). Thirty drift samples were collected at three locations during ascending and descending hydrographs to monitor drift volume at different flow stages.

## Background & Description

Before impoundment by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] supported eight native fish species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*). Colorado squawfish, roundtail chub, and bonytail have been extirpated from Grand Canyon. Humpback chub and razorback sucker are federally endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Interim flow criteria for [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operation were implemented in August 1991 to minimize damage to the Grand Canyon ecosystem. Few detailed investigations have been conducted on aquatic resources in [[Lower Grand Canyon]] and [[Lake Mead]] inflow prior to this study. Previous intensive fishery investigations extended only to Diamond Creek (RM 226) as part of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase I and Phase II.

The study reach was divided into four strata based on habitat characteristics:
- **Stratum A** (RM 226-239): Steep, narrow canyon with swift deep runs, vertical cliffs and talus, boulder-cobble-gravel substrate; major tributaries include [[Diamond Creek]] and [[Travertine Canyon]]
- **Stratum B** (RM 239-252): Steep canyon with moderate runs, sedimentary lake deposits, variable shoreline with vegetation; major tributaries include [[Separation Canyon]], [[Spencer Canyon]], [[Surprise Canyon]], [[Lost Creek]]
- **Stratum C** (RM 252-265): Wide canyon with continuous vegetated sedimentary deposits (willows, tamarisk, Baccus), sand/silt substrate; major tributary [[Quartermaster Canyon]]
- **Stratum D** (RM 265-286): Large open canyon with expansive vegetated lake deposits, low gradient braided channels with backwaters and isolated pools; major tributary [[Emery Falls Canyon]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic ecosystem of [[Lower Grand Canyon]] represents a transition zone between riverine and lacustrine (lake) environments, heavily influenced by [[Lake Mead]] water levels and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The reach from Diamond Creek to Lake Mead may contain significant populations of endangered species and serves as a potential invasion corridor for predaceous and competing non-native fishes from Lake Mead into upper Grand Canyon.

Water quality monitoring using [[Hydrolab Surveyor II]] and [[Hydrolab Datasonde]] with datalogger recorded parameters including temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, and redox potential at multiple locations. Mainstem water temperatures during late September-early October ranged from 14.7°C minimum to 17.5°C maximum above Pierce Bay, with warmer temperatures (20.0-23.0°C) in Pierce Bay itself, reflecting Lake Mead influence.

River stage monitoring at three locations documented flow fluctuations from Glen Canyon Dam operations. Maximum stage changes were: 36 cm at [[Bridge Canyon]] (RM 234.9) over 20.7 hours, 42 cm at Spencer Canyon (RM 246.0) over 27.7 hours, and 17 cm near Quartermaster Canyon (RM 262.0) over 14.2 hours. These fluctuations potentially affect spawning habitat, nursery areas, and food base productivity.

Temporary bench marks (TBM) were established at Bridge Canyon and below Quartermaster Canyon (marked with 1-cm yellow enamel paint dots on vertical rock faces) to facilitate reoccupation for long-term stage monitoring. Drift net sampling (560 micron mesh, 12×18 inch opening) collected 30 samples during rising and falling hydrographs to assess macroinvertebrate and organic matter transport.

The presence of extensive sedimentary lake deposits in Strata C and D, heavily vegetated with willows, tamarisk, and emergent vegetation, creates backwater and side channel habitats potentially suitable for native fish nurseries but also favorable for non-native species establishment. The braided channel region (RM 267-270) contained numerous young-of-year flannelmouth suckers, indicating successful reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Fish Community Composition**: A total of 2,078 individual fish representing 14 species and eight families were captured. Non-native species dominated: [[Red Shiner]] (1,224 individuals), [[Threadfin Shad]] (297), [[Fathead Minnow]] (203), [[Common Carp]] (167), [[Mosquitofish]] (40), [[Bluegill]] (37), [[Channel Catfish]] (31), [[Largemouth Bass]] (22). Native species were limited to [[Speckled Dace]] (37) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (16 total, including numerous young-of-year). Single individuals of [[Striped Bass]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Black Crappie]] were also captured.

**Gear Effectiveness**: Electrofishing (52 samples) and gill/trammel nets (87 combined samples) were most effective for large and medium-sized mainstem fish. Seines (24 samples) and minnow traps (26 samples) effectively captured small fish in shallow habitats and tributaries. One seine haul in the braided channels area produced 16 young flannelmouth suckers from a backwater, indicating successful reproduction in this stratum.

**Spatial Distribution Patterns**: Red shiners first encountered at [[Bridge Canyon]] (RM 235.2) and dominated tributary populations in [[Spencer Creek]], [[Surprise Canyon]], [[Lost Creek]], and [[Quartermaster Canyon]]. Mainstem species composition changed markedly near Pierce Bay, with large numbers of threadfin shad and largemouth bass found only in Pierce Bay and isolated pools near [[Scorpion Island]]. Species composition reflects transition from riverine to lacustrine conditions.

**Endangered Species**: Neither [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) nor [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were observed or captured during Trip No. 2, despite suitable habitat in Stratum A (steep canyon with complex eddy systems) and Stratum D (braided channels with backwaters). The absence of these federally endangered species from apparently suitable habitat warrants continued monitoring.

**Tributary Habitats**: Spencer Canyon contained abundant fish populations in its lower 1 km, requiring further upstream survey. [[Quartermaster Canyon]] featured a large wetland/slough not influenced by mainstem fluctuations. Unlike Trip No. 1, largemouth bass were not encountered in tributary streams during Trip No. 2 but were found in small numbers near Pierce Ferry.

**Parasitology**: Channel catfish encountered during Trip No. 2 did not exhibit the tapeworm infestation frequently observed during Trip No. 1, suggesting temporal or spatial variation in parasite prevalence.

**Hydrologic Monitoring**: River stage fluctuations of 17-42 cm over 14-28 hour periods demonstrate the influence of Glen Canyon Dam operations on downstream habitats. These fluctuations potentially affect egg desiccation, larval stranding, food base productivity, and habitat availability for all life stages.

**Recommendations**: The report identifies 14 specific recommendations including: (1) intensive mainstem seining to determine upstream red shiner distribution, (2) use of 300-foot large-mesh gill nets for mobile large fish, (3) 0.5-inch mesh trammel nets for small mainstem fish like threadfin shad, (4) backpack electrofishing in tributaries, (5) spring sampling of [[Lost Creek]] slough for razorback suckers, (6) intensive sampling of braided region (RM 267-270), (7) monitoring young-of-year suckers in Spencer and Surprise Canyons for possible razorback sucker presence, (8) sampling Quartermaster slough, (9) comprehensive tributary surveys for red shiner extent, (10) establishing permanent sample stations within each stratum and key tributaries, (11) preserving tapeworms from channel catfish for identification, (12) employing angling and trotlines for deep-hole surveys, (13) identifying locations for remote camera stations, and (14) surveying aquatic macroinvertebrates and algal communities.
