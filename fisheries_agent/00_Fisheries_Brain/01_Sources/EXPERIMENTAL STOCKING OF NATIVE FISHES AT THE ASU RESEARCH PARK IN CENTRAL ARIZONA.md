## Executive Summary
In late 1986, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Arizona Game and Fish Department collaboratively stocked three imperiled native fish species—[[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]—into controlled pond habitats at the Arizona State University (ASU) Research Park in Tempe, Arizona. This experimental program was designed as a multi-purpose conservation initiative, combining basic biological research, public outreach, and the establishment of a genetic refuge population ("ark" population) for species facing severe population declines in their native range within the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Background & Description
The source text is a program abstract and does not provide detailed morphometric data (e.g., fin ray counts, standard length measurements) for the specific stocked individuals. However, the taxa involved are well-documented components of the endemic [[Colorado River Basin]] ichthyofauna:
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): A member of the Cyprinidae family, historically characterized by a streamlined, slender body adapted for swift, turbulent river currents.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest native cyprinid in North America, historically an apex predator in the basin.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Catostomidae family, notable for a distinct bony keel or "razorback" ridge behind the head, believed to provide hydrodynamic stability in strong currents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native Habitat:** These three species are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically adapted to a hydrology of high spring flood flows, warm water temperatures, and high turbidity.
- **Experimental Habitat:** The study repurposes artificial pond systems at the [[ASU Research Park]] as a "controlled habitat," allowing researchers to isolate variables that are difficult to control in the wild river systems.
- **Species Interactions:** A primary ecological objective of the stocking is to observe and study the **potential interaction between these native fishes and non-native fish species**, which is a leading hypothesis for the historic decline of these natives in their wild range (e.g., predation/competition from introduced sport fish).
- **Conservation Status:** All three species are explicitly noted as having "imperiled status," reflecting their status as endangered or threatened species due to habitat modification (damming, flow regulation) and non-native species introduction.

## Key Findings
Since this document functions as a project proposal/summary rather than a results-based study, the "key findings" represent the stated objectives and hypotheses of the research program:

1.  **Controlled Biological Study:** The ASU Research Park ponds provide a unique, controlled environment to study the basic biology (growth, survival, reproduction) of *Gila elegans*, *Ptychocheilus lucius*, and *Xyrauchen texanus*—species otherwise difficult to study in turbulent, turbid wild rivers.
2.  **Non-Native Interaction Study:** The stocking allows for direct observation of interactions between native and non-native fishes, addressing a critical knowledge gap regarding species decline mechanisms.
3.  **Public Education:** The program serves as a conservation education tool, exposing the general public to native fish species and raising awareness of their imperiled conservation status.
4.  **Refugia/Ark Population:** The stocking establishes a "secure stock" (refuge population) of these three species, ensuring genetic material and living specimens are preserved for future reintroduction or recovery efforts should wild populations face further decline or extirpation.
5.  **Applied Conservation Goal:** Information gathered is explicitly intended to be applied toward the **recovery of these species in the wild**, linking this ex-situ pond study directly to broader in-situ [[Colorado River Basin]] recovery programs for other imperiled aquatic species.
