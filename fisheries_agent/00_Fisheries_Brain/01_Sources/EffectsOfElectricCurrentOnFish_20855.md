## Executive Summary

This review by Richard S. Wydoski (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Fisheries Center - Leetown) synthesizes the state-of-the-art knowledge (as of the late 1970s) on the physiological and behavioral effects of electric current on fish and aquatic invertebrates. [[Electrofishing]] techniques emerged from a British patent in 1863 and matured as a fisheries management tool after 1950, with major synthesis efforts occurring at the 1957 FAO International Fishing Gear Congress and the 1966 [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC) symposium. The paper covers the neurophysiological basis of fish response to electrical stimuli, categorizes the variables affecting capture efficiency (water medium, electro-technical, physiochemical, and physiological factors), compares AC, DC, and pulsed DC current effects, and reviews mortality/injury data and invertebrate drift responses to electrofishing.

## Background & Description

- **Body shape and susceptibility**: Compressed-body fish (e.g., sunfish) are more susceptible to electric current than fusiform species of equivalent length, due to differing body cross-sectional exposure to voltage gradients.
- **Body size**: Larger fish are more sensitive to electric current than smaller fish because they are subjected to higher body voltage and absorb more total current across a longer body length.
- **Species-specific electrical resistance** (Vibert 1967):
  - Trout: 818 ohm/cm
  - Perch: 963 ohm/cm
  - Carp: 1,149 ohm/cm
  - Gudgeon: 1,228 ohm/cm
- **Resistivity changes with temperature** (carp; Whitney and Pierce 1957):
  | Temperature (°C) | Resistivity (ohm-cm) |
  |---|---|
  | 5 | 2690 |
  | 10 | 1840 |
  | 15 | 1400 |
  | 20 | 975 |
  | 25 | 508 |
- **Physical injuries observed from electrofishing**: respiratory arrest, fractured vertebrae, curvature of the spine, ruptured arteries/veins, extreme dilation of blood vessels, and damage to swim bladders (Hauck 1949; Novotny and Priegel 1974; Pratt 1954; Taylor et al. 1957; Vibert 1967).
- **Sensory systems**: The acoustical-lateral line system of fishes is highly developed, enabling detection of sound and water movement, including anthropogenic noise (e.g., generators, outboard motors) from electrofishing boats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat-related vulnerability**: Anatomical/behavioral adaptation to habitat affects catchability. Bottom-dwelling species such as [[Sculpins]] and [[Darters]] are less readily captured than midwater species such as [[Bluegill]] and shiners; minnows occupying both habitats show intermediate catchability.
- **Water as a conductive medium**: Conductivity is governed by chemical composition and temperature. Potable U.S. waters range from 50–1,500 µmhos/cm; industrial-impacted waters may exceed 10,000 µmhos/cm. Seawater is ~500x more conductive than freshwater, altering electrofishing field requirements.
- **Depth and flow limitations**: Electrofishing is most effective in littoral waters ≤1.5 m deep; waters over 10 feet (~3 m) generally cannot be sampled effectively. Peterman (1978) found water velocities >5 ft/sec adversely affected electrofishing efficiency in the [[Yellowstone River]] by shrinking the effective response area around electrodes.
- **Substrate influence**: Conductive substrates reduce field effectiveness; vegetation growth tied to substrate type can foul or entangle stunned fish, reducing capture success.
- **Light penetration**: Capture efficiency of [[Largemouth Bass]] was negatively correlated with Secchi disc readings (Simpson 1978; Reynolds 1978) — greater light penetration reduced efficiency, motivating night sampling (Latta and Myers 1961).
- **Thermal ecology**: Water temperature governs metabolism and swimming ability, making vulnerability to electrofishing species- and temperature-dependent. Carp and eels ([[Anguilla anguilla]]) are noted as the most difficult fish to capture; pike and pike-perch reportedly the easiest (Vibert 1967), though Novotny and Priegel (1974) contradictorily found northern pike and muskellunge among the hardest to capture.
- **Invertebrate ecology**: Electrofishing increases invertebrate drift, indicating disturbance effects extending beyond target fish populations, affecting benthic invertebrate community dynamics downstream of sampled reaches.

## Key Findings

### Basic Physiological Responses
- Fish motion in an electric field results from direct stimulation of the central nervous system (voluntary reactions), autonomic nervous system (involuntary reactions), and muscles.
- Neural impulses travel via waves of electrical depolarization; stimuli must reach threshold intensity with sufficient duration, and sub-threshold stimuli can summate (all-or-none phenomenon).

### Response Differences by Current Type
- **Alternating Current (AC)**: Fish orient perpendicular to current lines, alternately facing anode/cathode, producing "false galvanotaxis" via cyclic body undulation; tetany occurs once threshold is reached. AC causes greater mortality than DC or pulsed DC.
- **Direct Current (DC)**:
  - Fish across the field typically turn toward the anode.
  - Facing anode (+): sequence of inhibited swimming → forced swimming → galvanonarcosis → tetanus.
  - Facing cathode (−): jerky body movements → irregular movements → tetanus.
  - Vibert (1963) found only ~1/4 of the voltage is needed for full tetany when fish face the cathode (tested in [[Anguilla anguilla]], [[Salmo trutta]], and [[Salmo gairdneri]]).
- **Pulsed Direct Current (PDC)**: Produces the greatest neurophysiological effect with the least physiological damage; requires less voltage, allowing smaller generators.

### Stress and Mortality
- Stress from electrofishing is cumulative with other environmental/pollutant stressors and can cause delayed mortality, increasing susceptibility to predators, disease, or parasites — a concern for population estimate accuracy (violates equal-mortality assumptions for marked vs. unmarked fish).
- Larger mortalities associated with AC compared to DC/PDC; mortality directly related to total current exposure (strength, impulse length, impulse rate, and exposure time).
- See related handling-stress physiology in [[Wydoski and Wedemeyer 1976]].

### Invertebrate Responses
- Invertebrates, due to smaller body size, likely receive less total current than fish under the same voltage gradient.
- A dose-response study on cercariae of *[[Nanophyetus salmincola]]* (Combs 1968) showed increasing mortality with higher voltage (220–440 volts/inch) and longer exposure time, in the context of hatchery water supply treatment.
- **Invertebrate drift studies**:
  - Bisson (1976), SW Washington (conductivity 50 µmho/cm): Shocked drift increased dramatically during treatment (86,023) vs. unshocked (4,381).
  - Fowles (1975), New Zealand (conductivity 75.5–80.7 µS): Downstream drift consistently exceeded upstream and control drift across three runs, suggesting increased electrical exposure time for downstream-drifting invertebrates.
- Confounding factor: physical disturbance from personnel wading likely contributes to drift independent of electrical effects.
