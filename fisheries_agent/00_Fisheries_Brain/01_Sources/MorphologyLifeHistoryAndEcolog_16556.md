# Morphology, Life History, and Ecology of an Unusual Stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) in the Queen Charlotte Islands, Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 study by G. E. E. Moodie (University of Alberta, received November 17, 1971; published in *Canadian Journal of Zoology* 50: 721–732) describes a divergent, melanic threespine stickleback, the "Black" stickleback, in [[Mayer Lake]], Queen Charlotte Islands. It is parapatric with the typical freshwater form, *leiurus*, which occupies vegetated inlet streams. The Black form occupies exposed lake habitat alongside predatory fish. The study compares morphology, breeding season, nesting preferences, fecundity and ecology between the two forms. The author concludes that the Black stickleback probably meets the requirements of a biologically defined species, while stopping short of a formal taxonomic name.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxon:** [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), a species with a wide circumboreal range in which freshwater populations are usually uniform across western America, eastern America and Europe.
- **Divergent form:** The Black stickleback is distinguished from [[Leiurus Stickleback]] (the typical unkeeled, partially plated freshwater form) by large size, long pelvic spines, high gill raker, vertebral and lateral plate counts, streamlined shape, melanism, and drab breeding colours.
- **Naming:** "Black" is a descriptive label only and does not imply a relationship with other melanic sticklebacks of the Pacific Northwest (e.g., [[McPhail 1969 Predation Study]]).
- **Sampling effort:** Field visits ran from 1966 to 1969 (multiple seasons). Fish were collected with seines, gillnets and [[Lift Net Capture Method]] for nesting males.

Morphological sample sizes include adult males counted in the field (n = 845) and 43 Black sticklebacks compared with 115 leiurus of similar size. Ages were estimated from 187 otoliths collected in July 1967. A 1929 museum sample (accession BC62-8, University of British Columbia) was used for a 39-year stability comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mayer Lake:** Lies 22 m above sea level and drains to the Pacific Ocean via the Mayer River. It is about 12 km long, less than 800 m wide on average, and up to about 9 m deep. The water is very dark brown; temperatures ranged from 10 to 23°C between May and August. Wind and depth prevent stratification. The lake froze for 6 weeks in winter 1968–1969.

**Inlet streams:** Three inlet streams, including [[Gold Creek]], [[Woodpile Creek]] and [[Cott Creek]], have sluggish dark brown water, steep channels up to 2 m deep, and organic ooze substrates. Their margins are choked by Sphagnum mats and emergent grasses. Mean temperature difference between Gold Creek and the lake was 0.6°C (11 readings, May 21–June 25, 1967).

**Littoral habitat preferences (Table 1):** Nesting Black sticklebacks were significantly associated with vegetation (P < 0.001), substrate (P < 0.01) and gradient (P < 0.001). Roughly 20% of the littoral zone lacked vegetation. Nesting occurred disproportionately on sand (66.5% of nests vs 55.7% of available habitat) and on gentle slopes (71.5% of nests vs 58.8%).

**Fish community:** Black sticklebacks shared the lake with [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), [[Prickly Sculpin]] (*Cottus asper*), [[Dolly Varden Char]] (*Salvelinus malma*) and [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*; migratory and uncommon). Leiurus was confined to the vegetated stream margins and mouths.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat segregation:** Black sticklebacks were never collected in the streams or stream mouths, whereas leiurus was found almost exclusively in the vegetated streams. Observations of schools of juveniles in open water support a pelagic or limnetic lifestyle, at least in part.
2. **Age and breeding season:** Breeding males were at least 2 years old (three summers). The breeding season began around May 1, peaked in the first week of June, and had few breeding fish by mid-August. Each breeding season lasted about 90 days, and males completed five or fewer cycles, each about 18 days.
3. **Size:** Mean standard length of sexually mature Black females was 89.94 mm (n = 105), and nesting males averaged 80.24 mm (n = 336). The largest Black stickleback recorded was a 116 mm female. Mature leiurus females averaged 50.68 mm (n = 21).
4. **Morphology:** Black sticklebacks have longer pelvic spines, more streamlined bodies, and higher counts of gill rakers, vertebrae, lateral plates and dorsal and anal rays. Size was the main separating character, since other traits overlapped. Six of eight characters showed lower variance than in leiurus.
5. **Stability over time:** Comparison with the 1929 sample showed the same modes in five of seven traits and no significant mean differences. Only lateral plate number differed in variance (P < 0.005), suggesting a phenotype stable for at least 39 years.
6. **Colouration:** Black males are uniformly melanic apart from silver opercula and anteroventral regions. Only 14–18% of breeding males developed a red throat, which was associated with fry in the nest (t = 3.6, P < 0.01).
7. **Fecundity:** Black females produced a mean of 257 eggs (n = 44 for regression), more than twice the leiurus value of 105 (n = 40). Mean chorion diameter was similar (1.90 mm vs 1.87 mm), so the higher fecundity is not due to smaller eggs. Mean clutch size was 773 eggs (n = 133), with a maximum of 2001. Each male must therefore mate with about three females per cycle.
8. **Nest-site correlates (1968):** Plate phenotype was correlated with water depth, distance to cover and stage of the breeding cycle. Nests with old fry were closer to shelter than nests with eggs or young fry (P < 0.01), suggesting that nests close to cover may be more successful.
9. **Sampling limitations:** The 1969 sampling schedule captured fish mostly early in the breeding cycle, so the 1968 correlations could not be confirmed.
10. **Mate choice:** In three series of choice tests, Black females chose Black males 40 times and leiurus males 8 times (pooled χ² = 20.14, P < 0.005), indicating behavioural isolation.
11. **Adaptation:** Many Black traits (body shape, gill raker number, counter-shading, dark colour) appear adapted to open-water life and to predation. Leiurus has disruptive colouring suited to complex littoral habitat. The Black form resembles the marine anadromous form, *trachurus*, in body shape, vertebrae and gill rakers.
12. **Taxonomic conclusion:** The author concludes that the Black stickleback is probably a distinct species, but declines to name it formally because selection against hybrids is undemonstrated and the taxonomy of the genus is unstable. Hybrids are occasionally found near stream mouths, especially Cott Creek.
13. **Hypotheses on origin:** The author regards speculation about the Black stickleback's origin from leiurus, trachurus or a common ancestor as unwarranted. Similarities may reflect convergence as easily as common descent.
