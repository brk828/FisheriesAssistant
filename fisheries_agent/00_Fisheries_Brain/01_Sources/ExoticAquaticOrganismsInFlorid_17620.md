# Exotic Aquatic Organisms in Florida with Emphasis on Fishes: A Review and Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 position paper by [[Water R. Courtenay Jr.]] and [[C. Richard Robins]], published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* on behalf of the Florida Chapter of the American Institute of Fisheries Research Biologists, reviews exotic aquatic organisms established in Florida. It concludes that most introductions were accidental, arising from [[Tropical Aquarium Fish Trade]] operations, with amateur aquarists, research suppliers and tourist attractions as secondary sources. Purposeful introductions for sport, food, aquarium supply and [[Biological Control]] are described as more extensive and potentially more damaging. The authors call for licensing, effluent controls, public education and a seven-step review process before any introduction.

## Background & Description

Florida is a water-dominated state whose drainage has been heavily altered by diking of [[Lake Okeechobee]], canal construction, flood control and back-pumping. Fertilizer and pesticide runoff, population growth and domestic and industrial wastes further weakened its aquatic systems by the 1960s. The authors define an exotic as any species not native to Florida and the United States; transfers between Florida watersheds are not counted as introductions.

Florida's warm climate, spring-fed thermal refuges and international air links attract tropical fish importers. Lachner et al. (1970) reported that in 1968, 75% of more than 64 million tropical fishes imported to the United States entered through Miami and Tampa. Florida's native fish assemblage includes [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes and basses), [[Ictaluridae]] (catfishes), and marine invaders such as snooks ([[Centropomidae]]), tarpon ([[Elopidae]]) and mojarras ([[Gerreidae]]).

The paper also introduces [[Trophic Displacement]]: an exotic seeks a trophic level that may already be occupied by natives, displacing them, and this is much more likely in degraded habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Aquarium fish farms:** Roughly 450 tropical aquarium-fish farms and importers operate in the U.S. (Axelrod, 1971), mostly in coastal Florida from Melbourne to Dade County and in Pinellas, Hillsborough and Manatee counties. Most use well water, and effluents often flow through open, unscreened pipes into canals. Outfalls provide direct routes to open water.
- **Established exotic flora:** [[Water Hyacinth]], watermilfoils, naiad, and [[Hydrilla]] have spread widely, clogging waterways and depleting oxygen. In some waterways adjacent to fish farms, nearly 100% of aquatic flora (excluding algae) is exotic.
- **Disease and transport water:** Imported fishes arrive with large volumes of transport water, estimated at a minimum of about 3 million gallons per year. The [[Schistosomiasis]] fluke and its snail host are endemic in Africa and have been established in parts of the Caribbean, and snail hosts were already present near Lithia Springs, Florida.
- **Hitchhikers:** The Asian clam *Corbicula* and the [[Oriental Goby]] (*Acanthogobius flavimanus*, established in California) illustrate non-fish species carried with aquaculture shipments.

## Key Findings

**1. Accidental introductions (aquarium trade)**
- At least 15 exotic fish species are established in Florida's fresh and brackish waters, 13 of them known to have come from fish farms (Table 1 lists 1970–72 collections).
- Thirty exotic fish species were recorded near southern Florida fish farms in 1970–72.
- Importers have sometimes dumped unordered stock; a planned release of pike cichlids (*Crenicichla*) was stopped in 1971.

**2. Amateur aquarists and research stocks**
- Amateur "Bambi complex" releases rarely establish because few fish are involved, but oral-brooding cichlids and livebearing poeciliids are exceptions. Amateurs share blame for established cichlids and at least one loricariid catfish.
- [[Pike Killifish]] (*Belonesox belizanus*) became established in southern Dade County after a dump of experimental stock. It has eliminated [[Mosquitofish]] from some canals, reducing natural mosquito control.

**3. Tourist attractions**
- Ponds at gardens and motels may release fish through management changes or flooding. No Florida introduction is confirmed to originate this way, though Schmitt (1933) suggested a freshwater shrimp was released by a St. Augustine hotel.

**4. Purposeful introductions**
- **Sport/food:** In 1964, about 10,000 juvenile peacock bass (*Cichla ocellaris*) were released in northern Dade County; all apparently died in the 1964–65 winter. [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Tilapia aurea*), introduced in 1961 from Auburn University stock, is now established in at least ten counties and reduces vegetation and native fishes where abundant.
- **Aquaculture:** Concern is raised over imported Japanese shrimp (*Penaeus japonicus*), Pacific oysters and [[Giant Freshwater Prawn]] (*Macrobrachium rosenbergii*), whose ecological impact is unknown.
- **Aquarium supply:** Proposals for anemonefishes, lionfishes, moorish idol and royal gramma received editorial support, but none had been established by 1973.
- **Biological control:** The [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) case is central. Florida's Commission denied an import permit after a literature survey, but political pressure led to approval. Three hundred young carp were procured and tested in pools, and attempted releases were proposed for Ocala National Forest and Eglin Air Force Base. Concerns include damage to sportfisheries, waterfowl, and the [[Everglades Kite]], whose food source is the apple snail (*Pomacea paludosa*).
- **Mosquito control:** Native Florida lies within the mosquitofish's natural range, so introductions for mosquito control are unnecessary and unjustified.

**5. Recommendations**
- Mandatory licensing of fish farms, dealers and exhibitors, tied to escape safeguards, effluent treatment (dry wells or non-residual chemical or heat treatment) and diked outdoor ponds.
- Statewide and federal legislation banning transport of fishes for release, which current Florida Statutes 372.26 and 372.265 only partially address; marine species were not covered by any state statute.
- Public education through fish-bag warnings, aquarium society bylaws and cooperation from the Florida Tropical Fish Farms Association and the national fish bag printer.
- Research on fish disease and parasites in imported stock, random inspection of shipments, and quarantine or treatment of transport water.
- A seven-step review for any proposed introduction: rationale, search for alternatives, preliminary impact assessment, publicity and expert review, confined experimental research, panel review with federal veto power, and release only after favorable review.

**6. Later addenda**
- The American Fisheries Society adopted some recommendations in a policy statement. Grass carp were then reported in more than 40 states, and the authors judged the sport-fishing potential "nil" and the rice danger "minimal." U.S. aquarium fish imports reached almost 99 million in 1971, with an estimated 110 million projected for 1972.
