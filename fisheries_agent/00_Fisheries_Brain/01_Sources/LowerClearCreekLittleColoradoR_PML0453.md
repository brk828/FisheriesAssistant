# Lower Clear Creek (Little Colorado River) Fish Survey, September 8-10, 2004

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report presents a fish sampling survey of lower [[Clear Creek]], a tributary of the [[Little Colorado River]] in Navajo County, Arizona, conducted September 8-10, 2004. It was prepared by Robert W. Clarkson (Bureau of Reclamation, Phoenix Area Office) and Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, School of Life Sciences) for the C-Aquifer Study Federal Team, as part of Reclamation's water-resource investigations with the Navajo Nation. The lowermost reach had no prior fish data. The survey found the fauna dominated by nonnative species, notably [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Green Sunfish]], with a single native [[Roundtail Chub]] and no [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (a federally threatened species) observed. A freshwater mussel shell was documented, but no living mussels were found.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Fish surveys were needed because native [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] populations in tributaries such as Clear and Chevelon creeks could be affected by changes to surface or groundwater flows, and fish data for the lower Clear Creek reach were lacking.
- **Study area:** Clear Creek drains north from headwaters on the [[Mogollon Rim]] to enter the Little Colorado River near Winslow, Arizona. Upper reaches are cold, perennial streams in ponderosa pine forest. Middle reaches are seasonally intermittent, in deep, steep-walled canyons. The surveyed 6.4 km lower reach is highly incised, with precipitous canyon walls, and runs from the head of [[Clear Creek Reservoir]] upstream to a point near Moon Canyon. Pools are often deep (>3 m) and long (>30 m).
- **Methods:**
  - Prior records were reviewed from the [[Arizona State University Fish Collection]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AZGFD). AZGFD had surveyed the reach upstream in 1999-2000, so this survey was limited to the Navajo County portion.
  - Reaches were selected using published and unpublished data, topographic maps, helicopter aerial survey, and ground reconnaissance. Criteria were lack of prior fish data, presence and permanence of water, and accessibility.
  - Sampling used a backpack electrofisher (Smith-Root model 12-B), gill nets set overnight, and seine or dip netting where appropriate. Specimens were identified, enumerated, vouchered (specimens or photographs), and fin clips were preserved in 95% ethanol.
  - Access required [[Helicopter Support]] for overnight camps and transport of gear and supplies, and inflatable kayaks to exit the canyon from Clear Creek Reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation:** The dominant riparian tree was [[Ash Trees]] (*Fraxinus* sp.). Cattail (*Typha* sp.), bulrush (*Scirpus* sp.), and giant reed (*Phragmites* sp.) were common, and saltcedar (*Tamarix* sp.) was sparse but common in middle reaches, where seeps and springs added high salt loads. Aquatic macrophytes included *Potamogeton* sp., *Chara* sp., and either *Myriophyllum* or *Ceratophyllum*.
- **Debris and disturbance:** Debris piles were evident on canyon walls and in the stream bed, pointing to historical high flows.
- **Nonnative pressure:** Largemouth bass and common carp were observed (not captured) at the head of Clear Creek Reservoir and in large pools immediately above it. The rock bass capture upstream of the reservoir may be only the second record of this species in the Little Colorado River drainage. Stocking records for [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] indicate rock bass were introduced to the area. The authors note that rock bass may be displacing green sunfish upstream.
- **Other fauna:** Canyon treefrog tadpoles (*Hyla arenicolor*) and an undetermined species of fairy shrimp were found in ephemeral pools of the upper reach. Shells of *Anodonta* sp. (a native freshwater mussel) were found, but no living specimens.

## Key Findings

1. **Nonnative dominance:** The fish fauna was dominated by nonnative species in both numbers and biomass. [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were the most abundant. [[Rock Bass]] was taken and seen in large numbers at the lower end of the reach. Table counts were not reliably recoverable from the source's scanned text, so exact per-reach numbers are not given here.
2. **Native fish:** Young-of-year and adult suckers (*Catostomus* sp., probably Little Colorado sucker) were captured in the upper reach, but only large adults were captured downstream. A single native [[Roundtail Chub]] was captured at the uppermost end of the sampled reach. No [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] were seen or captured.
3. **Historical native diversity:** The authors suggest Clear Creek historically supported at least five native fish species, including Little Colorado spinedace, roundtail chub, Little Colorado sucker, speckled dace, and others.
4. **Mussel record:** Shells of *Anodonta* sp. were found, a notable record, but no living mussels were observed.
5. **Amphibians and invertebrates:** [[Canyon Treefrog]] tadpoles and fairy shrimp were recorded in the upper reach's ephemeral pools.
6. **Access and logistics:** Poor access and extreme topography drove innovative logistics. The authors concluded that a third overnight helicopter camp would have improved the trip, and that an unmarked trail at the first campsite, accessible by vehicle, could greatly facilitate future sampling.
7. **Sampling limitations:** Gill nets were inappropriate for capturing small-bodied fishes, which were conspicuously visible in the lower reach but were not captured effectively. Physical exhaustion and time pressure reduced sampling effort on days 2 and 3.
8. **Management implications:** Nonnative fish, especially green sunfish, are linked to declines of native fishes elsewhere in the American Southwest. Nonnative predators are cited as preventing recovery of native fishes, and the authors suggest ongoing predator-naive native fishes are at risk.
