# Razorback Sucker Genetics Workshop Highlights (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #ConservationGenetics

## Executive Summary
This document summarizes the outcomes of the **Razorback Sucker Genetics Workshop** (September 20–21, 1994, Salt Lake City, UT), convened under the [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The workshop aimed to synthesize genetic data on the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) to inform management, broodstock development, and stocking strategies. Key conclusions established that genetic variation in the species is distributed largely *within* rather than *among* populations, meaning stocks must be delineated using **ecological criteria** (spawning site fidelity, geographic distribution) rather than genetic markers alone. The workshop produced concrete recommendations for broodstock composition, effective population sizes, and stocking protocols to balance the risks of inbreeding depression against "genetic swamping" of dwindling wild populations.

## Background & Description

- **Recruitment-size threshold:** Small razorback suckers in the Middle Green River were classified as recruits at **< 475 mm**.
- **Growth rate (juveniles):** Juvenile razorback suckers stocked into off-channel habitats in the upper basin grew **8–12 inches within a single growing season**, reaching sizes sufficient to escape predation by nonnative fishes.
- **Larval behavior:** Razorback sucker larvae are **phototactic**, enabling capture via light trapping; larvae captured this way exhibited **~85% survival** through ~22 days of rearing.
- **Longevity/Demographics:** The Lake Mohave (Lower Basin) adult population persisted at ~50,000 individuals for ~18 years before old-age mortality reduced the population to ~25,000 within three years, indicating a long-lived, aging cohort with little contemporary recruitment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical Range & Adaptability:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] has undergone approximately **7 range displacements** (north-south shifts) over the past **1.4 million years** in response to glacial advances, with the most recent shift 10,000–12,000 years ago — indicating strong physiological/ecological adaptability.
- **Habitat Degradation:** Major anthropogenic alterations to the [[Colorado River Basin]] have devastated nursery habitat:
  - Altered natural hydrograph
  - Fragmentation via irrigation diversion dams and high dams
  - Loss of floodplain connectivity due to dike construction
- **Nonnative Species Pressure:** [[Nonnative Fish Species]] constitute **≥75%** of fish community composition (by number) and **>90%** of biomass in many upper basin reaches. Predation and competition from these species are identified as primary limiting factors for razorback sucker early life stage survival.
- **Recruitment Failure:** Adults are long-lived and spawn viable larvae, but high early-life mortality (attributed to nonnative predation/competition and habitat loss) severely limits recruitment — the central demographic crisis for the species.
- **Hybridization:** Documented **introgressive hybridization** with the endemic [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] occurs naturally; management must avoid exacerbating this via human intervention (e.g., stock mixing in hatcheries).
- **Spawning Ecology:** Razorback suckers spawn on the **ascending limb of the hydrograph**, prior to nonnative fish spawning — a critical timing advantage exploited in bottomland habitat management strategies.
- **Lake Mohave Model (Lower Basin):** Larvae are collected from natural spawning via light traps and reared in nonnative-fish-free embayments as a stopgap "prevent extinction" strategy, given ~1/3 of wild adult females spawn in any given year (requiring ≥3 years of sampling to capture population-wide genetic diversity).

## Key Findings

### Genetic Structure
- Genetic variation occurs predominantly **within** populations rather than **among** them; **17 polymorphic characters** were identified via allozyme and mtDNA analyses, but **no strong geographic genetic structuring** was detected.
- Consequently, **genetic analysis cannot define management stocks** — stocks must instead be delineated using ecological data (spawning site fidelity, known movement/interaction between groups).
- Genetic diversity was historically greater in the **southern (lower basin)** range than the **northern (upper basin)** range.

### Identified Upper Basin Stocks
Three ecologically-defined stocks were recognized:
1. **[[Middle Green River|Middle Green/Yampa River Stock]]** — largest breeding aggregation (~350 adults, conservative estimate); some recruitment occurring but likely insufficient for self-sustainability. Documented interaction (3 adults) between Green and Yampa spawning sites ~70 km apart led to their combination into one management unit.
2. **[[Upper Colorado River]] Stock** — Severely declined: captures fell from 206 fish (1975) to zero (1989–1992); only 3 wild adults captured in 1993, none in 1994. Extirpated from the [[Gunnison River]].
3. **[[Lake Powell|Colorado River Arm of Lake Powell]] Stock** — Population status unknown; includes the **San Juan River Arm**, which is genetically indistinguishable and thus merged into a single "Lake Powell" stock designation. DNA-confirmed larvae collected in 1993 suggest upstream drift-spawning contribution.

### Etter Pond Case Study
- Razorback suckers in **Etter Pond** (off-channel gravel pit, Debeque, CO) are genetically confirmed **progeny of a few parents** — high inbreeding risk.
- These fish also show elevated **flannelmouth sucker allele frequency**, indicating hybrid introgression.
- Recommendation: Etter Pond fish should NOT be used exclusively for broodstock, but a **limited number (1 male, 1 female)** may be crossed with other upper basin stock for potential local adaptive value.

### Genetic Risk Management
- **Inbreeding depression** is the primary genetic risk for **reintroduction stocking** (especially with few founders or sibling/half-sibling matings).
- **Genetic swamping** is the primary risk for **augmentation stocking** of wild populations (introducing captive-reared fish into extant wild gene pools).
- Recommended breeding strategy for Upper Colorado River broodstock development (Attachment 1): **25 paired matings**, yielding an **effective population size (Ne) of 50**, capping inbreeding rate at **≤1%**.
- Broodstock composition for Upper Colorado River recovery combines:
  - Upper Colorado River stock (2 females, 1 male in captivity)
  - Lake Powell stock (2 females, 2 males in captivity)
  - Etter Pond stock (1 female, 1 male)
  - Remainder sourced from Middle Green/Yampa River stock

### Management Strategy Comparisons
- **Lower Basin (Lake Mohave) Approach:** Short-term goal = prevent extinction (via larval rescue/rearing in predator-free embayments); long-term goal = full recovery.
- **Upper Basin (Middle Green River) Approach:** Mirrors lower basin strategy — enhance flooded bottomland habitat to improve natural larval survival, supplemented by light-trap-captured larvae as needed. Consistent with the **"Genetics Management Guidelines"** preference for natural propagation as the first-line recovery method.
- A parallel precedent from **[[Bonytail]]** propagation at Dexter National Fish Hatchery showed that despite annual haplotype variation among cohorts from natural pond spawning, pooled multi-year genetic data resembled the parent stock — supporting the multi-year larval sampling approach.

### Stocking Policy Requirements
All captive-reared endangered fish stocking requires an approved **Stocking Plan** addressing:
- Species and source stock
- Fish size and release timing/location
- Identified genetic risks
- Evaluation methodology
- Physical conditioning of fish prior to release (critical for post-stocking survival)
