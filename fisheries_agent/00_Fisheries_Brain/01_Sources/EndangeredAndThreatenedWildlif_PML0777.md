# Final Rule: Critical Habitat Designation for the Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) issued a final rule, effective April 25, 2000, designating critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The designation totals approximately 1,448 km (900 mi) of rivers and creeks; all of it is critical habitat for spikedace, and approximately 1,302 km (807 mi) is critical habitat for loach minnow. Areas span the Gila, San Francisco, Black, Verde and San Pedro river systems and their tributaries in Arizona and New Mexico. Both species were listed as threatened in 1986 and the designation includes occupied habitat plus unoccupied areas judged essential for reestablishment. Tribal reservation lands were excluded under section 4(b)(2) of the Act.

## Background & Description

**Spikedace** is a small, slim fish under 80 mm long, with very silvery sides and spines in the dorsal and pelvic fins. It is the only species in the genus *Meda*. It inhabits moderate to large perennial streams, using shallow riffles, shear zones, sheet flow and eddies.

**Loach minnow** is a small, slender, elongated fish under 80 mm long, olivaceous and strongly blotched with darker pigment. Its mouth is oblique and terminal and its eyes point upward. It favors shallow, turbulent riffles with cobble substrate and swift currents, and it is rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces.

Both species were first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro, Arizona, and were described by Girard (spikedace in 1856, loach minnow in 1865). Both were once common throughout much of the [[Gila River Basin]] and now persist in a fraction of their range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range reduction:** Habitat destruction, and predation and competition from [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]], have severely reduced both fishes. Spikedace now occupies about 466 km (289 mi) of stream, roughly 1.0 to 15 percent of its historical range. Loach minnow occupies about 676 km (419 mi).
- **Flow regime:** Recurrent flooding and a natural hydrograph are essential for maintaining habitat and giving the species a competitive edge over invasive nonnative fishes.
- **Connectivity:** Designated complexes are interconnected so fish can move between areas, which is vital for recolonization after local depletion.
- **Land ownership:** Much of the designated habitat is on [[U.S. Forest Service]] and [[Bureau of Land Management]] land, with significant private holdings used for grazing and agriculture.
- **Primary constituent elements:** The rule lists 12 elements for spikedace and 14 for loach minnow, including permanent unpolluted flowing water, appropriate velocities and substrates, low fine sediment, water temperatures of about 1 to 30 °C, abundant aquatic insect food, periodic flooding, a natural hydrograph, and freedom from detrimental nonnative species.

## Key Findings

1. **Designated area:** Approximately 1,448 km of critical habitat for spikedace and 1,302 km for loach minnow, organized into 41 stream segments and 7 river complexes (Verde, Tonto, Middle-Upper San Pedro, Gila Box/San Francisco, Upper Gila, Black River, and others).
2. **Unoccupied habitat included:** Unoccupied areas are essential for recovery, since the 1991 recovery plans call for "secure self-reproducing populations in habitats from which the species has been extirpated."
3. **Historical range:** The designation represents about 45 percent (spikedace) and 50 percent (loach minnow) of the historically occupied range.
4. **Tribal exclusions:** Fort Apache, San Carlos Apache and Yavapai Apache reservation lands were excluded under section 4(b)(2) because the benefits of tribal self-governance and cooperative management outweighed the benefits of designation, with no risk of extinction.
5. **Black River complex:** Removed from the spikedace designation for biological reasons, since the species does not historically occupy that elevation.
6. **Regulatory effect:** Designation affects only actions with a [[Federal Nexus]]. In occupied areas little additional regulatory burden is expected. Unoccupied areas may require protections similar to those in occupied areas.
7. **Consultation history:** Since 1986 only three consultations concluded that a proposed action would likely jeopardize spikedace or loach minnow; reasonable and prudent alternatives were developed for each.
8. **Public process:** The initial 30-day comment period was extended to 65 days in total. Four public hearings drew 489 registrants. Of 441 comments (126 oral, 315 written), 50 supported and 263 opposed designation, with 17 providing information only or not relevant.
9. **Environmental review:** A final Environmental Assessment was prepared under NEPA; the Service concluded that an Environmental Impact Statement was not required. Economic analysis found little additional cost in occupied areas.
10. **Scientific disputes:** Responses to comments addressed the status of spikedace in the Verde River, loach minnow in Eagle Creek and the Blue River, and the San Pedro River as type locality. The Service reaffirmed that the best available information supports the designation and that delay for further data was not legally justified.
