# Systematic Analyses of Colorado River Chubs (Genus *Gila*) — Project Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a three-year research proposal from [[Arizona State University]] to clarify the taxonomy of Colorado River basin chubs of the genus [[Gila]] (Cyprinidae). Historically described as more than 15 taxa, the group is now treated as three monotypic species and one polytypic species with at least five subspecies. Identification remains difficult because morphometric and meristic variation is high and poorly explained, and possible [[Hybridization]] complicates matters. Several forms are federally listed or under consideration for listing, so unresolved taxonomy directly affects recovery decisions. The project pairs morphological analysis of museum specimens and photographic records with biochemical methods ([[Starch-Gel Electrophoresis]] and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] analysis) and aims to develop field-usable identification characters. The first-year estimated total is $79,948; the second and third years are estimated at $83,503 and $76,007.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* includes the [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*), *G. intermedia*, and the polytypic *G. robusta* complex. The *G. robusta* group includes subspecies such as *G. r. jordani* and *G. r. seminuda*, and a Moapa River form. Several of these are listed as threatened or endangered or are under review by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

The document identifies three core problems:

- **Morphological confusion:** Variation within and between forms is high. Its causes, including ontogeny, sex, and environment, are poorly understood.
- **Possible hybridization:** Interspecific hybridization may affect the taxonomic status of some stocks.
- **Management consequences:** Recovery plans and listing proposals depend on taxonomic status. Agencies have hesitated to commit recovery funds to forms that show shifting phenotypes over time and space.

The project's three objectives are:

1. Describe and evaluate sexual, ontogenetic, geographic, and other morphological variation that confounds species separation.
2. Build an independent biochemical data set to test congruence with the morphological patterns.
3. Determine whether basin taxa can be reliably separated using characters that can be assessed under field conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The project relies on historical specimens collected when rivers were largely free-flowing and before major human influence on aquatic resources. The document describes the current situation as one of reduced populations and fragmented geographic ranges. Its management concern is therefore tied to the conservation status of these fishes.

Rearing trials were proposed to separate genetic from environmental effects on body form. Preliminary, poorly controlled trials at Dexter and Willow Beach [[National Fish Hatchery]] facilities and at Arizona State University ponds reportedly showed no environmental modification of shape, but those results were not analysed or published and must be re-analysed.

Field collection for tissue sampling would be non-lethal where possible. Females of listed species would be captured, induced to ripen ova with minimal doses of human chorionic gonadotrophin, and stripped of eggs before release.

## Key Findings

**Morphometric approach**
- Historical specimens from six collections (including the [[Smithsonian Institution]] and University of Michigan Museum of Zoology) will be re-examined, along with type specimens described by Cope, Girard, and Baird & Girard.
- Morphometric and meristic analyses will be augmented by "truss" measurements from digitized video images using a microcomputer system.
- Photographic data will be validated against measurements from preserved specimens of non-listed populations before use.

**Variability studies**
- Ontogenetic variation will be studied with captive-reared cohorts of at least *G. cypha*, *G. elegans*, *G. intermedia*, and *G. r. robusta*.
- Sexual dimorphism, including nuptial tuberculation and urogenital papilla changes, will be assessed in museum and living fish.
- The aim is to separate size, sex, season, and environmental effects from true genetic differences before analysing geographic variation.

**Biochemical approach**
- A prior electrophoretic survey by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[Donald G. Buth]] covers 15 species (30 populations) across the Colorado River basin, western US, and Mexico.
- [[Bruce D. DeMarais]] is conducting a Ph.D. study of lower-basin relationships by starch-gel electrophoresis.
- The new project proposes [[Mitochondrial DNA]] restriction fragment analysis, with enzymes such as EcoRI and BamHI, in collaboration with [[Elliott S. Goldstein]]. The authors argue that mtDNA is well suited to this work because of its maternal inheritance, rapid evolution, and high polymorphism, and that it requires only small tissue samples.

**Field identification**
- Juvenile fish will be tracked from wild capture through rearing, with fin or gill clips for biochemical markers and video records of morphological change.
- Qualitative scoring characters are being developed in a companion paper (Douglas et al., in review).
- The authors acknowledge that no single character set may separate all forms. Results may support recognizing cryptic taxa, a superspecies, a subspecies complex, or a polymorphic group.

**Budget and timeline**
- Year 1 direct costs: $64,229 (total cost with 31.5% indirect: $79,948). Equipment is $1,500 (Zenith workstation, video and digitizer boards, and SONY video equipment). Salaries and wages are $34,132.
- Year 2 total: $83,503. Year 3 total: $76,007. Equipment outlay ends in Year 2, while biochemical supplies and publication costs increase.
- Field work is planned for May and June. Museum visits are planned for about two months, probably in July and August.

**Key uncertainties**
- The project assumes the morphological, biochemical, and field approaches will converge, but the authors note that character sets may be locally or regionally useful only.
- Hybridization and the causes of morphological variation remain unresolved.
