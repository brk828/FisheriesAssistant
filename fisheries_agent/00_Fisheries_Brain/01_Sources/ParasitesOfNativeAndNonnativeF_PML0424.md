# Fish Parasites of the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This two-year parasitological survey of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona, examined 1,435 fish representing 4 native and 7 nonnative species. The study identified 17 parasite species, of which 15 are first published records for the LCR and the Colorado River drainage in Grand Canyon. The parasite fauna is numerically dominated by introduced parasites, most notably the Asian tapeworm [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]], and contains only one native adult parasite. The LCR is critical habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], which hosted the highest parasite richness and the largest share of tapeworm infections. The authors argue that perennial tributary creeks act as reservoirs for intermediate hosts and infections, and that continued monitoring is needed to assess the impacts of these parasites on the Grand Canyon ecosystem.

## Background & Description

The LCR is a 536.5 km tributary of the [[Colorado River]] with a drainage area of about 69,800 km². Its lower 22 km are fed by [[Blue Spring]] and a series of springs, which make it perennially cold and clear. This contrasts with its historical condition as a seasonally flooded, muddy river. Following the construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, the river's physical nature and ecology were substantially altered, and at least [[Nonnative Fishes]] were introduced into the system.

The native fish fauna in Grand Canyon now comprises only four species: Humpback Chub, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]]. Nonnative species include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Common Carp]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Rainbow Trout]] (stocked from the Lees Ferry tailwater), and [[Brown Trout]], which is rarer. Some introduced species have reproducing populations in the LCR.

Two introduced parasites of concern are the Asian tapeworm and the anchor worm [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]]. The importance of the LCR as spawning habitat for all four native species, and as the most likely remaining refuge for the endangered Humpback Chub, made it the focus of this study.

The study objectives were to characterize the parasite fauna, host-parasite associations, and seasonal patterns of parasitism in this ecosystem.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Sampling covered the lower 18 km of the LCR, divided into three reaches:
- **LC1** (RK 0 to 2.5): nearest the mouth, containing all species present in the LCR, including rainbow and occasionally brown trout.
- **LC2** (RK 10.8 to 11.5): near Salt Creek, containing resident species and occasional rainbow trout.
- **LC3** (RK 13.6 and upstream): above Beaver Falls, a waterfall barrier, containing only Speckled Dace and nonnative minnows.

Two clear saline tributaries, [[Big Canyon Creek]] (BCC) and [[Salt Creek]] (SAC), drain into the LCR and were also sampled.

**Hydrology and habitat.** Flows in the LCR can reach 343.4 m³/s during monsoon floods (July to September) and spring runoff (February to April). Travertine deposition (calcium carbonate) on the streambed, banks, and rocks reduces benthic habitat stability and impedes flow. Riparian vegetation is sparse and includes [[Phragmites Australis]], Salix, and Tamarix.

**Water temperature.** Continuous temperature logging was conducted in BCC and SAC from 27 September 2000 to 6 June 2001, and LCR temperatures were obtained from a USGS logger near the confluence. A sharp temperature drop in BCC and SAC in late October was attributed to a flood event.

## Key Findings

**Fish sampling**
- 1,435 fish were examined: 4 native species and 7 nonnative species.
- Speckled Dace made up a large proportion of the sample. Sucker species were mostly under 100 mm, indicating many juveniles.
- Sampling was conducted with various gears, including hoop nets, minnow traps, trotlines, seines, and electrofishing.

**Parasite diversity**
- A total of 17 parasite species was recovered, including 2 trematodes, 3 cestodes, 4 monogeneans, 4 nematodes, 1 acanthocephalan, 1 acari (mite), 1 mollusk-associated trematode, and 2 crustaceans (copepods).
- [[Rhabdochona Sp.]] in Speckled Dace was the only native adult parasite found in the LCR. It appears to be a new species, currently being described elsewhere.
- Adult parasites are most likely introduced with their fish hosts. Larval or juvenile parasites mature in fish-eating birds, catfish, or aquatic invertebrates.
- Fifteen of the 17 species are first published records for the LCR and the Colorado River drainage in Grand Canyon.

**Dominant parasites**
- [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]] was found in all fish species examined. It was most abundant in cyprinids and was largely absent from catostomids, rainbow trout, and ictalurids in the same way.
- A total of 3,930 individuals of B. acheilognathi was recorded. Of these, 2,130 (54.1%) were found in Humpback Chub.
- Metacercariae of [[Ornithodiplostomum Sp.]] and [[Posthodiplostomum Sp.]] were also common, linked to piscivorous birds. Ornithodiplostomum is likely transmitted by a snail host, [[Physa Snail]].

**Host associations and community metrics**
- Most adult parasites showed high host specificity.
- Component community richness was highest in Channel Catfish (7 species).
- Mean infracommunity richness was highest in Humpback Chub (1.86), followed by Channel Catfish (1.23).
- Parasite component community diversity was remarkably uniform in Humpback Chub across the study period. Diversity was nearly identical between LC1 and LC2.
- Catostomid (sucker) component and infracommunities were consistently species-poor.

**Seasonal and spatial patterns**
- B. acheilognathi abundance was lowest in summer and significantly higher in the following fall (September).
- Abundance was consistently higher at LC2 than at LC1, possibly due to the presence of tributary creeks that provide copepod habitat.
- In Speckled Dace, B. acheilognathi abundance was significantly higher in BCC than in any other location. Spring infection values rose with temperature.
- Ornithodiplostomum abundance was significantly higher in BCC than elsewhere.
- A positive correlation was found between Humpback Chub length and B. acheilognathi burden (overall R² = 0.12), and between length and Ornithodiplostomum abundance (overall R² = 0.28).

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The parasite assemblage is species-poor but highly abundant for a few species, and it shows colonization potential from introduced hosts.
- Perennial tributary creeks such as BCC and SAC may serve as reservoirs for copepods and fish infections, and as refugia during floods.
- The authors recommend broadening monitoring to include the Colorado River mainstem, its tributaries, and warm feeder creeks, and tracking whether parasites spread under alternate flow regimes from Glen Canyon Dam.
