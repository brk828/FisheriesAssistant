#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by McDonald, Heimstra & Damkot (1968) investigates whether **agonistic (fighting) behaviour** in the [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) can be experimentally modified through a process of **social conditioning**, analogous to dominance-reversal effects previously demonstrated in mice and rats (Ginsburg & Allee 1942; Seward 1946). Paired green sunfish were established into dominant-submissive relationships based on attack frequency and coloration, then subjected to a controlled "conditioning" period in which dominant and submissive fish were separately exposed to larger fish, smaller fish, or isolation. The critical finding is that **dominance reversals occurred specifically when the previously dominant fish was exposed to a larger conspecific** during the conditioning period, supporting a learning-theory (conditioned fear/extinction) explanation of [[Dominance Hierarchy|dominance hierarchies]] in fish.

## Background & Description

- **Subject species**: Green Sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), total length 3–5 in., wild-caught.
- **Coloration as a social/status signal**: Submissive fish consistently displayed **darker coloration** than dominant fish; this color-based dominance assessment showed near-perfect correlation with attack-frequency-based dominance determination (per McDonald & Kessel, 1967).
- **Physical markers of subordination**: Submissive/defeated fish developed a "**tattered**" appearance to fins (especially the caudal fin) and showed dislodged scales after prolonged exposure to a dominant conspecific — physical damage resulting from repeated attacks.
- **Identification marking**: A small section of the caudal fin was clipped on one fish per pair for individual tracking (an artificial marking, not a natural morphological trait).
- Fin damage and scale loss were most severe in fish exposed to intense "head-on" locking-jaw combat bouts (observed specifically in Group III), suggesting morphological damage scales with agonistic intensity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Green Sunfish are a freshwater [[Centrarchidae|centrarchid]] species known for establishing **dominance hierarchies** and territorial [[Agonistic Behaviour|agonistic behaviour]] within social pairs/groups, consistent with prior findings in other game fish species (McDonald & Heimstra, 1964, 1965) and juvenile [[Atlantic Salmon]] (Keenleyside & Yamamoto, 1962).
- Experimental ecology was simulated in **20-gallon glass aquaria** divided by wire screens into paired compartments (12 in. × 12 in. × 15 in.), with controlled water temperature (67°F), aeration, filtration, and a 14-hr light cycle (6 a.m.–8 p.m.).
- Diet: commercial fish pellets supplemented with live earthworms.
- The study frames agonistic behaviour as a **socially plastic trait**, modifiable by relative body-size cues (larger vs. smaller conspecifics) and by social isolation — implying that in natural habitats, hierarchical status may be continuously renegotiated based on encounters with differently-sized conspecifics.
- Parallels are drawn to **mammalian social dominance systems** (mice, rats), suggesting shared underlying mechanisms of associative learning (conditioned fear, extinction, generalization) across vertebrate taxa despite phylogenetic distance — situating fish agonistic behaviour within a broader comparative ethology framework (Scott, 1958; Scott & Frederickson, 1951).

## Key Findings

### Experimental Design
Four experimental groups (n = 17–20 pairs each) were exposed to different conditioning regimes for 5 days after an initial pre-conditioning dominance assessment:
- **Group I**: Dominant fish paired with a *larger* fish; submissive fish isolated.
- **Group II**: Dominant fish isolated; submissive fish paired with a *smaller* fish.
- **Group III**: Dominant fish paired with a *larger* fish; submissive fish paired with a *smaller* fish.
- **Group IV (control)**: Both dominant and submissive fish isolated.

### Dominance Reversals (Table I)
| Group | Pairs | Reversals |
|---|---|---|
| I | 17 | 10 |
| II | 20 | 0 |
| III | 20 | 15 |
| IV | 20 | 0 |

- Reversals occurred **only** in groups where the dominant fish was exposed to a *larger* conspecific during conditioning (Groups I and III).
- No reversals occurred when the dominant fish was isolated (Groups II and IV), regardless of the submissive fish's treatment.

### Attack Frequency Changes (Table II, mean attacks/session)
| Group | Pre: Dominant | Pre: Submissive | Post: Dominant | Post: Submissive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 13.05 | 0.00 | 25.76 | 26.06 |
| II | 9.75 | 0.00 | 15.80 | 0.00 |
| III | 10.80 | 0.00 | 14.34 | 35.65 |
| IV | 11.20 | 0.00 | 12.10 | 3.40 |

- Increases in attack frequency were statistically significant (Wilcoxon Matched-Pairs Signed-Ranks Test) for both dominant and submissive fish in **Group I**, and for submissive fish in **Group III**.
- **Group III** showed the most dramatic increase in submissive-fish attacks (0.00 → 35.65) and the most intense combat, including mutual jaw-locking bouts, longer fights, and greater physical damage (torn fins, dislodged scales).

### Attack Initiation
- **Group I**: previously submissive fish initiated 100% of first post-conditioning attacks.
- **Group II**: previously dominant fish initiated all first attacks (no reversal).
- **Group III**: previously submissive fish initiated 19/20 first attacks.
- **Group IV**: previously dominant fish initiated 17/20 first attacks.

### Coloration and Dominance
- Coloration ratings tracked dominance status almost perfectly: the fish with fewer attacks was always rated darker.
- During conditioning, **dominance reversals were visible via coloration within 24 hours** of re-pairing with a larger or smaller fish — coloration change preceded/paralleled behavioral reversal.

### Theoretical Interpretation
- The authors propose a **[[Conditioned Fear Response|conditioned fear]] generalization** model: dominant fish exposed to larger conspecifics acquire a fear response that generalizes to the previously submissive partner upon re-pairing, producing reversal.
- **Extinction/disuse** of the submissive fish's fear response (via isolation or exposure to smaller fish) was predicted to increase attack behaviour; this held for Group I but only weakly for Group IV, and strongly for Group III (where successful attacks against smaller fish likely reinforced aggressive behaviour).
- The **experience of the dominant fish** during conditioning — not the submissive fish's experience alone — was identified as the most critical variable determining whether reversal occurs.
- Physical/appearance changes in the previously dominant fish (darkened coloration, tattered fins, possible olfactory "alarm substance" cues per Lagler, Bardach & Miller, 1962) are proposed as potential **releasing stimuli** eliciting attack from the previously submissive fish, though the authors note further study is needed to isolate exact stimulus mechanisms.

### Broader Significance
- Findings support extending **associative learning theory** (reinforcement, extinction, generalization, discrimination) — previously applied to mammalian social dominance (Ginsburg & Allee, 1942; Seward, 1946) — to explain [[Social Conditioning|socially conditioned]] agonistic behaviour in a non-mammalian vertebrate, the [[Green Sunfish]].
- Establishes a robust behavioural (attack frequency) and morphological (coloration) dual-metric for assessing fish dominance status under experimental manipulation.
