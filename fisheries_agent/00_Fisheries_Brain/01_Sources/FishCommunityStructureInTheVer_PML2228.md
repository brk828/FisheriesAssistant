# Fish Community Structure in the Verde River, Arizona, 1975-97

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This study describes the fish community of the [[Verde River]] in central Arizona across roughly 300 km, using two decades of [[Electrofishing]], seining and trammel netting data from the USDA Forest Service and the Arizona Game and Fish Department. The authors divide the river into four predesignated reaches and find a clear upstream-to-downstream gradient: native fishes dominate only the upper, least-disturbed reach, while [[Nonnative Species]] dominate the middle and lower reaches. The shift tracks a rising human footprint (reservoirs, stocking, groundwater pumping, diversions, population growth) and altered flood-driven hydrographs. The authors frame these links as correlations that require further analysis.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The Verde River runs over 300 km from headwaters in Big Chino Wash to its confluence with the Salt River. The upper 60 km above [[Sycamore Creek]] is described as quasi-pristine, with no flow-altering dams or major diversions.
- **Human modification:** Reach II (Sycamore Creek to Beasley Flat, including the [[Camp Verde]] area) supports municipalities, mining, groundwater pumping, diversions, recreation and livestock grazing. Below Beasley Flat, [[Horseshoe Reservoir]] and [[Bartlett Reservoir]] markedly change the natural hydrograph.
- **Native fish significance:** The river holds one of the few remaining native fish communities in Arizona, but nonnative species introduced mainly for sport fishing occur throughout it.
- **Objectives:** (1) Define relative native and nonnative components in four reaches; (2) define native fish components across the four reaches over two time periods; (3) relate historic stocking and museum collections of nonnatives to community structure; (4) identify factors that may drive observed changes and need further evaluation.
- **Data sources:** Forest Service surveys from the past four years, Arizona Game and Fish databases and collections over two decades, historic stocking records (Pringle 1996, unpublished), and [[Museum Collections]] of nonnative fishes from Arizona State University's Gila River Basin files. Sampling covered more than 150,000 individuals.
- **Reach designation criteria:** Linear position in the watershed, presence of stream gages, and potential human impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking history.** Sport fish stocking began about six decades ago in [[Bartlett Reservoir]] and in tributary streams and lakes. More than a dozen nonnative species were stocked, with the following scale:
- Over 850 stocking events in reservoirs and almost 4,500 in streams, mostly tributaries.
- Most tributary stocking was three trout species: rainbow, brown and brook trout.
- Nearly 3 million channel catfish, largemouth bass and sunfish were stocked into Bartlett Reservoir over a 40-year period.
- Overall, the text cites more than 15 million nonnative sport fishes stocked.

**Museum records of nonnatives.** Records track stocking history:
- Before 1950: five records, all from Oak and Wet Beaver creeks.
- 1950-64: records roughly doubled, with 6 of 11 now in the mainstream Verde.
- 1965-79: a fourfold increase, with a little over half of about 41 records from tributaries.
- 1980-95: records fell slightly (41 to 37), which the authors attribute more to poor specimen preservation than to real declines.

**Human population.** Historic population along the middle Verde was about 5 people per square mile (Pilas, unpublished). Current densities are 20 times higher. Population grew 135% in the watershed between 1980 and 1994, with a similar increase expected over the next four decades. Nearly half this growth occurred in the [[Middle Verde Valley]] (Reach II).

**Hydrograph and flooding.** Significant floods occurred in the mid-1960s, early 1970s, 1980s and mid-1990s. Floods raise the native share of the community in every reach, including nonnative-dominated ones, but the effect is temporary.

**Water quality and use.** [[Groundwater Pumping]] is greatest in Reach II, and hundreds of diversions and [[Stock Tanks]] are thought to cumulatively alter the hydrograph. Numerous diversions can increase suspended sediment on return flows, and warming and nutrient inputs in this populated reach may favor tolerant nonnatives such as catfish and carp over natives. The authors call water quality (suspended sediment and nutrients) the most significant information gap.

## Key Findings

### Community composition by reach

| Reach | Extent | Native share of community |
|---|---|---|
| I | Above Sycamore Creek | Natives predominate |
| II | Sycamore Creek to Beasley Flat | Natives generally 25% or less |
| III | Beasley Flat to Horseshoe Reservoir | Natives generally 25% or less |
| IV | Below Bartlett Reservoir | 10% or less |

- Exceptions occurred in 1974 and 1994-95, when natives made up 40-90% of the community in Reaches III and IV.
- **Reach I** (13 years): natives outnumbered nonnatives in 8 of 13 years. Natives fell below 20% in 1988, stayed lower than nonnatives until 1993 floods, then declined until nonnatives clearly dominated in 1997.
- **Reach II** (10 years): natives exceeded nonnatives in only 2 years, including 1994-95.
- **Reach III** (10 years): natives predominated only in 1995.
- **Reach IV**: nonnatives always predominated. Natives reached nearly half in 1994 after flooding, then were very low or absent. Data for 1995 are lacking. By 1996-97 natives were a very small component.

Occurrence is shown as years present out of years sampled.

| Species | Reach I | Reach II | Reach III | Reach IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sonora sucker | 13/13 | 10/10 | 10/10 | 3/6 |
| Desert sucker | 13/13 | 9/10 | 5/10 | 3/6 |
| Roundtail chub | 13/13 | 10/10 | 5/10 | 0/6 |
| Longfin dace | 12/13 | 7/10 | 2/10 | 2/6 |
| Speckled dace | 11/13 | 1/10 | 0/10 | 0/6 |
| Spikedace | 10/13 | 0/10 | 0/10 | 0/6 |

- The two suckers ([[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]) persisted in all reaches across the study, though they were collected only half the time in Reach IV.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] was absent from Reach IV in all six sampled years.
- [[Longfin Dace]] occurred in every reach but was present in only a third to a fifth of years in Reaches III and IV. It was collected in Reach III in 1995 and Reach IV in 1996, possibly carried in by tributary flooding in the mid-1990s.
- [[Speckled Dace]] was absent from Reaches III and IV in all years and appeared in only one of ten years in Reach II.
- [[Spikedace]] was the most restricted, found only in Reach I.

### Principal conclusions

1. There is a distinct upstream-to-downstream gradient in the ratio of native to nonnative species, with natives declining steadily in Reaches II to IV.
2. Smaller species (speckled dace and spikedace) appear most vulnerable to hydrograph change and nonnative increase.
3. Native populations began declining after reservoir construction and introductions of large nonnative sport and predatory fishes. This mirrors regional patterns across the desert Southwest.
4. Change began in Reach II, which still has a natural flow regime but growing human influence, so cumulative factors (groundwater pumping, diversions, stock tanks, grazing, agriculture, water quality) need investigation.
5. Natives increase after floods, while nonnatives dominate lower reaches and dip briefly after flooding. The authors suggest that the hydrograph, combined with [[Hydrograph Alteration]] from dams and diversions, is central to community structure.
6. Native fish decline ties to the rise of the [[Middle Verde Valley]] population. The authors call for "more detailed analyses and definition" of these relationships before firm management recommendations.
