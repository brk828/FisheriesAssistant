# Genetics and Morphology of the Yaqui Chub *Gila purpurea*, an Endangered Cyprinid Fish Subject to Recovery Efforts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether decades of translocation, hatchery propagation, and reintroduction have altered the genetic and morphological structure of the [[Yaqui Chub]], a federally endangered minnow restricted to the Rio San Bernardino in the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of Sonora, México, and Arizona, USA. Allozyme electrophoresis (32 loci examined, 6 polymorphic) and sheared principal components analysis of 26 morphological variables (144 preserved adults) were used. Genetic variation remained high and among-population differentiation low, and morphology showed no evidence of recovery-induced or ecophenotypic change. The authors attribute this resilience largely to high fecundity and rapid establishment of large effective population sizes, while stressing that habitat loss and non-native species remain the main obstacles to recovery.

## Background & Description

The Yaqui chub was first described as *Tigoma purpurea* by [[Girard]] (1857) from the Rio San Bernardino (Black Draw, or San Bernardino Creek, in the United States). Morphological differences led [[Demarais]] (1991) to describe *Gila eremica* for populations from western Sonora, leaving the Rio San Bernardino chub as the only population assigned to *G. purpurea*. Historically it was known from the San Bernardino Ranch area and a single unsubstantiated record from the endorheic [[Sulphur Springs Valley]]. The species was listed as endangered by the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1984 with critical habitat on the San Bernardino Ranch.

The paper also frames the wider problem. Of 364 North American freshwater fish taxa listed as threatened, endangered, or otherwise in jeopardy (Williams et al., 1989), at least 25 fishes in the arid Southwest are extinct, and the USFWS estimate that 35 southwestern species may require artificial propagation is considered too low by the authors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Decline.** Regional arroyo cutting in the late 1800s incised the Rio San Bernardino floodplain by more than 8 m and drained streamside ciénegas, except where maintained by springs or artesian wells. Native companions included the [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Sonoran Topminnow]].

**Habitat loss.** Astin Spring, the most upstream permanent water in Black Draw, failed in 1969. About 200 adults of Yaqui chub and an equal number of Sonoran topminnow were moved to [[Leslie Creek]] just before desiccation. The Yaqui sucker was extirpated from the United States and the beautiful shiner disappeared after an artesian well was capped around 1970. A 1975–76 drought nearly dried Leslie Creek, prompting transfer of about 225 chubs to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico in September 1976.

**Protected refuge.** The [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] was established from land purchased by [[The Nature Conservancy]] (1979) and transferred to USFWS in 1982. Leslie Creek was added in 1989. The refuge (about 1000 ha) aims to secure viable populations of seven or eight Rio Yaqui fishes. Management includes clearing emergent vegetation, renovating springs and ponds, removing or controlling non-native fishes, and excluding livestock. Non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) invaded House Pond and were incompatible with native topminnows; the pond was drained and poisoned in 1984–85 before restocking.

**Recolonization.** Chubs were not recorded in Black Draw until 1987, despite a 1980 reintroduction. Stream flow had resumed through much of the reach after wet years and erosion control. Post-1987 fish probably reflect immigration from Sonora, a hypothesis supported by genetic data and by the synchronous appearance of Mexican stoneroller (*Campostoma ornatum*) in 1987.

## Key Findings

**Population history and stocking**
- Stockings from Dexter NFH into San Bernardino Ranch aquatic habitats in 1980 released more than 30,000 fish into House Pond, about 1,100 into North Pond, and 50 into Black Draw. The House Pond and North Pond stockings succeeded.
- In 1986, roughly 8,600 chubs were moved from Dexter NFH to West Turkey Creek. The current Turkey Creek stock is five transfers and perhaps 30–40 generations removed from the 1969 Astin Spring population.
- Captive production was efficient: a 0.04-ha Dexter pond stocked with 200 adults in 1976 held more than 3,200 subadults and adults by 1980 (A = 3.56). A pond stocked with 100 adults in 1984 held more than 8,600 by 1986 (A = 9.27).

**Allozyme genetics (5 populations: Black Draw, Leslie Creek, Turkey Creek, House Pond, North Pond; about 30 fish per site)**
- 6 of 32 presumptive loci were polymorphic.
- Mean heterozygosity ranged from 0.037 ± 0.018 (Leslie Creek, observed) to 0.060 ± 0.029 (Turkey Creek and House Pond, observed), comparable to values reported for other *Gila* species and for diverse fishes (Nevo, 1978: 0.051 ± 0.034).
- Only one significant departure from Hardy–Weinberg expectations was found: an excess of heterozygotes at sAAT-A in fish from the outlet of House Pond.
- Spatial heterogeneity was significant at four of six polymorphic loci. Overall F_ST was small and negative (−0.0364), and about 96% of gene diversity lay within samples.
- Leslie Creek lacked one of three common sACON-A alleles present in all other populations. The authors attribute this to a likely genetic bottleneck during the severe 1976 drought.
- Black Draw fish carried uncommon alleles at PGM-A and PEP-D, which were monomorphic elsewhere. The authors consider it more likely that these alleles persisted in Sonoran populations than that the chubs descended from the 1980 stocking.

**Morphology (144 preserved adults; 26 variables; sheared PCA)**
- PC1 (95.6% of variance among 1943-and-later collections) reflected general size and gave no inter-sample separation.
- Sheared H2 (4.3% of variation) was driven by paired-fin length, which is sexually dimorphic; H3 (0.1%) reflected body depth and width, probably indicating reproductive condition.
- No divergence was found among populations, and temporally spaced Black Draw samples overlapped extensively. Only the 1989 Black Draw collection diverged slightly on H2, possibly through allometry or a discrete invading stock.
- Of 133 specimens preserved 1943–1989, only two fell within the polygon of 11 mid-1800s syntypes. The authors attribute this to preservation (alcohol versus formalin, plus long storage) rather than to real divergence.

**Reproduction and life history**
- Spawning runs from early March to October or early November, and reproduction may occur year-round in artesian-fed habitats. The species is suspected to be a multiple spawner.
- Young can appear within about 10 days of stocking. Growth to about 40 mm TL takes two to three months after hatching.
- Maximum body size is about 150 mm TL, with a life expectancy of three to four years. Under optimal conditions the authors estimate two to three generations per year.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Yaqui chubs responded favorably to recovery; a single stocking was sufficient to establish a self-sustaining population in every case except Black Draw.
- The authors conclude that the species' small size and high fecundity provide some resilience against genetic problems common in endangered species.
- Loss of suitable habitat and negative interactions with introduced species remain the main obstacles to recovery.
- Periodic genetic monitoring of broodstocks is recommended to minimize undesired changes in fish intended for reintroduction.
- The authors note that morphological similarity alone cannot rule out ecophenotypic or locally adapted lineages, and that quantitative genetics would be needed to resolve such cases.
