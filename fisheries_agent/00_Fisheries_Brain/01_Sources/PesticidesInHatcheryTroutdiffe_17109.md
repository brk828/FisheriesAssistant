# Pesticide Residues in Hatchery Trout, Commercial Fish Food, and Western US Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 document (Pesticides Monitoring Journal, Vol. 1, No. 2) contains two linked studies of persistent chlorinated pesticides. The first, by [[Cole et al. 1967]] (Pennsylvania State University and the Pennsylvania Fish Commission), tested commercial trout feed from four manufacturers and hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] raised on the same diet. Small residues were found in the feed and in all three trout species, with rainbow trout carrying the highest levels. The second, by [[Brown and Nishioka 1967]] of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], reports a monthly monitoring program on 11 western US streams. It found insecticides at very low concentrations (parts per trillion) and no herbicides.

## Background & Description

### Study 1: Trout and Fish Food (Benner Spring)

- **Purpose:** Determine levels of persistent chlorinated pesticides in [[Commercial Fish Food]] and in trout fed that food at the [[Benner Spring Research Station]].
- **Pesticides tested:** [[Heptachlor]], [[Heptachlor Epoxide]], [[Dieldrin]], [[DDE]], [[DDD]], o,p'-[[DDT]] and p,p'-DDT.
- **Sampling:** A 2-lb composite of pellets from each of four manufacturers. Seven fish per species, all 8 to 9 inches long, were taken from hatchery pools. All three species had been fed the same brand (Source No. 1) from fingerling stage onward.
- **Methods:** Lipids were extracted from ground feed and from whole, macerated fish using chloroform-methanol (2:1). Extracts were cleaned up and analyzed by [[Gas Chromatography]] with an [[Electron Capture Detector]]. Results are expressed on a lipid ("fat") basis. The [[Detection Limit]] was 0.002 ppm; lower values were reported as NR (no residue). Questionable peaks were confirmed on a QF-1 column and by thin-layer chromatography.

### Study 2: Western Stream Monitoring

- **Purpose:** Measure the extent and magnitude of pesticide contamination in streams selected from the USGS irrigation-network water-quality program.
- **Sites:** 11 stations on western rivers, including the Missouri (Nebraska), Arkansas (Colorado and Arkansas), Brazos, Colorado (Texas), Rio Grande (Texas), Colorado (Yuma Main Canal, Arizona), Sacramento (California), Yakima (Washington), Snake (Idaho) and Columbia (Oregon) rivers. Stations were chosen partly because they are mainly agricultural and have long records of water-quality data.
- **Analytes:** Nine insecticides (aldrin, DDD, DDE, DDT, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, heptachlor epoxide, lindane) and three herbicides (2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, silvex).
- **Sampling:** Monthly one-liter grab samples of water with suspended sediment, analyzed as received. Sampling began in October 1965. Duo-pak insulated containers were later adopted for air shipment, with no breakage in six months of use.
- **Methods:** Hexane extraction, cleanup, and [[Gas Chromatography]] on two columns (DC-200 and QF-1). Minimum measurable concentrations were 5 ppt for most compounds, 10 ppt for DDT, and 100 ppt for 2,4-D. Recovery was about 80% to 115% for insecticides and about 75% to 120% for herbicides. Data were not corrected for recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feed as a source:** Feed ingredients such as grains, meat scraps, alfalfa meal and fish oils are known to carry trace DDT and related compounds. These ingredients are common in trout feed.
- **Species differences:** Earlier work cited in the paper shows that fish species differ in pesticide tolerance (LC50 values) and that lots from different sources vary widely. Resistance has been documented in mosquitofish, golden shiners, green sunfish, bluegills and yellow bullheads.
- **Hatchery health:** Fish in the hatchery appeared normal, breeding stock was fed the same brand, and no reproductive problems had been noted. Water entering the hatchery showed no detectable pesticides.
- **Regulatory comparison:** No fish residue tolerances existed at the time. Rainbow trout levels were above FDA-accepted levels for beef fat.
- **Stream conditions:** Irrigation-network sites represent mainly agricultural areas, where pesticide residues are more likely. Stream discharge at the monitoring stations ranged widely, from a few hundred cubic feet per second to over 300,000 cfs at the Columbia River station.

## Key Findings

### Trout and Fish Food

1. **Feed contamination:** Feed from Source No. 1 contained all seven pesticides except DDD. Across the four sources, residues were small, and most were in the range of about 0.03 to 0.3 ppm. Source No. 3 contained only heptachlor and heptachlor epoxide, and no p,p'-DDT was detected in any feed sample.
2. **Trout residues:** All three species contained pesticides. Rainbow trout had residues of nearly every compound in nearly every sample, and the rainbow trout p,p'-DDT values were the highest recorded (up to about 4.0 ppm in the individual samples shown in Table 2).
3. **Species comparison:** Rainbow trout had significantly higher levels (0.05 confidence level, analysis of variance and studentized range test) of all seven pesticides than brook or brown trout. Brown trout had significantly higher heptachlor, heptachlor epoxide, dieldrin, DDE and DDD than brook trout.
4. **No p,p'-DDT in brook or brown trout:** Neither brook nor brown trout contained any p,p'-DDT isomer. The authors suggest this may reflect species differences in DDT degradation.
5. **Lipid conversion:** Extractable lipids were about 2.5% of fresh fish weight. Fresh-weight pesticide content can be estimated by dividing the lipid-basis results by 40.
6. **Open questions:** The paper does not establish whether differences reflect uptake, excretion or degradation, nor how much diet controls accumulation. Ongoing work aims to test whether genetic lines within a species show consistent differences in accumulation.

### Western Streams

1. **Insecticides detected:** All nine insecticides were found at one time or another, but not at every station. Concentrations were very small, from under 5 ppt of lindane up to 110 ppt of DDT.
2. **No herbicides:** No herbicide (2,4-D, 2,4,5-T or silvex) was detected at any station during the first year. The authors suggest rapid degradation, especially of 2,4-D, may explain this.
3. **Low concentrations:** About 50% or more of positive results were at the 5 ppt level.
4. **Seasonality:** Positive results were most often recorded in February through May, though no clear pattern emerged.
5. **Most and least common compounds:** Lindane was the most frequent insecticide (46 of 165 positive results). Aldrin was the least frequent, found only four times.
6. **Site differences:** The Rio Grande below Anzalduas Dam, Texas, had the most positive results (32, about 20% of the total). The Snake and Columbia river stations had the fewest (seven each).
7. **Limitations:** The data cannot show links to application practices or to sediment transport. The authors call for further work on transport, travel time and sampling methods.
