# Growth Rates of Fishes in Sub-Prison Lake Before and After Draining and Restocking

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This study compares fish growth in [[Sub-Prison Lake]], a 60-acre impoundment on the [[Stringtown State Game Refuge]] in Oklahoma, before and after a 1950–1951 draining, [[Rotenone]] treatment and restocking. A 1950 survey found an overpopulation of stunted fishes. After the lake was drained, treated and restocked in December 1951, growth rates in the July 1954 collections were higher for most species that appeared in both samples. The author attributes this to reduced density and the growth pattern typical of new impoundments. Angling success did not rise in step with growth, and high turbidity was identified as the main obstacle. The author concludes that stunting will likely return within two to three years unless harvest increases.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Sub-Prison Lake]] lies in Section 27, Township 1N, Range 12E, near Stringtown in Atoka County. It was formed by impounding a small intermittent tributary of [[North Boggy Creek]]. Its drainage area is about 1,500 acres.
- **Physical character:** The lake averages about 15 ft deep, with a maximum of 32 ft. It is slightly turbid to turbid, with shoreline buttonbush, willow and smartweed, and no submerged aquatic vegetation. The dam is earthen, with rock rip-rap and a concrete spillway.
- **Water conditions (1954 survey):** Average surface temperature was 89.8 °F and average bottom temperature was 66.1 °F at 32 ft. A [[Thermocline]] lay between 8 and 20 ft. Surface dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.6 to 7.6 ppm, and pH averaged 7.7.
- **Management sequence:**
  - An August 22, 1950 survey by the [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Department]] found an overpopulation of stunted fishes.
  - In December 1950 the lake was partly drained, and the remaining 10 acres were treated with rotenone to remove all fish.
  - On December 11, 1951, the [[Durant State Fish Hatchery]] restocked one-year-old fish: 6,000 [[Largemouth Bass]] (5.0 in), 4,000 [[Channel Catfish]] (4.0 in), 3,000 [[Black Crappie]] (3.0 in), and an unspecified number of [[Redear Sunfish]] (2.0 in). The redear count is not legible in the scanned source.
  - Apart from a small hook-and-line sample on March 12, 1953, the next full collection was made June 28 to July 15, 1954, by the [[Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory]] summer survey party, using seines, traps and rotenone.
- **Authorship:** Joe C. Finnell, Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory, Norman, Oklahoma. Reprinted from the *Proceedings of the Oklahoma Academy of Science*, Volume 35 (1954). Contribution Number 43 of the laboratory, a cooperative unit of the Oklahoma Game and Fish Department and the University of Oklahoma Biological Survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods**
- Ages and growth of 1950 fish were estimated by [[Scale and Spine Analysis]], assuming direct proportionality between body length and scale or spine radius, with a nomograph used for computation.
- Growth values are weighted averages per species, expressed as total length in inches.
- Only fish taken by rotenone on July 14, 1954 were used for post-drainage growth.
- [[Length-Frequency Histogram]]s of the six major species, based on all collection methods, appear in Figures 1 and 2. Species present in both samples are compared in Table 1. Species found in only one sample appear in Table 2.

**Community changes**
- Warmouth and pirateperch were present before draining but were apparently eliminated or did not escape in numbers sufficient to re-establish. Pirateperch age data had been published separately by Hall and Jenkins.
- Longear sunfish, orangespotted sunfish, spotted sucker and black bullhead were absent from the 1950 collection but appeared in 1954. The author suggests they were probably present but not collected earlier.
- Green sunfish, bluegill and golden shiner appeared in 1954 without being stocked (see Key Findings).

## Key Findings

**Largemouth bass**
- Pre-drainage (1950) growth was 4.9 in at the end of year one, then 7.6, 11.1, 13.8 and 15.9 in for the following years. The first-year value reflects hatchery growth.
- Post-drainage fish averaged 11.1 in and 14.0 in at the end of years two and three. The author calls this a significant increase over pre-drainage growth, but still below the state average for new small lakes.
- No two-year-olds were found. The author attributes this to stocked bass being too small to spawn the following spring. Spawning succeeded in spring 1953, and yearlings in the July 1954 sample measured 4.0 to 11.7 in.

**Channel catfish**
- Before draining, channel catfish were overpopulated, with one of the slowest growth rates in the state.
- All 1954 channel catfish were three years old (11.2 to 23.7 in). Mean length at the end of year three was 12.7 in, above the state average.
- No reproduction occurred after restocking, since no young-of-year or younger age classes were present in 1954. The 1954 sample contained 110 channel catfish.

**Crappies**
- White crappie were inadvertently stocked. In 1954 they were more numerous than black crappie (1,027 vs. 382 fish in the histograms) and grew faster than before draining. Both species exceeded state growth averages.
- Black crappie spawned in spring 1952, but the white crappie population lacked that year class.
- Black crappie were absent from the 1950 sample. In 1954 they averaged 3.3, 8.0 and 10.1 in at the end of years one to three.

**Redear sunfish**
- Growth increased after restocking, but the 1952 year class was absent. The 1954 sample contained 48 redear sunfish.

**Unstocked species**
- Green sunfish and bluegill were not stocked after refilling, yet appeared in 1954 with increased growth. Spawning in 1952 indicates that a few individuals survived the rotenone treatment or moved in from watershed ponds. The bluegill histogram covers 526 fish.
- Golden shiners, a prolific minnow, were not stocked but were abundant before and after draining. The 1954 sample was mostly yearlings, with only a few two-year-olds. The author concludes that survivors or immigrants repopulated the lake within two years, and growth increased significantly.

**Minor and single-sample species**
- Longear sunfish (8 fish, 2.8 in at year one), orangespotted sunfish (42 fish, 2.4 in at year one), spotted sucker (one fish, 5.7 in at year one, 11.6 in at year two) and black bullhead (7 fish, 5.0, 9.4, 12.0 and 14.2 in across years one to four) appeared only in the post-draining sample.
- Black bullheads of the 1950 and 1951 year classes suggest rotenone survival, but no later year classes appeared, indicating failed subsequent spawning.
- Warmouth (2 fish from 1950) and pirateperch (82 fish, 2.2 to 4.6 in across years one to four) were recorded only in the pre-draining sample.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- Removing the population and restocking converted an overpopulated, stunted fish community into one with accelerated growth, typical of new impoundments.
- Four species were stocked, but eleven well-established species were present three years later. The author attributes this to reproduction by the few fish that survived or immigrated.
- Among stocked species, only black crappie spawned in the first spring after planting. Largemouth bass and redear sunfish spawned successfully in 1953. Channel catfish did not reproduce.
- Angling success did not increase in proportion to growth. High turbidity has limited fishing of all types and must be corrected.
- Unless harvest increases, the author expects growth to decline and the lake to return to its stunted, overpopulated 1950 condition within two to three years.
