# Proceedings of the Second Annual Symposium on the Rare and Endangered Fishes of the Death Valley System (1970)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the summary of proceedings from the Second Annual Symposium on the Rare and Endangered Fishes of the [[Death Valley System]]. It was held November 17-18, 1970, at Death Valley National Monument Headquarters, Furnace Creek, California, and coordinated by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (Edwin P. Pister, symposium coordinator). Its participants were about 81 biologists, geologists, hydrologists, resource managers, and conservationists. The meeting was organized by the [[Desert Fishes Council]], which was formed at the meeting, and it reviewed the status of about ten endangered desert fishes. The central problem is that groundwater pumping for private irrigation in the [[Ash Meadows]] area of the [[Amargosa River Basin]] has been lowering the water tables that sustain these fishes. The main outcome was a consensus that the fishes can survive only if their home springs are restored and maintained in their pre-pumping condition. The meeting also produced a species-by-species action plan for 1970-71.

## Background & Description

The proceedings open with a preface quoting [[Alfred Russel Wallace]] (1863) on the value of preserving species, and a foreword by Pister that frames the effort as a race against time. Pister writes that the Pahrump killifish had already been lost, that the Cyprinodon diabolis might be too late to save, and that water levels in the upper Amargosa Basin continued to fall.

The fishes discussed are small, spring-adapted cyprinodontids and cyprinids that have survived since the [[Pleistocene]] in isolated "islands of water in a sea of sand." The [[Desert Fishes Council]] priority list of the ten most threatened species was:

1. Pahrump killifish, Nevada
2. Devils Hole pupfish, Nevada
3. Tecopa pupfish, California
4. Ash Meadows speckled dace, Nevada
5. Owens River speckled dace, California
6. Owens River chub, California
7. Ash Meadows pupfish, Nevada
8. Warm Spring pupfish, Nevada
9. Mohave chub, California
10. Owens pupfish, California

The [[Pupfish Task Force]] was created by the [[Department of the Interior]] in May 1970 to coordinate preservation. Its chairman was [[James T. McBroom]]. Agencies represented included the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[US Geological Survey]], the [[National Park Service]], and the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]]. The Desert Fishes Council elected Pister as chairman, and it was tasked with drafting a constitution, which was scheduled for adoption at the 1971 symposium.

The proceedings also contain an appendix of selected references, a hydrogeology paper, a proposal for captive breeding, and a list of about 130 attendees and their affiliations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat degradation.** The main threat is groundwater pumping. Pumping lowers the water table and reduces spring discharge. At [[Devils Hole]] the lower water level shades out the sunlight needed for algal food growth. It also exposes the limestone ledge that is the fish's only suitable spawning area. Researchers attribute declines at other springs, including [[Crystal Spring]], [[Jackrabbit Spring]], and [[Big Spring]], to the same causes. Several springs also suffered invasion by exotic fishes, and some were disturbed by human activity. The symposium noted that at Jackrabbit Spring a pump was found in July 1969, which destroyed the populations of the Ash Meadows pupfish and a *Rhinichthys* population.

**Ecological research.** Dr. James Brown described the central research questions. The first is evolutionary: how much divergence has occurred among isolated populations. The second is ecological: how pupfish have succeeded in so many different environments. He presented physiological tolerance data:
- Devils Hole pupfish tolerate short-term temperatures of 32°F to 108°F.
- Pupfish have been observed in salinities from nearly distilled water to several times that of seawater.
- Pupfish at "Tecopa Bore" (48°C water) live at their lethal temperature of 42°C.

Brown also cited [[Population Homeostasis]]. For example, 100 Ash Meadows pupfish introduced into Jackrabbit Spring grew to over 3,000 in about three months. At Mexican Spring the population never exceeded 50 fish in a full year.

**Groundwater hydrology.** The [[Winograd Hydrogeology Study]] (Appendix A) argues that the ground watershed feeding Ash Meadows is at least 4,500 square miles. It is bordered by the Spring Mountains and Sheep Range to the south and east. The study concludes that 35 to 45 percent of Ash Meadows discharge derives from the [[White River Basin]], and that underflow from Pahrump Valley is unlikely to be a major source. The water-budget estimate for the basin was 17,000 to 24,000 acre-feet annually. Dr. George Maxey concluded that the consultant to [[Spring Meadows Inc]] had overestimated available water, perhaps by as much as threefold.

**Land and water law.** Land holdings in Ash Meadows were roughly 35,000 acres of leased government land, 15,000 acres of private land, and about 5,000 acres exchanged to Spring Meadows Inc. There were 34 private landowners. Nevada follows the doctrine of [[Prior Appropriation]], under which beneficial use is the criterion. Nevada law prohibits water mining, though a "reasonable annual drawdown" is allowed in critical areas. Discussion noted that the Amargosa Valley Project might require a drawdown of as much as 100 feet. The Endangered Species Conservation Act of December 9, 1969 was reviewed. It lists 189 vertebrate species in the Secretary of the Interior's "Redbook."

**Owens Valley.** The [[Los Angeles Department of Water and Power]] owns over 300,000 acres in the Owens and Mono basins. The estimated safe yield of groundwater in Owens Valley was about 220 cubic feet per second.

## Key Findings

**Devils Hole (Cyprinodon diabolis)**
- The lowest recorded water levels were about 1.5 feet below the copper index washer (1956-67), 1.7 feet (1968), 2.3 feet (1969), and 3.08 feet (August 28, 1970).
- The 1970 low exposed about 55 percent of the limestone spawning shelf.
- An artificial fiberglass shelf and electric lights were installed to compensate. Fish reproduced well afterward.
- 24 fish were transplanted to Upper Warm Spring, Saline Valley, Inyo County, in August 1970. Four fish were seen there in mid-September. (The news release gives the transplant date as August 14, and the summary gives August 17.)
- Independent counts after the November symposium indicated the population had dropped to fewer than 300 individuals, approximately the normal seasonal level. The symposium recommended that no fish be taken for any purpose until a count is made in 1971.

**Pahrump Killifish (Empetrichthys latos)**
- 31 individuals were collected from Manse Spring on June 4, 1970, and placed in two springs near Lake Mohave, with about half in each.
- Dr. Deacon estimated about 200 fish remained in Manse Spring. Goldfish were also present there.
- The symposium recommended refugia at Shoshone, Spring Valley, White Pine County, Nevada, as the best known transplant site.

**Owens Pupfish (Cyprinodon radiosus)**
- 28 fish were introduced into the Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary at Fish Slough on June 30, 1970. The population grew to several thousand.
- 39 fish were moved from Fish Slough to Warm Springs Sanctuary on October 28, 1970.
- A third sanctuary was planned at BLM Spring.

**Ash Meadows Pupfish (C. n. mionectes)**
- Crystal, Jackrabbit, and Forest springs had all lost their populations. The subspecies persisted in several other springs.
- A cooperative agreement from January 1970 limited pumping at Jackrabbit Spring to a pumping pit.

**Mohave Chub (Gila mohavensis)**
- 150 chubs from Zzyzx Spring were transplanted to Piute Spring on December 18, 1966. This was considered temporary because of flash-flood risk.
- 140 chubs were sent to South Coast Botanical Gardens in January 1970.
- Two-hole Spring was fenced, and an introduction was made using fish under four inches long.
- A proposed introduction into Lark Seep Lagoon, China Lake Naval Weapons Center, was announced.

**Owens River Speckled Dace and Chub**
- 18 dace were collected at Mathieu's Ranch in summer 1970. 16 were transplanted to Warm Springs Sanctuary near Big Pine on September 7, 1970.
- Owens River chubs are known only from below Crowley Dam, where populations above and below the dam hybridized with exotic forms.

**Karyotype and Genetic Research**
- Dr. Teruya Uyeno and Dr. Robert Miller found that all 13 Cyprinodon forms studied have 46 chromosomes. A population from El Potosi showed male counts of 47 and female counts of 48, the only known sexual dimorphism in chromosome number among fishes.
- Cyprinid and catostomid fishes were compared. The catostomids apparently arose by polyploidy, with 100 chromosomes.

**Management Actions**
- Symposium priorities were reassigned, and an action plan for 1970-71 was adopted.
- The [[Bureau of Land Management]] reclassified 56 acres to protect Jackrabbit Spring and 60 acres to protect School Spring, and proposed reclassification of 2,020 acres in the Devils Hole area.
- The Bureau of Land Management lost its authority to sell land on December 23, 1970.
- The US Geological Survey groundwater study received $50,000 in funding. Its primary aim is to determine whether pumping is the cause of the Devils Hole decline.
- A proposal by Dr. Richard Haas of Fresno State College recommended distributing fish pairs to members of the [[American Killifish Association]] for captive breeding as insurance against extinction. The symposium asked that a formal proposal be prepared.
- Dr. Bruce Kuebler reported on the Los Angeles land acquisition and water export program. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] began a feasibility study for the Amargosa River Basin Project to turn "thousands of acres of desert into fertile farm land."

**Principles Articulated**
- Dr. James Brown argued that natural extinction is tolerable, but man-caused extinction can never be justified.
- The symposium concluded that transplants must not contaminate genetically pure populations. Contamination of the new Point of Rocks sanctuary by *C. n. mionectes* was noted as an example.
- Editors' notes emphasized caution. Conservation efforts should not damage other biota, and research should not disturb the populations being studied.
