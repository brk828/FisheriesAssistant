# Wyoming Fishes (Bulletin No. 4, 1946)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 bulletin of the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]], written by James R. Simon, is a field guide and annotated checklist of the fishes known to occur in Wyoming. It is intended for anglers, students, and conservation-minded readers rather than specialist ichthyologists. The author treats 69 full species of native fishes, plus eight native subspecies, two intergrades, and seven provisional species, and 20 introduced species. The volume combines identification keys, species accounts (distribution, size, spawning, food, angling value), drainage-by-drainage distribution lists, and a discussion of fish management history and policy.

## Background & Description

The work is based on field collecting in Wyoming since 1933 using seines, gill nets, traps, and electrofishing-era hand methods. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and examined at the University of Wyoming laboratories. Taxonomic identifications were checked by Dr. Carl L. Hubbs of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]].

The introduction explains basic [[Fish Morphology]] and identification methods:
- **Scales and rings:** Age is estimated from annuli ("winter lines") on scales, which are read under a microscope.
- **Fin formulae:** Dorsal and anal ray counts are written as, for example, "D X, 12; A V, 11" (spiny rays in Roman numerals).
- **Body proportions:** Descriptors such as "Head 5" or "Depth 4" express ratios against standard length, since fish grow continuously.
- **Taxonomic keys:** Family, genus, and species keys are provided, with a dichotomous family key using ventral fin position, adipose fin presence, scales, and teeth.

Thermal classes are defined: cold-water fishes (trout, grayling, whitefish, optimum below about 65°F), cool-water fishes (perch, pike; 65 to 75°F), and warm-water fishes (bass, crappie, sunfish; optimum above 75°F). Fishes are also grouped as [[Forage Fishes]], [[Game Fishes]], [[Hybrid Fishes]], and [[Bait Fishes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrography.** About one-fourth of Wyoming is mountainous, and the state's streams drain to four major systems: the [[Missouri River Basin]] (Platte, Powder, Big Horn, Belle Fourche, Cheyenne), the [[Colorado River Basin]] (Green, Little Snake, Yampa), the [[Columbia River Basin]] via the Snake River (Snake, Salt, Greys), and the [[Great Basin]] (Bear River). The Continental Divide crosses the state. The author estimates about 15,000 miles of fishable streams and well over 300,000 acres of lakes, excluding national park and purely artificial reservoirs.

**Habitat and reservoirs.** Reservoirs were found to be less productive than natural lakes because of water-level fluctuation, turbidity, and clearing of reservoir bottoms. At North Platte reservoirs (Pathfinder, Seminoe, Alcova, Guernsey; combined capacity 2,309,000 acre-feet), a 1939 drawdown reduced storage to 40,000-odd acre-feet and caused a complete loss of fish in one reservoir.

**Aquatic vegetation** was identified as essential for oxygen, food, and spawning cover, and turbidity was noted to limit plant growth and plankton, which in turn limits fish food.

**Introduced species and management.** Fish culture began in 1879 with the territorial fish commissioner. Early stocking of exotic species (carp, goldfish, bass, crappie, brown trout, and others) is documented, and the author notes that bass stocking in cold-water lakes was unsuccessful, with hundreds of thousands of largemouth bass wasted.

## Key Findings

**Species checklist.** The text covers 69 native species, 8 native subspecies, 2 intergrades, and 7 provisional species, plus 20 introduced species. Major groups include:
- **Trouts and salmons (Salmonidae):** Cutthroat trout (Yellowstone and Colorado River races), rainbow trout, brown trout, brook trout, Mackinaw (lake) trout, golden trout, Kokanee/redfish, landlocked salmon (provisional), and hybrids.
- **Suckers (Catostomidae):** About 15 species and subspecies, including mountain, flannelmouth, and bigmouth suckers and the endangered-status [[Humpback Sucker]].
- **Minnows (Cyprinidae):** Over 40 taxa, including chubs, dace, shiners, and the introduced carp and goldfish.
- **Sunfishes and bass (Centrarchidae):** Largemouth bass (Wyoming record 7 lb), bluegill, green sunfish, pumpkinseed, black crappie.
- **Perches (Percidae):** Yellow perch, walleye, sauger, and darters.
- **Catfishes (Ictaluridae), sculpins (Cottidae), and burbot (Gadidae).**

**Key species accounts.**
- The Yellowstone cutthroat trout is described as reproducing naturally in only one Wyoming segment (upper Yellowstone River and tributaries) without hatchery supplementation. Egg counts from 67 Reservoir (May 1938) ranged from 3,120 to 6,500 eggs per fish.
- The [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]] is described as among the most important game and food fishes in Green River lakes, with commercial catches on Jackson Lake recorded at 6,885 eggs per pound.
- Brown trout are described as hardier and more often caught by night than other trouts; the largest Wyoming brown trout record is given as 34 pounds (Jackson Lake area, 1945 record cited in the text as "one 34 pounds" with a 10-lb ordinary fish considered very large).
- The Mackinaw trout is described as the largest trout in the state, reaching 60 pounds in general range and 24 pounds for a Wyoming record.
- The [[Utah Chub]] and [[River Roundtail]] are discussed as predators and important food fishes in the Green River basin.

**Conservation and policy conclusions.**
- The author argues that stocking must be controlled and tied to closed seasons so that native trout can reproduce naturally.
- Forage fish (minnows, suckers) are defended as essential food for game fish, and the bait-fish trade is described as a significant sideline business that may spread exotic species.
- Protection of native trout, especially cutthroat trout, is recommended over stocking exotic trouts, and "stocked waters" should not be closed to allow stocked fry to grow.
- Hybridization between native cutthroat and rainbow trout is documented, with Green River populations mostly pure rainbow.

**Notable records.** Several species are noted as rare or extirpated in Wyoming, including the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]], the [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (only a few records, now provisional), the [[Sturgeon Chub]], and the [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]], restricted to a single spring system.
