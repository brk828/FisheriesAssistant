# Oral Tradition, Environmental Change and River Basin Management: Case Studies from Queensland and Victoria

#river-geomorphology #environmental-history #river-management #oral-tradition

## Executive Summary

This source is a 1995 *Australian Geographical Studies* paper by [[Brian Finlayson]] and [[Sandra Brizga]] (University of Melbourne). It tests popular, oral accounts of post-European river change against the historical documentary record and field evidence on two eastern Australian rivers: the [[Nogoa River]] (Central Queensland) and the [[Avon River]] (Gippsland, Victoria). Both oral histories proved to be substantially inaccurate, yet managers largely adopted them. The paper concludes that the oral tradition is a useful starting point but must be verified against documents and field evidence. The document also includes the opening pages of a second paper, by [[Brad Pillans]] and [[Pat Walker]], on the landscape and soils of the [[Monaro Basalt]] in New South Wales. That paper is truncated, so only its abstract and introduction are covered here.

## Background & Description

The authors identify three sources of evidence for environmental change in post-European Australian rivers:

- **Oral tradition**, including local knowledge of specific change events.
- **Documentary records**, such as written descriptions, maps, survey data and photographs.
- **Physical evidence** in the landscape, such as [[Palaeochannels]], pollen and sediments.

Australia's centrally organised colonial administration, officially sponsored exploration that followed rivers, and aerial photography from the 1940s make its documentary record unusually good. The authors note that a widespread managerial belief holds that European settlement destabilised a previously stable landscape. Research, by contrast, emphasises natural instability in Australian alluvial rivers, on which human disturbance was superimposed. The paper asks whether similar discrepancies between folklore and evidence occur at the scale of individual river basins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nogoa River.** The Nogoa is a tributary of the Fitzroy River at about 400 m altitude, east of the Great Divide. It has a Köppen BSwh climate with 500–600 mm annual rainfall. Climate is variable: 10% of years are arid, 50% semi-arid and 40% humid (Dick, 1964). The river flows over the impervious [[Moolayember Formation]] and below the [[Precipice Sandstone]], a recharge aquifer of the [[Great Artesian Basin]]. Overflow springs from that aquifer maintain continuous flow in the upper Nogoa. Land use has been low-intensity cattle grazing on large leases.

**Avon River.** The Avon rises in Palaeozoic mountain country and flows southeast to Lake Wellington in the [[Gippsland Lakes]]. Its climate is Köppen Cfb, with rainfall from over 1000 mm in the mountains to under 600 mm on the floodplain, and it is less variable than the Nogoa. Most of the floodplain was cleared for agriculture, and the land is largely freehold, with the Macalister Irrigation District nearby.

**Streamflow comparison (Table I, from Finlayson and McMahon, 1988).** The table compares mean annual runoff (MAR) and coefficient of variation (Cv) for the two rivers, Australia and the world. The Nogoa has MAR of 35 mm and Cv of 1.35. The Avon has MAR of 124 mm and Cv of 0.69. Australia has MAR of 416 mm and Cv of 0.70. The world figures are MAR of 612 mm and Cv of 0.43. The Nogoa is therefore far more variable than the Avon.

**Monaro Basalt.** The basalt upland south of Cooma sits at about 1000 m and forms a continental divide between east-coast rivers and the [[Murray-Darling Basin]]. K/Ar dating gives 34–54 Ma (Lower Eocene to Lower Oligocene). Pollen evidence suggests some of the oldest flows are uppermost Palaeocene. Weak dissection and many enclosed lakes indicate prolonged erosional stability, in contrast to the strongly dissected coastal escarpment.

## Key Findings

**1. The Nogoa "Lake Salvator" was a swamp, not a lake.**
- [[Thomas Mitchell]] discovered the Nogoa in July 1846 and named it the River Salvator. He reported a large lake, Lake Salvator, which he could not find an outlet for.
- The evidence indicates the "lake" was a 13 km stretch of swampy floodplain where the river split into anastomosing channels. Overflow springs from the Precipice Sandstone kept it wet.
- The downstream bed gradient is 0.003. A lake of Mitchell's mapped size would have needed a 26 m wall to impound water, which is impossible in this landscape (Finlayson, 1984).
- An 1889 survey traversed the "lake" and recorded running water. Lands Department files from early settlement never call it a lake. The "drained" or "silted up" account appears only in reports from 1931 and 1937, and it became local folklore.

**2. Sediment dating shows young, repeatedly reworked alluvium.**
- Radiometric dating of wood and charcoal gives c. 3,500 years at 3.5 m depth. The authors infer repeated stripping and redeposition, possibly linked to a threshold fan gradient (cf. Schumm, 1977).

**3. Folklore drove legislation and management on the Nogoa.**
- The silted-lake belief was used to justify [[Salvator Rosa National Park]] (1957), even though cattle were then allowed to graze there until destocking and fencing in 1982.
- A [[Watercourse Reserve]] was created by Act of Parliament to stop a new lessee from "re-creating" the lake and cutting water to downstream leases. The authors note this legislation rests on folklore rather than evidence.
- Noogoora Burr control was complicated by the belief that a buried seed bank lay under the silt.

**4. The Avon's channel change is more complex than folklore suggests.**
- The oral tradition holds that the Avon was a "normal" narrow, sinuous stream until a major transformation in the 1880s or 1890s. The 1916 Hansard anecdote describes a river now "300 or 400 yards wide".
- SRWSC officer [[H.G. Strom]] estimated the bed deepened by up to 12 feet (3.7 m) and the channel widened to as much as "half a mile".
- Documentary and field evidence shows dramatic change only between Weirs Crossing and Redbank. Change there began before the 1890s, though a large 1891 flood caused spectacular erosion near the Stratford railway bridge.
- Upstream of Weirs Crossing to Valencia Creek, the channel was already incised at European settlement and has changed little since. A photograph from 1872 shows high eroding banks.
- Below Redbank, the documentary record shows the channel was once navigable by ocean-going steamers in the mid-nineteenth century but has since filled with sand. Here the oral account was accurate and helped guide the archival research.

**5. Land-loss estimates were based on a methodological error.**
- SRWSC officers compared the channel area mapped in the 1870s with that in 1936 and concluded that nearly 1800 acres (728 ha) was eroded. About 200 acres (81 ha) became inferior grazing land, leaving almost 1600 acres (648 ha) "consumed" by the river.
- The comparison set the 1936 flood channel against the 1870 low-flow channel. Like-for-like comparison shows virtually no change in channel size. The SRWSC also misread the limits of the incised floodplain as channel banks.

**6. Management on the Avon was intensive and rested on the same folklore.**
- Before 1951, works were site-specific. The Avon River Improvement Trust was constituted in 1951 under the River Improvement Act of 1948.
- The Trust carried out extensive bank stabilisation using groynes and willow planting, justified by the idea that erosion was unnatural and that restoring a "stable" channel was a moral duty, and by the economic loss of land.
- No economic, environmental or social benefit analysis of this spending has ever been done, and spending was continuing at the time of writing.

**7. Discussion and conclusions.**
- Both case studies show that oral histories contained serious inaccuracies, and that managers used the more accessible folklore rather than the documentary record.
- The oral tradition can be a valuable starting point, but it must be verified by documentary and field evidence. This approach follows Hooke and Kain (1982).
- Inaccuracies often have traceable origins, such as Mitchell's writings or the 1891 flood. Erroneous ideas gain legitimacy simply by being repeated in official documents.
- A further example comes from the Snowy River near Orbost, where the popular belief that the channel is aggrading is unsupported by 1920s cross sections or earlier records.
- The authors conclude that history must be investigated "with the same rigour as any other branch of science."

**8. Monaro Basalt landscape (truncated paper).**
- The abstract describes prolonged landscape stability, evidenced by weak dissection and many small lakes. Poorly developed soils and weathered zones are inconsistent with prolonged Cainozoic weathering under climates different from today.
- Low depositional landforms called "lake shadows" lie on the eastern perimeters of most lakes. They are formed of clay pellets blown from basin floors during dry phases.
- The authors attribute the thin soils and sediment cover mainly to deflation during Quaternary glacial periods, reactivated today. The lake basins are partly solutional and partly deflational, and the modern soil cover reflects the modern climate.
- The text ends before the full argument is presented.
