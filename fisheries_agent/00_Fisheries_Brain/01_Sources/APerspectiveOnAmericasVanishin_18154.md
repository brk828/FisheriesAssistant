# America's Vanishing Streams: Hydropower Development, Stream Protection, and the Remaining Free-Flowing Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This presidential address by [[Arthur C. Benke]] (Department of Biology, University of Alabama) synthesizes available data on the physical degradation of streams in the contiguous United States, focusing on hydropower dams. It combines [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC) hydropower data with a previously unanalyzed subset of the [[Nationwide Rivers Inventory]] (NRI) to evaluate how much high-quality stream habitat remains and where it lies. The paper concludes that only 42 free-flowing rivers longer than 200 km remain in the 48 contiguous states, that most large rivers are severely altered, and that existing federal protection is insufficient, especially in the eastern U.S.

## Background & Description

The paper frames a central policy question: what is the appropriate balance between water development (water supply, hydropower, irrigation) and conservation of natural streams? The author notes that biotic impoverishment of freshwater organisms has received little attention compared with tropical terrestrial losses, partly because extensive freshwater biological inventories have never been launched.

Key historical context:

- Major North American river modification began in the early 19th century, with accelerated water development from the 1930s for flood control, water supply, navigation, and electricity.
- Three federal agencies drove the largest projects: the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] (the Corps), the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), and the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (TVA).
- By 1988 the Corps operated 80 power plants (about 28% of U.S. hydropower), BOR had 56 plants under its jurisdiction, and TVA owned 31 hydropower plants.
- These three agencies and smaller federal bodies controlled 52% of conventional hydroelectric capacity; municipal utilities held 22% and private utilities 26%.
- FERC, established in 1920, holds sole licensing authority for new and renewed hydropower plants.

The paper's objectives were to (1) synthesize hydropower trends and projections from FERC data, (2) review stream protection progress, and (3) analyze the NRI to map high-quality streams and identify the largest remaining free-flowing rivers. Analysis was limited to the 48 contiguous states and to hydropower impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author emphasizes that flow regulation is particularly damaging to invertebrates and fishes below dams and can sever critical river-floodplain interactions. The [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al. 1980) describes structural and functional change from headwaters to large rivers, but the author asks whether it applies to existing North American streams or whether [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] (Ward and Stanford 1983) better describes them. Only two free-flowing rivers remaining in the 48 states (the Yellowstone and the Salmon) approach the size envisioned by the River Continuum Concept, and the six 200-km rivers that flow unimpounded to the sea are all low-gradient [[Coastal Plain]] rivers in the southeastern states, which deviate from the original concept.

The NRI criteria for stream selection were: at least 40 km length; degree of free-flowing condition; degree of corridor development; and outstanding natural and cultural values. The NRI did not explicitly address biological diversity, biological integrity, or water quality, though the author argues most selected streams probably have good aquatic communities. The Cahaba River in Alabama, free-flowing and over 200 km long, supports 131 fish species (Pierson et al. 1989), yet only 113 km were listed in the NRI, illustrating how the inventory can under-represent high-quality systems.

## Key Findings

**Stream extent and quality**

- The NRI estimated about 5,200,000 km of streams in the contiguous 48 states (excluding Alaska and Hawaii).
- The original NRI listed 1,524 segments totaling approximately 99,300 km (1.9%) of high-quality stream. The author's updated database, including National and new Wild and Scenic Rivers, totaled 110,554 km.
- Abstract framing: about 2% (<100,000 km) of streams have sufficient high-quality features to merit federal protection consideration.

**Hydropower trends (FERC 1988)**

- Reliance on hydropower peaked in the 1930s at about 30% of total U.S. generating capacity; hydropower capacity has since increased 7-fold but now contributes only 12%.
- In 1988, 71 million kW of the 146 million kW of conventional hydropower capacity was in use; use was projected to reach 80 million kW by 2000.
- About 32 million kW of potential capacity was precluded by federal river protection legislation, and 44 Wild and Scenic Rivers added in November 1988 will lower the ceiling further.
- Of 2,010 operational conventional sites in early 1988, the North Atlantic has the most developed sites, while the Pacific has the most potential sites. The Pacific has the greatest capacity but 62% is already used; the East-South-Central is the only other region with substantially more than half its capacity developed.
- Plants over 100 MW make up 7% of plants but provide 75% of capacity; plants under 5 MW make up 63% of plants but only 2% of capacity.
- Over 1,100 projects above 5 MW were planned or projected in 1988, a 55% increase in sites that adds only 2% to total hydropower capacity and 0.3% to U.S. electric generating capacity.

**Protection status**

- Wild and Scenic or National River status protected almost 16,000 km (abstract), with 14,900 km and 119 segments cited in the text after the 1988 additions; fewer than 1,600 km lie east of the Mississippi River.
- The first National River system was Missouri's Current River and Jack's Fork (1964), followed by the Buffalo (Arkansas, 1972), South Fork Cumberland (Tennessee, 1974), and New River Gorge (West Virginia, 1976).
- The Wild and Scenic Rivers Act (1968) prohibits federal approval or assistance for projects that may adversely affect designated segments, but offers weak protection against private development.
- More than half of states have their own wild and scenic river systems, designating over 22,000 km.

**Regional patterns in NRI streams**

- The South-Atlantic region holds the greatest length of high-quality rivers (23,488 km) yet has the least federal protection.
- Corrected for land area, eastern regions have the highest river densities; the Pacific and Mountain regions have the highest protected fractions but low density.
- The Coastal Plain and Central Lowland physiographic provinces contain the most NRI river length, while the Great Plains and Basin and Range have less, mainly due to low rainfall and intensive water use.
- Delaware (98.7 m/km²) and New Hampshire (68.9 m/km²) have among the highest densities; Montana (6.8 m/km²) and Arizona (6.8 m/km²) among the lowest.

**Free-flowing rivers**

- Only 42 rivers longer than 200 km remain free-flowing in the contiguous 48 states. The longest is the Yellowstone (Montana), followed by the Salmon (Idaho). Georgia and Idaho each have five.
- The greatest concentration of these rivers is in the South-Atlantic region.
- Only eight of the 42 hold federal protection (Salmon, Middle Fork Clearwater, St. Joe, John Day, South Fork Owyhee, Buffalo (AK), Current, and Black Creek); the rest are candidates for future protection.
- Comparison with [[Stanford and Ward]] (1979), who identified 50 free-flowing rivers using a 100 km minimum, found 21 rivers in common, with 21 additional rivers identified by the NRI analysis.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Hydropower and other exploitation (domestic and agricultural water supply) will continue to pressure undeveloped streams; other exploitation types are harder to document.
- Eastern river protection is expected to remain meager even if new federal protection is obtained in the early 1990s.
- The author argues that the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act alone is insufficient and recommends National River Parks protecting entire watersheds or other comprehensive protection.
- The American Rivers "outstanding rivers list" (Echeverria and Fosburgh 1988) totals over 483,000 km, more than four times the updated NRI list, reflecting different selection criteria.
- The author urges professional societies, including the [[North American Benthological Society]], to play a greater role in conserving the remaining natural systems.
