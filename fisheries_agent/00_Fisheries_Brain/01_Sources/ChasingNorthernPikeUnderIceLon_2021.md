# Chasing Northern Pike under Ice: Long-Distance Movements Following Catch-and-Release Ice Angling

#ichthyology #esocidae #recreational_fisheries #telemetry #winter_ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) is an important target species for ice angling in northern regions. In [[Buffalo Pound Lake]], pike can reach sizes over 110 cm total length and 15 kg. The study tracked 15 individuals ranging from 56 to 98.5 cm total length. Age, maturity, fecundity, and growth parameters are not reported in this document. Pike are characterized as sit-and-wait ambush predators with large mouths and numerous sharp teeth. Larger fish are specifically targeted for catch-and-release ice angling.

**Habitat Associations**: Buffalo Pound Lake is a shallow eutrophic lake (mean depth 3.6 m, maximum depth 5 m, surface area 2,950 ha) in the [[Qu'Appelle River]] system of southern Saskatchewan. The lake is long and narrow with little depth heterogeneity or underwater structure. Water is highly regulated by dams. Water temperature during the study was 1–3°C. High conductivity in the water makes radiotelemetry difficult. Most relocations of tagged pike were near the center of the lake, with few close to shore. The lake supports [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]], and Northern Pike populations and is heavily used for recreational ice fishing from late November to end of March.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: All but one of 15 tracked pike moved unexpectedly long distances of several kilometers over the 7-day tracking period. For fish tracked over all 7 days, path lengths ranged from 1.4 to 18.4 km. Minimum daily distance traveled ranged from 0 to 4.6 km and varied significantly by tracking day; fish moved shorter distances on day 4 postrelease. Most minimum daily movements were between 0.5 to 1.5 km. Pike moved both west (upstream; n=9) and east (downstream; n=6) of the release area, spreading widely over eastern and central portions of the lake. Several individuals made notably directional movements (e.g., fish 336 moved west consistently, covering >10 km in 6 days). Other fish moved west initially but turned back toward the release area at the end of tracking. Only one fish (386) remained close to the release site. These movements were larger than expected given cold water temperatures and the species' ambush hunting strategy.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Northern Pike are active foragers in winter. The large movements documented may reflect normal foraging patterns. Larger fish may require more movement and a larger area to find appropriate prey. The document notes that pike are sit-and-wait predators, but observed movements suggest more active space use than previously envisioned. Specific prey items and feeding observations are not reported.

**Environmental Drivers**: Water temperature (1–3°C) and ice cover (84–100 cm thick) characterized the winter environment. Despite cold temperatures that might physiologically constrain movement, pike demonstrated substantial mobility. There was a significant interaction between fish size and day postrelease affecting movement patterns. Larger fish moved shorter minimum daily distances and dispersed shorter distances from the release site over the first 2 days of tracking. By day 5 after release, both the largest and smallest fish had dispersed the longest distances from the release site, while intermediate-sized fish dispersed the least. The cumulative path length traveled by day 5 did not reveal significant size-based patterns.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Not reported. The document notes that Buffalo Pound Lake has been a main source of brood stock for Walleye and Northern Pike stocking programs in Saskatchewan since 2013.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Winter catch-and-release angling is a popular recreational activity. Recent studies showed that pike were not often deep-hooked on tip-ups and suffered no short-term mortality after ice angling. However, larger pike are generally more difficult to play, handle, and dehook than smaller ones, which may increase the likelihood of stress and injury. The study found no apparent mortality during tracking (all fish moved). One fish (353) was caught and killed by an angler on March 10, 2018, after 2 days of tracking. The acoustic signal for fish 335 disappeared after day 2 and could not be relocated despite extensive search effort. Handling issues for pike include prolonged handling and air exposure due to their tendency to clamp jaws shut and the use of tip-up devices that may allow fish more time to swallow bait.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Habitat and space requirements for Northern Pike in winter can encompass much larger areas and more habitat features than has been previously envisioned. Management should consider that relatively large movements may be common for Northern Pike in winter despite cold water temperatures. Fish size is an important modifier of postrelease behavior and should be considered in management decisions. Larger fish may require an initial recovery or rest period prior to resuming normal movements. The study suggests that pike may be generally resilient to careful winter catch-and-release angling when fish are not deep-hooked and handling/air exposure times are short. More information about fish welfare, potential behavioral changes, and fish habitat and space use in winter is required to inform best practices for ice angling.

**Biological Reference Points**: Not reported. The study tracked fish ranging from 56 to 98.5 cm total length, with the largest fish in the study above 90 cm, substantially larger than any used in previous studies.
