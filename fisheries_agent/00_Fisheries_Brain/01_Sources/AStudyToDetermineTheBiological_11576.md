# PIT Tag Feasibility Study for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #fish-tagging #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report evaluates the biological feasibility of passive integrated transponder ([[PIT Tags]]) for marking endangered fishes in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The study tested PIT tags on [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) as a surrogate for endangered *Gila* species. Conducted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] from 1987-1989, the study examined tag retention, wound healing, and equipment performance in both field (pond) and laboratory (hatchery) settings. While PIT tags demonstrated 100% retention and verification rates with minimal tissue damage, the field studies suffered from poor fish recovery rates that limited statistical analysis. Laboratory studies successfully demonstrated that juvenile Colorado squawfish as small as 120-165 mm TL (11-28 g) could be PIT tagged with high survival.

## Background & Description

The [[PIT Tag]] is a cylindrical, glass-encapsulated transponder measuring approximately 11 mm × 2.1 mm and weighing < 0.05 g. The tag is completely passive, emitting no energy until activated by an external electromagnetic exciter that transmits a uniquely identifiable signal. Each tag can be individually coded with one of approximately 34 billion codes. The tag is detected using a portable, hand-held, battery-powered detector/decoding system (Identification Devices model HS-0033) that can read tags through several centimeters of tissue without restraining or anesthetizing the fish.

Previous tagging methods for [[Colorado River]] endangered fishes included [[Carlin-Dangler Tags]], anchor (Floy) tags, coded-nose-wire tags, and fin clipping with liquid nitrogen mutilation. Concerns about tag retention and potential injury from these methods prompted investigation of PIT tags. The Carlin-dangler tag, used extensively over the previous 10 years, raised concerns about secondary infections from needle punctures and unsatisfactory retention.

Two anatomical sites were selected for PIT tag implantation: the body cavity (approximately 10 mm anterior to the pelvic girdle and 5 mm lateral to mid-ventral line for fish > 200 mm; immediately posterior to pectoral fin for fish < 165 mm) and the dorsal musculature (5-10 mm below and posterior to dorsal fin origin). Tags were implanted manually using a modified hypodermic syringe with 12-gauge needle, following protocols developed for [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus* spp.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Study sites included four ponds in the [[Grand Valley]] near [[Grand Junction]], Colorado: [[Gardner Pond]], [[13th-Hole Pond]] (at Tiara Rado Golf Course), [[West Pond]], and [[Humphrey Pond]]. These gravel-pit and golf-course ponds presented uncontrolled environmental variables including winter ice cover and dissolved oxygen fluctuations. Winter water quality monitoring in January-February 1989 revealed adequate dissolved oxygen (> 7 mg/L) in most ponds, but critically low levels in Gardner Pond (3 mg/L on 9 February) and Humphrey Pond (2 mg/L on 9 and 24 February).

The ponds contained other fish species including [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). Despite low dissolved oxygen in some ponds, overwinter survival of these resident species appeared high, suggesting that factors other than winter conditions contributed to poor recovery of study fish.

Laboratory studies were conducted at [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (NFH), Colorado, using circular tanks (2.4 m diameter × 0.51 m deep, capacity 2,275 L) with constant groundwater supply at approximately 15°C. Fish were fed commercially-prepared dry trout pellets (size No. 4) and naturally-grown amphipods (*Gammarus* spp.).

## Key Findings

**Tag Performance:**
- Tag retention was 100% for all recovered fish across all species and anatomical sites
- Tag verification (successful detection and code reading) was 100% for all recovered fish
- The scanner/decoder unit reliably detected and accurately read tags *in vivo*, usually with only one pass
- Tags did not migrate substantially from insertion points: body cavity tags were found embedded in posterior pyloric caeca or adjacent adipose tissue; dorsal musculature tags remained within 5 mm of insertion point

**Wound Healing:**
- Healing began by day 2 post-implantation for all species
- By study termination (up to 437 days), wounds on all PIT-tagged fish had healed completely with only small scars barely noticeable
- Wounds from body cavity insertion were less noticeable than dorsal musculature insertion
- No swelling, edema, or infection was observed near tags
- A four-point wound classification scale showed progression from open wound (Code A) to completely healed with minimal scar (Code D)

**Species-Specific Results:**

*Roundtail Chub (Gardner Pond):* 25 fish per group (control, body cavity, dorsal musculature) established June 1988. Recovery was extremely poor: 0 fish on day 133, 3 fish on day 343, 1 fish on day 351, 3 fish on day 430. Initial size: 262-435 mm TL, 125-702 g.

*Roundtail Chub (13th-Hole Pond):* 25 fish per group established July 1988. Some mortality before release (2 control, 7 body cavity, 3 dorsal musculature). Recovery: 14 fish on day 114, 14 fish on day 296, 11 fish on day 360, 0 fish on day 437. Initial size: 277-423 mm TL, 158-592 g.

*Colorado Squawfish (West Pond):* 20 fish per group established 30 September 1988. Recovery by angling was ineffective: 0 fish on day 212. Rotenone treatment on day 310 recovered only 12 of 60 original fish (20%): 5 control, 4 dorsal musculature, 3 body cavity. Initial size: 220-298 mm TL, 36-164 g. Low recovery attributed to angling stress and dense aquatic vegetation.

*Razorback Sucker (Gardner Pond):* Initial groups of 6 fish each; 8 fish died before release. No fish recovered on days 133, 343, 351, or 430. Initial size: 301-373 mm TL, 300-600 g.

*Razorback Sucker (Humphrey Pond):* 3 fish PIT tagged (dorsal musculature only) 30 March 1989. All 3 fish recovered on day 163 with 100% tag retention and verification. Initial size: 415-429 mm TL, 852-984 g.

*Colorado Squawfish (Hotchkiss NFH):* Control (38 fish) and body cavity group (37 fish) established 27 July 1989. Six fish accidentally lost during tank cleaning. On day 70, tag retention and verification was 100% for remaining 34 PIT-tagged fish; 85% showed only small scars. **This demonstrated successful PIT tagging of fish as small as 120-165 mm TL (11-28 g).**

**Study Limitations:**
- Poor fish recovery from ponds precluded statistical analysis of differences between groups
- Small sample sizes prevented assessment of PIT tag effects on growth and survival
- Exact fate of non-recovered fish unknown, though likely due to capture/handling stress rather than tagging effects
- Wild riverine roundtail chub may have been unable to adapt to pond environments
- Similar mortality patterns across control and test groups suggest factors unrelated to PIT tags

**Equipment and Methodology:**
- PIT tags cost $3.75-$6.00 per tag depending on order quantity
- Scanner/decoder unit cost approximately $1,750
- Recommended equipment modifications: longer coiled power cord, more durable metal connector, smaller hand-held scanner
- Tags and needles were disinfected with 70% isopropyl alcohol
- Fish were anesthetized with MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate) prior to handling
- External fin marks distinguished control and test groups

**Management Implications:**
- PIT tags appear biologically compatible with tested species based on limited data
- High cost may limit use to small lots of fish (brood stock, genetics management) rather than batch marking for augmentation
- Tags could be recovered from senescent brood fish and reused to reduce costs
- Not identifiable by general public, limiting utility for some monitoring programs
- Requires sophisticated equipment and training, but no special licenses

**Additional Work:**
- 164 adult Colorado squawfish and 527 juvenile and adult razorback sucker were PIT tagged at [[Dexter NFH]] in September 1989 for ongoing monitoring
- Companion studies evaluating V.L. (visible implant) tags, Carlin tags, acrylic dyes, and Floy-anchor tags were planned for Dexter NFH starting fall 1989
- Field studies of PIT tags on wild [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in lower Colorado River basin were ongoing

The report recommends continuing PIT tag evaluation in controlled hatchery settings rather than ponds, developing written protocols for various tagging systems, and conducting workshops to train researchers in proper application techniques. No single tag is optimal under all conditions; multiple tagging systems may be necessary depending on fish size and study objectives.
