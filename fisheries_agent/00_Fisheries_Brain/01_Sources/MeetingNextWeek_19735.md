# Glen Canyon Dam Experimental Flood Planning Proposals (1995–1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation of planning documents, dated mainly October–November 1995, assembles proposals from federal, state, tribal and university groups to study the effects of a planned spring 1996 experimental high flow from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The flow was planned at 45,000 cfs for about seven days, released between an 8,000 cfs steady baseline in March–April 1996. The agencies framed the flood as a manipulative experiment to support adaptive management of the dam, not merely a demonstration. Proposals cover sediment transport, [[Sand Bar]] dynamics, [[Lake Powell]] limnology, primary production, fish, invertebrates, backwaters, riparian vegetation, birds, cultural resources and the endangered [[Kanab Ambersnail]].

## Background & Description

The document set includes:

- A meeting memo and logistics notes for a planning meeting on 8–9 November 1995 at the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Field Center in Flagstaff, organized by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES).
- Summary abstracts from the USGS on five sub-projects: streamflow and sediment, predictive modeling, sand bars in lateral separation eddies, Lake Powell thermal and chemical structure, and primary production.
- Proposals from [[Northern Arizona University]] (sand bar surveys and GIS vegetation study), the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), AGFD with BIO-WEST and the [[Hualapai Tribe]] (Hualapai Department of Natural Resources), a backwater rejuvenation study, Section 6 and FY96 proposals on the Kanab Ambersnail, drift and food-base studies, Hualapai aquatic, riparian and cultural monitoring, and a National Park Service monitoring plan for Glen Canyon National Recreation Area.

The experimental flow was tied to the 1995 Environmental Impact Statement and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion. Several proposals describe the earlier [[Interim Flows]] regime as having shrunk backwater habitat and eroded sand bars.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and sand.** Sand enters the Grand Canyon reach from the [[Paria River]] and the [[Little Colorado River]]. Dam operations were predicted to aggrade downstream debris fans, although recent work suggested that dam releases significantly erode these deposits. Sustained flows well above powerplant capacity had not occurred since 1986, and about half of the sand bars were reported eroded and lost during that period.

**Lake Powell and tailwater.** [[Lake Powell]] processes control many water qualities in the Grand Canyon reach. The proposed high release would remove a large volume of water quickly, raising questions about source water, temperature patterns, currents and up- or downwelling at the end of the release.

**Food base.** The trophic base of the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] tailwater trout fishery was described as the benthic alga [[Cladophora]], its epiphytes and grazers, plus drifting Cladophora that supports food webs further downstream. Most primary production occurs in the reach below the dam.

**Backwaters.** [[Return Current Channel]]s (RCCs) are the largest and most common backwater type in Grand Canyon and serve as warm, shallow rearing habitat for young native fish. Over half of the historical study sites have return channels that may be backwater habitat.

**Listed and sensitive species.** The Kanab Ambersnail is endangered and known from only two locations: the type locality near Kanab Creek, Utah, and [[Vasey's Paradise]], a spring-fed riparian gallery about 31.8 miles below Lees Ferry. The [[Northern Leopard Frog]] colony between the dam and Lake Mead is noted as the only known one on that reach. [[Humpback Chub]] is a listed native fish of concern.

## Key Findings

**1. Sediment and geomorphology (USGS, NAU)**
- Proposed remeasurement of 199 cross sections first surveyed in 1984, to refine high-stage channel geometry for a discharge-routing model.
- Channel roughness was estimated from dye-tracer velocity at 425 m³/s (15,000 ft³/s) and channel geometry at 792 m³/s (28,000 ft³/s); new measurements aim to cover the lowest and highest likely flows.
- Sand storage was to be documented at a network of 100 monumented cross sections before and after the high releases.
- Bar survey design: 32 study sites surveyed on three trips (pre-flood baseline, immediately post-flood, and about six months after). Each trip requires at least 17 days. Survey monitoring began in 1990 and continued biannually.
- Null hypothesis: no detectable change in size and morphology of sand bars. Alternative: a net increase, with eddy return channels larger than before the flood.
- The USGS sub-project on eight lateral separation eddies aims to test a computational model of eddy sediment transport.

**2. Lake Powell (USGS)**
- Conductivity-temperature-depth (CTD) profiles were planned on 30 March and twice daily from 31 March to 8 April at six sites between the dam and Romano Narrows (LP Mile 13).
- Acoustic Doppler current profiling was planned at the same six transects, with closer-interval transects from 8 to 12 April to document upwelling or downwelling at the end of the release.

**3. Primary production (USGS, with AGFD)**
- Method: pH and dissolved oxygen measured every 15 minutes for 24-hour periods before and after the flood. Diel oscillation amplitude is expected to be proportional to primary production. Reduced amplitude after the flood would indicate reduced production.

**4. Fish and aquatic food base (AGFD, Lees Ferry reach)**
- Electrofishing and monitoring during the 8,000 cfs flows before and after the 45,000 cfs flood, with sampling from the Paria River to Badger Rapid to estimate downstream displacement.
- Concern: the flood could transport young-of-the-year rainbow trout and possibly collapse the fishery.
- Creel surveys: four weekdays and four weekend days per month, before and after the flood.
- CPOM sampled in triplicate at several stages of the flow; benthic invertebrates collected with Hess samples at three sites; periphyton biomass measured on ten samples and chlorophyll a on six per site and time.
- Hypotheses Ho1 to Ho7 test whether distribution, density, diet, health, angler use, CPOM, benthos, periphyton and Chara beds differ before and after the flood.

**5. Upper and lower river fishes and backwaters (AGFD, BIO-WEST, HDNR)**
- Four AGFD river trips: one pre-flood and three post-flood.
- Native fish studied: humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace. Non-native fish: fathead minnow, plains killifish, rainbow trout.
- Hypotheses cover fish distribution, juvenile habitat selection, backwater sediment particle size, invertebrate and zooplankton recolonization, backwater physical change, and backwater number.
- Ten adult humpback chub were to be radio-tagged about 20 days before the flood to track movement.

**6. Backwater rejuvenation (multi-agency)**
- Proposed intensive study of a large RCC at Mile 55.5R, with bathymetry, grain-size, geochemistry, groundwater flow (using lithium bromide tracer), weather stations, thermistor arrays along up to eight transects, and weekly to monthly plankton tows.

**7. Kanab Ambersnail (AGFD, GCES, Stevens et al.)**
- The experimental flow was expected to inundate and potentially scour 11 to 16 percent of available habitat at Vasey's Paradise and could eliminate a proportionally high share of the population.
- Planned work: mid-March 1996 pre-flood mapping; immediate post-flood survey in mid-April; monthly monitoring May–September; final photogrammetry in November 1996; results due 30 September 1996.
- Mitigation requirements from the Fish and Wildlife Service had not yet been identified.

**8. Riparian vegetation and birds (NAU, Hualapai)**
- NAU GIS study of nine sites (RM 43.1 to 209), using vegetation polygons mapped in 1994–1995, to assess loss of wetland plants such as Typha and Juncus and weedy exotics such as Lepidium latifolium, Salsola iberica and Alhagi camelorum.
- Hualapai bird monitoring at eight sites, with nest searches and chi-square goodness-of-fit tests comparing pre- and post-flood community organization.
- Hualapai vegetation plots: ten 1 m² plots at ten inundation-prone sites, revisited at one and six months.

**9. Drift and benthic food base (Shannon and Blinn)**
- Stream drift sampled one week before and one to two months after maintenance flows, using a 48 cm diameter net (500 µm mesh) for macroinvertebrates and 154 µm mesh for zooplankton.

**10. Cultural resources (Hualapai Office of Cultural Resources)**
- More than 400 culturally significant sites lie below Glen Canyon Dam; about half are below the 45,000 cfs line and likely to be affected.
- Planned work includes ethnobotanical plots, archaeological documentation (including roasting pits), and pre- and post-flood photographs of rock writing.

**11. National Park Service concerns (Glen Canyon National Recreation Area)**
- Nine principal concerns were raised for the roughly 16-mile reach between the dam and Lees Ferry, covering archaeological erosion, leopard frog persistence, waterfowl feeding beds, invasive tamarisk, Russian olive and ravenna grass, old high water zone remnants, willow flycatcher breeding, water quality, beaches and flannelmouth sucker populations.
