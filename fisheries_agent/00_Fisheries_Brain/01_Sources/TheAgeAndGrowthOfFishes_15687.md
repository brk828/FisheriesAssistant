# The Age and Growth of Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Centrarchidae #Percidae #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1948 publication from the Michigan Department of Conservation describes methods for determining fish age through scale analysis and presents research on growth rates of Michigan freshwater fishes. The document focuses on a chemical treatment experiment conducted at [[Booth Lake]] to address stunted fish populations, demonstrating significant improvements in growth rates following population reduction. The work provides foundational data for fisheries management decisions regarding stocking policies and lake improvement strategies.

## Background & Description

Fish age is determined by locating and counting year-marks on scales. Two types of scales are described: those with small spines (found on [[Yellow Perch]], bass, and yellow pikeperch, making them rough to touch) and those without spines (found on trout and suckers). The exposed section of scales contains these spines, while the remainder is embedded in skin like shingles on a roof.

Black rings around scales are actually ridges on the scale surface, representing stages in growth. These ridges are laid down along the edge of the scale as it grows, forming annual marks that can be counted to determine age. Scale analysis reveals growth patterns across different life stages and environmental conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The primary ecological concern addressed is stunted fish populations, where overabundance of fish relative to available food supply results in slow growth rates. The document draws an analogy to agricultural production: a lake, like a garden plot, can produce only a certain number of pounds per acre. Increasing fish density does not increase production, just as planting corn hills twice as close does not increase yield.

Environmental factors affecting fish populations include:
- Shelter availability
- Spawning areas
- Food supply
- Stable water levels

The document notes that different lakes require different management approaches, comparable to farm land requiring varied treatments (lime, phosphates, nitrates).

## Key Findings

**Growth Rate Data:**
- [[Bluegill]] reaches legal length of six inches during its fourth summer of life under average conditions
- Similar data was collected for six other Michigan fish species (specific data not provided in excerpt)

**Booth Lake Experiment:**
- South basin of [[Booth Lake]] was chemically treated to remove part of the existing population
- Channel between south and north basins was sandbagged to prevent fish migration during treatment
- Total of 20,192 fish were recovered, including 6,060 panfish, perch, and rock bass
- Only 42 fish were of legal length (37 [[Yellow Perch]] and 5 [[Rock Bass]])

**Post-Treatment Growth Improvements:**
- [[Rock Bass]] showed increased growth rates in all age-groups following population reduction
- Example: One fish (5.1 inches) showed growth in the year following reduction almost equal to the first three years combined
- Pre-treatment: Fish at end of fifth summer averaged 4.3 inches
- Post-treatment: Fish beginning fifth summer reached 5.1 inches
- Fish increased in both length and weight/condition

**Management Implications:**
- Slow growth indicates overabundance of fish for available food supply
- Stocking more fish in lakes already showing slow growth is counterproductive
- Population reduction (thinning) may be more appropriate than additional stocking
- Current Michigan Department policy focuses stocking on:
  - Waters with insufficient populations
  - Establishing species in suitable lakes
  - Rebuilding populations after catastrophic events (e.g., winterkill)
