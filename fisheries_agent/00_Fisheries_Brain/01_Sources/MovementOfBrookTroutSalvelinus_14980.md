# Movement of Brook Trout in Four Small Subalpine Streams in Northern Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines movement of adult [[Brook Trout]] ([[Salvelinus Fontinalis]], age 2 and older) in four small subalpine streams in northern Colorado. Researchers used [[Mark-Recapture]] with [[Electrofishing]] over 1987–1991 and, in 1991, [[Two-Way Weirs]] on two streams. Half of each 500-m reach was randomly assigned as a treatment section and fitted with ten [[Log-Drop Structures]] in summer 1988, which increased pool volume, cover and depth. Recapture rates of marked adults were low, and large numbers of unmarked trout, apparently immigrants, were captured each year. More immigrants entered treated sections, and immigrant and resident numbers were related to cover. The authors propose that high movement is an adaptive response to the patchy habitat of small mountain streams.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the role of dispersal in animal populations has not been firmly established, and that empirical studies are limited compared with theoretical models. Earlier reviews (e.g., [[Gerking 1959]]) and many studies on [[Stream Salmonids]] reported restricted movement, and the general consensus was that most adult stream-resident salmonids move very little. The authors argue that the nature of available habitat, including competition for space, may control movement.

The study targeted brook trout, which reproduce naturally in all four streams. Study streams were remote, with little anglers' use (creel surveys and time-lapse photography indicated rare or no angling). Jack Creek also supported a small population of [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (12–28% of fish caught).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Four streams with gradients of 1.0–2.4% were sampled in 500-m reaches. Study reaches contained little wood debris and few pools. Late-summer baseflow discharge was less than 0.1 m³·s⁻¹. Water chemistry was typical of local subalpine streams: alkalinity <5–51 mg·l⁻¹, conductivity 35–117 µS, and nitrate nitrogen <0.02–0.18 mg·l⁻¹. Elevations ranged from 2730 m ([[North Fork Of The Cache La Poudre River]]) to 2925 m ([[Jack Creek]]).

**Habitat measures.** Physical habitat was described with transects at 5-m intervals, measuring depth, mean water velocity and substrate. Pool volume and five cover types (undercut bank, log, overhanging vegetation, wetted brush, rock) were also measured. Cover was defined by minimum size and water depth criteria, and vegetation counted only if it concealed at least 50% of the area beneath.

**Pre-treatment and post-treatment habitat changes.** The following values come from the reported results:

| Measure | Treatment, pre | Treatment, 1990 | Control, pre | Control, 1990 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean pool volume (m³) | 15.8 (SD 15.9) | 128 (SD 9.8) | 19.5 (SD 9.2) | 17.8 (SD 4.8) |
| Mean total cover (m) | 41.6 (SD 32.6) | 76.2 (SD 11.2) | 35.6 (SD 14.8) | 28.6 (SD 16.0) |

Mean depth was significantly greater and mean velocity significantly lower in treatment sections after installation (P<0.01 for both, ANOVA).

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling quality**
- Each section was electrofished at least once yearly in 1987–1990 at 250–400 V DC, usually with 3 passes (4 passes in some sections in 1989). Jack and Walton creeks were not sampled in 1987.
- Estimated capture probabilities indicated at least 95% capture on all but 6 of 30 sampling occasions. The lowest was 76% (North Fork, July 1988).
- Of 2122 trout held overnight after handling, 11 (0.5%) died, indicating very low electrofishing mortality.
- In 1991, all brook trout over 120 mm were individually tagged on Jack Creek and the North Fork, and fins were excised for identification.

**Immigration and residency**
- Between 17.5% and 84.3% (median 52.0%) of age-2+ trout captured bore no fin clip, indicating they had not been marked in the section before. In 77% of cases, fewer than 50% of captured trout bore marks from the previous year.
- The authors reject fin regeneration as the cause and conclude that most unmarked trout were immigrants. Immigrant estimates are considered conservative because fish from adjacent sections were excluded.
- Post-treatment immigrant numbers were significantly greater in treatment than control sections (P=0.03, paired t-test). The exception was Colorado Creek in 1990.
- The proportion of residents remaining between annual samples was consistently below 50% (22.1–41.6%, median 30.2%). Treatment (31.9%) and control (31.5%) proportions were nearly identical (P=0.89).
- Immigrant numbers were positively related to cover in the year sampled (r²=0.34, P=0.03). Resident numbers were positively related to cover in the preceding year (r=0.33, P=0.03). The sample was n=14 post-treatment section-years, and the authors did not use multiple regression because the habitat variables were intercorrelated.

**Body size**
- Residents were significantly larger than immigrants in both sections of all streams in 1990, and in Colorado Creek in 1989. In the upstream treatment section of Jack Creek in 1989, residents were also larger.
- The authors suggest this pattern fits exclusion of mobile trout by larger dominant fish, although it did not appear consistently in earlier years.

**Weir results (1991)**
- Jack Creek: 322 brook trout captured over 49 days of trapping. Trap mortality was 2.3% in downstream traps and 0.3% in upstream traps.
- North Fork: 22 of 267 trout (8.2%) were found dead, all in downstream traps. The fish were diseased, and many appeared to have died before reaching the traps. The cause of the disease remains unknown.
- Upstream captures exceeded downstream captures on both streams (79% on Jack Creek, 71% on the North Fork; P<0.0005, chi-square).
- Jack Creek catches fell from about 10 fish per day in the first three weeks to fewer than 2 per day by mid-September. The North Fork catch stayed near 5 fish per day.
- Downstream migrants were smaller than upstream migrants: Jack Creek 123.5 mm vs 135.6 mm (P<0.05); North Fork 139.5 mm vs 164.2 mm (P<0.05).

**Emigration and movement distance**
- Jack Creek: 98 trout emigrated from treatment and 108 from control sections (no significant difference). 91 recaptures were made. Of these, 41 (45%) were within about 50 m of the original capture, and 90% of moves were upstream. The mean upstream movement was 68.3 m.
- North Fork: significantly fewer trout emigrated from control (58) than treatment (91) sections (P<0.01). Of 69 recaptures, 52 (75%) moved upstream and 11 (16%) stayed in place. The mean upstream movement was 125.8 m, nearly twice the Jack Creek value.
- Electrofishing had little effect on movement. Rates were unchanged in the 7-day periods before and after sampling, and the single dip after sampling was attributed to barrier nets.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- The authors argue that many brook trout in these streams are mobile, and that low recapture rates alone do not prove movement because mortality can also be high. The large number of immigrants does indicate movement.
- The static fraction of the population appears to move little. Long-range movements may be missed by standard sampling.
- Upstream movement tendencies match earlier work by [[Saunders And Smith 1962b]] and [[Flick And Webster 1975]], whereas [[Chisholm Et Al 1987]] reported downstream winter movement in Wyoming.
- Smaller, subordinate trout may be pushed downstream. This is consistent with patterns seen after hatchery releases.
- Cover increased immigration in summer but did not lower emigration in summer. The authors suggest that habitat-linked movements may matter more in winter.
- The authors conclude that movement in small mountain streams, which are patchy mosaics of riffles and pools, may be an adaptive response to shifting habitat, with aggression acting as a proximate trigger. Dispersal is likely to increase as habitats become patchier ([[Dispersal]], [[Habitat Heterogeneity]]).
- Management implication: if many fish are transients, abundance or biomass data may misrepresent habitat quality. Studies should also be scaled to the movement range of the species.
