# Aravaipa Creek Fish Monitoring and Survey Results from 1999 and 2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program, Technical Report 198, November 2002) presents fish monitoring and survey data from [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed, largely unaltered perennial stream in the Galiuro Mountains and a tributary of the San Pedro River. The stream supports one of the most diverse native fish assemblages in a single stream in the desert Southwest. Monitoring at nine stations in three reaches (East End, Wilderness Area, West End) used [[Electrofishing]] and [[Seining]] during 1999 and 2000. The study documents declining native species abundance trends alongside rising [[Nonnative Fish Species]] occurrence, and recommends continued monitoring above and below newly built [[Fish Barriers]].

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek flows nearly 75 km from its headwaters near Klondyke to its confluence with the San Pedro River. Its [[Bimodal Hydrograph]] of flash-flood spates has historically limited nonnative establishment. Seven native fishes occur in the stream, two of which are federally listed as threatened:

- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), threatened
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), threatened
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)

Nonnative species reported or collected include [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Green Sunfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Common Carp]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. The red shiner, native to the Mississippi and Rio Grande basins, is considered a major threat due to predation on larval suckers and competitive displacement of spikedace.

A cooperative protocol established in 1990 by Arizona State University, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] monitors invasions, population changes and responses to discharge variation. Funding came from the Heritage Fund, license revenues, Nongame Wildlife Checkoff contributions, and federal agreements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat mosaic:** Riffles, runs, pools and backwaters were sampled. East End reaches were flatter and more alluvial, with shallow braided sections and finer substrates favoring longfin dace. The Wilderness Area had more run and riffle habitat. Pools were a very small share of habitat in most reaches.
- **Reach-level gradient:** Nonnative abundance was highest at the West End, lower in the Wilderness Area, and essentially absent from the East End (one green sunfish at Turkey Creek in 1999 was the exception).
- **Substrate and classification:** Substrates were coded from bedrock to silt, with sand under 3 mm and gravel 3–64 mm; fish were recorded as juvenile or adult using estimated total length thresholds (e.g., loach minnow 38 mm, spikedace 45 mm, desert sucker 125 mm).
- **Flow regime:** The hydrograph shows bimodal spates, which the authors link to the historic isolation of the stream from nonnatives.
- **Barrier context:** Fish barriers on lower Aravaipa Creek were completed after data collection; red shiner relative abundance remained above 10% below the barriers in an October 2001 USBR survey.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and totals**
- Nine stations were sampled across three reaches in October 1999 and November 2000 (Sycamore Tree lacked habitat measurements in 2000).
- Electrofishing captured far more loach minnow than seining; at Chimney Rock in 1999, loach minnow CPUE was 20 times higher by electrofishing.

**Native species trends**
- **Loach minnow:** Relative abundance was 16.8% in 1999 and 23.8% in 2000. Electrofishing relative abundance reached 51.9% (East End, 2000) and 9.2% (West End, 1999).
- **Spikedace:** Collections fell from 1,025 fish in 1999 to 68 in 2000 (about 15-fold). Relative abundance dropped from 24.7% to 5.2%. Spikedace were concentrated in the East End and Wilderness Area and nearly absent from the West End. The authors consider the decline unlikely to be purely habitat-driven.
- **Longfin dace:** The most collected species, at 37.9% of catch in 1999 and 51.6% in 2000. Only about 10% of longfin dace came from the Wilderness Area in each year.
- **Roundtail chub:** Under 3% of catch in all cases; largest cyprinid; most abundant at East End and absent from the West End. Authors suggest pool scarcity and evasion of gear may explain low counts.
- **Speckled dace:** Least encountered native fish; all were caught at the East End reach.
- **Sonora sucker:** 5.7% (1999) and 4.9% (2000) of catch; more frequent in the Wilderness Area in 2000.
- **Desert sucker:** 8.9% (1999) and 10.9% (2000) of catch; second most common fish in the Wilderness Area in 2000.

**Nonnative species**
- Yellow bullhead, red shiner and green sunfish were collected in both years; mosquitofish only in 1999.
- Red shiners were collected at Painted Cave, Wagner Ranch and White's Ranch in 1999, and also at Sycamore Tree in 2000.
- Electrofishing caught more yellow bullhead and green sunfish; seining caught more red shiner and mosquitofish.

**Habitat findings**
- In 1999, East End had the highest run share (70.5%), West End the highest riffle share, and East End the most backwater habitat.
- In 2000, the Wilderness Area had the highest run share (78.2%), East End the highest riffle share (32.0%), and East End was the only reach with pool habitat (0.1%).

**Management recommendations**
- Monitor the reaches above, below and between fish barriers to assess their effectiveness against nonnative invasion from the San Pedro River.
- Conduct a systematic watershed-wide fish survey, including cattle tanks, and consider a nonnative eradication project if warranted.
- Combine 1999–2000 data with historic AGFD, BLM, TNC and ASU collections to evaluate long-term trends.
- Expand public education and interpretive programs, given that the Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area permits up to 50 people per day and lies within easy reach of Arizona's two largest metropolitan areas.
