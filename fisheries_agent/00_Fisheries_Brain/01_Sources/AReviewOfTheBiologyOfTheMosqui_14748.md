# A Review of the Biology of the Mosquitofish, Gambusia affinis (Pisces: Poeciliidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #invasive-species #reproductive-ecology

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review synthesizes the biology of [[Gambusia affinis]], a small sexually dimorphic poeciliid fish native to the Gulf Coast and lower Mississippi drainage. Since 1905, the species has been introduced globally for [[Mosquito Control]], now inhabiting freshwater and brackish areas across five continents. The mosquitofish exhibits remarkable physiological tolerance (salinity 0-80 ppt, temperature 3-38°C), internal fertilization with live birth after 16-80 day gestation, and opportunistic carnivorous feeding. Males stop growing at maturity (16-32 mm SL), with both genetic and social factors controlling maturation timing. Females are heterogametic (WZ) and can store viable sperm for months. The species demonstrates exceptional adaptability through broad environmental tolerances and phenotypic plasticity, but has been implicated in the extinction or reduction of numerous native fish and crustacean species following introduction.

## Background & Description

[[Gambusia affinis]] belongs to the family [[Poeciliidae]], a New World group of [[Cyprinodontiform]] fishes that likely originated in Central America. The family is characterized by striking sexual dimorphism, with males possessing a modified anal fin called the [[Gonopodium]] used as an intromittent organ. Males (16-32 mm SL) are considerably smaller than females and cease growth at maturity, while females continue growing throughout life.

The genus [[Gambusia]] shares with other [[Gambusiinae]] a primarily carnivorous habit that grades into omnivorousness under food stress. The species is distinguished by the upswept, sometimes notched pectoral fin of males—a morphological modification related to mating behavior. The native range extends from Veracruz, Mexico, northward to Indiana and along the Gulf Coast to New Jersey. At least two subspecies exist: *G. affinis affinis* (Veracruz to Alabama) and *G. affinis holbrooki* (Alabama to New Jersey), distinguished by gonopodial characters.

The flattened dorsal surface represents an adaptation for surface feeding and surviving in anoxic waters. The diploid chromosome number is 2n=48, with females being the heterogametic sex (WZ, male ZZ), the W chromosome being metacentric.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Gambusia affinis]] occupies shallow, weedy freshwater and brackish habitats. In its native Texas Gulf Coast range, it inhabits bayou edges and penetrates far inland in creeks and ponds, coexisting with [[Poecilia latipinna]] (sailfin mollies), [[Fundulus]] species (killifish), [[Cyprinodon variegatus]] (sheepshead minnows), and various sunfish. The species is most abundant in waters below 1 ppt salinity but tolerates up to 20 ppt in nature and experimentally survives gradual acclimation to 80 ppt.

Thermal tolerance is exceptional, near the maximum known for fish. The thermal tolerance domain spans approximately 1033-1065°C², comparable to catfish and goldfish but far exceeding salmonids (450-530°C²). Fish can rapidly adjust from 4°C to 35°C when acclimated at 20°C, with absolute limits near 3°C and 38°C. The species survives anoxic conditions by skimming the oxygen-rich surface film.

[[Gambusia]] feeds opportunistically on planktonic and benthic crustacea (cladocerans, copepods, ostracods), insect larvae (especially mosquitoes and midges), and surface-fallen insects. When zooplankton is scarce, it supplements diet with algae. The species demonstrates selective feeding, actively preferring mosquito larvae (especially *Aedes aegypti* over *Culex fatigans*) and avoiding dragonfly larvae. Food items range from 0.15 mm rotifers to 15 mm prey, with larger fish consuming larger items (r=0.34, p<0.0001).

The species has proven highly destructive to native fauna. Introduced populations have completely eliminated [[Daphnia]], [[Chydorus]], copepod nauplii, and chironomid larvae from experimental ponds, and severely depressed rotifer numbers. Field introductions correlate with extinction or severe reduction of native fish including [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] (Gila topminnow, eliminated in 2-4 years), other [[Poeciliopsis]] species in the American Southwest, and endemic Hawaiian crustaceans. The mechanism involves both direct predation and competitive exclusion.

## Key Findings

**Reproductive Biology:**
- Internal fertilization accomplished after relatively simple courtship involving frontal or lateral displays followed by gonopodial thrust
- Gestation period: 16-80 days depending on temperature (60 days at 20°C, 34 days at 25°C, 24 days at 30°C), season, and local adaptation
- Females retain viable sperm in ovaries for several months, enabling serial production of broods without male contact
- Superfetation (multiple broods in different developmental stages) occurs but is rare in *G. affinis* (1.7% of pregnant females in Hawaii)
- Embryos either maintain or lose dry weight during development; intimate maternal nourishment appears limited to respiratory gases, water, and mineral salts
- Breeding season in temperate regions: March-October; year-round in tropics with seasonal variation

**Fertility Patterns:**
- Fertility increases with female size and food intake
- A 30 mm SL female produces 9.6-33.8 young per brood depending on location and season
- Fertility decreases with female age and time of year in seasonal environments
- Hawaiian populations showed 50% reduction in brood size and reproductive effort at end of dry season (November) compared to wet season (January)
- Interbrood interval averages 26.2 days (range: 16-60+ days)
- Food restriction delays maturation and reduces first brood size
- Very old females experience reduced brood sizes and sterile senility

**Male Maturation Control:**
- Both genetic and social factors control age and size at maturity in males
- In laboratory experiments with Hawaiian stock at high density (10 males/15 liter tank), juveniles reared with small adult males (16.8-19.8 mm SL) matured significantly younger (55-168 days) and smaller (20-22.6 mm SL) than those reared with large (22.6-24.7 mm SL) or broad-range adults (104-191 days, 22-26 mm SL)
- At lower density (5 males/15 liter), Texas stock showed no significant difference between treatments
- Dominance hierarchies observed in laboratory aquaria break down at extreme densities
- Field studies suggest most laboratory behavioral observations are artifacts of crowding; no dominance relationships or territoriality detected in natural populations

**Environmental Tolerances:**
- Salinity: typically <1.1 ppt in field; experimentally survives gradual acclimation to 80 ppt (30% mortality at 30 ppt)
- Temperature: absolute range 3-38°C; thermal tolerance domain 1033-1065°C²
- Oxygen: survives anoxic conditions by surface skimming
- Develops chloride-secreting cells in gill epithelia during salt acclimation
- Hypophysis (particularly prolactin and ACTH) necessary for osmoregulation
- Digestion rates increase with both temperature and salinity

**Feeding Ecology:**
- Stomach contents from Hawaiian reservoirs: planktonic crustacea, benthic/littoral invertebrates, autochthonous insects
- Forage ratios indicate active selection for aquatic dipteran larvae (mosquitoes, midges)
- Avoids dragonfly larvae despite availability
- Can consume extremely small prey (rotifers averaging 0.15 mm, ostracods 0.39-0.59 mm)
- Feeding efficiency decreases to 30% of still-water rate as water velocity increases
- Feeds most efficiently under red light (monochromatic conditions)

**Predator Avoidance:**
- Learns to orient toward shallow, safe water using sun compass and local landmarks
- Adults learn new shoreward directions within 2-3 days when displaced
- Compensates for time of day in orientation
- Aggregates in aquatic vegetation or shallows when predators present
- Freezes when encountering predators during displacement

**Invasive Success Factors:**
The review identifies a syndrome of traits explaining global success:
1. Viviparity reduces juvenile mortality and enables reproduction in fluctuating habitats
2. Exceptional physiological tolerance (salinity, temperature, oxygen)
3. Broad, opportunistic diet with ability to exploit very small prey
4. Phenotypic plasticity in fertility and growth responding to food and temperature
5. Capacity for rapid local adaptation (demonstrated in 67-70 years of Hawaiian isolation)

**Conservation Implications:**
The author advises against introducing *G. affinis* to areas with unique and valuable aquatic fauna unless alternative mosquito control methods are impractical and mosquitos pose serious public health threats. Source stocks for introductions should come from areas as similar as possible to target locations to maximize effectiveness.
