# Recreation Opportunities and Problems in the National Forests of the Northern and Intermountain Regions

#recreation #national-forests #land-management

## Executive Summary

This 1962 Research Paper from the [[Intermountain Forest And Range Experiment Station]], written by S. Blair Hutchison, describes the recreation situation on the national forests of the [[Northern Region]] and [[Intermountain Region]]. It argues that rapidly growing recreation use is creating management problems that the [[Forest Service]] is not yet fully prepared to handle, and it uses these problems to define a focused research agenda. Its three central concerns are the place of these forests in American recreation, the place of recreation within the forests, and management measures to prevent deterioration of recreation focal points such as campgrounds and picnic areas.

## Background & Description

The paper covers 34 national forests and 4 national grasslands, which together contain 56,757,907 acres of Federal land. This is about one-fifth of the total land area of the two regions and a much higher share of the mountain country where most recreation occurs. Recreation was historically light, with about 3 million visits in 1940, but it expanded sharply after the [[Second World War]]. Facility building began in the 1930s with the [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] and continued as finances allowed. Congress funded rehabilitation and expansion through the [[Operation Outdoors]] program, which provided $5 million for the two regions for July 1, 1960 to June 30, 1962.

The author is chief of the Division of Forest Economics Research at the station and is in charge of recreation research there. The preface credits D. B. Partridge, a veteran of outdoor recreation, for counsel during preparation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site deterioration.** Heavy use has degraded many camp and picnic sites through trampled vegetation, exposed tree roots, loss of topsoil, reduced shade, thinning shrub screens, and dust. Examples include the picnic areas on the [[Wasatch National Forest]] near [[Salt Lake City]], where thousands of yards of soil washed away at Storm Mountain Picnic Area, and Holland Lake Campground on the [[Flathead National Forest]]. The paper notes that these areas have limited people-carrying capacity because of short growing seasons, scarce midsummer moisture, and arid pinyon-juniper and ponderosa pine sites.

**Aesthetic impacts.** Even without physical damage, too many people can reduce the appeal of a site. The paper suggests a lake that is attractive with 10 people on its shore may remain so with 50 or 250, but that at some threshold sheer numbers transform the experience.

**Wilderness fringe.** Although 99.99 percent of the [[Bob Marshall Wilderness Area]], [[Sawtooth Wilderness]], and [[Uinta Mountains]] wilderness is described as unspoiled, a small share of land near large hunter and trail-riding camps is heavily abused. Trampling by horses, garbage dumps near kitchens, and bullet-holed pots and tin cans near water are described there.

**Sanitation and litter.** Garbage collection and latrine maintenance are growing burdens. Some ranger districts run municipal-type garbage trucks. Wash water tossed into bushes creates grease and soap buildup.

**Overgrazing.** Saddle and pack stock consume forage near overnight stops. A large group planning a week-long trip needs about 2.5 horses per "dude." Weed introduction through imported hay is a concern, including [[Canada Thistle]], [[Leafy Spurge]], [[Whitetop]], [[Russian Knapweed]], and goatweed.

**Aquatic and fish resources.** Lakes and streams on national forests support high mountain lakes and remote streams with large fish, while many of the larger rivers lie outside forest boundaries. [[Rock Creek]] in western Montana is described as a naturally productive, quality fishing stream. Spirit Lake on the [[Ashley National Forest]] is described as overfished. Tiny trout were observed in a creek in a wilderness camp polluted by refuse. The paper notes that the states manage the fish while the Forest Service manages the lakes, streams, and surrounding land.

**Multiple-use conflicts.** Recreation competes with water development and grazing. A proposed low dam at [[China Meadows]] would convert meadows to mudflat for part of the year. A reservoir on the North Fork of the North Fork Sun River would separate the [[Sun River Elk Herd]]'s summer and winter ranges. Logging has at times improved elk habitat in western Montana, and reservoirs have improved some fishing.

## Key Findings

**Scale and use**
- The region's national forests and grasslands span 56,757,907 acres. Wilderness, wild, and primitive areas total 6,228,198 acres, about 30 percent more than the 4,806,733 acres of national parks, monuments, and related units.
- An estimated 95 percent of the regional population lives within 50 miles of national forest land, and 79 percent lives within 25 miles.
- In Idaho, Montana, Nevada, and Utah, 56 percent of deer and elk harvested by hunters were taken on national forests.
- 19 of the 22 principal ski slopes in the regions are partly or entirely on national forest land.
- There were 994 picnic and campgrounds on these forests at the time of the report.
- Projected use reaches as many as 86 million visitor days of picnicking and camping by the year 2000 across the two regions.

**Costs and sanitation**
- The annual policing and maintenance of the anticipated 78,000 camping and picnic units by 2000 is projected to cost more than $51 million.
- The Redfish Lake campgrounds on the [[Sawtooth National Forest]] were built for about 300 people, but a count on July 4, 1961 found 2,500 people present.

**Management priorities**
- Water (sprinkling systems), planting of shrubs and trees, surfacing, traffic channeling, and advance development before use are recommended measures. Pre-use site preparation is emphasized, as at Pelican Point on the [[Fishlake National Forest]].
- Controlling use through capacity limits, reducing units per acre, and overflow camps used only at peak times are proposed.
- Horse traffic should be concentrated at a hitching area away from camps. Hay packing, trail development, and possibly excluding horses from some wilderness areas are proposed for overgrazing.
- Public education and cooperation with commercial packers are recommended, with the frontier "leave no trace" ethic cited as a model.

**Planning proposals**
- A camping network in the western national forests for long-distance travelers is proposed, since the 105 national forests and grasslands in 11 western states are well scattered between national parks.
- A "[[Micro-Wilderness]]" concept of small primitive units reachable on foot is proposed, using Spirit Lake as an example and trails to nearby lakes.
- Coordination is needed with [[National Park Service]] efforts, state and county governments, and municipal park systems, particularly in the [[Wasatch Front]] canyons.

**Research agenda**
- Priority questions are: the nature of the recreation opportunity and how to measure it (starting with fisheries), management of pressure points, and the pattern of present use.
- Six subjects for pressure-point research include people-carrying capacity, minimizing wear, testing shrubs and trees, the effect of management alternatives on land resilience, management costs, and new approaches for handling visitors.
- Four further lines concern development patterns, concessions and resorts, multiple-use criteria, and integration with other agencies.
- Long-range demand forecasting is considered less urgent than managing existing erosive use.
