# On Borrowed Land: Public Policies for Floodplains

#ichthyology #marine-biology #floodplain-policy

## Executive Summary

This Lincoln Institute of Land Policy report (1996, by Scott Faber of [[American Rivers]]) synthesizes a September 1994 conference, "Community Land Policy and River Flooding: The Great Flood of 1993," attended by officials from 33 upper Midwest municipalities and policy experts. It argues that the [[Great Flood Of 1993]] was driven by weather and topography, but its human costs reflected land use decisions that increased runoff and encouraged development in floodplains. Participants concluded that governments cannot control weather but can improve floodplain management. Their central recommendations are "tough love" (local responsibility for flood risk), watershed-wide planning and information sharing, reform of [[National Flood Insurance Program]] incentives, and "inverting the pyramid" so that land use decisions flow up from localities while information and standards flow down from federal and state governments.

This document contains no fish or aquatic-species data. Its aquatic content concerns river hydrology, wetlands and floodplain ecology.

## Background & Description

The report is part of a Lincoln Institute policy-focus series that pairs workshops with practitioner experience. The conference drew local officials from 33 municipalities across nine states (Iowa, Illinois, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota, Wisconsin) and professionals in planning, economics, geography, law, ecology and engineering. Speakers included [[Raymond Burby]], [[Jack Kartez]], [[Leonard Shabman]], [[Jerold Kayden]] and Brigadier General [[Gerald Galloway]].

The report is organized into eight chapters: causes of the 1993 flood; land use and river flooding; floodplain semantics; "tough love" and local responsibility; relocation and rebuilding; flood insurance and regulation (including takings); regional strategies; changing structural flood control; and intergovernmental relations, followed by a conclusions chapter on unstable environments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Urbanization, wetland filling, channelization and drainage increase the speed and volume of runoff. Forested land releases water slowly, while impervious surfaces prevent infiltration.
- **Wetland loss:** An estimated 53 percent of the nation's original 221 million acres of wetlands were drained between 1780 and 1990. In the nine Midwestern states affected by the 1993 floods, 37 percent of original wetlands had been converted. Nationally, about 250,000 acres per year were estimated to be lost in recent years, with 87 percent of mid-1950s to 1970s losses attributed to conversion to cropland.
- **Channel changes:** The Missouri River was narrowed from an average of 2,363 feet to 789 feet, bringing roughly 180,000 acres of former open water, islands and sandbars into cultivation. Levees and channelization raise flood stages; the Mississippi at St. Louis crests about ten feet higher than before 1927, and at Chester, Illinois, about sixteen feet higher.
- **Natural functions:** Floodplains provide flood storage, renew soils and sustain habitat. Wetlands at the Charles River store water and improve water quality.
- **Urban floodplain growth:** More than 500,000 acres of Midwestern floodplain were urbanized by 1993, putting more than 70,000 homes and businesses at risk.

## Key Findings

- **Cause of the 1993 flood:** Rainfall between June and August was 200 to 250 percent of normal in the northern plains and central Corn Belt. The flood caused 38 deaths and nearly $16 billion in damage. Officials nonetheless argued that much of the damage was predictable from land use policy.
- **Levee performance:** A federal sample of ten private Missouri River agricultural levees found failures about once every five years on average. Three-quarters of failures occurred where levees were built along or across recently active channels. Levees and channelization were shown to increase flood heights.
- **Relocation:** About 20,000 Midwesterners voluntarily relocated more than 8,000 homes and businesses, the largest voluntary post-flood relocation in U.S. history. More than 50,000 acres of flooded farmland were converted to wetlands. St. Charles County, Missouri, had 2,044 repetitive-loss structures in 1990 and relocated or acquired more than 1,500 homes and businesses by 1995.
- **Flood risk language:** The "100-year flood" is a one-percent annual chance, not a once-per-century event. Over a 30-year mortgage the report cites roughly a one-in-four chance of inundation. Floodplain maps may be off by as much as 45 percent, and more than half of flood damages occur outside mapped floodplains.
- **Insurance reform:** Before 1969, flood insurance was generally unavailable. The NFIP, begun in 1968 and expanded in 1973, has been criticized for subsidized rates and low participation (about 1.7 million of 10 million at-risk structures insured). The National Flood Insurance Reform Act of 1994 required lenders and federal agencies to enforce the mandatory purchase requirement, with fines of $350 per violation up to $100,000 per year.
- **Watershed planning case studies:**
  - *Charles River, Massachusetts:* The Corps of Engineers protected 17 wetlands as the Natural Valley Storage Project. Acquisition and easements cost about $10 million, roughly 10 percent of the estimated cost of an alternative dam, and the wetlands store about 50,000 acre-feet of water.
  - *Bucks County, Pennsylvania:* A stormwater plan covered 34 municipalities across 237 square miles.
  - *Tulsa, Oklahoma:* After severe floods, the city and Corps shifted from a single-purpose detention design to multipurpose basins with recreation and wetland benefits.
  - *Upper Mississippi River Basin Association:* A state-led interstate body that continues regional planning after federal commission funding ended.
- **Structural flood control:** The report states that flood control structures generally operated as designed in 1993. Federal structures were credited with preventing about $19 billion in additional damage. Levee or floodwall overtopping or failure was involved in roughly one-third of flood disasters. Raising Mississippi and Missouri levees was estimated to increase losses in St. Louis and cost more than $6 billion.
- **Policy recommendations:** The conference endorsed the Galloway Report, *Sharing the Challenge: Floodplain Management into the 21st Century* (1994), and called for an executive order requiring federal agencies to work collaboratively with all levels of government. Other recommendations included tax incentives for preserving floodplain functions, surcharges on floodplain development, and tying disaster aid to predisaster planning and insurance purchase.
- **Legal framework:** Under the Dolan v. City of Tigard decision, regulations must be "roughly proportional" to the harm caused. Freyfogle suggests regulations are most defensible when they apply uniformly to similar parcels.
- **Conclusion:** The report argues that human efforts to stabilize unstable landscapes (rivers, coasts, fault zones and fire-prone forests) can increase the eventual scale of disasters. It concludes that "tough love," more information sharing and reduced reliance on post-disaster aid are necessary to manage floodplains responsibly.
