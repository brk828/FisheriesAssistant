# Observations on Neosho River Larval Fish in Coffey County, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae #larval-fish #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by Greg R. Wedd, published in the *Emporia State Research Studies* (Vol. XXXIV, No. 1), describes the 1981 larval fish drift above and below [[John Redmond Reservoir]] on the [[Neosho River]] in Coffey County, Kansas. Sampling ran from 25 April to 31 July at three locations and collected 27,905 eggs, larvae, and juvenile fish representing 11 families and 30 taxa. The assemblage was dominated by [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), with [[Catostomidae]] (suckers) co-dominant at Hartford. The study also provides morphometric data for 14 taxa, which were compared with published accounts to support taxonomic assignments and document regional variation. The work was framed as the first description of larval fish populations in Kansas rivers, apart from consultant monitoring reports.

## Background & Description

Early life history stages (from spawning through juvenile development) were identified as critical to North American freshwater fish population dynamics, yet they had received little attention from fishery managers. Historically, identification was hampered by:

- **Collection difficulties:** Larval fish were sampled with zooplankton-type nets, positioned variously on bridge abutments, towed by boat, or boom-mounted.
- **Identification challenges:** Adult diagnostic traits are often absent in larvae, so features such as myomere counts, eye-gas bladder distance, and head proportions are used instead (see [[Larval Fish Identification]]).
- **Terminology:** Early developmental classification schemes were inconsistent. [[Snyder Developmental Terminology]] (protolarval, mesolarval, metalarval, juvenile), refined by the [[American Fisheries Society]] Early Life History Section, was adopted because it avoids reliance on yolk retention.

The [[Study Area]] comprised three river sites:

- **Location 2, Hartford:** A 300 m reach of 30–40 m width with mud, gravel, and rubble bottom, at the lotic-to-lentic transition.
- **Location 1, John Redmond Reservoir Tailwaters:** A 300 m reach below the spillway, flow entirely dependent on reservoir discharge, width 7–90 m, bedrock and rubble bottom.
- **Location 3, Burlington:** A 300 m reach upstream of the Burlington City Dam, forming a small impoundment at low flow.

The reservoir has a surface area of 3,800 ha at conservation pool elevation (316.7 m MSL). The Neosho drains roughly 16,300 km², of which about 15,000 km² lies in Kansas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and weather:** The middle Neosho experienced moderate drought in late 2080-era preceding conditions (as reported: the year before the study) and below-average rainfall in the first 16 weeks of 1981. Inflows to the reservoir stayed below 200 cfs on most dates until May, then rose sharply, peaking in July at a daily average of 2,632 cfs. Flow in the study area showed a four-month low-flow period, a three- to five-fold increase in June through July, and near-normal flows afterward.

**Operational constraints:** High flow at Burlington prevented collections on four occasions, and a recurring boat trailer bearing problem caused incomplete circuits. Location 3 was sampled only nine times, compared with 15 dates for Location 1 diurnal sampling. In-net flowmeters failed on or after 5 June, so boat speed from a remote meter was used for water volume calculations from 5 June to 10 July.

**Ecological interpretation:** The authors interpreted Hartford drift as allochthonous input from the upstream Neosho. JRR tailwater larvae largely represented reservoir releases, including shad with little available habitat below the dam. Burlington data reflected both reservoir releases and local production. [[Pomoxis]] (crappie) production in the Burlington pool was proposed as a nursery supporting downstream and tailwater fisheries. Catostomid larvae were rare at Burlington, which the authors attributed to suckers moving to backwaters, oxbows, and tributaries during middle developmental stages.

## Key Findings

**Overall assemblage**
- Total catch: 27,905 fish (23,194 larvae, 2,501 eggs, 2,210 juveniles), from 30 taxa in 11 families, across 51 samples (each with two replicates).
- No statistically significant differences were found in mean total concentrations among locations, nor between diurnal and nocturnal John Redmond data (AOV and Student's t-test).

**Location 2: Hartford**
- 4,837 fish total (2,499 eggs, 2,330 larvae, 8 juveniles); 18 taxa, 7 families.
- [[Catostomidae]] and [[Clupeidae]] each comprised about 48% of larvae.
- Larval density ranged from 9.4 to 1,246.7/100 m³, with a primary peak on 28 May.
- Dominant catostomid larvae were *Ictiobus* spp., with *Carpiodes carpio*-type larvae present for about four weeks.
- Notable absences: *Moxostoma* and *Cycleptus* larvae, plus [[Morone Chrysops]] and [[Pomoxis Annularis]], which were expected but not collected.

**Location 1: John Redmond Reservoir Tailwaters**
- 19,950 fish total: 7,969 diurnal and 11,981 nocturnal; 21 taxa in 10 families.
- 13 taxa occurred diurnally and 19 nocturnally.
- *Dorosoma cepedianum* comprised 98.0% of diurnal and 95.2% of nocturnal larvae.
- Maximum densities near 5,000/100 m³ occurred on 13 June (diurnal 5,274.9; nocturnal 5,419.6 on 13 June, 3,998.0 on 5 June).
- Minimum: no larvae on 15 May (diurnal); 2.7/100 m³ (nocturnal).

**Location 3: Burlington**
- 3,118 fish total (2,525 larvae, 593 juveniles, no eggs); 14 taxa in 7 families.
- *Dorosoma cepedianum* 81.4%, Centrarchidae 10.1%, Sciaenidae 6.4%, Cyprinidae 1.1%.
- Densities ranged from 6.3/100 m³ (18 July) to 1,766.4/100 m³ (19 June).
- *Pomoxis* taxa appeared earlier and at higher densities than at the reservoir, indicating local production.

**Taxonomic and morphological results**
- Morphometric tables (Tables 9–22) cover 14 taxa and generally matched published descriptions (e.g., [[Snyder]] 1981b, [[Hogue]] et al. 1976, [[Conner]] 1979).
- Lepisosteidae: two individuals (Location 2), including one counted metalarva with 15 postanal myomeres.
- *Pomoxis* identification: Eye-gas bladder distance of 15% fell in both *P. annularis* and *P. nigromaculatus* ranges due to rounding differences. Because no *P. nigromaculatus* adults were collected in 1981 or the three previous years, all 1981 *Pomoxis* larvae were assigned to *P. annularis*. The 15% threshold should be used cautiously in this system.
- *Notropis* spp. were identified, with one taxon assigned to *N. buchanani* on the basis of round eyes, pigmentation, and eight anal rays. This assignment was tentative.
- Taxa recorded for the first time in Neosho River larval studies (per [[Bliss]] 1978–1980): *Fundulus notatus* (Cyprinodontidae), *Labidesthes sicculus* (Atherinidae), and *Stizostedion*-type Percidae.

**Management implications**
- The authors noted that drift of recruitable [[Common Carp]] and [[Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus*) into the reservoir represents an undesirable input of rough fish.
- Abundant shad drift from the reservoir suggested high early-life mortality, given limited habitat in the Neosho below the dam.

**Limitations**
- Sampling was constrained by flooding, equipment failure, and flowmeter malfunction, so the results should be read with these gaps in mind.
