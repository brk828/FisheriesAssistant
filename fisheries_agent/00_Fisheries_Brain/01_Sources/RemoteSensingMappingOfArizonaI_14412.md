# Remote Sensing Mapping of Arizona Intermittent Stream Riparian Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report, Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Report 112 (June 1997), concludes the [[Statewide Riparian Inventory and Mapping]] (SRIM) project by applying remote sensing to riparian areas along intermittent streams. The study tested [[Landsat Thematic Mapper]] (TM) satellite imagery in a high contrast zone and [[Aerial Videography]] in a low contrast zone. Overall accuracy of TM riparian mapping in the high contrast zone was 58.8%, below the desired 80% standard, and aerial videography class separation was mixed. The authors mapped approximately 10,000 km of intermittent streams and did not recommend producing high contrast zone riparian maps without buffering and further refinement.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Inventory and map riparian areas along intermittent streams, extending earlier perennial-stream work (Valencia et al. 1993; Winstead 1997) under the [[Waters-Riparian Protection Program]] (WRPP) and amended [[Arizona Revised Statute 45-101]].
- **Working definition:** Riparian areas are characterized by "deep-rooted plant species that depend on having roots in the water table or its capillary zone." Following [[Lowe 1961]], riparian associations differ in species or life-forms from the surrounding nonriparian vegetation.
- **Project context:** Carried out by AGFD staff (Wahl, Boe, Wennerlund, Winstead, Allison, Kubly); funded by the Heritage Fund (Identification, Inventory, Protection, Acquisition and Management portion).
- **Test area:** Partial TM scene (Path 36 Row 36, imaged June 20, 1993) covering about 5000 km², within the Salt River watershed north and east to [[Tonto Basin]], containing desert, middle elevation and high elevation vegetation.
- **Vegetation framework:** [[Brown and Lowe 1980]] biotic communities, with [[Brown et al. 1979]] classification used for riparian associations.
- **Riparian importance:** Riparian areas support high wildlife diversity relative to their extent; vertebrate and invertebrate diversity correlates positively with vegetation structural complexity (Carothers et al. 1974; Knopf 1985; Nelson and Andersen 1994).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods overview**

- **Intermittent hydrology base:** Built on the USGS digital line graph using agency source maps (AGFD, [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[National Park Service]], [[US Forest Service]]). Segments under 0.8 km were deleted. Disputed reaches were excluded.
- **Image processing options:** Four methods were tested; a band 4/band 3 ratio with density slicing performed best.
- **Field verification:** 782 randomly sampled plots (30 m diameter), located with GPS (differentially corrected, PFINDER software).
- **Low contrast zone:** Aerial videography flown from a Cessna 206 with GPS and VHS video. Plots were 10 × 15 m, with shrubs (<3 m) and trees (>3 m) recorded separately.
- **Riparian scoring:** Species ranked 1 (obligate wetland) to 5 (upland) using [[Reed 1988]], [[Dick-Peddie and Hubbard 1977]], [[Dick-Peddie et al. 1987]], and [[Szaro 1989]]. Plot score ≤4 = riparian; >4 = upland.

**Zones and key environmental conditions**

- **High contrast zone:** Lower elevation communities where TM worked partially (e.g., [[Chihuahuan Desertscrub]], [[Lower Colorado River Sonoran Desertscrub]], [[Arizona Upland Sonoran Desertscrub]], [[Mohave Desertscrub]], [[Semidesert Grassland]], [[Great Basin Desertscrub]], [[Plains and Great Basin Grassland]]).
- **Low contrast zone:** Higher elevation communities (e.g., [[Interior Chaparral]], [[Madrean Evergreen Woodland]], [[Great Basin Conifer Woodland]], [[Petran Montane Conifer Forest]], [[Petran Subalpine Conifer Forest]], [[Subalpine Grassland]]).
- **Canyon shading:** Aerial flights were conducted in the morning to reduce shading effects.
- **Dominant riparian/upland species** referenced include [[Velvet Mesquite]] (*Prosopis velutina*), [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Salt Cedar]], [[Arizona Sycamore]], [[Willow]] (*Salix* spp.), [[Creosote Bush]] (*Larrea tridentata*), and [[Shrub Live Oak]].
- **Problem factors:** Narrow riparian corridors fall below the TM minimum mapping unit; 1993 floods and vegetation growth separated imagery (1993) from field data (1995–1996).

## Key Findings

**Intermittent stream extent**

- Approximately **10,000 km** of intermittent streams in Arizona (10,237 km in Table 3), about **25% more** than the 8,100 km of perennial streams (Valencia et al. 1993).
- About **54%** of intermittent stream length lies in the low contrast zone; **46%** in the high contrast zone.
- The hydrology estimate is likely somewhat conservative (some streams omitted; disputed reaches excluded).

**Field crew consistency**

- Field crews were consistent at least **85%** of the time in classifying dominant species as riparian or upland; nine species were **100%** consistent.
- 82 plant species were dominants on 681 plots; only 28 dominant species occurred on at least five plots and were used for consistency testing.

**High contrast zone TM accuracy (not meeting standard)**

- Overall accuracy: **58.8%** (460/782).
- Riparian producer accuracy: **67.2%**; upland producer accuracy: **54.7%**.
- Riparian user accuracy: **42.3%**; upland user accuracy: **77.1%**.
- Both user and producer accuracies exceeded the desired **>80%** in only two of seven Brown and Lowe classes.
- Riparian user accuracy ranged from **100%** (Chihuahuan Desertscrub) to **11%** (Plains and Great Basin Grassland).
- Producer accuracy ranged from **93%** (Mohave Desertscrub) to **54%** (Plains and Great Basin Grassland).
- Velvet mesquite was classified as riparian **64.1%** of the time by remote sensing; crews classified it as riparian **68.6%** of the time.

**Non-site-specific assessment**

- Allowing a 30 m (1 pixel) tolerance improved accuracy, but only **9 of 34** reversals checked against USGS topographic maps were supported; this equals about **26%** (CI 10–39%) of the reversals.
- The authors judged the adjusted accuracy still insufficient to use the method without refinement.

**Reasons cited for accuracy problems**

- TM band-ratio riparian designation applied to the whole high contrast area, because no intermittent hydrology base existed at the time.
- Narrow, linear riparian corridors produced mixed pixels (minimum recommended width 2–3 pixels, or 60–90 m).
- Temporal gap of about three years between satellite imagery (mid-1993) and field data (1995–1996).

**Aerial videography (low contrast zone)**

- Class 1 (cover <25%) vs. classes 2–4: **18 of 19** reaches correct (**95%**; overall matrix 94.7%).
- Class 2 vs. class 3: overall accuracy **55.6%**, well below the 80% minimum.
- Class 2 vs. class 4: **100%** accuracy in the test sample.
- Classes 1 and 4 vs. classes 2 and 3: **100%** accuracy.
- Final merged classes: (1) streamside vegetation more dense and more green than adjoining upland; (2) not more dense and green.
- Of **5230 km** filmed (370 km of 5600 km not classifiable), **3442 km (34%)** had denser/greener streamside vegetation.
- By reach frequency, **48%** of reaches had denser/greener vegetation, suggesting such reaches are shorter and more fragmented.

**Products**

- GIS intermittent stream hydrology base and riparian maps; field databases (HICONFT2.DBF, 782 cases; LOWCON.DBF, 31,685 cases).
- Riparian plant lists and wetland ratings (Appendices B and C), with ratings from Reed (1988) and related sources.
- Camp Verde example map (Figure 6) of potential riparian areas.
- ASU contract report by Wasklewicz (1996) on riparian community composition and hydrogeomorphic variables.

**Recommendations**

1. Integrate and attribute relational ground-truthing databases in a GIS, with metadata documentation.
2. Distribute databases to AGFD Regional offices and other users, with a work unit assigned to communicate updates.
3. Do not produce high contrast zone intermittent riparian maps without further work. If produced, apply a buffer algorithm that excludes riparian designations beyond a set distance from an intermittent streamcourse.
4. Complete a riparian classification system using perennial and intermittent ground-truthing data. It should include structural elements important to wildlife diversity and relate to wildlife functions and values.
5. Develop a Riparian Vulnerability Index, similar to the Aquifer Vulnerability Index (EPA and ADEQ), to identify riparian areas needing protection, restoration or remedial action.
6. Integrate the AGFD [[Heritage Data Management System]] into the GIS of riparian areas to support a wildlife management model for species dependent on this terrestrial–aquatic ecotone.

**Future outlook**

- The authors note that 5 m imagery is now available, versus the 30 m Landsat TM pixel, which would improve mapping of narrow riparian corridors.
