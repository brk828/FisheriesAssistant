# Scale Reduction in an Anatolian Aphanius Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Helmut Grimm (1976) examines scale reduction (squamation loss) in [[Aphanius Anatoliae]] from southwest Anatolia, Turkey. Fully scaled fish occur in freshwater, while populations with reduced scales live in brackish, sulphate-rich water. Grimm tested whether sulphate concentration affects the hatching and survival of eggs and larvae, and found that sulphate tolerance does not separate the scaled and partly scaled forms. Scale count differences were not significant, and selection tests showed no clear selective value for scale cover. The author concludes that the biological function of scales must be understood before the cause of their loss can be determined, and frames the reduction as a possible case of regressive evolution.

## Background & Description

*Aphanius anatoliae* (Leidenfrost, 1912, sensu Villwock, 1964) shows wide variation in the degree of [[Squamation]]. Some individuals are fully covered with normal scales, while others have naked patches and generally reduced scales. Within a single population, every transitional form between fully scaled and nearly naked fish can be found.

Scale reduction is most common on the ventral and dorsal regions and on the caudal peduncle. The most extreme individuals retain only a row of scales along the [[Lateral Line]] (MLL). As scales are lost elsewhere, the number of scales along the MLL increases.

Members of the genus *Aphanius* are generally euryhaline, meaning they tolerate a wide range of salinities. Earlier work by Axsiray and Villwock (1962) proposed a link between scale reduction and water chemistry. They argued that pleiotropic mechanisms give partly scaled fish a physiological advantage in sulphate-rich brackish water, while fully scaled fish are adapted to freshwater.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fully scaled *A. anatoliae* occur in freshwater, while reduced and variably scaled populations occur in brackish water dominated by magnesium sulphate (MgSO₄) and sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄). Field observations by Axsiray and Villwock (1962) reported that partly scaled fish live in extreme brackish biotopes with very few other animals or plants, unlike the biotopes of fully scaled forms. Grimm argues that these water chemistry conditions produce a harsh environment in which only a few organisms survive, and that the fish's preadaptation to these conditions allowed it to reduce its scales.

The study also draws an analogy to [[Cave Fauna]]. In cave animals, eyes and pigmentation often degenerate because they lose their function in darkness. Grimm proposes that scale loss may follow a similar pattern, in which a stabilising selection pressure that once maintained full squamation has weakened.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design.** Eggs were placed immediately after laying into tap water containing varying amounts of magnesium or sodium sulphate. Four groups were compared: [[Aphanius Dispar]] (imported from Egypt), [[Aphanius Iberius]] (imported from Spain), and two *A. anatoliae* forms, the fully scaled Düğer population and the partly scaled Gemiç population. [[Hatching Rate]] and [[Larval Survival]] were compared across sulphate concentrations.

**Main results:**

1. **Egg tolerance:** Eggs of *A. anatoliae* withstood sulphate concentrations above 40 g SO₄²⁻/l. Eggs of *A. dispar* and *A. iberius* tolerated only up to 15–20 g/l. Grimm suggests this difference reflects genetically based ionic regulation mechanisms.
2. **Egg versus larva:** Eggs of *A. anatoliae* tolerated higher sulphate levels than larvae. The 10 g/l sulphate level used in the experiments is far above what occurs in the natural biotope, which Grimm interprets as preadaptation to these special habitats.
3. **No difference between scale forms:** Eggs and larvae of the Düğer and Gemiç populations showed the same tolerance to sulphate. This does not support the proposal by Axsiray and Villwock that the two forms have different physiological adaptations.
4. **Scale counts:** Fish raised in different sulphate concentrations showed very little variation in lateral line scale numbers and transverse scale rows. The differences were not statistically significant. Selection tests over several generations also showed no selective value for different degrees of scale cover.

**Interpretation.** Grimm concludes that sulphate tolerance does not explain scale reduction. The central unanswered question is the biological function of squamation. Under normal conditions, fully scaled fish presumably need their scales for a reason. In reduced populations, the factor that maintained full squamation has apparently disappeared, possibly because scales, which may have protected against predators or parasites, became superfluous.

**Regressive evolution.** The populations at Salda and Gölcük, which show the greatest scale reduction, along with the naked genus *Kosswigichthys* from eastern Anatolia, may represent a final stage of this regressive process. The other partly scaled populations would then represent intermediate stages. Grimm states that these patterns cannot be fully explained until the function of scales is known.
