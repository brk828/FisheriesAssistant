# Evaluation of Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction Programs in Central Arizona, 1986–1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by [[Dean A. Hendrickson]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department, Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program, 1993) evaluates the reintroduction of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) into the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] of central Arizona, based on fish surveys conducted from 1986 to 1990. Between 1981 and 1990 more than 1.1 million hatchery-produced razorback suckers and about 250,000 Colorado squawfish were stocked in Arizona interior waters, where the native populations had been extirpated. Only 519 recaptures of stocked fish were obtained during intensive electrofishing and seining surveys. Survival appeared better in the upper Verde River than in the Salt River. Most recaptured fish were judged to have lived only a few months in the wild, although some groups persisted for more than a year in isolated habitats, and growth in the wild was documented. No unequivocal evidence of wild reproduction or recruitment was found. The author concludes that predation by non-native fishes, parasitism, poor physical condition, downstream displacement into diversions and habitat fragmentation are the main obstacles, and recommends a shift from production toward experimental research, improved planning, data management and museum curation.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish are native to the Colorado River system, including the Gila River basin of Arizona, where both were historically widespread. Both were extirpated from the lower Colorado River basin and from the Salt and Verde rivers by about 1960. Following a 1980 memorandum of understanding between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Department, stockings of young fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] began in 1981 for razorbacks and 1985 for squawfish. Stockings of squawfish were restricted to experimental, nonessential reaches of the Verde and Salt rivers. The study objectives were to create a standardized monitoring database, assess survivorship of stocked fish through field surveys, compile all stocking records, review existing data across the Gila basin, and make recommendations for research and management.

Sampling used boat-mounted pulsed-DC electrofishing (Coffelt WP-15), seines, gill and trammel nets, and [[Passive Netting]]. The author notes that the Verde and Salt rivers differ strongly in discharge stability, riparian forest, and catchment size, although both are now dominated by non-native fishes in their lower reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Verde River:** The 286 km river drains about 16,900 km² and is spring-fed in its upper reaches, with a stable base flow of roughly 24–28 cfs at Verde Ranch. Its lower reaches are highly variable and are affected by irrigation diversions and urbanization in the Verde Valley. The upper Verde supports one of the most diverse native fish communities remaining in the American Southwest, although native species make up only 58% of the historic fauna. Non-native species included red shiner, fathead minnow, mosquitofish, carp, channel catfish, flathead catfish, and both black and largemouth bass. [[Smallmouth Bass]] is judged the principal predator on stocked small fish.

**Salt River:** Headwaters arise in the White Mountains at about 3,000 m and the river is more flow-variable, dependent on surface runoff. Discharge in the 1980s ranged from a minimum daily mean of 92 cfs to a maximum of 60,100 cfs. Thirty-one fish species have been recorded above the Verde confluence, of which 12 are native and only five still occur naturally. Flathead and channel catfish are considered the major predators in this river. Riparian vegetation is limited by floods, and the lower Salt is fragmented by [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] and a chain of reservoirs.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking effort:** Over 11.8 million razorback suckers (fry to 250 mm mean TL) and about 270,000 Colorado squawfish (about 35–125 mm TL) were released in Arizona interior waters covered by the report.
- **Recaptures:** Only 519 stocked fish were recaptured, mainly during boat electrofishing and seining in the Verde and Salt rivers. Nearly 200,000 fish specimens were processed in total, and more than 90% of the catch came from seines and electrofishing.
- **Survival:** Most recaptured razorbacks and squawfish had not survived more than a few months in the wild. Survival of more than one year was documented in isolated habitats such as Granite Creek backwaters and beaver ponds.
- **Growth:** A cohort stocked in Granite Creek averaged about 76 mm TL at release, 114 mm at 5 months, and 165 mm later; a 1990 sample in the upper Verde averaged 371.8 mm TL (n = 34).
- **Habitat preference:** Razorbacks and squawfish were captured disproportionately in connected backwaters, and 63% of squawfish sampling events with captures were in this habitat, against 21–29% of total effort.
- **Movement:** Razorbacks moved mostly downstream but were captured near stocking sites; squawfish moved rapidly, mostly at night, with some individuals recaptured more than 2 h of travel per day and found over 10 km away.
- **Condition:** Verde razorbacks had a lower length-weight relationship than hatchery or other populations, and condition factor declined with time in the wild (K ≈ 1.12 declining toward 1.03 with time since stocking; P < 0.0007). Heavy infestations of the parasitic copepod *Lernaea* were observed on razorbacks, with up to 80–100 per fish.
- **Reproduction:** No unequivocal evidence of wild spawning was found; ripe male razorbacks were collected in December 1989 in the upper Verde.
- **Predation:** Nearly all stomachs of smallmouth bass and black bullhead sampled after squawfish stockings contained squawfish.
- **Dispersal into diversions:** Squawfish were found in irrigation canals and gravel pits, and diversions are judged a major cause of loss.
- **Recommendations:** Develop a master recovery plan with defined objectives; reorient the program toward experimental research; standardize marking (otolith marking suggested); establish a central database; publish results in peer-reviewed journals; control flathead catfish in the Salt River; and maintain annual monitoring with more passive netting and angling.
