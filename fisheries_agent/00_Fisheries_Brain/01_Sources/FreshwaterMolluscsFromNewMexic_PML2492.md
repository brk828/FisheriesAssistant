# Freshwater Molluscs from New Mexico and Vicinity

#malacology #freshwater-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 monograph by [[Dwight W. Taylor]] (Oregon State University), published by the [[New Mexico Bureau of Mines & Mineral Resources]], systematically treats the freshwater molluscs of New Mexico and adjacent areas. It describes new species, redescribes known ones, and uses their distributions to reconstruct the late Tertiary drainage history of the region. Most taxa are small spring-dwelling snails of the family [[Hydrobiidae]], especially the genus [[Fontelicella]] and the genus [[Tryonia]]. The work argues that these snails, which cannot disperse through flowing rivers, preserve evidence of drainage changes that geology alone does not resolve.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Species were collected during studies for the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], and the [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] area surveys. The work was partly funded by these agencies and supports a regional fauna manual.
- **Taxonomic approach:** Identification relies on penial structure (ornament and glandular patches) and the bursa copulatrix. Taylor developed a symbolic notation for penial glands. Shell and operculum measurements are tabulated for each species (sample sizes usually N = 20–30).
- **Taxa treated:** The monograph describes new species in *Assiminea*, *Fontelicella*, *Tryonia*, and the new genera *Apachecoccus* and *Yaquicoccus*, plus one sphaeriid clam, *Pisidium sanguinichristi*. Several new subgenera (for example *Paupertryonia*) are also erected. Exact species counts are partly garbled in the scanned abstract, so this note does not give a total.
- **Key morphological notes:** In *Fontelicella*, species relationships are based on male structures. Female reproductive anatomy is conservative and "almost stereotyped," and it is useful only in a few groups such as the [[Fontelicella kolobensis Group]] and the [[Fontelicella neomexicana Group]]. In *Tryonia*, no detailed hierarchy has been achieved; many groups are monotypic.
- **Habitat:** The snails occur on moist earth beside seepages or spring-brooks, often under salt grass or sedges, and not beside standing water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spring-brook specialists:** Local species are restricted to flowing spring water. Their present distributions are tied to solution-subsidence basins and spring systems.
- **Pecos River and lower Rio Grande:** Species are concentrated in the Roswell and Delaware basins of southeastern New Mexico and Trans-Pecos Texas, sites of major springs. Their spring habitats imply late Tertiary age, which conflicts with a view of the [[Ogallala Formation]] as an alluvial blanket over the Pecos Valley.
- **Gila River:** Endemic forms of eastern Arizona ([[Tryonia gilae]] and *Apachecoccus*) relate to forms in the Río Santa María, Chihuahua. Species of the upper Gila in New Mexico are sharply distinct from each other and from others in the region.
- **Upper Rio Grande:** Only three living species are restricted to the upper Rio Grande, including the *Fontelicella neomexicana* group and [[Tryonia alamosae]]. The drainage seems always to have had a sparse fauna, even in the headwaters of southern Colorado.
- **Socorro thermal springs:** Associated organisms include the Socorro isopod *Thermosphaeroma thermophilum* and common snails such as *Physa mexicana* and *Lymnaea modicella*.
- **Extinction and rarity:** *Fontelicella neomexicana* is now extinct at its type locality near Socorro. Several species are listed as candidate endangered species, including *F. chupaderae* and the Pecos spring snail (*F. pecosensis*).

## Key Findings

1. **Drainage history:** Mollusc distributions support an interpretation in which the Roswell and Delaware basins were not part of the Ogallala alluvial fan sequence. They were already in a different environment and drainage. [[Reeves]] (1972) had earlier proposed northward extension of the head of the Pecos Valley through solution-subsidence depressions.
2. **Upper Rio Grande:** The molluscan evidence does not support an ancient, greater Rio Grande draining to the Gulf of Mexico. Instead, the snail fauna is consistent with an upper Rio Grande that was initially interior-draining and joined the lower Rio Grande only in the mid-Pleistocene.
3. **Gila isolation mechanism:** Two distinctive *Fontelicella* species occur in the former separate Mimbres drainage, presumably isolated by capture of the Mimbres by the Gila. The canyon of lower Sapillo Creek is deeply incised, which suggests capture by a more rapidly eroding Gila River.
4. **Fish concordance:** Taylor notes that the inferred drainage history is broadly in accord with interpretations based on fish distribution (Smith & Miller, 1986).
5. **New species:** The monograph adds new species and genera to New Mexico, Arizona, and Texas faunas, including *Fontelicella davisi* (Texas, type locality a tributary of Limpia Creek near Fort Davis), *Fontelicella pecosensis* (Pecos spring snail), and *Fontelicella chupaderae* (Chupadera spring snail).
6. **Fossils:** Pleistocene fossils from the Alamosa Formation and other sites add to the record of local faunas.
7. **Biology:** Penial glandular patches vary widely and help group species, but variation is so great that it often prevents ready identification. Shell size and form differ little among closely related species.
8. **Fauna sparsity:** In most lineages, the spring-brook fauna is sparse and locally restricted, so each population has conservation significance.
