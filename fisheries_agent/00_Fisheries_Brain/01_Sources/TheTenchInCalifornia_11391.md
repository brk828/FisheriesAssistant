# The Tench in California

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #introduced-species #historical-documentation

## Executive Summary

This historical report documents the introduction and establishment of the [[Tench]] (*Tinca tinca*), a European cyprinid, in California reservoirs. Originally brought from Italy in 1922 and planted at [[Lobitas Creek]] near [[Half Moon Bay]], the species has spread throughout parts of [[San Mateo County]] and [[Santa Cruz County]]. The author provides the first published account of the introduction history and distribution of this non-native species, noting its reproductive success in California reservoirs and raising concerns about its status as an undesirable species similar to [[Carp]].

## Background & Description

The [[Tench]] (*Tinca tinca* Linné) is the largest European species in the family [[Cyprinidae]], to which [[Carp]] and various chubs belong. Historically known as the "physician fish" due to fanciful claims of medicinal properties, it is extensively cultivated in European ponds as a food fish.

**Physical characteristics**: Specimens photographed at 8.4 inches length (male).

**Growth rates**: European data indicate [[Tench]] are slower growers than [[Carp]]. One-year-old fish weighing 0.25 ounces at season start gain 2 ounces during the first growing season and 6 ounces during the second season, compared to [[Carp]] gaining 12 and 32 ounces respectively over the same periods.

**Reproductive biology**: In other regions, spawning extends over May-July with average egg production of 350,000-400,000 eggs per female. The species exhibits remarkable tenacity of life in low-oxygen conditions, surpassing [[Carp]] in hardiness.

**Habitat preferences**: Primarily a lake species; thrives on mud bottoms. In Italy, [[Tench]] inhabit ditches throughout agricultural areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Introduction history**: Original specimens brought from Italy by Mr. Graviati in 1922 with express purpose of securing [[Tench]] for speculative purposes. Fish transported on ship in 2.5 or 5-gallon can with heavy losses. Approximately 12-24 fish (4-6 inches length) survived and were stocked in reservoir on Balanesi ranch at [[Mackay Radio Station]] site, [[Lobitas Creek]], approximately 6 miles south of [[Half Moon Bay]], [[San Mateo County]]. Introduction occurred sometime after May 1922.

**Population establishment**: Fish reproduced freely in original reservoir. On March 20, 1925, five specimens transferred to [[Steinhart Aquarium]] in San Francisco. Soon after, aquarium employees seined "a large number" of [[Tench]] from the ranch site.

**Distribution expansion**: Species gradually spread by ranchers from reservoir to reservoir throughout large parts of [[Santa Cruz County]] and [[San Mateo County]]. Documented locations include:

- May 11, 1940: Ponds of Mr. Jack Bandy at [[Bonny Doon]], [[Santa Cruz County]]
- November 1940: Reservoir south of [[Half Moon Bay]], [[San Mateo County]]
- November 12, 1941: Two reservoirs in [[Santa Cruz County]] (one north of Santa Cruz city; Big Ranch Reservoir north of [[Davenport]], stocked with 1,000 [[Tench]] in 1933-1934)
- August 2, 1941: Reservoir near [[Princeton]] (6 miles north of [[Half Moon Bay]]), where fresh-water sculpins ([[Cottus]]) reportedly consumed all eggs and young, preventing successful reproduction
- [[Mud Lakes]] (series of enlarged sag ponds), [[San Mateo County]], stocked approximately 1934 from pool near [[Half Moon Bay]]

**Stream colonization**: Some fish probably escaped into local streams, but survival/thriving doubtful in small coastal streams given species' lake-dwelling nature.

**Habitat characteristics**: Distribution area characterized by almost treeless coastal landscape with reservoir systems.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory concerns**: California Fish and Game Code Section 561 prohibits planting live fish into state waters without written permission from Fish and Game Commission. [[Tench]] considered undesirable addition for same reasons as [[Carp]]—interference with production and catching of more valuable fishes.

**California population characteristics**:
- Reproduces freely in most reservoirs where currently found
- Commonly reaches 2-3 pounds weight
- Occasional specimens reported at 4-6 pounds
- Spawning time and egg production data not available for California populations

**Feeding ecology**: Food habits in California reservoirs unknown. European literature reports [[Tench]] takes snails more readily than other food items when available.

**Fishery**: Usual capture method is netting. Generally difficult to take with hook and line year-round; one report indicates November as optimal angling period. Bait includes "Tyee" (preserved salmon egg clusters) or worms. Local Italian population prizes species highly as food fish, prepared in various ways.

**Traditional capture methods**: In Italy, [[Tench]] caught by blocking ditch sections with earthen dams, muddying water to force fish to surface for hand capture.

**Management implications**: Species establishment represents successful but unauthorized introduction of non-native cyprinid with potential to compete with native and desirable fish species in California freshwater systems.
