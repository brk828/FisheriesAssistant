# Behavior and Hybridization of Two Species of Etheostoma (Percidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 laboratory study examines the breeding behavior, egg development, larval morphology and hybridization of two darters, [[Etheostoma Radiosum Cyanorum]] and [[Etheostoma Spectabile]]. Using breeding tanks in which all four possible mating combinations were tested, the author shows that the two species spawn together in captivity and produce hybrid embryos, but that reciprocal crosses differ markedly in hatchability. Hybrid offspring show excess fin rays, an incomplete lateral-line system, and partial parthenogenetic development of unfertilized eggs. The author concludes that the natural hybrid previously reported from Blue River probably arose from spawning between the two species, and that habitat siltation may be increasing hybridization.

## Background & Description

The paper places its work in the context of experimental fish hybridization. Earlier cytologists found that eggs of one fish species can be fertilized by sperm of distantly related forms, but success was thought to track systematic relationship (Moenkhaus, 1911; Hubbs, 1955; Hubbs and Strawn, 1957a). Newman (1915) argued that egg characteristics, not relatedness alone, governed success. Natural hybridization had been recorded most often in the [[Centrarchidae]], [[Catostomidae]] and [[Cyprinidae]], and in Etheostominae through artificial crosses. The [[Blue River]] natural hybrid described by [[Linder 1955]] served as the starting point.

The two species are small darters that lack a swim bladder and typically inhabit swift streams, resting on the bottom beneath rocks and vegetation. Laboratory fish were inactive, moving in short darts powered by the caudal fin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diet:** Dwarf white worms (small oligochaetes) were the most suitable food; cladocerans and copepods were accepted by smaller fish. Fish preferred living prey and refused non-moving food.
- **Temperature:** Fish were usually refrigerated at 18–20 °C in summer, but unrefrigerated aquaria held at 30 °C or more for 70 days caused no ill effects. Spawning occurred between 17 and 26 °C, often after a sudden temperature change.
- **Seasonality:** In nature, spawning is expected to begin in late winter. Females from Dog Creek were gravid in March 1955, and ripe radiosum females were collected from Blue River in late March and early April. Laboratory spawning began in mid-December.
- **Substrate:** Eggs were buried in sand and gravel rather than placed in a defined redd. The author links the female's diving behavior to gravel and sand deposits above and below riffles, not riffle stones.
- **Habitat change:** Farming-related siltation is thought to have reduced gravelly spawning habitat in Blue River.

## Key Findings

- **Reciprocal crosses differed in success.** Radiosum females crossed with Dog Creek spectabile males produced few hatchlings (approx. 2.3% hatched), while the reciprocal cross was more successful (approx. 5.3%). The pattern reversed with Mill Creek spectabile.
- **Normal conspecific matings were also poorly hatched.** Radiosum × radiosum produced no viable embryos, and some spectabile matings hatched at low rates. The authors attribute low viability in part to diet and laboratory conditions.
- **Spawning behavior is shared.** The male pursues, nips, and mounts the female, and the female dives into the sand to spawn, with spawning acts lasting only seconds. Both species and hybrid crosses followed the same pattern.
- **Behavioral incompatibility.** Larger radiosum attacked smaller spectabile at first; antagonism lessened when similar-sized fish were paired.
- **Egg and embryo development.** Eggs are spherical, transparent, demersal and adhesive, averaging about 1.35–1.36 mm. Embryonic development was the same in hybrids and pure spectabile; hatching took six to seven days at 21–25 °C and eight days at 18 °C.
- **Abnormalities.** Common defects included undeveloped optic cups, pigment blotches, enlarged pericardial sacs and open mouths. The author links these to diet deficiencies, citing Hubbs and Stavenhagen (unpublished).
- **Lateral-line loss.** Most laboratory-reared young, including pure spectabile, lacked most of the lateral-line system, which the author suggests reflects altered embryonic conditions. This is significant because lateral-line development is used in taxonomy.
- **Excess fin rays in hybrids.** Some hybrids had abnormally high soft-ray counts in unpaired fins, which the author interprets as likely a result of hybridization.
- **Parthenogenesis.** Unfertilized hybrid eggs (ten obtained on November 5, 1954) developed to about the yolk-plug stage and then died. All nine hybrids examined in April 1955 were female, though the sex was uncertain in life.
- **Hybrids did not spawn with parents.** Eggs were not obtained from tanks containing hybrids and parental males, so the viability of hybrid eggs was not fully tested.
- **Conclusion on natural hybrids.** The author concludes that the Blue River natural hybrid most likely arose from radiosum females spawning with spectabile males. Its scarcity may reflect low egg numbers and high mortality, and the author predicts that hybridization will increase if siltation continues.
