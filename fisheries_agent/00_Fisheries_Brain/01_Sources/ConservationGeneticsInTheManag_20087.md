# Conservation Genetics in the Management of Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #conservation-genetics #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper addresses the application of population genetics to conservation and management of North American desert fishes, a highly endangered fauna experiencing alarming declines. At least 20 taxa have gone extinct in recent decades due to habitat destruction and exotic species introductions. The authors develop two zoogeographic models—the [[Death Valley Model]] and the [[Stream Hierarchy Model]]—to describe patterns of isolation and gene flow in desert fish populations. These models reveal fundamentally different genetic structures requiring distinct management approaches. The [[Death Valley Model]] describes completely isolated populations with no gene flow (m = 0), low within-population diversity, and high among-population divergence. The [[Stream Hierarchy Model]] describes hierarchically connected populations in dendritic river systems with varying degrees of gene flow, higher within-habitat diversity, and lower among-habitat differentiation. The paper emphasizes maintaining genetic diversity while respecting natural population structure, and recommends experimental approaches to optimize management of threatened desert fishes.

## Background & Description

North American desert fishes inhabit the [[Sonoran Desert]], [[Mohave Desert]], [[Great Basin]], and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]—extremely arid regions with high geographic relief, heavily dissected drainages, and widely dispersed surface waters. Natural aquatic systems are fragmented at both broad geographic and local scales. Desert aquatic systems experience enormous physico-chemical fluctuations in streams and rivers, or constant but harsh conditions in springs. This extreme isolation and severe abiotic conditions have led to high phenotypic divergence, often with small populations in small habitats.

Many taxa are relicts from better-watered times, trapped in isolated springs and streams during the last 10,000-12,000 year post-pluvial period, resulting in high endemism. Genetic and demographic consequences include: (1) local divergence via natural selection or genetic drift; (2) little or no gene flow among isolated demes that might moderate losses of genetic variability after population crashes; and (3) little or no recolonization after local extinction.

Anthropogenic disturbances include habitat destruction (impoundment and diversion of rivers, groundwater pumping, drying of surface springs) and introduction of exotic fishes that prey on, compete with, or hybridize with native species. Two demographic factors affect divergence among local colonies: genetically effective population size (Ne) and migration rate (m). Divergence occurs as a product of Ne and m. As long as Nem > 1, local colonies will not diverge significantly by chance alone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Death Valley Model]] applies to fish populations in [[Death Valley]] and similar intermontane basins containing extremely isolated springheads and small streams that were connected in pluvial times. Geographic isolation among colonies is complete (m = 0), and divergence is accelerated by small colony sizes and possibly strong local selection pressures. Total genetic diversity (Ht) consists of diversity within colonies (Hc) plus differences among colonies (Dct). This model is exemplified by pupfishes (genus [[Cyprinodon]]) naturally isolated in remnant springs and streams.

Turner's (1974) electrophoretic studies of pupfishes in and near [[Death Valley]] revealed little overall allelic variation, with most variation occurring among rather than within populations. For [[Cyprinodon nevadensis]] subspecies in isolated habitats of the [[Amargosa Basin]], most of thirty gene loci were homozygous within populations, but four were polymorphic in one population. [[Cyprinodon diabolis]] and [[Cyprinodon salinus]], each occurring in single isolated habitats, appear genetically depauperate but contain unique alleles that may have arisen via mutation since isolation.

The [[Stream Hierarchy Model]] applies to desert fishes inhabiting dendritic river systems with varying degrees of connectedness and gene flow. Local demes are only partially isolated, with some probability of gene flow among them. Periodic or permanent connections allow migration, and local population divergence is less extreme than in the Death Valley Model. A hierarchical genetic structure results, wherein higher frequencies of gene flow correspond to greater similarity among demes.

Total genetic diversity (Ht) consists of within-colony diversity (Hc) plus differences among colonies within a river (Dcr) plus differences among rivers within major drainage systems (Drs) plus divergence among drainages (Dsd). Natural climatic variation connects and separates local demes in a hierarchical structure that is a function of geographic distance and river discharge. This model is typified by numerous species in the [[Gila River]] drainage of southern Arizona.

## Key Findings

**Death Valley Model Characteristics:**
- Complete geographic isolation (m = 0) among populations
- Very little polymorphism within populations
- Considerable divergence among populations
- Management goal: maintain strong, isolated populations without creating artificial gene flow
- Example: [[Owens Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon radiosus]]) nearly went extinct in August 1969 when its only known habitat completely dried; approximately 800 fish were rescued
- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon diabolis]]) threatened by groundwater pumping; Supreme Court decision halted development

**Salton Sea Variant:**
[[Cyprinodon macularius]] populations around [[Salton Sea]] show modified Death Valley pattern due to repeated cycles of isolation and reconnection. Polymorphism within lakeshore colonies accounts for approximately 70% of total genetic variance, with differences among colonies accounting for 30%. The most isolated population in [[Quitobaquito Springs]], Arizona, exhibits genetic differentiation comparable to Death Valley pupfish populations and was described as distinct subspecies [[Cyprinodon macularius eremus]].

**Stream Hierarchy Model Characteristics:**
- Hierarchical genetic structure based on geographic connectivity
- Higher within-habitat genetic diversity than Death Valley Model
- Lower among-habitat differentiation than Death Valley Model
- Habitat destruction and population segregation is greatest threat
- Management must recognize hierarchical structure and avoid mixing major genetic subdivisions

**Sonoran Topminnow Case Study:**
[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] (Sonoran topminnow) exemplifies Stream Hierarchy Model. Native to lower [[Colorado River]] drainage of Arizona and New Mexico, and several major drainages in Sonora, Mexico. Extremely abundant through [[Gila River]] basin as recently as 1940s. Rapid decline over last four decades led to Federal Endangered Species List inclusion in 1973. In 1983 occurred in 15 natural habitats in United States; presently in 12 natural localities in Arizona, all isolated headwater springs or streams.

Decline attributed to widespread habitat destruction and replacement by introduced [[Mosquitofish]] ([[Gambusia affinis]]). Genetic study of 21 populations (5 in Arizona, 16 in Mexico) revealed:
- Local diversity (Hc) declined at northern periphery and in upstream sections
- Three genetically-defined groups identified via Distance-Wagner clustering
- Group I: Gila River of Arizona, Rios de la Concepcion and Sonora of Mexico
- Group II: Rio Matape, Rio Yaqui, lower Rio Mayo
- Group III: upper Rio Mayo (most genetically divergent)

**Hierarchical Partitioning of Genetic Diversity in P. occidentalis:**
- 52.8% divergence between three geographical groups (Dsd)
- 21.3% within colonies (Hc)
- 25.5% differences between colonies within rivers (Dcr)
- 0.4% differences between rivers within major subdivisions (Drs)

Management implication: Conservation should distinguish geographic groupings and maintain their separation. Combination of fishes from different groups could erode largest proportion of genetic variance.

**Hatchery Management:**
[[Sonoran Topminnow]] propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico since 1976 for reintroduction. At least 100 field sites stocked through 1985. Initial stock from [[Monkey Spring]], Santa Cruz County, Arizona—an isolated, thermally stable springhead with no detectable allelic diversity at 25 loci examined. This genetically invariant group has low fecundity and likely physiological intolerance.

Recommendation implemented: Replace hatchery stock with fish from [[Sharp Spring]], a population from same genetic subdivision with higher fecundity, growth rate, and detectable allelic variation (H = 3.7%). Sharp Spring population naturally experiences wide environmental fluctuations, making it better candidate for broad-scale reintroduction.

**Chihuahuan Desert Pupfish Populations:**
Four pupfish species in [[Chihuahuan Desert]] of New Mexico and Texas show Stream Hierarchy pattern:
- [[Cyprinodon pecosensis]]: 7.7% of total genetic diversity attributable to differences among populations along 200 km of [[Pecos River]], Texas; estimated Nem ≈ 3
- [[Cyprinodon elegans]]: restricted to single creek; Dct = 10.8%; estimated Nem ≈ 2
- [[Cyprinodon bovinus]]: restricted to 10 km of [[Salt Creek]]; Dct = 1.4%; estimated Nem ≈ 17-18
- [[Cyprinodon tularosa]]: several partially isolated habitats over several hundred kilometers in Salt Creek drainage, New Mexico; Dct = 19.0%; estimated Nem ≈ 1

Management recommendation: Exchange of one individual between populations per generation within river system would maintain most allelic diversity without swamping opportunity for local adaptation. Movement of fish among colonies within river poses no problems (except possibly C. tularosa), but precautions needed to avoid mixing between species.

**Recommended Experimental Research Areas:**

1. **Test relationship between heterozygosity and individual fitness:** Studies needed of relationships between genetic diversity and growth rates, survivorship, fecundity, developmental stability, and competitive ability to determine importance of maintaining high heterozygosity populations.

2. **Experimentally mix genetic stocks within subdivisions:** Evaluate effects of mild outcrossing within same genetic subdivision. Example: [[Cienega Creek]] and [[Monkey Spring]] P. occidentalis populations both homozygous for all loci but fixed for alternative alleles at two loci. Crosses should increase heterozygosity; fitness tests of hybrids versus parental stocks and [[Sharp Spring]] stock would indicate if outcrossing produces more robust fish for stocking. Natural localities should always remain genetically pure.

3. **Test relative roles of genetic adaptation versus developmental plasticity:** Desert fishes show high phenotypic differentiation among localities. Critical experiments needed to separate true genetic differentiation (local evolutionary change) from environmental induction of unique morphs. Important for determining whether to maintain diversity represented by differences among colonies (Dct).

**Conservation Philosophy:**
Authors raise fundamental question: What should we conserve—species, subspecies, individual populations, or unique alleles? Answer may depend on organism and economic constraints. For desert fishes, maintaining regional, local, or even allelic diversity is feasible—they can be easily manipulated, local habitats are available or can be created, and fish can be kept in small refugia.

[[Sonoran Topminnow]] identified as model organism for developing conservation programs with sound genetic basis. Species is more abundant than many endangered species, much is known about its ecology, physiology, and genetics, easy to raise in captivity, and hardy under field and experimental conditions. Concerted recovery efforts could benefit endangered species programs at large by developing sound management schemes including application of genetic principles and field/laboratory experimentation of fitness responses to management programs.
