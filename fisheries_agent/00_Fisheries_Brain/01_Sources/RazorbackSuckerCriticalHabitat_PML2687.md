# Razorback Sucker Critical Habitat Designation and Draft Recovery Planning

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service letter, dated January 1992 and sent from the Utah Field Office, summarizes the December 1991 meeting of the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and requests information for establishing [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Razorback Sucker]], a federally listed endangered fish under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The letter defines critical habitat, lists the physical and biological [[Constituent Elements]] the team identified, names priority river reaches, and proposes a tentative draft [[Recovery Goal]] that sets criteria for [[Downlisting]] and [[Delisting]]. It also encloses a [[Razorback Sucker Critical Habitat Information Form]] and an attachment describing candidate river segments across several states.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large, long-lived catostomid that depends on warm, turbid, large-river habitats. The letter treats the species as a federally listed endangered fish whose recovery depends on protecting key river reaches throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]].

The letter quotes the [[Endangered Species Act]] definition of critical habitat. It has two parts:

- Specific areas within the geographic range occupied by the species at listing that contain physical or biological features essential to conservation and that may require special management or protection.
- Specific areas outside the occupied range that are essential for conservation.

Conservation is defined as the use of all methods needed to bring a species to the point at which protective measures are no longer necessary. Critical habitat is therefore the set of areas needed to achieve recovery.

The Service asked agencies and experts to review and refine the constituent elements and the candidate areas. It stressed that the lists were a "first cut" by team members and outside biologists, and that boundaries, areas, and elements could be modified, dropped, or added. Final designation would weigh biological needs against economic and other impacts, and economic considerations were not yet included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The team identified four groups of constituent elements, which are the physical and biological features essential to recovery:

1. **Spawning and early-life areas:** tributary or mainstem areas with coarse, clean substrates and water velocities below about 1.5 feet per second.
2. **Nursery habitat:** low-velocity or standing water, such as backwaters, where water temperatures warm to a threshold level. The exact value is illegible in the scanned source.
3. **Drift corridors:** river stretches that carry drifting larvae between spawning and nursery areas.
4. **Feeding and rearing areas:** mainstem portions of moderate to large rivers, including runs, pools, and eddies up to about 10 feet deep, shallow-to-deep channels next to midstream sandbars, backwaters, oxbow lakes, floodplain bottomlands, and submerged sandbars.
5. **Movement corridors:** connections between the areas above.

The team noted that factors limiting recruitment, meaning how young fish survive to join the adult population, made it hard to predict which areas could support recovery. Establishing populations in every listed area was not considered necessary for recovery.

The enclosed form asks respondents to document habitat that currently supports spawning, feeding, and nursery needs, adequate water quality and flows (quantity and hydrograph), low predator loads, and any potential for significant population recovery if limiting factors are reduced.

## Key Findings

**Critical areas for survival (highest priority):**
- Yampa and Green rivers from Lily Park downstream to Sand Wash.
- Colorado River from Rifle, Colorado, to Westwater Canyon.
- Lower Colorado River from Hoover Dam to Palo Verde Dam.

**Areas important for recovery (ten additional reaches):** These include the Green River from Sand Wash to its confluence with the Colorado River; the Gunnison River from Delta to the Colorado River; the Colorado River from Westwater Canyon to the San Juan Arm of [[Lake Powell]]; the [[Animas River]] from Durango to Farmington; the [[San Juan River]] from Farmington to Lake Powell; the [[Verde River]] from Sullivan Lake to Horseshoe Reservoir; the [[Salt River]] from Apache Falls to Roosevelt Lake; the [[Gila River]] from San Carlos Reservoir to the New Mexico border; the Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Lake Mead]]; and the Colorado River from Palo Verde Dam to [[Imperial Dam]].

**Draft recovery goal:**
- **Downlisting** will occur when habitats are legally protected and self-sustaining populations are established in three of five basins: Green, Upper Colorado, Lower Colorado, San Juan, and Gila.
- **Delisting** will occur when habitats are legally protected and at least one self-sustaining population is established in each of the five basins.

The term "self-sustaining population" was still to be defined. The goal and all listed areas were explicitly tentative.

**Attachment:** The enclosed table lists candidate segments by river, length in river miles, and state (Colorado, Utah, Arizona, Nevada, California, and New Mexico), and gives each segment's boundaries.

**Timeline and actions requested:**
- Return the completed information form and comments on the candidate areas by February 1992.
- Share a draft recovery and critical habitat package for review by December 1992.
- Agencies were encouraged to join the process early and remain involved.

**Contacts:** Questions go to Reed E. Harris, Field Supervisor, or Henry Maddux of the Utah Field Office.
