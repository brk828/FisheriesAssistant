# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: Meeting Notes, Study Plans and Gaging Station Assessment (June-July 1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation transmittal (July 1989) packages meeting notes from the June 15 Environmental Study Team (Flagstaff, Arizona) and June 16 Economic Study Team meetings of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program. It also includes draft study plans for seven Conservation Measures tied to the [[Biological Opinion]] on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, a participant list for the July 1989 Research Integration Trip, and a U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) letter recommending a network of gaging stations for sediment, water quality and flow monitoring.

The central species concern is the [[Humpback Chub]], an endangered fish of the Little Colorado River and Grand Canyon mainstem. The package sets out a $1,346,000 plan, a sediment and flood-frequency research agenda, and a set of economic modeling steps for power and recreation values.

## Background & Description

The document is a set of program records, not a research paper. It has four main parts:

- **Meeting notes (June 15, Environmental Team):** Covers the endangered fish trip, the Biological Opinion status, river trip scheduling, the scientific process, and sediment study direction.
- **Meeting notes (June 16, Economic Team):** Covers power economics prototype models, non-power economics, and the cost of October 1989 test flows.
- **Draft study plans:** Summarizes the seven [[Conservation Measures]], each with responsibility, timing, cost and funding source.
- **USGS letter (June 13, 1989):** Written by Julia Graf, Hydrologist, for Duncan Patten and the GCES Environmental Team. It describes gaging station needs and costs.

Attachments listed include an attendance list, a participant list for the [[Research Integration Trip]], flow charts, and a resource response diagram.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Humpback Chub status and sampling (May 1989 trip).** Research focused on the [[Little Colorado River]], the mainstem, secondary tributaries and backwaters. Objectives were to measure distribution, abundance, population structure, reproduction, age-growth relationships and movement of Humpback Chub.

- Total Humpback Chub collected: 1,887.
- Of captured fish, 33% were tagged in 1989; another 33% carried tags from 1986-1988.
- Recapture rate for [[PIT Tags]] was 11%. PIT tags were chosen for a 10-year life expectancy and because they avoid the injury caused by external tags.
- Reproduction of all native fish was considered favorable in the Little Colorado River this year.
- Planned thermal-shock experiments on larval chub failed because the broodfish were spent and could not be induced to ovulate. The team noted a possible size "window" when small fish are vulnerable to mainstem temperatures.
- Mainstem water temperature was about 15 °C, slightly above previous years.
- Striped bass were observed at [[Havasu Creek]], the furthest upstream movement documented.
- A razorback sucker or hybrid was documented in the collection. Catfish predation was also discussed.
- Fish and sediment samples were sent to the EPA laboratory in Las Vegas for priority pollutant analysis.

**Sediment dynamics.** Fluctuating flows were degrading certain beaches that had made net gains from tributary input for two years before fluctuating flows resumed. Fine particles, once entrained, were said to stay entrained. The bed scoured in spring and refilled during summer monsoon low flows. The USGS letter states that sand in [[Recirculation Zones]] (camping beaches and backwaters important to aquatic vegetation and fish) is more likely lost when main-channel bed elevation is low.

**Paria River findings (USGS letter).** About 90% of sediment is carried during short-duration summer and fall floods, and loads are hard to sample because peaks often occur at night. Floodplain aggradation may have reduced sediment loads by about one third from around 1940, and it ceased around 1980. Loads estimated from 1949-1976 samples may not represent 1980s conditions.

**Water quality.** The USGS letter says water and sediment quality between the dam and [[Lake Powell]] was largely ignored in Phase I. Phase II must address it if effects of releases on the [[Lees Ferry]] trout fishery and downstream Humpback Chub are to be determined.

**Flow regime and river operations.**
- Operators must keep flood releases to a frequency of no more than 1 in 20 years to maintain backwaters and sediment.
- Annual release is set at the 8.23 million acre-feet minimum under the Law of the River for the next two water years. The third year is roughly a 50/50 chance of another 8.23 million acre-foot year.
- Four days of constant 5,000 cfs flow are planned for October 1989. The notes say the study plan's six items depend on these flows.

**2. Conflicting goals on flood frequency.** Limiting floods conflicts with evidence that floods may benefit Humpback Chub. The team said a measure that benefits sediment should not be pursued unless the sediment-chub relationship is well defined, which it is not.

**3. Gaging station recommendations (USGS).** The USGS identified eight current or former stations as priorities for interim monitoring. Costs are planning estimates and exclude travel by helicopter or raft.

- **09379910, Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam:** reestablishment $8,000; operation $10,500/yr. Suited to verifying power-discharge relations and sampling water chemistry just below the dam.
- **09380000, Colorado River at Lees Ferry:** operating since 1895. NASQAN station with monthly water chemistry samples. Additional sediment sampling $2,500/yr. Upper boundary of a 15-mile trout reach.
- **09383100, Colorado River above the Little Colorado River near Desert View:** reestablishment $15,000; operation $18,000/yr. Access difficult.
- **09402500, Colorado River near Grand Canyon:** operating since 1922. Adding sediment sampling and monthly visits $23,205/yr.
- **09404120, Colorado River above National Canyon near Supai:** reestablishment $15,000; monthly sampling $18,000/yr.
- **09404200, Colorado River above Diamond Creek near Peach Springs:** reestablishment $15,000; monthly sampling $12,000/yr. Approximate end of the river and start of lake conditions.
- **09381000, Paria River at Lees Ferry:** sediment sampling cost $17,200/yr.
- **09402300, Little Colorado River:** $35,000 first year and $28,000 in later years to add sediment collection. Largest tributary and main sand source.

**4. Economic findings.**
- Western estimated the cost of four days of 5,000 cfs flows in October 1989 at about $90,000, net of revenue from releasing stored water during peak demand. Western said actual cost depends on the October energy market.
- A power economics prototype draft is due to the team in September 1989 and a final recommendation by November 1989.
- The hydrology input is one 30-year trace of monthly inflow. The team stressed that multiple traces are needed for probabilistic analysis.
- Rich Bishop will form a work group on non-power economics, including recreation and broader social values of the Grand Canyon. All economic work must follow Water Resources Council Principles and Guidelines so values are comparable.

**5. Study process and governance.**
- Duncan Patten was selected as senior scientist (40% time, IPA agreement) to integrate the GCES components.
- The team agreed research tests hypotheses while monitoring detects change and tests whether management is working.
- The Research Integration Trip (July 7-16, 1989; participant list in attachments) will develop a framework for an integrated research plan and timetable.
- A Congressional/Constituent Trip planned for August 11-19 (the economic notes give August 11-20) was limited to 25 participants. Its purpose is to educate decision makers.
- Other 1989 trips include a September radiotelemetry prototype trip, an NPS archeological monitoring trip in September, and a resource rehabilitation trip on November 7, 1989. Rehabilitation includes vegetation planting to stabilize beaches.
- The team proposed an integrated data system, such as a [[Geographic Information System]], with a custodian responsible for quality assurance and quality control.

**6. Open questions raised.**
- What is the role of vegetation in stabilizing sediment, and how effective is beach stabilization?
- What thresholds of response exist for each ecosystem component, and can they be predicted at a 75% level of certainty?
- How should the study boundaries extend beyond present limits?
- What happens if the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service lists the razorback sucker as endangered?
