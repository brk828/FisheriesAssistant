# Reconstructed Stream Flow for the Salt and Verde Rivers from Tree-Ring Data

#hydrology #water-resources #dendrochronology #streamflow-reconstruction

## Executive Summary

This 1981 paper in the *Water Resources Bulletin* by Lawrence P. Smith and Charles W. Stockton uses [[Tree-Ring Reconstruction]] to extend annual and seasonal runoff records of the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] in central Arizona back to A.D. 1580. The study tests whether the gaged record (roughly 1914 onward for the Salt, 1895 onward for the Verde) adequately represents long-term flow. It finds that the gaged period contains an above-average number of high flows, that several sustained low-flow periods in the past 400 years were more severe than any since 1890, and that low flows on the Salt recur about every 22 years. The authors conclude that water supply and flood planning in the [[Salt River Valley]] should account for these patterns.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The Salt and Verde basins cover about 13,000 square miles and supply metropolitan [[Phoenix, Arizona]] and irrigated agriculture. The Verde joins the Salt at Granite Reef to form the largest tributary of the [[Gila River]].
- **Water management:** The [[Salt River Project]] (SRP), described as the oldest multipurpose reclamation project in the nation, delivers water to 250,000 acres and electricity to more than 280,000 customers (SRP, 1980). Its dams, including [[Roosevelt Dam]], were built for water supply and hydropower, not flood control.
- **Storage:** Combined system storage is about 2.08 million acre-feet, roughly twice the average combined annual discharge of 1.19 maf. The median combined annual discharge (1914–1979) is 0.51 maf.
- **Climate:** Elevations reach about 12,000 ft. Precipitation ranges from about 8 to more than 32 inches. Winter storms come from Pacific cyclonic systems and dominate runoff; summer convective storms from Gulf of Mexico moisture contribute little to streamflow.
- **Motivation:** Urbanization since World War II has increased water demand and flood risk. The authors cite the [[Colorado River]] allocation, which was based on an unusually wet gaged period, as a warning about short records.
- **Methodological precedent:** The approach follows [[Stockton (1975)]] and Stockton and Boggess (1980), developed for the Upper Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a hydrological and dendroclimatological study rather than a fish or aquatic-ecosystem study. Relevant environmental context includes:

- **Tree species:** Samples came from [[Ponderosa Pine]] (*Pinus ponderosa*), restricted to the lower forest border, and [[Pinyon Pine]] (*Pinus edulis*). Sites were open-grown trees on exposed, shallow soils dependent on precipitation alone, to maximize climate sensitivity.
- **Sampling network:** Two cores from each of about ten trees were collected at 13 sites. Indices were based on a minimum of four cores.
- **Drought and tree physiology:** Extended dry periods deplete tree food reserves, producing lagged growth responses of several years after moisture returns. This lag explained underestimation of the 1905 and 1916 high flows.
- **Regional climate:** Low-flow periods on the Verde coincided with those on the Salt, attributed to the widespread [[Southwest Climate]] regime.
- **Verde baseflow:** Verde low flows are controlled largely by spring discharge rather than climate, which weakened the spectral signal and the low-flow reconstruction.
- **Historical drought:** A drought in the mid-1600s (driest five-year period ending in 1670) affected the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest.

## Key Findings

**Data and reconstruction**
- Gaged records: Salt River near Roosevelt from 1914 (drainage about 4,306 sq mi); earlier gage 1901–1913 (about 5,830 sq mi, including Tonto Creek); Verde below Tangle Creek from 1945 (earlier Verde record 1895–1979 used for calibration).
- Reconstructions extend back to A.D. 1580 using multiple linear regression of log-transformed discharge on tree-ring series, with predictors significant at the 99% level.
- Calibration R² (adjusted) for annual Salt River flow over the 1702–1979 common period ranged from 0.593 to 0.757 across models; the 1580–1979 annual model had R² of 0.534–0.564.

**Water supply implications**
- The gaged mean annual flow of the Salt River (1914–1979, 626.8 thousand acre-feet, Table 2 actual) was not significantly different from the 400-year mean, but was significantly greater than the means of several 80-year periods. The authors therefore caution that the gaged mean may not be a reliable estimator of the dependable supply.
- Several periods of sustained low annual flow were more severe than any comparable period since 1890.
- The 1728–1739 period (12 years) averaged only 43% of the 400-year mean, with no year above the mean.
- Spectral analysis of October–April Salt River flow found one significant periodicity at **22.2 years**, interpreted as a recurring tendency toward deficient flow. The authors link a similar 22-year drought rhythm to the Hale solar cycle (Mitchell et al., 1979).

**High-flow and flood implications**
- The 1905–1920 period was the wettest in the 400-year reconstruction. The mid-1780s to mid-1790s was the only comparable period.
- The gaged record has an above-average number of high seasonal and annual flows relative to 400 years. Gaged peak-flow records may therefore be biased high, making designs based on them "inherently conservative."
- The 1979 October–April flow of 1.59 maf was equaled or exceeded only three times since 1900. Its reconstructed frequency (about once in 30 years) was similar to the actual frequency (about once in 27 years), so 1979 was not anomalous.
- Average frequency of occurrence of annual and seasonal discharges associated with peak floods appears to have remained constant over 400 years.

**Verde River**
- Calibrated for October–April and December–March flows, since surface diversions affect other seasons. Verde high-flow characteristics resemble the Salt.
- The most severe Verde low-flow period was from the mid-1770s to mid-1780s. The 1580–1620 low-flow period rests on a single tree-ring series and may be unreliable.
- Spectral analysis of the Verde reconstruction showed no significant periodicity.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The gaged period is not fully representative of long-term flow. Water supply planning must incorporate the 22-year low-flow tendency and the possibility of multi-year droughts more severe than any since 1890.
- Flood and water-supply design should account for the above-average frequency of high flows in the gaged record.
