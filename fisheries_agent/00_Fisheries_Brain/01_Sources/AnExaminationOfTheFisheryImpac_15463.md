## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Reclamation study (Mueller, Bryant & Burke) examined fishery impacts of concrete-lining a 3.4 km section of the [[Coachella Canal]] in Imperial County, California, by dewatering and directly sampling the fish community in November 1984. The study is a direct follow-up to pre-lining surveys by [[Minckley Et Al 1983]] and complements independent data from [[Beaty Et Al 1981]]/[[Beaty Et Al 1984]] collected in unlined canal reaches. Sampling yielded only **6 fish species** and **3,110 individuals**, with an average density of **0.037 fish/m²** and biomass of **4.171 g/m²**. Compared against pre-lining and unlined canal benchmarks, concrete lining was associated with reductions in species diversity (-62.5%), fish density (-59% to -83.8%), and biomass (-48.1% to -91.6%). The findings support the hypothesis that river-adapted, current-tolerant species like [[Channel Catfish]] persist well in lined canals, while lentic, cover-dependent species such as centrarchids (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], sunfishes) and [[Flathead Catfish]] decline sharply or disappear.

## Background & Description

- **Channel catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): dominant species by number and biomass; individuals ranged from small juveniles (10–25 cm, dominant size class) to adults exceeding 57 cm total length. Length-frequency data showed strong modal peaks at 8–26 cm, likely representing year classes 0–3 ([[McCarthy And Marsh 1982]]). Fish appeared in good health with no spinal or skeletal abnormalities, contrasting with prior reports of deformities in threadfin shad ([[Marsh And Rinne 1983]]).
- **Common carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*): relatively few individuals but large-bodied, averaging **1,381 g/fish**, driving disproportionate biomass contribution (up to 46.1% of biomass in Section D despite only ~1% of numeric abundance).
- **Threadfin shad** (*Dorosoma petenense*): small-bodied, prone to escaping through 2.5 cm mesh blocking nets, biasing numerical undercounts; low biomass contribution (~0.5%).
- **Largemouth bass** (*Micropterus salmoides*): only a single individual (940 g) collected in the entire lined reach, indicating near-total elimination of adult cover-dependent morphotypes.
- **Redbelly tilapia** (*Tilapia zilli*): a single small juvenile (3–4 cm) observed but evaded capture — evidence of some recruitment/reproduction despite lining.
- Sixteen species historically recorded in the pre-lined/earthen canal (Table 4) spanned Ictaluridae, Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae, Cichlidae, Poeciliidae, and Moronidae — representing a much broader morphological and ecological range (e.g., mosquitofish, sailfin molly, striped bass) than observed post-lining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat transformation**: Concrete lining converted the [[Coachella Canal]] from a wider (~20 m), shallower (2–5 m depth), irregular earthen channel with submergent/bank vegetation and variable current, into a narrower (16.3 m top width, 4.9 m bottom width), deeper (max 3.2 m), higher-velocity (1.4 m/s operational) concrete channel largely devoid of vegetation and structural cover.
- **Microhabitat refugia**: The only heterogeneity in the lined canal came from periphyton encrusting check-drop walls and slopes, and drifting/scalloped sand deposits on the canal bottom (most pronounced near check drop 7.4, diminishing downstream). These sand accumulations may have functioned as minor cover/food refugia, correlating with higher fish density in upstream Section A (0.136 fish/m²) versus downstream Section D (0.022 fish/m²).
- **Species-habitat associations**: [[Channel Catfish]], a river/current-adapted species, maintained high relative abundance (96.6%) in the lined canal, consistent with prior predictions that lotic-adapted fishes would tolerate lining well. In contrast, lentic and cover-oriented taxa — centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, redear sunfish, warmouth, black crappie) and [[Flathead Catfish]] — were essentially extirpated from the lined reach, reflecting loss of structural cover needed for spawning, juvenile refuge, and foraging.
- **Macroinvertebrate food base**: Referenced companion studies ([[Marsh 1983]]; [[Marsh And Stinemetz 1983]]) found that while macroinvertebrate species composition was similar between lined and earthen canal, biomass declined 5–20% due to loss of large *Corbicula* clams and *Smicridea* caddisflies (an important channel catfish food source), yet catfish adapted to the altered prey base and sustained high densities.
- **Recruitment dependency**: The lined canal's fish community is hypothesized to depend on downstream migration/drift from earthen sections of the All-American Canal and Colorado River, or from active stocking (California Department of Fish and Game), rather than in-situ reproduction — since reproductive cover is largely absent.
- **Sampling artifacts affecting niche interpretation**: Small-bodied species (red shiner, threadfin shad) could pass through the 2.5 cm blocking-net mesh, likely biasing numeric (though not biomass) estimates downward for these taxa.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness decline**: 16 species (pre-lining, [[Minckley Et Al 1983]]) → 6 species (post-lining) = **-62.5%** diversity loss.
- **Density decline**: Ranged from **-6.9% to -83.8%** depending on comparative scenario/dataset used (worst case vs. Minckley 1980; most conservative vs. Beaty's control data).
- **Biomass decline**: Ranged from **-43.5% to -91.6%**, following the same scenario-dependent framework (Table 5).
- **Three comparative scenarios** were modeled:
  1. **Scenario 1** (Minckley 1980 vs. Mueller 1984, worst case): -83.8% density, -91.6% biomass.
  2. **Scenario 2** (Averaged Beaty 1980/1984 + Minckley 1980 vs. Mueller 1984): -73.0% density, -81.5% biomass.
  3. **Scenario 3** (Using Beaty's unlined control trend 1980→1984 as baseline natural variability): only -6.9% density and +43.5% biomass difference attributable to lining beyond natural fluctuation.
- **Channel catfish dominance increased** post-lining: from 78.8–86.1% relative abundance in earthen canal to **96.6%** in the lined canal — supporting the prediction that current-adapted species benefit relatively from lining-driven competitor/predator/cover-species declines.
- **Cover-dependent species collapse**: Largemouth bass, sunfishes, and flathead catfish comprised 7.9–15.6% of the earthen canal fishery but were reduced to just 0.03% (a single bass) in the lined canal; sunfish and flathead catfish were entirely absent from the lined-canal sample.
- **Carp biomass paradox**: Despite representing only ~1.0% of numerical abundance, common carp contributed **25.9%** of total lined-canal biomass (up to 46.1% in Section D) due to large average individual size (1,381 g).
- **No significant within-canal spatial variation** in relative abundance/biomass was found among the four lined sample sections (A–D), though absolute density/biomass trended higher near the upstream check drop (Section A) and declined progressively downstream, plausibly linked to diminishing sand-cover microhabitat.
- **Fish condition**: Captured channel catfish were in good physical condition with size structure (year classes 0–3) similar to unlined-canal populations sampled by Beaty, suggesting adequate food/habitat for the surviving population's current life stages, despite reduced macroinvertebrate biomass.
- **Anomalous pre-lining size structure**: Minckley's 1980 sample contained an unusually strong 3rd/4th year-class mode of large (>30 cm) channel catfish, not explained by CDFG stocking records — flagged as potentially atypical/non-representative baseline data.
- **Management implication**: As canal-lining programs expand across the Imperial and Coachella Valleys, cumulative loss of protective/reproductive cover may increasingly necessitate active fish stocking to sustain canal fisheries, since natural recruitment via downstream drift from earthen reaches or the [[Colorado River]] may become insufficient.
