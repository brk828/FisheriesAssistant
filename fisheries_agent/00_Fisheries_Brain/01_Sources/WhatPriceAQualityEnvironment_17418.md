# What Price a Quality Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pollution-economics

## Executive Summary

This 1969 essay by Jack W. Carlson (Bureau of the Budget), adapted from a presentation at the Soil Conservation Society of America's 23rd Annual Meeting in Athens, Georgia, argues that sound economic policy should guide public investment in environmental quality. Carlson holds that current knowledge is too weak to set a reliable price for a "quality" environment, but that abatement decisions can still be made by comparing control costs with the benefits of control. Fish are not the focus. [[Trout]] and [[Shad]] appear only as examples of economic losses from degraded water quality, and [[Recreational Fishing]] appears as one beneficiary of water pollution abatement.

## Background & Description

The article appears in the May-June 1969 issue (Volume 24, Number 3) of the *Journal of Soil and Water Conservation*, published bimonthly by the [[Soil Conservation Society Of America]]. Its masthead lists officers, a council, and an editorial board. Membership dues were $10.00 a year, with $2.30 of that going to the journal. Carlson's piece is one of several articles in the issue, which also covers the [[Interstate Compact]], Hawaiian farmland urbanization, the [[Morrow Plots]], and water table recording.

The author argues that the central problem is one of externalities. Polluters do not bear the full cost of environmental decline, and individuals cannot easily capture the benefits of their own abatement. The United States has chosen to require polluters to meet minimum quality standards. The alternative would charge those who benefit from improvements. Carlson notes that the standards approach relies on police power, but it does tie production costs to consumers and encourages least-cost cleanup.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Carlson rejects both extremes: restoring a pre-Columbian environment, and dismissing degradation as unimportant. He argues that maintaining an existing ecology is not inherently good or bad. Change is acceptable when prevention costs more than the damage, and some ecological change may even be desirable. The "ideal" standard is reached when control costs equal the benefits of control.

Environmental effects discussed include:
- **Water pollution:** damage to recreation, shell and other fisheries, and agricultural water supply. Carlson cites the economic loss of [[Trout]] and [[Shad]] when water quality drops below a threshold.
- **Air pollution:** damage to crops, equipment, and buildings, plus human health effects such as the lung cancer link, which is confounded by [[Cigarette Smoking]].
- **Noise:** hearing impairment in people and animals, and reduced work efficiency.
- **Landscape pollution:** solid waste, [[Junk Automobiles]], and visual clutter.

Carlson also stresses interdependence. Improving one pollutant may have little effect on human stress until related problems are treated together. Management units, he argues, should follow the "problemshed": river basins for water, airsheds shaped by meteorology and topography for air, transportation corridors for noise, and urban regions for solid waste.

## Key Findings

**1. Measuring benefits is difficult but not hopeless.** Some water benefits can be expressed in dollars, including recreation, fishery yields, and agricultural water. Health and aesthetic benefits are harder to price. Carlson suggests [[Cost-Effectiveness Analysis]] as a fallback, comparing alternative spending for the same health goal. He says that sociology, psychology, and anthropology have supplied too few hypotheses and indicators for human well-being.

**2. Discounting and intergenerational equity.** Benefits and costs should be discounted using a rate that reflects forgone investment returns. Risk should be handled in the cost-benefit calculation rather than in the discount rate. The minimum rate for the least risky investment should not be below the then-current government bond yield, "now about 6 percent." Per capita GNP, "now $4,250," is projected to reach $10,000 by 2000.

**3. Abatement costs were lower than expected.** A federal study (reference 1) had participants guessing about $20 billion a year. The findings were far lower:
- Reducing sulphur oxides and particulates by 60 to 75 percent in metropolitan areas (70 percent of the population): about three-quarters of a billion dollars a year, with manufacturing and electric power bearing about one-half ($350 million).
- Providing the equivalent of 85 percent treatment of BOD and suspended solids: $925 million a year, with industry bearing about $275 million and municipalities about $650 million.
- Combined air and water abatement: about $1½ billion a year, with manufacturing and electric power at about $3 billion.
- Per capita urban cost: about $3 for water and about $12 total if borne by city dwellers.

**4. Cost estimates are often inflated.** Assuming only one abatement method, such as treatment plants alone or fuel substitution alone, inflates estimates. Examples given:
- Ignoring the location of dischargers in the Delaware River Basin could raise costs by 50 percent for the same water quality.
- Ignoring the location of emitters and receptors in an airshed can double costs.
- Ignoring that harmful air levels occur on less than 5 percent of days can quadruple costs.
- Poor management may inflate control costs by 200 to 400 percent, compared with 20 to 40 percent in less favorable estimates.

**5. Distributional effects.** Groups that pay (municipalities, industries) tend to resist high standards, while groups that benefit without paying (fishermen, outdoor recreationists) tend to demand them. Carlson judges that water abatement income redistribution is "regressive," moving benefits from lower- to upper-income groups. He suggests that targeted groups hurt by abatement, such as the 25,000 to 43,000 high-sulphur coal miners, be helped through alternative employment rather than by weakening environmental standards.

**6. Industry impacts vary.** The chemicals and allied products industry would pay about four times the average abatement cost of other manufacturing industries. Electrical machinery would pay about one-tenth of the average. Carlson recommends Small Business Administration and Economic Development Administration assistance for "hardship plants."

**7. Capital subsidies are inefficient.** Capital costs are about one-third of water abatement costs and as low as 15 percent for air. Carlson calls a capital subsidy that encourages overuse of capital "undesirable." Raising the tax credit from 7 to 20 percent would cost federal taxpayers $302 million a year ($241 million water, $61 million air).

**8. Future costs will rise.** Metropolitan population is projected to grow from 140 million to about 195 million by 1985, and the national population from 200 million to about 265 million. Per capita cost could more than double to about $30, roughly one-third of 1 percent of per capita income. Sanitary solid waste disposal alone may need about $1 billion more in urban areas, about $7 per city dweller per year.

**9. Preventive and institutional approaches.** Carlson favors:
- Screening new technologies for environmental effects before wide adoption, comparable to drug side-effect testing.
- Basin-wide rather than segmented river management, though he notes that state representatives resist competing power centers.
- Combined heating, electric power, and solid waste incineration, which can reduce air pollution while generating steam.
- Planning cities with attention to freeway and airway locations, which affect congestion, pollutant concentrations, and noise.
- New towns or smaller cities, since per capita abatement costs rise with each additional city dweller.

**10. Conclusion.** Carlson concludes that the current crude estimates, together with intuition about health and stress damage, are "sufficient to justify action to improve environmental quality or at least to resist further deterioration." He calls for better damage estimates and stronger institutions, and notes that scholarly work on institutional focus is limited.
