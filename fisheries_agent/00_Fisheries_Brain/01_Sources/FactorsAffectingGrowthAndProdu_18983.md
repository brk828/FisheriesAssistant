# Factors Affecting Growth and Production of Channel Catfish, *Ictalurus punctatus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This University of Kansas Museum of Natural History monograph (Vol. 17, No. 4, pp. 191-256, published June 6, 1966, by [[Bill A. Simco]] and [[Frank B. Cross]]) reports roughly a decade of experiments, mostly 1960-1964, on the pond culture of [[Channel Catfish]]. The experiments were conducted in earthen ponds of the [[State Biological Survey of Kansas]] in Lawrence. The work examined growth on natural food versus supplemental feeds, dietary protein and carbohydrate, feeding rate, stocking rate, crowding, carrying capacity, and the relation of physical-chemical pond conditions to production.

## Background & Description

The channel catfish is native to Kansas, popular with anglers, and tolerant of turbid water, unlike [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]], which the authors note perform poorly in turbid ponds. Channel catfish can exceed 60 lb as a species record and reach about 17 lb in farm ponds. They are omnivorous and opportunistic, readily use supplemental feed, and seldom overpopulate ponds through natural reproduction. Fingerlings were available from state, federal and private hatcheries.

Experimental ponds were about 1/9 acre (roughly 114 x 40 ft) with depths of 2 to 5 ft. Eleven ponds were filled and drained independently from a 20 acre-foot reservoir fed by grassland runoff. Two ponds were subdivided by hardware cloth into sections. Fish were seined at two-week intervals and recovered at autumn draining.

Key terms defined by the authors:
- **Carrying capacity:** maximum poundage a pond supports over a stated interval.
- **Standing crop:** poundage present at a given time.
- **Production:** weight gain over a period, usually one growing season.
- **Yield:** total poundage obtained at draining.
- All expressed as lb/acre (lb/A).

Fingerling means fish of the previous year's spawn; subadult means a two-year-old fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reproduction:** Mortality in 41 experiments ranged from 0 to 22% (mean 5%). No spawning occurred in the experimental ponds, which lacked suitable nest sites; most fish were under three years old, the earliest age of normal spawning.

**Water chemistry and physical conditions:** Records covered temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity and total dissolved solids (TDS) from 1961 to 1964, measured by thermistors, the Winkler method, a Beckman pH meter, titration and conductivity bridges.
- Surface temperatures stayed above 25 °C (77 °F) from June to mid-September, then fell to about 15 °C by late October 1961. Ponds stratified daily and mixed nightly.
- Morning dissolved oxygen was often below 4 ppm in summer 1963; deep-water oxygen was sometimes below 2 ppm in the afternoon.
- pH was consistently between 7 and 9, with diurnal swings exceeding seasonal ones.
- Total alkalinity ran from about 50 to 150 ppm, mostly as bicarbonate, and declined in late summer.
- TDS ran about 200 to 500 ppm and peaked in early September, opposite to the alkalinity trend.

**Fertility:** Liquid ammonium phosphate (8-25-0) at 50 lb/A per application produced dense plankton blooms within four days. Blooms suppressed rooted vegetation.

**Physiology:** Citing [[Moss and Scott]] (1961), resting channel catfish showed little change in metabolic rate between 25 and 35 °C and tolerated oxygen from about 8 ppm down to less than 1 ppm over 15 days. The critical oxygen level was usually slightly below 1 ppm.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth on natural food.** Nine experiments with no supplemental feed or fertilizer produced 98 to 162 lb/A in one season (mean 131 lb/A). Four two-season trials yielded 135 to 183 lb/A (mean 168). The authors judged carrying capacity on natural food to be below 200 lb/A. Stocking rates of 500 to 2250 fingerlings/A did not significantly change yields.

**2. Protein in synthetic diets (trough studies).**
- 1960 force-feeding trials with capsules versus syringes: capsules gave better gains. Growth was too slight to judge protein effects over 55 days.
- 1961 ad lib trials (12 rations, 20 to 60% protein): gain correlated with protein level, and carbohydrate type mattered. Cellulose-based diets best reflected the protein effect. Starch outperformed dextrin, which outperformed sucrose at lower protein levels; the carbohydrate effect vanished at 60% protein.

**3. Protein in pelleted feeds (ponds).**
- 1961 open ponds (20, 30, 40, 50% protein): yields were 550, 703, 783 and 624 lb/A. The 30% pond (Pond 30A) was statistically better than the 20% ration.
- 1962 open ponds: yields were 1103 (20%), 1515 (30%), 1314 (40%) and 1245 (50%) lb/A. The 30% ration gave the highest standing crop and fastest growth after mid-August.
- Across comparable experiments, the 20% feed produced 17 to 29% less than the 30% feed (mean 23%). The 30% feed cost about 4.5 cents/lb versus 8.5 cents/lb for 20%, a 22% saving.
- **Conclusion:** the 30% protein feed was the best tested.

**4. Subadults versus fingerlings.** In 1962, fish raised on 40% feed as fingerlings gained more absolute weight as subadults than fish switched to 20%. Gains were largest when the ration was unchanged between years. The 40% advantage shrank in larger subadults, which gained less than threefold in one season versus more than twentyfold for fingerlings.

**5. Feeding rate.** Rates were tested at 2, 3.5, 5 and 7% of body weight per day.
- Feed conversion worsened with rate: 1.25 at 2%, 2.20 at 3.5%, 3.20 at 5%, 4.28 at 7%.
- Open-pond production peaked at 3% (up to 1936 lb/A) and was lower at 7%.
- The authors recommend 2 to 3% per day for economical production. Limiting feed to 80 lb/A/day was judged unnecessary.

**6. Condition.** Fed catfish were plumper than wild Kansas catfish at equal length. Length-weight exponents were 3.099 (fed), 3.304 (Kansas reservoirs) and 3.407 (Oklahoma). The condition advantage declined as fish grew and standing crops rose.

**7. Stocking rate.** In 1963 two ponds stocked at 6500 fingerlings/A produced 1922 and 2023 lb/A, among the highest recorded in one season. High stocking gave more pounds but smaller fish. The authors suggest stocking at 5000 to 6500/A and partially cropping at 6 to 8 inches (about 0.15 lb).

**8. Crowding.** In 1964 sections stocked with 10, 20, 42 or 90 fish showed no significant growth difference, though yield and gain were eight times higher in the 90-fish sections. Catfish were gregarious, with no agonistic behavior observed.

**9. Carrying capacity.** Unfed ponds stayed near 1200 lb/A with supplemental feeding. Two ponds stocked with 2160 subadults/A in 1961 stalled from July 10 to August 8 despite oxygen above 6.5 ppm. Thinning one pond raised its harvest to 906 lb/A. Nocturnal aeration in another pond raised its yield to 2215 lb/A. The authors concluded that aeration works when chemical factors, not space, limit production.

**10. Aeration (1963).** Aeration did not account for the high 1963 productivity, and dissolved oxygen remained adequate in unaerated ponds.

**11. Correlations.** Correlations used data from seven ponds in 1963.
- Unfed ponds: growth and condition rose with total alkalinity and with its diurnal change.
- Fed ponds: growth did not correlate strongly with alkalinity. Condition fell when daily oxygen and pH swings were largest.

**12. Summary conclusions.**
- Unfed ponds yielded 98 to 183 lb/A; fed ponds yielded 550 to 2215 lb/A.
- Catfish stopped growing and lost weight in late summer or early autumn in most ponds.
- Oxygen below 3 ppm seemingly retarded growth, although fish surfaced only below 1 ppm.
- Feed was poorly used once water temperatures fell below about 70 °F (21 °C).
