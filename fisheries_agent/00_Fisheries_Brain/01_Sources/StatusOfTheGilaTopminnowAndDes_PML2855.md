# Status of the Gila Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Nongame Branch technical report (No. 118, June 1997) by [[David A. Weedman]] and [[Kirk L. Young]] reviews five years (1992–1997) of monitoring, reintroduction and management for two federally endangered desert fishes: the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the [[Desert Pupfish]]. Both species once were widespread in Arizona's desert rivers and springs but have been reduced to a handful of natural populations. The report documents that the number of extant reintroduced Gila topminnow populations within historic range is below the 20 needed for downlisting, and that desert pupfish persist in Arizona at a single reintroduced site. It presents extirpation criteria, a three-tier reintroduction framework, a site-by-site index, and a proposal to reintroduce fish at 78 localities over roughly a decade.

## Background & Description

### Gila Topminnow
- A small live-bearing minnow in the family Poeciliidae; the genus *Poeciliopsis* has 18 known species.
- The subspecies *P. o. occidentalis* is the only native poeciliid of the Gila River Basin.
- Historically occupied shallow margins of main channels, backwaters, tributaries and springs; prefers quiet, warm water with dense aquatic vegetation.
- Natural populations were historically reduced to refugia (springs, cienegas) during droughts and re-expanded after rains.
- Once "the most common fish in the Gila River basin" (Hubbs and Miller 1941), it was reduced to 10 known isolated populations by the mid-1990s (per Abarca et al. 1994), largely due to dams, non-native fishes, swamp drainage and groundwater pumping.

### Desert Pupfish
- Family Cyprinodontidae. Historically two Arizona subspecies, *C. m. macularius* and *C. m. eremus* (Quitobaquito pupfish), plus an undescribed Monkey Spring pupfish, now extinct (late 1960s, largemouth bass introduction).
- *C. m. macularius* was eliminated from Arizona by the 1950s; its natural populations no longer persist in Arizona.
- *C. m. eremus* is known only from Quitobaquito Spring, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument; estimated abundance under normal conditions is 5,000–10,000 individuals (USFWS 1993).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Non-native predators and competitors ([[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], bullheads, goldfish, guppies, red shiners), desiccation and drought, flooding and scour, cattle trampling and grazing, sedimentation and cattail/aquatic vegetation overgrowth, cold temperatures at high elevation, and groundwater depletion.
- **Hybridization risk:** Several reintroduced populations derived from [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] stock held at the University of Arizona may be of questionable genetic integrity.
- **Associated fauna:** Native species include [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Chub]] and speckled dace; the report lists 17 species codes monitored, including non-natives.
- **Water quality and habitat:** Monitoring recorded dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature and conductivity; several failed sites had very low dissolved oxygen (e.g., Replaced stocking site Mesquite Spring, below 1 ppm) or pH of 3.9 (Unnamed Pond near Bitter Creek).
- **Survey methods:** Annual sampling April–November with dipnets, seines, minnow traps and backpack electrofishers; mesh 3.2 mm (1/8 in); voucher specimens deposited at the ASU Vertebrate Museum.

## Key Findings

- **Management history:** Gila topminnow recovery began with the 1967 federal listing; the 1984 recovery plan set downlisting at 20 successful wild populations. Downlisting criteria were met 1985–1990, but downlisting was not pursued, and the count fell below 20 in 1991 and has stayed below.
- **Reintroduction totals:** Since 1936, more than 269 reintroductions or natural dispersals have occurred at 176 wild locations; this report counts 175 wild localities (stocking of five could not be verified). Gila topminnows no longer exist at 157 of 175 localities; failure causes were speculated for 95 sites, most commonly drying/drought (36%) and unknown causes (39%).
- **Extant populations:** 17 extant reintroduced populations within historic range: 7 Category 1, 6 Category 2, 2 Category 3, 2 Category 4. Only five meet the draft recovery plan's downlisting criteria. Average age of extant reintroduced populations is reported as 13 years.
- **Natural populations:** Fifteen known natural localities historically; four no longer support topminnows (Cocio Wash, an unnamed spring along the San Pedro near Mammoth, Sheehy Spring, Ash Spring). Three populations are relatively secure and lack mosquitofish (Cottonwood Spring, Monkey Spring, Sonoita Creek).
- **Desert pupfish status:** Only one reintroduced population remains extant in Arizona, Cold Springs (#85). Of 15 wild sites stocked for *C. m. macularius*, 14 were established before 1992 and have failed. Bog Hole and Finley Tank are of questionable genetic integrity; Bog Hole has not yielded pupfish for at least 10 years.
- **Extirpation criteria:** A site may be declared extirpated after three documented consecutive surveys (first and last at least two years apart) that cover most of the habitat, are conducted April–November, include voucher specimens, and, if dry, include surveys two miles up- and downstream.
- **Three-tier framework (Gila topminnow):** Level 1 natural populations; two Level 2 (primary) populations per natural population, which must persist at least 10 years to qualify for downlisting; three Level 3 (secondary) replicates per natural population. With 10 natural populations extant, 50 replicates are needed for downlisting; four existing replicates leave 45 to establish.
- **Desert pupfish recovery plan:** Arizona is to establish 10 Tier 2 populations in the lower and middle Gila River and tributaries and at least 45 Tier 3 populations; delisting of *C. m. macularius* was considered not feasible in the foreseeable future.
- **Policy issues:** A 1981 MOU led to all reintroduced populations being treated as fully protected, requiring Section 7 consultation for land management, which eroded interagency trust. In 1988, the Arizona Game and Fish Commission decided not to stock these species except as experimental non-essential under Section 10(j) of the ESA; the Commission later authorized AGFD to again pursue reintroduction of protected populations.
- **Recommendations:** Proposed reintroduction and management at 78 identified sites; an aggressive 4–5 year reintroduction program stocking 15–18 sites annually beginning in 1998, with more sites than required to allow for Tier 3 losses; Section 7 consultations on a site, watershed or allotment basis; and expedited finalization of the draft Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan. Downlisting could be pursued about 10 years after Tier 2 and 3 populations are established, possibly by 2072.
- **Site evaluations:** 78 localities (69 failed sites recommended for restocking plus nine newly evaluated sites) are listed in Table 5 and Appendix A, with recommendations such as renovation to remove non-natives and installation of barriers (e.g., Lime Creek, Tule Creek, Redfield Canyon).
- **Notable sites:** [[Hidden Water Spring]] is the longest continuously surviving reintroduced population; [[AD Wash]] was stocked in 1993 with both species and persisted only briefly; [[Tule Creek]] rebounded after a 1993 flood within its exclosure.
