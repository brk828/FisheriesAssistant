# Fish Oil Industry in South America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Engraulidae

## Executive Summary
This chapter (Chapter 26 of *Fish Oils*, reprinted as U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries Circular 282, December 1967) by J. R. Sanchez Torres of the Instituto del Mar del Peru describes the fish oil industry in South America, focusing on Peru. Peru has been the world's largest fish oil producer since 1961, almost entirely from the [[Peruvian Anchovy]] reduction industry. Production was 154,900 metric tons in 1963. The chapter covers anchovy composition, oil characteristics, extraction, refining, hydrogenation for edible fats, uses and prospects for expansion.

## Background & Description
- Peruvian fisheries include pelagic species ([[Peruvian Anchovy]], tuna, bonito, mackerel), the bottom fish merluza (*Merluccius gayi*, given as "gally"), and predators such as tollos (small sharks) and rays. Merluza is dispersed from San Gallan to Punta de Parinas, with commercially suitable concentrations between Chimbote and Paita.
- The first Peruvian fish oils came from shark and bonito livers. The anchovy fishery (1961-1964) was exploited by over 2,000 vessels and accounted for over 95% of all fish handled in Peru.
- Anchovy oil is a by-product of [[Fish Meal]] manufacture, generally recovered by centrifuging press liquor.
- Fresh anchovy average composition: solids 19.2% (protein 16.0%, minerals 3.2%), oil 8.0%, water 72.8%. Oil content is the most variable component and depends on season, habitat, fish condition, [[Plankton]] distribution (hydrological factors), spawning and migration. Oil content rises through life and drops at spawning when fish stop feeding.
- Minimum 1964 oil contents: Ilo 3.5%, Chimbote 4%, Callao 4% (325 samples, 65,000 specimens, Technological Department of the Instituto del Mar del Peru).
- Typical crude oil (10 samples): free fatty acid avg 2.2%, moisture 0.415%, impurities 0.0495%, iodine number 184, unsaponifiable 1.0%, Gardner color 13. A single-sample fatty acid analysis showed major components C16:0 (20.50%), C14:0 (12.40%), C18:1 (14.35%), C16:1 (11.10%) and a C20 polyunsaturated fraction (X2, 16.70%).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Oil accumulation is tied to [[Upwelling]] and plankton-driven productivity along the Peruvian coast, with seasonal and geographic variation among [[Callao]], [[Chimbote]] and [[Ilo]].
- The anchovy reduction fishery dominates landings, so the industry depends on a single pelagic stock. The text does not discuss stock assessment or sustainability.
- Monthly average oil yields (1962, 14 plants) ranged from 11.8 kg per metric ton (August, September) to 31.2 kg per metric ton (June).

## Key Findings
- **Production and trade (FAO 1963):** Peruvian production rose from 10,000 t (1958) to 118,900 t (1961), 150,800 t (1962) and 154,900 t (1963). Peruvian exports rose from 1,600 t (1958) to 125,500 t (1963). Chile rose from 700 t to 12,500 t (exports 11,800 t in 1963). Argentina, Brazil and Venezuela stayed flat and small.
- **Domestic use:** Peruvian consumption grew from 426 t (1954) to 37,071 t (1963) as refining capacity expanded. Exports went mainly to Holland, Norway, Denmark, England, Germany, Colombia and France, as raw or semi-refined oil. Rapid output growth depressed international prices.
- **Extraction:** Fish are cooked 12-15 min at 98-100°C and pressed. Press-cake holds about 45% solids and 3-5% oil. Press liquor is heated to 92-95°C and centrifuged (DeLaval, Sharples, Titan, Westfalia, Kraus Maffei). Stickwater (about 0.6% oil) is evaporated to fish solubles.
- **Refining:** Four stages: neutralization, washing, drying and bleaching. Caustic soda is the main agent. Batch neutralization is small-scale; continuous neutralization is used above 40 t/h. Five Peruvian plants use the [[DeLaval Short-Mix Process]] (45-140 t/day), where vacuum drying cuts moisture from about 0.5% to 0.005%. Bleaching earths include Tonsil, Superfiltrol, Activite and Alsil, at 104-110°C.
- **Refining losses:** For 1,000 t of crude oil (2.95% free fatty acids, 1.15% moisture and impurities), losses were 87 t in refining and 10 t in bleaching, giving 903 t of bleached oil.
- **Hydrogenation (batch only):** A 10.3 t charge of refined oil (iodine value 202) was hydrogenated in two stages with a nickel catalyst (22-23% Ni). Stage 1 ran at 150°C and 1.5 atm for 4 h 10 min (iodine value 100, melting point 30.2°C). Stage 2 ran at 190°C for 1 h 40 min (total 5 h 50 min), giving iodine value 72 and melting point 37°C. Post-refining reduced free fatty acids to 0.08%. Deodorization uses superheated steam, preferably continuous.
- **Uses:** Margarine, soap, vitamin concentrates, steel industry, tanneries and export.
- **Outlook:** Anchovy oil's high iodine number makes it unstable and easily oxidized, so edible use requires [[Hydrogenation]]. Because fish oil is cheaper than scarce vegetable oils in Peru, hydrogenation remains economically favorable. The author considers the industry's future encouraging given rising South American consumption.
