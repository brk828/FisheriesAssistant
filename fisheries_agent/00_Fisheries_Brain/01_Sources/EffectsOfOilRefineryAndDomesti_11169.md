# Effects of Oil Refinery and Domestic Sewage Effluents on Fish Populations of a Southwestern Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #pollution-ecology #stream-fish

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis by Hilton Allison Phillips (Oklahoma State University, 1965) documents a year-long study (June 1963–June 1964) of fish populations in [[Skeleton Creek]], Oklahoma, examining impacts of oil refinery and treated domestic sewage effluents on species diversity and abundance. The study employed twice-monthly seine collections at eight stations along a 60-mile gradient and monthly 24-hour field bioassays using livecars. A total of 18,917 fish representing 24 species were collected. Results showed that species diversity was strongly influenced by seasonal water-level fluctuations and migratory behavior. Below the combined [[Enid Outfalls]] (oil refinery and municipal sewage), fish populations exhibited marked seasonal variation with maximum abundance in November 1963, followed by winter decline and spring recovery correlated with migration patterns. Upstream populations above the outfalls were more stable, possibly benefiting from fertilization effects of dilute sewage. No single species demonstrated preference for polluted waters, though certain species dominated specific stations. Bioassay survival rates correlated strongly with fish presence in the stream, validating this method for environmental assessment.

## Background & Description

[[Skeleton Creek]] originates northeast of Enid in Garfield County, Oklahoma, flowing south-southeasterly through Kingfisher and Logan Counties before entering the [[Cimarron River]] approximately six miles north of Guthrie. The permanent stream is approximately 75 miles long, drains 410 square miles, has a mean gradient of six feet per mile, and averages 20 feet in width. The drainage basin lies in a transitional zone between pedical and pedalfer soils, with the stream occupying an alluvial belt developed from Permian red shales and sandstones (Upper and Lower Enid Formations) and Quaternary/Tertiary sands and clays. Soils are neutral to slightly alkaline.

The climate is continental with long summers, wide temperature fluctuations (occasionally exceeding 100°F in summer, dropping below zero in winter), and average annual precipitation of 30 inches distributed fairly evenly but with wettest months April–October. Severe droughts are common.

The stream receives effluents from three sewage treatment plants (secondary treatment) and one oil refinery. The [[Enid Municipal Sewage Plant]] discharges 2–2.5 million gallons per day into [[Boggy Creek]] one mile above its junction with Skeleton Creek. The oil refinery (uniquely using treated sewage effluent for refining processes) discharges into Boggy Creek 175 yards upstream of the municipal outfall. A military installation sewage plant (185,000 gallons/day) is located nine miles upstream on Boggy Creek, and a state hospital also discharges into Skeleton Creek.

Eight sampling stations were established: Stations 6, 15, 33, 46, and 60 were located 6, 15, 33, 46, and 60 miles downstream from the Skeleton-Boggy Creek confluence; Stations 1 and 5 were 1 and 5 miles upstream on Skeleton Creek; Station 2 was on Boggy Creek 2 miles upstream from Enid outfalls. Stations varied in substrate (sand, mud, gravel, red clay, parent material), depth (few inches to over 5 feet), shading, and vegetation. Station 6 exhibited heavy organic matter accumulation and algal growth with clear to slightly turbid, greenish water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area represents a typical southwestern U.S. stream ecosystem subject to multiple anthropogenic stressors. The [[Permian Red Beds]] geology contributes naturally high turbidity and alkalinity. Stream fauna must cope with extreme environmental variability including temperature fluctuations, periodic droughts, flash floods, and naturally high sediment loads characteristic of Great Plains streams.

Fish collections employed two common-sense minnow seines (10×3.5 ft with 1/8-inch mesh; 6×4 ft with 1/4-inch mesh) deployed in both pools and riffles to capture species with different habitat preferences. Approximately equal effort was expended at each station during twice-monthly sampling (23 total sampling periods, with only one collection in December).

Monthly 24-hour field bioassays used livecars (36×25×20 inches, 1/4-inch galvanized hardware cloth) with 10 test fish per trial. Test species were adult [[Notropis lutrensis]] (Red River Shiner), [[Notropis percobromus]] (Plains Shiner), and [[Notropis stramineus]] (Sand Shiner), selected for abundance, size uniformity, and occurrence in less-polluted stream sections. Fish were transported in aerated 10-gallon styrofoam tanks with gradual temperature acclimation. Sick or hemorrhaging individuals were excluded. Four to nine bioassays were conducted at stations 6–33.

Environmental factors known to influence stream fauna include watershed character (soil type, fertility, land use), flood frequency and erosion, water volume and flow rate, stream depth, substrate type, bank character, shading, temperature variation, and catastrophic events. Biological factors include migration, metabolism, morphological/physiological adaptations, diet, size, activity level, disease, and species associations.

## Key Findings

**Species Composition and Distribution**: Collections yielded 18,917 fish from 7 families, 15 genera, and 24 species. Below Enid outfalls, 13,247 fish were collected; above outfalls, 5,670 fish. Species richness did not follow a simple upstream-to-downstream gradient. Station 46 showed increased species count due to single occurrences of [[Campostoma anomalum]] (Stoneroller), [[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]] (Black Crappie), and [[Fundulus kansae]] (Northern Plains Killifish, likely washed from tributary). Station 33 had high species diversity including occasional [[Carpiodes carpio]] (River Carpsucker), flood-transported [[Notemigonus crysoleucas]] (Golden Shiner), and [[Ictalurus melas]] (Black Bullhead). Station 15 showed sporadic species composition with fish absent in 9 of 23 collections. Only one fish was collected at Station 6. Stations 1 and 2 supported similar species numbers to Station 15, while Station 5 had greater diversity than all stations except 46 and 33.

**Dominant Species Below Outfalls**: Six species comprised 96% of fish populations below Enid outfalls. [[Notropis lutrensis]] (6,240 individuals, 47%) and [[Notropis percobromus]] (3,149 individuals, 24%) together comprised 71% of total catch. Other dominants included [[Gambusia affinis]] (Mosquitofish, 1,077, 8%), [[Hybognathus placita]] (Plains Minnow, 1,049, 8%), [[Notropis stramineus]] (764, 6%), and [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow, 431, 3%). N. lutrensis ranked first at all downstream stations. N. percobromus ranked second except at Stations 33 and 15 where G. affinis was more abundant. H. placita ranked third at all stations except 33 (where P. promelas ranked third).

**Dominant Species Above Outfalls**: Seven species constituted 95% of populations above Enid outfalls. N. lutrensis dominated (2,943 individuals, 52%), ranking first at all upper stations. N. stramineus ranked second in total abundance (719, 13%) and at Stations 1 and 2. G. affinis ranked third overall (556, 10%). P. promelas ranked fourth (484, 8%). [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (Green Sunfish, 374, 7%) ranked fifth overall and second at Station 5. [[Lepomis macrochirus]] (Bluegill, 128, 2%) ranked third at Station 5.

**Seasonal Variation in Abundance**: Below Enid outfalls, fish numbers increased during late summer and fall, reaching maximum in November (3,196 individuals). Sharp decreases occurred in December and January, with minimum in February (253 individuals). Wide fluctuations occurred from spring through July. Above Station 60, only one specimen was collected in January–March. April collections paralleled December levels. Significant numbers reached Station 15 only in May, when many males showed breeding coloration and females were gravid. Station 2 consistently exceeded Station 1 in fish numbers except December and January. Station 5 showed relatively stable numbers year-round.

**Migratory Patterns**: Evidence strongly suggests seasonal migrations below Enid outfalls. Spring migration from the Cimarron River extended upstream to Station 15 in April. Heavy rains in May (forcing fish downstream) and July 11 (6.5 inches) caused population reductions. No heavy rains occurred August–November, allowing adult and juvenile numbers to increase. Absence of fish above Station 33 in December corresponded with downstream winter migration, probably initiated by severe cold weather. Low stream flow and reduced turbidity (dark green with algae) characterized January–March when no appreciable fish numbers occurred above Station 60. Upstream populations (above Enid outfalls) showed no migratory movements, likely prevented by limited upstream habitat and negative reactions to heavily polluted waters downstream.

**Population Stability and Fertilization Effects**: Fish populations above Enid outfalls were more stable, with fluctuations dependent on water-level changes from precipitation rather than seasonal migration. High waters brought influx of fish from upstream tributaries; receding waters reduced available space and fish numbers. Station 2, located below a small sewage treatment plant receiving dilute sewage, supported approximately five times more fish than Station 1, suggesting fertilizing effects of small sewage quantities that do not seriously affect dissolved oxygen. Station 1 had fewer fish than Station 2, probably due to limited habitat, but more than Stations 6 and 15, possibly indicating synergistic effects or greater concentrations of combined oil refinery and sewage wastes at lower stations.

**Station 5 Unique Habitat**: This station's fish presence and abundance depended on overflow frequency of a small lake 1/4 mile upstream. Following heavy rains, receding floodwaters caused fish to aggregate in deeper holes, with a sandbar at the lower end serving as natural barrier preventing downstream movement. Fish populations were gradually depleted by sampling until the next overflow event.

**Station 15 Pollutional Barrier**: Few fish species reached Station 15 and presence was sporadic, dependent on stream flow. Periods of low flow lasting more than 2–3 weeks resulted in complete fish absence regardless of season, suggesting a pollutional barrier preventing upstream migration beyond this point.

**Bioassay Results**: Twenty-four-hour field bioassays validated environmental conditions. At Station 6 (6 miles downstream), average survival was only 11% (biased upward by 100% survival in November and 43% in February; all other tests showed 100% mortality). No sick fish were observed—individuals either died or survived. Station 7 showed 15% survival with over half in poor condition. Conditions improved markedly in the next five miles: Station 12 showed 55% survival with reduced sick percentage. Survival increased steadily to 90% maximum at Station 33, with sick fish leveling at 10% from Station 15 to 25, then increasing to 20% at Station 33. High bioassay survival consistently occurred when fish were abundant in the stream; low survival occurred when fish were few or absent, particularly at Station 15. Many instances of 100% survival at downstream stations suggested minimal mortality from handling or confinement stress.

**Station 7 Fish Kills**: No fish were collected at Station 7 except one L. cyanellus captured October 24, 1963 (five days after 2.5-inch rainfall), likely carried downstream by floodwaters. Several dead fish found at this station showed mouths agape, maximally spread branchiostegals, and considerable hemorrhage—symptoms suggestive of suffocation. Similar observations occurred on several other occasions following high waters.

**Species-Habitat Relationships**: Certain fishes were more numerous at specific stations and during certain periods, but no single species showed preference for polluted waters. The study documented species-specific spatial and temporal patterns but found no pollution-tolerant indicator species uniquely associated with degraded conditions.

**Methodological Considerations**: Methods were roughly quantitative and data not amenable to statistical analysis. However, observed differences in species diversity were sufficiently marked that sampling errors were deemed relatively unimportant. The study demonstrated that field bioassays proved valuable for evaluating stream environment, with survival rates correlating strongly with fish presence/absence patterns.
