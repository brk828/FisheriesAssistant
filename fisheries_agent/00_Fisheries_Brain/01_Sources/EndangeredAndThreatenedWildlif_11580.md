# Proposed Endangered Species Listing: Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus)

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register notice (May 22, 1990) proposes listing the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Once abundant throughout 5,635 km of the [[Colorado River Basin]], the species now occupies approximately 25% of its original range (~1,530 km). The razorback sucker exhibits no evidence of natural recruitment in the past 30 years, with existing populations composed primarily of aging adults. Major threats include habitat destruction through dam construction and operation, water diversion, altered flow and temperature regimes, predation and competition from nonnative fishes, and loss of spawning and rearing habitat. The largest remaining population occurs in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, with smaller populations in the upper [[Green River]], lower [[Yampa River]], and [[Lake Powell]].

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was first described by Abbott in 1861 from a Colorado River specimen, initially placed in genus *Catostomus* but later assigned to its own genus *Xyrauchen* by Eigenmann and Kirsch. Adult fish are readily identifiable by an abrupt sharp-edged dorsal ridge (keel) behind the head and large fleshy subterminal mouth typical of suckers. Adults are robust, often exceeding 3 kg (6 lbs) and 600 mm (24 in) in length. Younger fish less than 150 mm (6 in) lack the distinct dorsal keel and are difficult to distinguish from other sucker species.

Historically, the species was abundant throughout the Colorado River basin in mainstem and major tributaries across seven U.S. states and two Mexican states. Early accounts document abundance during settlement: Jordan (1891) reported razorback suckers "very abundant" at [[Green River]], Utah in 1889; residents near Clifton, Colorado observed several thousand during spring runoff in the 1930s-1940s. A significant commercial fishery existed in southern Arizona in the early 1900s.

Current distribution is severely reduced: approximately 1,208 km (750 mi) in the upper basin and 322 km (200 mi) in the lower basin. Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 758-1,138 razorback suckers in the upper Green River. The species is considered one of the rarest endemic fishes in the Colorado River basin, second only to the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning Biology**: Adult razorback suckers migrate considerable distances to specific spawning areas. In the lower basin, spawning occurs late January through April at water temperatures 11.5-18°C (52.7-64.4°F). In the upper basin, Tyus (1987a) observed ripe fish May 3 to June 15 at temperatures 10.5-18°C (50.9-64.4°F). Spawning typically involves several males accompanying a single female over gravel bars swept free of silt by currents. In reservoirs ([[Lake Mohave]], [[Senator Wash Reservoir]]), spawning occurs on gravel bars cleaned by wave action. Eggs settle into gravel interstices; larvae remain in gravel until swim-up.

**Recruitment Failure**: Multiple investigators collected viable fertilized eggs and larvae in spawning areas, but few collected larvae larger than 14 mm (0.6 in), indicating little or no successful recruitment. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) recovered larvae up to 20 mm (0.8 in) in an isolated [[Lake Mohave]] backwater where predators had been eradicated, but these fish disappeared within a month following predator reinvasion.

**Age Structure**: McCarthy and Minckley (1987) determined Lake Mohave razorback suckers collected 1981-83 were 24-44 years old; 89% of 70 sampled fish hatched prior to impoundment. They predict the Lake Mohave population may be extirpated before 2000. Scales are inadequate for age determination; most captured fish are much older than scales indicate.

**Habitat Use**: Outside breeding season, radio-telemetered adults (Tyus 1987b) utilized main channel of Green and [[Duchesne Rivers]] in depths 0.6-3.4 m over sand/silt substrates with velocities 0.1-0.6 m/sec. Fish selected near-shore runs in spring, shifting to shallow waters off mid-channel sandbars in summer. Except for spawning migrations, fish were relatively sedentary, moving only a few kilometers over several months. Winter tracking (Valdez and Masslich 1989) found most fish moved less than 5 km, using slow runs, slack waters, and eddies at depths 0.6-1.4 m and velocities 0.03-0.33 m/sec.

**Food Habits**: Adults are bottom-feeding plankton consumers. Marsh (1987) found Lake Mohave specimens consumed benthic fauna/flora and inorganic materials. Jonez and Sumner (1954) reported algae as most common food item in [[Lake Mead]], followed by plankton, insects, and decaying organic matter. Chironomids were prominent in Lake Mohave specimens. Vanicek (1967) found Green River specimens with stomachs packed with mud/clay containing chironomid larvae, plant stems and leaves.

Larval food habits studied by Marsh and Langhorst (1988) in Lake Mohave showed larvae selected *Bosmina* spp. (Cladocera) and avoided Copepoda and Rotifera. Papoulis (1986) found experimentally that food density of 10 organisms/liter was needed within 10 days of yolk-sac absorption; death occurred at 20-30 days with insufficient zooplankton. Lake Mohave averaged only 1.5 zooplankters/liter, with larvae disappearing at ~20 days, suggesting low food availability may limit recruitment.

**Temperature Requirements**: Bulkley and Pimentel (1983) determined adult preference range of 22-25°C (71.6-77°F) with avoidance below 14.7°C (58.5°F) and above 27.4°C (81.3°F). Dam operations have created reaches with temperatures below preferential range: below [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] for 105 km where summer temperatures average <15°C (59°F), and below [[Lake Powell]] for 384 km where summer temperatures rarely exceed 15°C (59°F). Razorback suckers rarely captured in these reaches since dam completion.

Egg incubation time varies inversely with temperature: 19.4 days at 10°C, 11.1 days at 15°C, 6.8 days at 20°C (Bozek et al. 1984). Poor hatching success occurs below 15°C with total egg mortality below 10°C.

**Nonnative Species Impacts**: Tyus et al. (1982) recorded 42 nonnative species established in the Upper Colorado River Basin; Minckley (1979) listed 37 in the lower basin. Predation documented on eggs and larvae by [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieui*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bluegill]] (*L. macrochirus*), and [[Redear Sunfish]] (*L. microlophus*). Larvae and larger razorback suckers found in stomachs of green sunfish, warmouth (*L. gulosus*), channel catfish, [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), and [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*).

Competition for food and space occurs with [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), common carp, and channel catfish. Recent range expansions include [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) in the Green River. In the lower basin, two tilapia species and flathead catfish have become dominant.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Threats**:
- **Range Reduction**: Distribution reduced from 5,635 km (3,500 mi) historically to approximately 1,530 km (25% of original range)
- **No Recruitment**: No evidence of natural recruitment in past 30 years; populations composed of aging adults
- **Population Estimates**: 758-1,138 individuals in upper Green River (Lanigan and Tyus 1989); sizeable numbers only in Lake Mohave
- **Age Structure Crisis**: Lake Mohave fish aged 24-44 years; 89% hatched before impoundment; population predicted extirpated before 2000

**Habitat Alteration (Factor A)**:
- **Dam Impacts**: 15 dams constructed on lower Colorado River and tributaries since 1910, dewatering or impounding nearly entire lower basin. [[Lake Powell]] and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] impounded 500 km and lowered temperatures in another 105 km
- **Flow Regime Changes**: 18% reduction in mean annual discharge at Green-Colorado confluence; spring runoff reduced 28% (Green River) and 37% (Colorado River) during May-June. Daily flow fluctuations increased 400% at Jensen, Utah due to peaking power operations
- **Temperature Alterations**: Reaches below Flaming Gorge (105 km) and Lake Powell (384 km) maintain summer temperatures <15°C, below species' preferential range of 22-25°C
- **Migration Barriers**: Dams obstruct access to historical spawning areas. One tagged fish recaptured 208 km downstream from original capture site
- **Future Threats**: Several major reservoirs and diversions in planning/construction (Animas-La Plata Project, Muddy Creek Reservoir, Sandstone Reservoir, Two Forks Reservoir, Central Utah Project). Over 100 section 7 consultations conducted since 1980 on water depletion projects. Central Utah Project will divert 165,000 ac-ft from Green River; Two Forks Project will divert 45,000 ac-ft from Colorado River

**Predation and Competition (Factors C and E)**:
- **Predation on Early Life Stages**: Concentrations of carp, green sunfish, bluegill, channel catfish, and largemouth bass in spawning areas. Larvae exhibit no defensive behavior and inhabit upper water column for first few days after swim-up
- **Stocking Losses**: Marsh and Brooks (1989) reported total loss of razorback suckers stocked in [[Gila River]] due to channel catfish and flathead catfish predation. Langhorst (1989) documented predation on 171 mm juveniles by channel catfish and largemouth bass
- **Competition**: Red shiner, common carp, and channel catfish share and compete for food and space
- **Continuing Introductions**: Northern pike invaded Green River mainstream; smallmouth bass introduced to Lake Powell; triploid [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) legalized in California and Arizona; [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) proposed for Lake Powell

**Hybridization**: High incidence of hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] reported in upper basin: ratios of 16 hybrids to 73 razorback suckers (Vanicek et al. 1970) and 40 hybrids to 53 razorback suckers (Holden 1973). Lake Mohave population shows <5% flannelmouth sucker gene introgression, considered insignificant (Buth et al. 1987).

**Regulatory Inadequacy (Factor D)**:
- State protections exist in all basin states except Wyoming (extirpated) and New Mexico (no records), but do not address habitat destruction or nonnative species introduction
- [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Implementation Program]] established with management goal to establish self-sustaining populations, but is not a regulatory mechanism and applies only to upper basin (excluding San Juan River)
- Cooperative Agreement may be amended, terminated, or parties may withdraw
- No mandate to consider razorback sucker in Federal actions (unlike listed species)

**Conservation Measures Proposed**:
- Endangered listing would provide full protection under Endangered Species Act
- Section 7 consultation required for all Federal actions
- Multiple Federal agencies involved: [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Western Area Power Administration]], [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], [[Environmental Protection Agency]], [[National Park Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Bureau of Land Management]]
- Recovery Program would coordinate recovery efforts, giving razorback sucker equal status with three currently listed Colorado River fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], bonytail chub)
- Prohibitions on take, import/export, interstate commerce, and possession of illegally taken specimens
- Permits available for scientific purposes, propagation, and incidental take

**Critical Habitat Determination**: Critical habitat not designated because (1) not determinable—limited information on specific habitat needs and existing habitats not producing recruitment; (2) not prudent—would not provide additional protection beyond endangered listing, would not address predation/competition threats, Federal agencies already aware of species presence, and notification benefits achieved through listing and Recovery Program.

**Previous Listing History**: Originally proposed as threatened April 23, 1978 (43 FR 17375); withdrawn May 27, 1980 due to 1978 Act amendments requiring critical habitat designation and 2-year completion timeline. Current proposal responds to March 14, 1989 petition from Sierra Club, National Audubon Society, Wilderness Society, Colorado Environmental Coalition, Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance, and Northwest Rivers Alliance.
