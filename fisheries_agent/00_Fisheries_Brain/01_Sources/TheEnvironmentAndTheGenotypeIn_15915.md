# Environment and Genotype in Polymorphism

#evolutionary-biology #polymorphism #population-genetics #aphidoidea

## Executive Summary

This 1975 theoretical essay by W. C. Clark, of the [[University of Canterbury]] in Christchurch, New Zealand, argues that [[Polymorphism]] is a useful broad term for any discontinuous variation affecting the same developmental stages within a population, regardless of cause. The author proposes two categories: [[Genetically Determined Polymorphism]], where the genotype dominates morph determination, and [[Environmentally Cued Polymorphism]], where the environment interacts with a universally shared genome to produce a morph suited to current conditions. The paper is not about fish. It concerns insects, aphids, cladocerans, nematodes, and general genetic theory, with the human [[Blood Group]] system and [[Sexual Dimorphism]] used as illustrations.

## Background & Description

The essay opens by noting that polymorphism remains a source of confusion because of the many competing definitions in the literature. The working definition the author accepts is "discontinuous variation within a population with the rarest morph existing at a frequency greater than that which can be maintained by recurrent mutation alone." Clark attributes this to a series of definitions by [[E. B. Ford]] published between 1937 and 1965, and notes that Ford's own restrictions are often omitted when the definition is cited.

Clark distinguishes the two categories by their underlying mechanism:

- **Genetically determined polymorphism** results from genetic material that is discontinuously distributed through a population but continuously active. Examples include [[ABO Blood Groups]] and other blood factors, most protein polymorphisms, some diapause behaviour, and the chromosome polymorphisms of [[Drosophila]] studied by [[Dobzhansky]].
- **Environmentally cued polymorphism** depends on genetic resources that are universally distributed but differentially activated or suppressed. Examples include seasonal forms of [[Aphids]] (apterae and alatae, sexuparae and gynoparae), [[Cyclomorphosis]] in [[Cladocerans]], the infective and free-living juveniles of nematodes such as *Strongyloides papillosus* and *S. stercoralis*, solitary and gregarious phases in [[Locusts]], seasonal forms in butterflies, and caste determination in Hymenoptera.

Clark also describes intermediate cases. Male-pattern baldness in humans has a clear genetic basis but depends on testosterone levels for expression, which explains its apparent sex linkage. Vestigial wing expression in Drosophila varies with rearing temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay's central argument is an ecological one about cost and fit. Environmentally cued polymorphism produces morphs matched to current conditions. In fluctuating or alternating environments it therefore avoids much of the penalty of selection for conditions that no longer apply, a problem the author calls the [[Epaminondas Effect]]. Genetically determined polymorphism, by contrast, produces variant morphs regardless of current selection pressures, so it yields many maladapted individuals and is "a particularly costly method of achieving adaptation" in fluctuating environments.

Clark uses the [[Sickle Cell Trait]] to illustrate the cost of [[Heterozygote Advantage]]. The superior fitness of heterozygotes is realised only by half of the offspring of a pair of heterozygotes. If the environment changes so that heterozygotes lose their advantage, selection favours the homozygous dominants and the sickle cell allele drops to a new, lower frequency.

The author also discusses the [[New Zealand Aphid Fauna]]. *Myzus persicae* occurs in New Zealand in two forms: one produces sexuals in autumn and overwinters as diapausing eggs, while the other reproduces entirely parthenogenetically (Cottier, 1953). Clark suggests the purely parthenogenetic form has persisted because the environment repeatedly called for it. *Rhopalosiphum padi* exists only as a parthenogenetic morph. Its overwintering host, *Prunus padus*, was extremely rare in New Zealand and possibly absent when the aphid was introduced. Parthenogenetic reproduction on autumn and winter cereals gives the species a marked numerical advantage over egg-overwintering aphids in spring.

Clark argues that adaptation to regularly varying environments often needs no polymorphism at all. Life histories can instead become cued to season, with [[Diapause]] serving as an "opting out" mechanism. Such mechanisms may be environmentally cued in multivoltine insects and genetically determined in univoltine species.

## Key Findings

1. **Definition.** Polymorphism should be a broad term covering all discontinuous variation, whatever its cause. Clark proposes the two-category scheme above to make discussion easier.
2. **Mechanisms.** Genetically determined polymorphism reflects normal changes in gene frequency. Environmentally cued polymorphism reflects differential gene activity, which is part of normal development and differentiation.
3. **Adaptive cost.** Genetically determined polymorphism is expensive in changing environments, because it produces unrelated and often maladapted variants. Environmentally cued polymorphism is less wasteful and can be more advantageous when it avoids producing overtly maladapted morphs.
4. **Genetic resources.** Environmentally cued systems avoid the loss of genetic variation that occurs when consistent selection removes less-fit alleles. Clark argues that they therefore avoid the Epaminondas Effect, though they can lose the capacity to produce an alternative morph if one morph is repeatedly evoked (as in the New Zealand aphids).
5. **Neglect.** Because of attempts to restrict the term polymorphism, environmentally cued polymorphism has been "largely unstudied by geneticists," although entomologists have studied it extensively. No detailed genetic theory yet explains its evolution or mechanism. Clark suggests a possible mechanism through neurosecretions and gene activation, as in control of chromosome puffing by juvenile hormone in insects.
6. **Critique of prior work.** Clark criticises [[Williams & Mitton (1973)]] for assuming that all phenotypes in asexual lines are genetically fixed. He argues this overlooks environmental cueing and the possibility of limited genetic change during [[Endomeiosis]].
7. **Sexual dimorphism.** Sex is often polygenic and, in some organisms, environmentally cued. It is one of the few genetically determined polymorphisms that has persisted for a long time in a balanced state, and it is hard to explain through heterozygote advantage.
8. **Reproductive isolation.** Clark suggests that under different selection pressures, the two forms of a polymorphism could lead to reproductive isolation and speciation of parts of a population.

The essay contains no original data, sample sizes, or statistics. Its conclusions rest on a literature review, mathematical models summarised from other authors, and a small number of field examples, mainly from aphids.
