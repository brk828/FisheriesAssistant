## Executive Summary
This document is an internal 1996 US Fish and Wildlife Service memo from San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge (Douglas, Arizona) outlining an **Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) eradication strategy** targeting the endangered [[Yaqui Catfish]] and associated native aquatic fauna, including [[Beautiful Shiner]], unspecified native topminnows, and native springsnails. Rather than presenting new morphological or ecological research data, the source is a **management action plan** detailing proposed strategies for fish removal/translocation, pond dehydration and renovation, exotic species (herpetofauna) control, native snail re-establishment, vegetation/habitat restoration, refuge policy changes, and experimental "radical alternatives" for restoring perennial flow to Black Draw. It provides valuable insight into the applied conservation ecology and threat landscape facing the Yaqui drainage's endemic aquatic species.

## Background & Description
No morphological, meristic, or anatomical data for [[Yaqui Catfish]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], native topminnows, or springsnails are provided in this source. The document is exclusively focused on management logistics rather than species description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Primary habitat type:** Isolated refuge ponds, steel holding tanks, stock tanks, springs/seeps (e.g., Mesa Seep), and ephemeral/perennial stream segments (Black Draw) within the historical [[Yaqui River Drainage]] distribution in Arizona and adjacent Mexico.
- **Co-occurring native taxa:** [[Beautiful Shiner]], unspecified native topminnows (housed at ASU ACF alongside Yaqui Catfish), and native springsnails — the latter tied closely to spring-fed conditions and vulnerable to fish predation, evaporation, and thermal loading if habitat is modified.
- **Threats identified:**
  - **Parasitic infection** by Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), a nonnative cestode requiring pond-level eradication efforts.
  - **Exotic herpetofauna** (e.g., bullfrogs) competing with or preying upon natives; targeted for removal via trapping, shooting, or coordinated desiccation-based "mass removal."
  - **Habitat degradation**: unwanted vegetation (cattails, bulrush, mesquite, tamarisk, lovegrass) encroaching on pond margins and springs.
  - **Predation/evaporation/thermal risk** to springsnails from poorly designed pond-spring interfaces, including risk of heron predation (linked to yellow grub, *Clinostomum* sp., transmission).
- **Vegetation of conservation concern:** [[Water Umbel]] (*Lilaeopsis* sp.), mapped for its response to wetland/pond manipulation, burns, and nutrient pulses — with concern that habitat treatments could inadvertently favor exotic weeds over native *Lilaeopsis*.

## Key Findings
- Proposed **fish management options** for the Yaqui Catfish and cohabiting species included: removal prior to pond treatment, translocation to secondary sites (e.g., Austin's, Magoffin's, stock tanks, Leslie Creek, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]), long-term storage in steel tanks or repurposed swimming pools, sacrificial sampling for genetics/museum vouchers, propagation, and species-specific separation during treatment based on differential parasite response.
- **Pond dehydration** ("dry and completely dry substrates") was proposed as the primary method for breaking the Asian tapeworm life cycle, with open questions about required dryness duration, pumping destinations (to prevent reinfection of adjacent wetlands), and the utility of liming to kill cysts or aid desiccation.
- **Pond renovation** plans (informed by "Matt's designs") emphasized deepening, vegetation removal, substrate sealing, and single-species pond design to ease long-term management.
- **Exotic herpetofauna control** was recommended to be timed with pond desiccation events and breeding seasons to maximize removal efficiency, alongside temporary exclosures for native species protection.
- **Springsnail reintroduction** was proposed contingent on habitat renovation mimicking natural spring conditions, with specific interest in assessing spring flow restoration potential at **Mesa Seep** via soil coring (supported by persistence of a nearby hackberry tree indicating subsurface water).
- **Vegetation control** (prescribed burns, chemical treatment, heavy equipment) was proposed as a dual-purpose tool for both tapeworm containment (limiting bullfrog/water movement) and ecosystem restoration, with caution needed near fire-sensitive riparian species like cottonwoods.
- **Policy recommendations** included restricting scientific collecting equipment reuse across sites, limiting refuge traffic, and mandatory decontamination (high-pressure spraying) of borrowed heavy equipment to prevent introduction of seeds, cysts, and other propagules (e.g., tamarisk, lovegrass).
- A **"radical" restoration alternative** proposed dedicating well/runoff water to sustain short perennial stretches of **Black Draw**, promoting riparian vegetation recovery, reducing sediment loss, and stabilizing banks for gabion structures — an early ecosystem-based restoration concept for the refuge.
