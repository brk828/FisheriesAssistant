# Defenders of Wildlife Comments on Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument General Management Plan Supplement

#conservation #endangered-species #desert-ecology #wildlife-management #NEPA-compliance

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from [[Defenders of Wildlife]] to the Superintendent of [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], submitted June 12, 1996, regarding the Supplement to the Draft General Management Plan. The letter, representing 135,000 members nationwide including 2,000 in Arizona, raises extensive concerns about inadequate protection for threatened and endangered species under the proposed management alternatives. While acknowledging some beneficial proposals (elimination of developments in [[Rosy Boa]] habitat, protective grates at [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] roosting sites, marked trails at [[Quitobaquito Springs]]), the organization finds the plan fundamentally flawed in its NEPA analysis and ESA compliance. Major concerns include: inadequate protection for nine endangered/threatened species; failure to address Highway 85 impacts causing wildlife mortality and habitat fragmentation; insufficient analysis of increased visitation impacts on sensitive species; lack of baseline data and carrying capacity studies; and failure to incorporate species recovery objectives into management planning. The letter threatens potential legal action under NEPA and ESA if concerns are not addressed.

## Background & Description

[[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] contains critical habitat for multiple federally listed species in the [[Sonoran Desert]] ecosystem. The Monument hosts the largest known maternity colony of [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] (Leptonycteris curasoae yerbabuenae) in the United States, located in a mine adit near [[Alamo Canyon Campground]]. [[Quitobaquito Springs]] represents the only remaining natural occurrence of [[Quitobaquito Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius eremus) in the United States and is designated critical habitat for this subspecies. The springs also support [[Quitobaquito Snail]] (Tryonia quitobaquitae), [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (Kinosternon sonoriense), and provides habitat for [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]] (Glaucidium brasilianum cactorum) and [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (Empidonax traillii extimus).

The Monument is bisected by State Route 85, maintained by Arizona under perpetual easement, which creates a significant barrier to wildlife movement and causes substantial wildlife mortality. The endangered [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] (Antilocapra americana sonoriensis) population, numbering as low as 100 individuals in the United States, appears to avoid the eastern half of the Monument due to Highway 85, effectively fragmenting available habitat.

Other species of concern include [[Rosy Boa]] (Lichanura trivirgata), [[Underwood's Mastiff Bat]], [[Sonoran Desert Tortoise]] (Gopherus morafkai), and [[Acuna Cactus]]. The document notes that twenty additional rare and imperiled species occur on the Monument but are not individually detailed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Sonoran Desert]] ecosystem at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] faces multiple cumulative threats beyond direct management actions. Groundwater depletion in Mexico's Sonoyta Valley is causing groundwater drainage below the Monument, threatening [[Quitobaquito Springs]]—critical habitat for the [[Desert Pupfish]]. The springs have experienced documented introductions of exotic predatory fish, a common problem across the Southwest where exotic species have eradicated native fish through predation and competition.

Highway 85 receives heavy traffic and creates what the 1994 [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] Recovery Plan identifies as a significant deterrent to expanding pronghorn populations. Long-time residents reported that pronghorn observations along Highway 85 were "not uncommon" in past years, suggesting historical use of areas now avoided. The highway causes wildlife mortality across multiple taxa and prevents the pronghorn from utilizing viable habitat on the Monument's eastern half.

Human disturbance impacts vary by species but are documented as significant. [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] populations are highly sensitive to disturbance; data indicate "a single brief visit is sufficient to cause a high proportion of lesser long-nosed bats to temporarily abandon their day roost." The bats exhibit extreme mobility, flying long distances from day roosts to forage each night, making both roosting sites and nighttime foraging ranges critical for protection.

Current conditions at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] show concerning trends. The plan acknowledges that human visitation "had an adverse effect on the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] population which is declining and may face extirpation," and warns that "even more dire consequences may be faced by the [[Quitobaquito Snail]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] whose continued existence as a species and subspecies, respectively, may be jeopardized."

Additional threats inadequately addressed include: herbicide and pesticide drift from adjacent agricultural areas; invasion of non-native flora and fauna; military overflights; erosion from increased human use; and cumulative water consumption increases in surrounding communities (Ajo, Why, Lukeville, Sonoyta).

## Key Findings

**Alamo Canyon Campground Expansion Concerns:**
The proposed action includes four new drive-in campsites plus day-use parking for six additional vehicles at [[Alamo Canyon Campground]]. The plan concludes this expansion would have "no adverse effect" on [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] or [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]], but provides no reasonable scientific basis for this conclusion. The campground expansion "could result in the presence of twice as many campers in the area at dawn and dusk," periods when the owl actively forages and when bats return to roost. The adit hosting the largest known maternity colony of [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] in the United States is accessible from this campground, yet no data are provided on frequency or intensity of previous disturbances or impacts on bat populations. Critical questions remain unaddressed: impacts from visitor noise (shouting into adit for echoes), campfires, artificial lighting within bat nighttime foraging range, vegetation degradation, increased fire risk, and whether bat experts were consulted.

**Quitobaquito Springs Visitation and Carrying Capacity:**
The plan proposes no visitor use restrictions for [[Quitobaquito Springs]] despite acknowledging current adverse effects on species populations. No baseline data quantify current visitation levels, seasonal patterns, or specific environmental impacts. No carrying capacity analysis has been conducted. The proposed network of marked trails and improved parking may attract increased visitation, yet no quantification of this increase or analysis of resulting impacts is provided. The [[Desert Pupfish]] faces particular risk from exotic fish introductions via visitor bait buckets—a documented problem with multiple incidents. Once exotic fish establish, selective removal is "exceedingly difficult" according to Fish and Wildlife Service publications. The plan proposes only "monitoring" rather than preventive measures. No permit system or mandatory guided tours are included in the proposed action, though the non-selected "new ideas" alternative suggests such restrictions.

**Highway 85 Impacts and Management Failure:**
State Route 85 causes severe ecological impacts including wildlife mortality across multiple taxa and habitat fragmentation preventing [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] access to the Monument's eastern half. Despite these documented problems, the Park Service "is not proposing in any of the alternatives to re-route traffic, establish a toll road, or construct a fee-collection station" along Highway 85. The agency dropped its previous consideration of reducing the speed limit by 10 mph due to opposition from local interests and Arizona Department of Transportation. The plan defers all analysis of Highway 85 options to "some future, unspecified date" when the agency might "work with" state entities. No data are provided on wildlife mortality rates per year or season. No alternatives propose immediate remedies despite the plan's acknowledgment that increased Monument visitation will increase Highway 85 traffic. Under every alternative except "no action," four new pullouts are proposed along Highway 85, yet no site-specific wildlife surveys, habitat descriptions, or impact analyses for these pullouts are provided.

**Inadequate NEPA Analysis:**
The plan fails to provide a reasonable range of alternatives. No alternative proposes decreasing visitation levels to sensitive areas despite documented species declines under current conditions. The "no action" alternative maintains current use; the "proposed action" expands facilities and visitation; the "new ideas" alternative restricts some overnight use but continues day use without quantified limits. No alternative addresses Highway 85 modifications. No alternative proposes removing existing campsites or restricting current activities where species are declining. The cumulative impacts analysis totals only two pages for the proposed action and provides no discussion of cumulative adverse impacts to riparian and aquatic species at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] or to other endangered species. Range-wide threats are not analyzed in context with Monument impacts.

**Baseline Data Deficiencies:**
The plan lacks fundamental baseline information necessary for impact assessment: current visitation numbers (Monument-wide and site-specific); seasonal visitation patterns; current identifiable impacts from existing use levels; wildlife population trends and health status; frequency and intensity of disturbances to sensitive sites; vegetation density and ground cover conditions; water quality parameters; and wildlife mortality data from Highway 85. Without such data, conclusions of "no adverse effect" or "no significant adverse effect" lack rational basis. Visitation projections for future conditions are contradictory; the plan states restricted access at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] would decrease visitation due to inconvenience, yet claims unrestricted access would result in no different visitor use levels—an illogical assertion.

**ESA Section 7(a)(1) Recovery Obligations:**
The ESA requires federal agencies to take affirmative steps to recover listed species, not merely avoid jeopardy under Section 7(a)(2). The plan fails to incorporate species recovery objectives into management decisions. For [[Sonoran Pronghorn]], the 1994 Recovery Plan specifically identifies Highway 85 as a barrier and recommends highway modifications and minimizing human disturbance from recreational usage. For [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]], the Recovery Plan (1993) emphasizes protection of major roost sites and food plants, recommending "restrictions or closures during times when the bats are normally present." For [[Desert Pupfish]], the Recovery Plan (1993) states populations "cannot be considered secure in habitats occupied by non-native fishes" and requires "protecting the habitat against contamination/re-contamination by non-native fishes" with provisions potentially including "imposition of regulations locally prohibiting possession of non-native fishes." The plan postpones recovery planning while simultaneously facilitating increased visitation that may preclude future recovery options.

**Species-Specific Concerns:**
[[Rosy Boa]]: Inadequate impact analysis for increased visitation and road mortality; no data on whether proposed pullouts are in boa habitat. [[Acuna Cactus]]: No monitoring program for human visitation to cactus locations. [[Sonoran Pronghorn]]: No analysis of impacts from increased human presence (hiking, camping, vehicle use) in viable pronghorn habitat; population may be as low as 100 individuals in United States with "incredibly high" mortality rates. [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]]: No surveys conducted to determine presence near [[Alamo Canyon Campground]]; area contains viable habitat and parts proposed as critical habitat; no consultation with pygmy owl experts documented. [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]: No analysis of impacts to this endangered neotropical migrant using [[Quitobaquito Springs]]. [[Sonoran Desert Tortoise]]: No analysis of visitation impacts on local population.

**Methodological Deficiencies:**
No methodology section identifies assumptions, defines terms, identifies data sources and models, explains analytical procedures, or establishes significance criteria for determining when baseline changes are "significant." The plan cannot be replicated or independently verified by the public. Statements regarding habitat "restoration to natural conditions" or "revegetation" provide no details on species composition or ecosystem components to assess whether compensatory measures truly offset habitat losses. Net habitat loss calculations are unclear and may not account for indirect and cumulative losses in surrounding areas (Yuma, Tucson, Ajo, Lukeville, Sonoyta).

**Trail System Expansion:**
All three action alternatives propose significant new trail construction: proposed action seeks 8 new trails (8.9 miles); non-selected new ideas alternative seeks 8 new trails (10.1 miles) plus four new hiking routes (37 miles); former action alternative sought 9 new trails (9.9 miles) plus two new hiking routes (13.5 miles). Impact analysis for these trails is cursory with no wildlife impact assessment. The plan vaguely mentions "seasonal or short-term closures" to protect wildlife "whenever necessary" but provides no detail or impact analysis.

**Water Resources:**
Proposed construction of additional housing and facilities at Lukeville and Why will increase water consumption, though the plan asserts this will not be "statistically significant." No data support this conclusion. Water conservation measures are promised but not specified for public review. Cumulative water use increases in surrounding communities (including Sonoyta, Mexico) under NAFTA and increased Monument prestige are not quantified. Groundwater depletion in Mexico's Sonoyta Valley threatens [[Quitobaquito Springs]] aquifer, yet the plan proposes no action to address this through NAFTA or BECC environmental processes.

**EPA Concurrence:**
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Region IX submitted comments (July 10, 1995) that remain unaddressed by the supplement. EPA recommended examining "an alternative that evaluates minimizing use of the Monument and the sensitive areas within the Monument," conducting separate carrying capacity analysis for [[Quitobaquito Springs]], providing baseline data on current conditions and overutilization, and significantly discussing "potential impacts from implementation of the alternatives to these threatened and endangered species."

**Legal Implications:**
[[Defenders of Wildlife]] provides notice of potential legal challenge under NEPA and ESA if concerns are not timely and adequately addressed. The organization argues the plan causes jeopardy to both [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]] and [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]], adversely affecting their chances for recovery, and that the decision to postpone Highway 85 planning "for reasons of political expediency, is arbitrary, capricious and not in accordance with law."
