# Spot-Poisoning Applied to the Massachusetts Lake and Pond Fisheries Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 article by Richard H. Stroud of the [[Massachusetts]] Division of Fisheries and Game describes replacing [[Seining]] with [[Spot-Poisoning]] using [[Rotenone]] for the 1951 Massachusetts lake and pond fisheries survey. The method was adapted from [[Tennessee Valley Authority Reservoirs]] practice, with modifications for small Massachusetts waters: smaller treatment areas, a [[Stop Net]], and cove site selection. Across 96 ponds, spot-poisoning collected more species, more fish, and more small and hard-to-capture fishes than seining. The author also argues that spot-poisoning gives limited but usable data for judging [[Population Balance]] in ponds, while seining does not. Two example ponds are classified as balanced and unbalanced.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of the method:** Spot-poisoning was first applied routinely by Eschmeyer and coworkers in impounded [[Tennessee River]] waters (Chance and Miller 1952). Its original objectives were growth-study materials, quick population indices, and year-to-year trends.
- **Barriers to adoption:** Biologists doubted its reliability against seining. There were fears of public backlash in populated areas and concern about prolonged toxicity.
- **Massachusetts setting:** About 450 public fishing waters averaging 85 acres in surface area (excluding one reservoir of about 25,000 acres). These waters have high transparency, abundant aquatic vegetation, rocks, rubble, trash, and irregular bottoms, so they can be seined only with great difficulty or not at all.
- **Survey tools:** Echo sounding replaced handline sounding (Stroud and Grice 1952). The survey was funded under Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration, Project No. F-3-R, and field work was led by Dr. Britton C. McCabe and Thomas Andrews.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spot-poisoning technique (as modified for Massachusetts):**
- Treatment areas were coves, usually longer than wide with a constricted mouth. Maximum depths rarely exceeded 6 feet, occasionally reaching 10 feet.
- Coves under 0.5 acre gave poor results. The author judged the optimum near 0.75 acre, though he stated this could not be substantiated statistically. Treatment areas ranged from 0.1 to 1.5 acres and averaged 0.5 acre.
- A 12-thread small-mesh seine, 200 feet long and 8 feet deep, served as the stop net. Many adult chain pickerel, yellow perch, and largemouth black bass tried to flee the poisoned area and were retained by the net.
- Rotenone was applied as an emulsifiable form at a target concentration of 0.025 ppm, sprayed from a boat with a 5-gallon hand pump. Summer water temperatures of 70–85°F caused fish to begin dying within minutes.
- Wind-driven mixing diluted the toxin below harmful levels within 4–6 hours. Reinvasion was usually possible the same evening or the next day. Two kills in adjacent coves occurred when offshore winds carried toxic surface water.

**Factors affecting collection:**
- Centrarchids sink quickly after poisoning, making recovery harder in deep or turbid water.
- Poor visibility from turbidity or dense vegetation can reduce pick-up efficiency.
- White perch and adult golden shiners may avoid shoal water, which biases samples.
- Spatial distribution variation was judged the largest source of bias in quantitative estimates.

## Key Findings

**1. Cove versus shoreline sites**
- Coves were treated in 68 ponds and open shoreline in 28 ponds, with average area 0.5 acre in both.
- Coves yielded about 2.4 times as much fish by weight: 32.2 lb/acre versus 13.3 lb/acre for shorelines.

**2. Spot-poisoning versus seining (23 identical ponds, 1951)**
- Largemouth black bass were collected in 61% of ponds by rotenone versus 22% by seining, a near threefold gain in efficiency.
- Brown bullheads were found in about seven times as many ponds, and Johnny darters in five times as many.
- Golden shiners, chain pickerel, and black crappie were found in about twice as many ponds.
- Pumpkinseeds and bluegills appeared in about 36% and 18% more ponds, respectively.
- Yellow perch were found in 25% more ponds, although fewer fish were collected than by seining.
- Redfin pickerel, banded sunfish, bridled shiner, alewife, chubsucker, and common shiner were missed entirely by seining but detected by rotenone.
- White perch, white suckers, and killifish were found in fewer ponds with rotenone. Carp, found in one pond by seining, were not found by rotenone.

**3. Historical comparison (central Massachusetts)**
- The 1944–45 survey (McCabe 1948; 53 lakes and ponds, gill nets and seines) recorded 22 species.
- The 1951 survey (52 lakes and ponds, gill nets and rotenone) recorded 27 species. Seven were added: redfin pickerel, eastern brook trout, banded sunfish, yellowbellies, bridled shiner, spot-tail shiner, and common shiner.
- Fish per pond averaged 103.6 in 1944–45 and 315.5 in 1951. The author attributes part of this increase to improved gill-netting but a substantial share to rotenone.

**4. Size composition (106 ponds, summer 1951)**
- The rotenone samples included 24,981 fish, and the seine samples 4,059 fish.
- Overall, 88% of rotenone-collected fish were under 6 inches, compared with 69% from seining.
- Chain pickerel (74% under 6 inches with rotenone), brown bullheads (95%), golden shiners (97%), and largemouth black bass (86%) were especially well represented among small fish.
- Black crappies, pumpkinseeds, bluegills, and white suckers showed no clear size-distribution difference between methods.

**5. Population balance**
- Straight minnow seines have been challenged for Indiana farm ponds (Krumholz 1951). The author states that both straight and bag seines have "failed rather miserably" in Massachusetts natural and impounded waters.
- Seining cannot reliably indicate reproduction or support the quantitative balance measures suggested by Swingle (1950). Summer spot-poisoning data allow limited assessment.

**6. Yield benchmarks**
- The largest recovery from the 96 spot-poisoned ponds was 70.1 lb/acre, excluding one pond with sea-run alewives that yielded 147.5 lb/acre.
- Best current estimates place average standing populations of Massachusetts ponds at 100 lb/acre or more.

**7. Case study: Ponkapoag Pond, Canton (Norfolk County)**
- A 1.0-acre cove treated in August 1951 yielded 43.3 lb of fish. About 56% by weight were predators, and the rest were pan fish.
- Overnight gill nets yielded 28.0 lb, made up of 38% predators, 28% pan fish, and 24% weed fish.
- About 60% of all fish were game or pan species over 0.25 lb.
- Conclusion: the pond was considered **in balance**. All species were reproducing, the predator ratio was high, and harvestable sizes were available. Yellow perch and bluegill growth was somewhat depressed but better than in most ponds.

**8. Case study: Winneconnet Pond, Norton (Bristol County)**
- A 0.5-acre cove treated in 1951 yielded 27.5 lb of fish, of which 5% were predators, 12% weed species, and 82% pan fish.
- Gill nets weighed 67.0 lb: 13% predators, 22% pan fish, and 65% weed fish.
- About 30% of all fish were desirable game or pan species over 0.25 lb.
- Conclusion: the pond was **out of balance**, with a low predator ratio and low availability of harvestable sizes. Although all species were reproducing, growth of bluegills, yellow perch, and white perch was depressed, and the author's data suggest overcrowding in several species.

**9. Methodological caveats**
- The author states that the 1951 spot-poisoning findings are "quantitatively comparable among ponds" only to the extent a stop net was used, and that the stop net's necessity falls as treatment area size increases.
- Caution is needed because of unassessed factors such as time of day, pond basin shape, and pick-up efficiency.
