# Decision to Destroy Endangered Virgin River Chub Offspring

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A newspaper report describes the decision by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) to destroy about 18,000 hatchery-produced fingerlings of the endangered [[Virgin River Chub]]. The fish were destroyed on Oct. 26, 1989, because USFWS staff judged them genetically deficient relative to the naturally occurring wild population. Only 2,500 fish were saved. The hatchery manager called the decision "biologically right" but "political suicide." The source includes statements from fisheries managers, a university researcher, and a water district official, and it leaves the matter unresolved pending a review of the genetic data.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Virgin River Chub]] is a silvery fish that reaches 8 to 10 inches in length and lives exclusively in the Virgin River. It was placed on the endangered species list in August 1989. The same river also supports the endangered [[Woundfin Minnow]], whose numbers are reported to be declining.
- **Broodstock:** In fall 1988, 162 chubs were collected from the Virgin River drainage (161 adults and one young fish). At the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], 18 males and six females were used to spawn the offspring.
- **Facility:** The Dexter National Fish Hatchery is operated by the USFWS and lies 18 miles south of Roswell, New Mexico.
- **Study background:** [[Wendell Minckley]], an Arizona State University zoology professor, first surveyed the Virgin River in 1963–64 and was hired to produce genetic data on the chub. The Washington County Water Conservancy District (WCWCD) ran its own five-year fish survey, and its former consultant [[Terry Hickman]] conducted a 1984–89 study focused on the chub and woundfin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Quail Creek Reservoir breach:** A breach of the off-river [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] on Jan. 1, 1989 seriously affected both the chub and woundfin populations. The WCWCD study was meant to assess the effects of its water projects, including this reservoir, on native fish.
- **Rotenone treatment:** Before the chub spawning, part of the river was poisoned with [[Rotenone]] by the [[Virgin River Recovery Team]] to eradicate the exotic [[Red Shiner]]. Officials believed the chub offspring could have carried unusual traits that might swamp the gene pool of the existing wild fish.
- **Native population:** Dexter hatchery manager [[Buddy Jensen]] described the wild chub population as "substantially different" from the original naturally occurring population. Hickman reported that the existing native population appears to be in good shape, although woundfin numbers are down.
- **Life history:** Because chubs have a long lifespan, a more genetically representative stock could have been reproduced later, according to Minckley.

## Key Findings

**1. Destruction of hatchery stock**
- Of about 18,000 endangered fingerlings held at the hatchery, 2,500 were saved. The remainder were destroyed on Oct. 26, 1989, and were fed to [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Jensen stated the decision was "biologically right" because the hatchery-produced offspring could have threatened the wild population, but "politically... apparently wrong."

**2. Scientific disagreement over genetic status**
- The USFWS concluded the offspring were "genetically deficient" and "substantially different" from the wild population.
- Minckley advised against restocking on genetic grounds, but his report was still under review by geneticists when the article was written. He stated that he stands by his recommendation not to restock.
- Hickman stated that the chub may have been held as a future restocking resource or as an insurance program for the existing wild population.

**3. Internal USFWS disagreement**
- Jensen said that he and [[Don Archer]] (a former USFWS fisheries biologist who now works for the state Division of Wildlife Resources) agreed not to restock, but that Archer later called back and questioned the decision.
- The regional USFWS office in Albuquerque reportedly told Jensen to do what he thought best. The office has since contradicted this statement.
- Jensen said that [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] fisheries biologist Williamson in Denver also agreed not to restock, and that the Denver office may not have agreed with Archer's position.
- Archer stated that the issue was debatable within the USFWS and that a "good error" would have been made on the side of the fish if the offspring had been stocked. He said he wanted to see the decision in writing before the remaining fish were disposed of.

**4. Restocking capacity and status**
- The hatchery could still produce 100,000 Virgin River Chubs, according to the source.
- Denise Knight, head of the Virgin River Recovery Team and a state non-game fish coordinator, confirmed that Minckley's report was under review by geneticists.
- Jim Brooks, fishery management biologist at the Dexter hatchery, stated that the matter would remain unresolved until Minckley's evaluation is completed.

**5. Concerns about future attention**
- Hickman expressed concern that the Virgin River may no longer receive the attention it once had, since Archer left the USFWS.
- [[Ron Thompson]], manager of the WCWCD, said he was upset when he learned of the killing, and that he had not been notified beforehand.

**6. Outcome**
- The destruction was described as "irreversible" by the hatchery manager. Knight said the fish were destroyed "without being notified" and "under circumstances" that were unfortunate.
- The Virgin River chub remains endangered and the situation is described as a "political nightmare."
