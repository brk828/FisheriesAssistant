# Numbers of Species and Faunal Resemblance of Marine Fishes in California Bays and Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multi-family-fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

Horn and Allen (1976) examined fish species richness and faunal composition in 13 California bays and estuaries and the adjacent [[California Continental Shelf]]. Species number correlated positively with surface area (log-log slope z = 0.21). In stepwise multiple regression, [[Mouth Width]] was the only significant predictor. Presence/absence cluster analysis separated southern and northern bay groups split near [[Point Conception]], and three broad distributional categories of fishes were recognized. The authors frame bays as partially isolated extensions of the shelf within [[Island Biogeography Theory]], and argue the results are relevant to bay preservation and management.

## Background & Description

Bays and estuaries are highly productive feeding and nursery grounds for fishes, but they act as natural sinks for pollutants and are increasingly altered by human activity. The authors cite Murdoch (1975), who called estuaries perhaps the single most endangered ecosystem in the United States.

The California bays and estuaries are partially isolated water bodies of varying size, irregularly spaced along the coastline. Together they hold 51 percent of the 439 fish species recorded from the California continental shelf. The study had three aims:

1. Test whether bays function as insular habitats under the [[MacArthur-Wilson Equilibrium Model]].
2. Assess the relative importance of environmental variables in explaining species numbers.
3. Compare faunal resemblance among bays using a dissimilarity coefficient and cluster analysis.

**Methods (summary):** The base dataset was a list of 224 coastal species in 78 families, excluding midwater larval forms (3 species), introduced species (3 species) and freshwater forms. Coastal species were defined as those mainly found shallower than 400 ft (122 m), following Miller and Lea (1972). Seven environmental variables were measured for each bay: surface area, latitude, distance to nearest bay, diurnal tidal range, mean annual rainfall, mouth width and mean annual coastal temperature. Area-species relationships were fitted with the power function S = CA^z. Stepwise multiple regression used the BMD02R program, with a minimum F of α = 10 for inclusion. Faunal similarity used the [[Canberra Metric Dissimilarity]] index with group-average clustering.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Bays studied (13):** Tijuana Estuary, San Diego Bay, Mission Bay, Los Penasquitos Lagoon, Newport Bay, Anaheim Bay, Alamitos Bay, Morro Bay, Elkhorn Slough, San Francisco Bay, Bolinas Lagoon, Tomales-Bodega Bay and Humboldt Bay. Latitudes span 32.5°N to 40.8°N.
- **Species counts per bay:** Ranged from 22 (Los Penasquitos Lagoon) to 121 (San Francisco Bay). Humboldt Bay (88) and Tomales-Bodega Bay (103) are also high-richness systems.
- **Surface area:** Ranged from 12 ha (Los Penasquitos Lagoon) to 129,555 ha (San Francisco Bay). Humboldt Bay covers 6,478 ha.
- **Mouth width:** Ranged from 0.13 km-scale values in small lagoons to 1,773 m at San Francisco Bay (as tabulated, with the largest bays having the widest mouths).
- **Faunal boundary:** [[Point Conception]] (34.5°N) is a widely recognized temperature-based faunal boundary, consistent with Hubbs (1948, 1960).
- **Alteration:** Dredging, filling, marina and breakwater construction, and pollutant inputs affect bays. San Francisco Bay has lost about 40 percent of its surface area since 1850. Tijuana Estuary is described as relatively pristine.
- **Comparative systems:** [[Chesapeake Bay]] (37°N, the largest U.S. bay) falls near the California area-species curve. [[King Harbor]], an artificial enclosure at Redondo Beach, lies well above it.

## Key Findings

- **Area-species relationship:** S = 1.20A^0.21 (r = 0.93, P < 0.01) for 13 bays plus the shelf. Excluding the shelf gives S = 1.28A^0.18 (r = 0.86, P < 0.01). The low z value fits partially isolated habitats.
- **Environmental predictors:**
  - Linear model: seven variables explained 69% of variation. Mouth width alone explained 53% and was the only significant variable (P < 0.005).
  - Log model with mouth width: 80% of variation explained, with mouth width alone at 77% (P < 0.001).
  - Log model without mouth width: 74% explained, with surface area as the only significant contributor (P < 0.001).
  - Latitude, rainfall and coastal temperature were intercorrelated and not significant. Distance to nearest bay was not significant.
- **Faunal clustering:** Two groups emerged: 7 southern bays (32.5°N–33.8°N) and 6 northern bays (35.4°N–40.8°N). Newport Bay and Anaheim Bay were most closely associated. Humboldt Bay was the most dissimilar northern bay. Morro Bay and Elkhorn Slough formed a central California pair.
- **Distributional categories** (from the two-way table):
  - 37 species widespread across southern and northern bays.
  - 59 species mainly southern (14 of these occur in one to three northern bays).
  - 128 species mainly northern (11 occur in no more than one southern bay).
- **Biogeographic hypothesis:** Of 55 primarily southern species, 25 percent occur in one to three northern bays. Of 128 northern species, only 9 percent occur in no more than one southern bay. This supports the hypothesis that Point Conception is a stronger barrier to southern species.
- **Restricted species:** Of 55 species found in only one bay, 48 occur in one of the four largest bays and none occur in the four smallest. Only five species (topsmelt, bay pipefish, staghorn sculpin, shiner surfperch and arrow goby) occur in all 13 bays.
- **Management implications:** Mouth width should be considered in breakwater and structural design. Bays falling above or below the area-species curve can be flagged for further study or conservation attention. Structural modification may maintain or enhance fish use, but truly estuarine habitats and specialist species may be lost.
- **Limitations:** Species lists vary in completeness, sampling intensity was not quantified, and the study used presence/absence data without accounting for alteration.
