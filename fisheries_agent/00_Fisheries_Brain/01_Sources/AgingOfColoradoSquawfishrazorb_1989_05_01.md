# Aging of Colorado Squawfish & Razorback Sucker by Otolith Examination

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #otolith-aging #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary
This 1989 final report by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University), submitted to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, describes an effort to extend **otolith aging methodology** to two imperiled Colorado River fishes: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), from the **upper Colorado River basin**. Traditional scale-based aging methods had proven unreliable for native lower-basin fishes due to subtropical winter temperatures preventing clear annulus formation. The study validated polished-otolith techniques against **known-age hatchery fish** (Dexter National Fish Hatchery) and applied them to wild-caught specimens supplied by USFWS (Vernal, Utah). Results confirm that otoliths, examined via phase-contrast light microscopy, provide a usable — though imperfect — tool for age estimation, particularly for younger individuals, with growing uncertainty in older, slow-growing fish.

## Background & Description
- **Structures examined**: Otoliths — specifically the *asteriscus* and/or *lapillus* for Colorado squawfish; *lapillus* only for razorback sucker.
- **Sample sizes**:
  - 48 wild-caught Colorado squawfish
  - 10 wild-caught razorback suckers
  - 13 known-age hatchery Colorado squawfish (year-classes 0, I, II, IV, V) used for validation
- **Preparation methods**:
  - Wild-caught fish otoliths: mounted on glass slides in thermoplastic cement, polished on emery paper (per [[McCarthy and Minckley 1987]] methodology).
  - Hatchery fish otoliths: mounted but examined **unpolished**.
- **Microscopy**: Nikon phase-contrast binocular microscope, 40–400X (typically 40–100X); variable light quality/quantity was critical — standard light microscopy was markedly less effective.
- **Annulus structure**: Annuli visible as alternating close-spaced circuli (slow winter growth) and wide-spaced circuli (faster summer growth).
  - In hatchery fish (4–5 years old), summer growth surge occurred **March–April through October–November**.
- **Common preparation issues**: Sections too thick, incorrect orientation/embedding, breakage requiring multiple fragment viewing, and variable technician skill in polishing — cited as sources of inter-investigator disagreement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Both species are native to the **Colorado River Basin**, considered threatened/endangered, with limited baseline biological data (age structure, growth) prior to this and related studies.
- **Growth pattern — Colorado Squawfish**: Slow growth in first 1–2 (sometimes 3) years, followed by 2–3 years of faster growth, then a return to slow, regular growth in older year-classes.
- **Growth pattern — Razorback Sucker**: Rapid growth for first 3–5 years reaching ~half of maximum size (~35 cm upper basin; ~70 cm TL lower basin), followed by markedly slower growth (<1 cm/year) for remainder of life.
- **Habitat/Dam-related recruitment signal**: Age data revealed a strong year-class of razorback suckers (mean age 25.5 years, i.e., hatched ~1962) coinciding with the closure of **Flaming Gorge Dam**. A parallel pattern was noted in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) tied to **Davis Dam** closure, suggesting a pulse of reproduction/recruitment following dam impoundment before establishment of non-native predatory/competitive species.
- **Maximum longevity**: Razorback sucker maximum age estimated at 40–50 years, consistent between upper and lower basin populations.
- Related validated species using similar otolith methodology: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*).

## Key Findings
1. **Validation success**: All 13 known-age hatchery Colorado squawfish were aged precisely and consistently via unpolished otolith annuli, supporting the reliability of the technique up to 5 years of age.
2. **Inter-investigator variability**: Three independent investigators showed generally similar mean age estimates for wild squawfish (Table 1: means ranging 6.3–7.7 years) but individual-level disagreements of 2+ years were common (Table 2), largely due to inconsistent treatment of the "edge" as a potential annulus and preparation quality issues (thickness, orientation, breakage).
3. **Younger fish easier to concord on**; disagreement most often hinged on presence/absence of the **first-year (inner) annulus**; re-setting/repolishing frequently resolved this.
4. **Older fish paradoxically more reliable**: Larger otoliths from older squawfish were easier to mount and polish uniformly.
5. **Razorback sucker age estimates were more consistent** across investigators than squawfish (Table 3: means 25.2–28.7 years), and ages skewed markedly older overall.
6. **Dam-closure year-class signal**: A cohort of razorback suckers aged ~24–27 years matched the 1962 closure of Flaming Gorge Dam, mirroring known post-impoundment recruitment pulses documented at Lake Mohave.
7. **Growth-rate/age difficulty**: Markedly slow growth in older individuals of both species compresses circuli/annuli spacing, making precise aging of older fish difficult; the authors recommend **scanning electron microscopy** for higher accuracy in future work.
8. **Methodological recommendations**:
   - Use a single, well-trained technician for otolith mounting/polishing to reduce variability.
   - Avoid mounting-medium bubbles, overheating/darkening, and polishing-induced heat damage.
   - Otolith breakage linked to storage dryness and rushed handling caused sample loss.
9. **Unresolved needs**: Otolith-size to body-size relationships remain unestablished for both species, limiting back-calculation of length-at-age (previously attempted for razorback sucker by [[McCarthy and Minckley 1987]]).
10. **Future validation priority**: Recommends continued validation via re-introduced/tagged fish recovered after known time intervals in the wild, as both species are subjects of active reintroduction efforts in the lower Colorado basin.
