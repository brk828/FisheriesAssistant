# The Razorback Sucker, Xyrauchen texanus, in the Upper Colorado River Basin, 1974-76

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #catostomidae #endangered-species #colorado-river-basin

## Executive Summary

This technical paper documents the distribution, abundance, life history, and reproductive ecology of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] during 1974-76. The species, recommended for "threatened" status, was found in relatively large concentrations at two restricted locations: the mouth of the [[Yampa River]] at [[Echo Park]] and a flooded gravel pit near [[Grand Junction]], Colorado (Walker Wildlife Area). The study employed trammel nets, electrofishing, seining, and ultrasonic telemetry to track movements and spawning behavior. Razorback suckers spawned in April-May at water temperatures of 6-15°C over gravel bars. Estimated ages ranged from 4-9 years (all mature), with fish from the Colorado River significantly larger than those from the Green and Yampa rivers, likely due to warmer water temperatures. The decline of this endemic species correlates strongly with dam construction and river alterations throughout the basin. Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) was documented, and reproductive failure appears to be a major factor limiting population recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is one of four endemic [[Colorado River]] fishes that have declined dramatically due to anthropogenic alterations of the river system. The species historically ranged throughout the lower and upper Colorado River basin (Fig. 2 in source), but by the 1970s had been eliminated from most of the lower basin and the [[Gila River]] drainage. Early observers reported razorback suckers in large numbers; Everman (1916) found them "rather abundant" in the [[Salton Sea]] after its formation in 1905, and Chamberlain (1904) reported them "rather common" in the [[San Pedro River]].

The species is characterized by a distinctive bony nape (dorsal ridge). Mature males exhibit a curved last ray on the anal fin, while females have a straight ray. The razorback sucker possesses long gill rakers, suggesting adaptation for plankton filtering (Hubbs and Miller 1953). Adults collected during this study ranged from 461-622 mm total length and weighed 990-1,570 g.

The [[Colorado River Basin]] has been extensively modified by dam construction for hydroelectric power and irrigation. These alterations have resulted in: (1) decreased seasonal streamflow fluctuations and increased daily fluctuations, (2) altered water temperatures (cooler summers, warmer winters below dams), and (3) reduced turbidity. The [[Yampa River]] remains the largest free-flowing river in the basin and plays a vital role in maintaining suitable habitat for native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Characteristics:**
The primary study sites differed substantially in physical characteristics. [[Echo Park]] (station 3) featured narrow canyons with deep, fast water, rocky substrate (boulders to 20 m diameter), and quiet eddies. The [[Walker Wildlife Area]] gravel pit (station 8) covered 25.6 ha at 0.5-2.0 m depth, with gravel bottom covered by thick silt layers and cobble-lined shores. The pit connected permanently to the [[Colorado River]] via a 6-m canal.

**Water Conditions:**
The region is arid with 25 cm average annual precipitation. Air temperatures range from -25°C (winter) to 40°C (summer). Water temperatures range from 0°C to 30°C. Spawning occurred at 6-15°C water temperatures during April-May. Turbidities during spawning ranged from 350-2,000 Jackson turbidity units (JTU).

**Fish Community Composition:**
Native fish comprised 73% of the catch at [[Echo Park]] but only 43% at [[Walker Wildlife Area]] (Table 4). [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) dominated collections, occurring 7 times more frequently than razorback suckers in trammel nets and 5 times more in electrofishing samples. Other common native species included [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. discobolus*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). Introduced species at Walker Wildlife Area included [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*), which together composed nearly one-third of the catch.

**Spawning Habitat:**
Spawning sites featured gravel bars with cobble substrate (20-50 mm diameter stones), water depths of approximately 1 m, and water velocities around 1 m/s. The primary spawning bar in the [[Yampa River]] was located 400 m upstream from the river mouth. Fourteen razorback suckers (2 females, 12 males) were captured over this bar during a 2-week period in May 1975.

**Food Habits:**
Limited stomach content data from other studies indicated razorback suckers consume midge larvae, algae, plant debris, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, and larval Diptera. In lower basin reservoirs, the species consumed large amounts of plankton. The long gill rakers and preference for quiet backwater areas (where plankton is densest) support the hypothesis of planktivorous feeding.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and Abundance:**
- Razorback suckers were most common at two locations: mouth of [[Yampa River]] at [[Echo Park]] (station 3) during early spring, and [[Colorado River]] at [[Walker Wildlife Area]] (station 8) throughout the year
- Total of 107 razorback suckers collected during study period (Table 1)
- At Echo Park, razorback suckers composed 3.0-4.3% of trammel net catches in spring and fall
- At Walker Wildlife Area, razorback suckers totaled 0.8-4.2% of catches
- Species absent from upper [[Yampa River]] stations (Lily Park, Castle Park) despite extensive sampling
- Occasional individuals found at Island Park and Sand Wash on [[Green River]]
- Historical range drastically reduced; species eliminated from entire [[Gila River]] arm of lower basin and most lower basin locations
- Lakes Havasu, Mead, and Mojave still contain razorback suckers, but only mature fish found, indicating reproductive failure

**Age and Growth:**
- Estimated ages based on scale analysis ranged from 4-9 completed growing seasons
- All collected fish were mature adults
- Scale reading difficult due to indistinct annuli and closely spaced circuli
- Age validation not possible; no young-of-year or juvenile fish collected
- Fish from [[Colorado River]] significantly larger than same-age fish from [[Green River|Green]] and [[Yampa River|Yampa]] rivers (P < 0.10 for males, P < 0.01 for females)
- Females significantly larger than males of same age in both river systems
- Faster growth in Colorado River attributed to higher water temperatures
- One recaptured fish showed no growth over 1.5 years; another grew only 8 mm in 3.5 years
- Otolith analysis from California specimens suggested much greater longevity (17-22 years) than scale-based estimates

**Length-Weight Relationships:**
- Logarithmic equation: Log W = 2.31 Log TL - 4.12 (Colorado River, sexes combined)
- No significant difference between sexes or between river systems in length-weight relations
- Colorado River fish tended to be longer and heavier than Yampa River fish of same estimated age
- Average lengths for age-5 fish: Colorado River females 571 mm (range 538-595), males 522 mm; Yampa/Green River females 510 mm (498-561), males 483 mm (442-514)

**Reproduction:**
- Spawning occurred April-May at water temperatures 6-15°C
- Estimated fecundity ranged from 24,490 to 76,576 eggs (mean 46,740 ± 19,201 SD)
- Fecundity showed no significant correlation with total length or weight
- Sampling error in fecundity estimates only 3-4% based on actual counts
- Primary spawning site: gravel bar in [[Yampa River]], 400 m upstream from mouth
- Spawning substrate: predominantly cobble (20-50 mm diameter)
- Water depth at spawning sites: approximately 1 m
- Water velocity: approximately 1 m/s (0.89-0.95 m/s measured)
- Spawning behavior similar to that described by Douglas (1952) and Jonez and Sumner (1954) in lower basin reservoirs
- Possible spawning attempted in Walker Wildlife Area gravel pit (2 ripe females, 5 ripe males captured together)
- No evidence of successful reproduction in gravel pit, possibly due to predation by introduced species
- Apparent reproductive failure throughout upper basin; few or no young-of-year collected
- Holden (1978) collected two juvenile catostomids tentatively identified as razorback suckers in summer 1977

**Movement Patterns:**
- Ultrasonic telemetry used on 8 fish (1 female, 7 males); effective range limited to 10-150 m depending on water conditions
- Fish in Walker Wildlife Area gravel pit most active late evening (1900-2200) and late morning (1000-1200)
- Fish remained in deeper water (3 m) during most of day, moved to shallow areas early morning (0100-0600)
- Five males tracked in [[Yampa River]] moved no farther than 1 km upstream before returning to mouth
- One fish detected on gravel bar at Green-Yampa confluence for 4 hours in water 0.3 m deep, velocity 0.42 m/s
- Anchor tag recaptures (11 of 98 tagged fish):
  - One female moved 21 km upstream from Island Park to Yampa River mouth in 2 weeks (probable spawning migration)
  - One fish moved 26 km upstream from Walker Wildlife Area, recaptured in flooded backwater with 40 other razorback suckers
  - One fish moved 130 km upstream over 3.5 years (tagged December 1974 at Sand Wash, recaptured April 1978 above Horseshoe Bend)
- Eight recaptures at Walker Wildlife Area indicated tendency to remain in quiet water for extended periods
- Vanicek (1967) recaptured 4 of 13 tagged fish; none moved more than 1.6 km, all movement downstream
- Fish apparently moved into Echo Park area in late fall, remained through winter, joined by additional fish in late April
- Fish left Yampa River mouth in late spring/early summer as streamflow decreased and temperature increased

**Hybridization:**
- Three catostomid hybrids reported from study area: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] × razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker × [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*), bluehead sucker × white sucker
- Eight flannelmouth × razorback hybrids collected during study (3 from Walker Wildlife Area, 5 from Echo Park)
- Holden (1973) collected 40 specimens of this hybrid between 1968-1972
- Holden and Stalnaker (1975b) found 75% as many hybrids as razorback suckers, suggesting increased hybridization incidence
- Spawning razorback and flannelmouth suckers collected together from same habitat, facilitating hybridization
- Younger hybrids difficult to identify due to resemblance to flannelmouth sucker
- Large hybrids readily identifiable by small dorsal ridge and intermediate lip structure
- White sucker (introduced species) responsible for two other hybrid combinations
- Only one bluehead × flannelmouth hybrid reported historically (Hubbs et al. 1943); none collected recently

**Conservation Implications:**
- [[Yampa River]] plays major role in continued survival of razorback sucker and other native fishes
- Yampa River partly restores historical water conditions in [[Green River]] below confluence
- About 22% of Colorado River basin length inundated by reservoirs
- Four rare native fish (Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, humpback chub, bonytail chub) eliminated from Green River above Yampa confluence after Flaming Gorge Dam closure
- Alterations to Yampa River temperature regime could be detrimental to threatened/endangered fishes
- Green River from Yampa confluence to Canyonlands National Park contains most remaining large-river habitat in Colorado basin
- Reproductive failure appears major factor in species decline, but specific causes unclear
- Closely related [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] with similar spawning requirements remains abundant, complicating understanding of razorback sucker decline
- Species recommended for "threatened" status under Endangered Species Act
- Introduction of exotic species and competition for food/space may contribute to decline
- Water development for energy resource extraction (oil, oil shale, coal, uranium) threatens remaining habitat quality and quantity
