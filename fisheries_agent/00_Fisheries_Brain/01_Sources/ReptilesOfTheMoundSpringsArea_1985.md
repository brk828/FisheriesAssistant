## Executive Summary

This chapter reports a herpetological survey conducted from 30 September–7 October 1978 across the mound spring complexes near [[Lake Eyre]] region of South Australia, including [[Coward Springs]], [[Blanche Cup]], [[The Bubbler]], [[Hermit Hill Springs]], [[Strangways Springs]], [[Beresford Hill]], and associated artesian bores and sand dunes. Survey methods included hand capture, burrow excavation, overturning of rocks/logs/railway sleepers, pitfall and pitfall/drift-fence trapping, and spotlighting. No frog or tadpole species were detected in any mound spring, bore, dam, or roadside pool surveyed, attributed to high salinity of spring and bore waters. Twenty-two reptile species were recorded (21 lizards, 1 snake), with two additional snake species reported anecdotally by a local station manager. The key ecological finding is that, unlike vegetation and avifauna, reptile species composition and population density in the study area are largely **unaffected by the presence of free water** from springs and bores; instead, the exposed limestone mound structure itself provides critical rock-refuge habitat.

## Background & Description

While this is a faunal survey rather than a morphological study, several species-specific ecological/behavioral traits relevant to habitat use are noted:

- **[[Heteronotia Binoei]]** (Prickly Gecko) — diurnal, insectivorous heliotherm; requires basking; common under flat rocks on mound surfaces.
- **[[Cryptoblepharus Plagiocephalus]]** (Snake-Eyed Skink) — insectivorous heliotherm; co-occurs with *H. binoei* on rocky mounds.
- **[[Gehyra Variegata]]** — thigmothermic, nocturnal gecko; shelters under stones/shrubs and in man-made structures (railway sleepers, ruins); forms communal egg-laying sites.
- **[[Egernia Stokesi]]** (Gidgee Skink) — rock-dwelling species; detected only via sloughed skin evidence, not live capture.
- **[[Delma Australis]]** (a legless lizard, Pygopodidae) — found via live specimen and sloughed skin under stones near shrubs.
- **[[Tympanocryptis Intima]]** and **[[Tympanocryptis Tetraporophora]]** (Earless Dragons) — adapted to barren, sparsely vegetated substrate away from water outflows.
- **[[Trachydosaurus Rugosus]]** (Stumpy-tailed Skink) — favours open plains; tracks observed in sand at hill bases.
- **[[Ctenotus Brooksi]]** (Striped Skink) — basks near shrubs on sand mounds, burrows beneath them for thermal/predator refuge.
- **[[Varanus Gouldii]]** (Sand Goanna) — inferred from large burrows in samphire-vegetated sandy areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recent mound springs (Coward Spring, Blanche Cup, The Bubbler):** Rocky mound surfaces support dense heliothermic lizard assemblages (*H. binoei*, *C. plagiocephalus*, *G. variegata*). Vegetated sand hummocks at mound bases (dominated by shrubs) shelter burrowing species such as *[[Morethia Adelaidensis]]* and *[[Amphibolurus Pictus]]* (Painted Dragon). Notably, **no reptile species showed specific association with the free water itself** — habitat selection tracked rock cover and vegetation, not moisture.
- **Hermit Hill Springs & Finnis Swamp:** Briefly surveyed; species diversity likely underrepresented due to poor weather during survey. *Ctenotus* sp. and *Egernia stokesi* sloughed skin recorded at Finnis Swamp claypans; no reptiles found on bare claypan.
- **Strangways Springs:** Extensive rocky outcrops but comparatively **low lizard density**; communal *G. variegata* egg-sites documented; *[[Lerista Muelleri]]* collected.
- **Degraded spring (NW of Beresford Hill):** Heavy stock trampling degraded habitat, yet *G. variegata* persisted, sheltering within a railway sleeper — indicating resilience/opportunistic use of anthropogenic microhabitat.
- **Extinct mound springs (Hamilton Hill, Near Kewson Hill, Beresford Hill):** Mesa-like relict hills with steep rocky flanks and basal sand dunes support the richest rocky-habitat assemblage, including the nocturnal **[[Phyllurus Millii]]** (Thick-tailed Gecko), absent from active spring mounds in this dataset.
- **Artesian bores (Coward Bore, Welcome Bore):** Despite large swamps and waterfowl populations, bores exert **minimal influence on reptile fauna**; only isolated incidental records (*Trachydosaurus rugosus* at Coward Bore; *Varanus gouldii*, *H. binoei*, *T. tetraporophora* at Welcome Bore), with sightings apparently unrelated to bore presence.
- **Sand dunes (~8 km east of Curdimurka):** Red sand dunes with bluebush and *Zygochloa paradoxa* proved the **most productive site** for pitfall/drift-fence trapping (30 traps, 300 m fencing, 3 days/nights), yielding 10 species and increasing overall regional species diversity, though no rare/unusual taxa were captured here.
- **Anthropogenic habitats:** Abandoned buildings, ruins, and railway sleepers at Beresford, Margaret, and elsewhere provide substitute refuge for *H. binoei*, *Morethia adelaidensis*, *Gehyra variegata*, and even *[[Pseudonaja Nuchalis]]* (Western Brown Snake).

## Key Findings

- **22 reptile species** documented directly (21 lizard + 1 snake species), spanning families Gekkonidae (5 spp.), Pygopodidae (1 sp.), Varanidae (1 sp.), Agamidae (5 spp.), Scincidae (9 spp.), Elapidae (1 sp.); two further snake species (Boidae: *[[Morelia Spilotes]]*; Elapidae: *[[Pseudechis Australis]]*) reported anecdotally but not directly observed by surveyors.
- **Complete absence of amphibians** (frogs/tadpoles) across all surveyed mound springs, bores, dams, and roadside pools, attributed to excessive salinity.
- **Free water availability has negligible effect on reptile species composition or density** — a striking contrast to plant and bird communities in the same mound spring ecosystem, which are strongly water-dependent.
- **Limestone mound structure (not water) is the primary ecological driver** for reptiles, providing critical rocky refugia for heliothermic and thigmothermic species.
- **Habitat degradation from stock grazing** reduces but does not eliminate reptile presence, with resilient species (*G. variegata*) persisting in anthropogenic microhabitats (railway sleepers, ruins).
- **Sand dune habitat outside the mounds** contributes disproportionately to regional species richness via pitfall trapping, despite lacking spring influence.
- Several species show clear microhabitat specialization: rock-obligate (*Phyllurus millii*, *Egernia stokesi*), sand/shrub-burrowing (*Ctenotus brooksi*, *Amphibolurus pictus*, *Morethia adelaidensis*), and open-plain generalists (*Trachydosaurus rugosus*, *Amphibolurus vitticeps* — the latter recorded as ubiquitous across all habitat types surveyed).
- Overall conclusion: mound springs influence the region's [[Reptile Fauna]] indirectly through geomorphology (exposed limestone/rock cover) rather than through hydrology, distinguishing reptiles ecologically from other taxonomic groups dependent on the springs' aquatic resources.
