# Fish Remains from the Century Ranch Site LAn-229, Los Angeles County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zooarchaeology #fish-remains

## Executive Summary

This appendix by W. I. Follett reports the identified fish remains from [[LAn-229]], a [[Chumash]] archaeological site on the [[Century Ranch Sites]] in western Los Angeles County. The collection of 574 specimens, mostly vertebrae in fragmentary condition, yielded at least 19 species in 18 genera and 13 families. The assemblage is interpreted as evidence of both inshore and offshore fishing, including pelagic tunas captured far from land, using canoes. The report was published as an appendix to the wider site report by King, Blackburn and Chandonet.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The report is a reprint from the *Archaeological Survey Annual Report 1968* (Vol. 10, University of California, Los Angeles), published August 1, 1969. It accompanies the site report on the three [[Century Ranch Sites]] (LAn-225, LAn-227 and LAn-229). The specimens are deposited at the Department of Anthropology, University of California, Los Angeles, under Accession Number 351.

**Site setting.** LAn-229 lies in a small, shallow valley on the inland side of the [[Santa Monica Mountains]], on the edge of [[Las Virgines Creek]] where a large spring once flowed into the stream. The site is a dark brown adobe midden about three feet deep with numerous flecks of shell. It is roughly 1000 feet north of [[LAn-227]].

**Dating.** LAn-229 is tentatively dated to approximately 1550 A.D. to 1800 A.D. LAn-227 is dated to approximately 500 A.D. to 1300 A.D.

**Methods.** Identifications rest on vertebrae and other skeletal elements, with the elasmobranch centrum as the most persistent element. Comparisons draw on earlier Follett studies and on regional fish references (e.g., Phillips 1957; Roedel 1953; Fitch 1958). Vertebral counts by depth level (0–6 to 48–51 in.) were used to infer fish size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Assemblage composition.** Of 574 specimens, 541 are vertebrae. Of these, 292 are identified (287 to species, 5 to genus only), and the remainder are too fragmentary for identification. The table of distribution by level indicates a total of 574 for LAn-229 and 268 for LAn-227.

**Habitat groupings.** The author sorts the species by habitat:

- *Pelagic and offshore:* [[Albacore]], [[Oceanic Skipjack]], and deep-water [[Rockfishes]] (such as bocaccio) occur far from shore. The blue shark (Prionace glauca) was found at LAn-227 but not LAn-229.
- *Near-shore:* [[Mako Shark]], [[Pacific Barracuda]], [[Yellowtail]], [[Pacific Mackerel]] and [[White Seabass]] were found at both sites. Narrowtooth shark and Pacific jackmackerel occur at LAn-229 only; kelp bass and California bonito occur at LAn-227 and Arroyo Sequit, not LAn-229.
- *Estuarine and sandy-shore:* [[Leopard Shark]], [[Pacific Angel Shark]], [[Shovelnose Guitarfish]] and [[California Halibut]] were found at both sites. Bat stingray, Pacific sardine and black croaker occur at LAn-229 only. Soupfin shark was found at LAn-227 only.
- *Rocky-shore:* [[California Sheephead]] occurs at both sites; black rockfish was found only at LAn-229.

**Kelp beds.** The author notes that the [[Kelp Beds]] and adjacent waters provide habitat where most of the near-shore species are found.

**Seasonality.** Yellowtail occurred in the Chumash region mainly from May to October. Skipjack and albacore occurred mainly from July to October (Landberg 1965).

## Key Findings

- **Assemblage size:** 574 specimens (541 vertebrae); 292 identified, 287 to species and 5 to genus; at least 19 species, 18 genera, 13 families.
- **Largest families by taxon count:** Carcharhinidae (leopard shark, narrowtooth shark), Scombridae (Pacific mackerel, albacore, skipjack), Scorpaenidae (rockfishes), Carangidae and Sciaenidae.
- **Most abundant identified taxa (counts reported in text):** Leopard shark (66 centra), Pacific angel shark (63 centra), shovelnose guitarfish (67 centra), albacore (12 vertebrae), and Pacific mackerel (13 vertebrae).
- **Size estimates:** Sharks and rays range from about 103 to 150 cm total length; the largest bony fish estimates include yellowtail (about 1 m) and white seabass (about 105 and 124 cm). Smaller species include Pacific sardine (about 25 cm).
- **Offshore fishing evidence:** Remains of albacore and oceanic skipjack, and of deep-water rockfishes, indicate Chumash fishing far from land. The author concludes that the tunalike remains "point unquestionably to the canoes of the Chumash."
- **Inshore emphasis at LAn-229:** About twice as many individual fishes were represented at LAn-229 as at LAn-227. LAn-229 has many more leopard shark, Pacific angel shark and shovelnose guitarfish remains, which the author links to simple handline fishing from shore, possibly near estuary mouths. Fewer yellowtail and albacore remains and no California bonito support this interpretation.
- **Fishing technology:** The Chumash used hook and line, basket, net and harpoon from frameless plank canoes for offshore fishing; hook, basket and net from canoes for estuaries; and handline from sandy beaches for surf fishing (Craig 1967).
- **Comparative sites:** Similar faunas are reported from [[Arroyo Sequit Shellmound]] (LAn-52), [[Conejo Rock Shelter]] (Ven-69) and Drakes Bay sites.
- **Sex-named species:** The Pacific sardine was called *sex* by the Chumash, and the yellowtail may correspond to *gaja* in the Ventureño language (Craig 1967).
