# An Experimental Gill Net Based on the Principle of the Latin Square

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-sampling

## Executive Summary

Houser and Ghent (1964), of the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] and the University of Oklahoma, describe a new experimental [[Gill Net]] built on the [[Latin Square]] principle from experimental design. Conventional gill nets place each mesh size in a single block of webbing, separating the smallest and largest meshes by about 100 feet. The authors instead arrange six mesh sizes in an 18-cell (3 row by 6 column) grid so that each mesh appears once per row and once in each paired column. In a seven-day field test in a cove of [[Lake Texoma]], the latin-square net caught fewer fish per unit of effort than six conventional nets, but its species composition and size-class distributions did not differ significantly from those of the conventional nets. The authors conclude that the net is suitable for small-habitat ecological sampling, despite high construction labor.

## Background & Description

Gill nets remained the most widely used sampling gear for fish populations, although their selectivity was well recognized. Factors cited as affecting selectivity include fish movement, nonrandom distribution, spines, mesh size, season, and time of day (Moyle, 1950; Carlander, 1953). Standard Oklahoma experimental nets used six 25-foot sections of ⅝-, 1-, 1¾-, 2-, 2½-, and 3-inch square mesh, so the largest and smallest meshes sat roughly 100 feet apart and often fished different depths. Sampling narrow streams was especially difficult, because the net had to be zigzagged across the channel to fish all meshes at once.

The latin-square net used the same six meshes. A square block design would have required a 12-foot minimum depth, which was judged excessive, so the authors adopted a 6-foot depth and a rectangular 18-cell layout. Each cell was 2 feet square. The net was built from 8 blocks joined into a single 96-foot by 6-foot net, using size 120 braided nylon rope for lead and float lines and number 9 nylon twine for cell margins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The test was conducted in a large cove of [[Lake Texoma]] on June 15, 1961, under fair weather, with the authors assuming that environmental conditions stayed constant. The seven nets were set in a radial pattern around a central ring and rotated counterclockwise daily so that each net fished every position. The authors designed the test to be as homogeneous as possible, and they note that first-day captures could have removed local populations of territorial species. Their interpretation of the catch-rate difference emphasized behavioral factors, including the greater visibility of the patchy latin-square surface and repeated contacts by fish moving in a consistent direction. Bluegill, which prefer weedy, shadowy habitat, were overrepresented in the latin-square net, which the authors suggest may reflect attraction to its patchy surface.

## Key Findings

- **Catch rate:** The latin-square net caught 72 fish (one per 8.00 ft²) against 633 fish (one per 5.45 ft²) for the six conventional nets, or 68.2% of the conventional catch rate per unit effort. Expressed per 12- by 6-foot block, this was 9.0 versus 13.2 fish over seven days. Gar (42 conventional, 2 latin-square) were excluded from analysis.
- **Catch-rate difference:** Fitting observed catches to a 6:1 ratio gave χ² = 9.54 (1 df), p ≈ 0.002. The authors state that the disparity was consistent across meshes and days, though the mesh-specific differences themselves were not significantly different (χ² = 5.60, 4 df).
- **Species composition:** Tests of species frequencies gave χ² = 9.54 (6 df, 10–20% chance) and a contingency χ² = 8.45 (6 df, 20–30% chance). Neither showed significant difference. Kendall's Tau for species rank order was τ = +0.7380 (z = +4.16, p ≈ 1 in 50,000), indicating strong similarity. About 87% of pairwise comparisons were ordered the same way, and the proportion of positive scores was 135/148 (91%).
- **Species representation:** Of 21 species recorded, 6 were taken only in the conventional nets (e.g., channel catfish, at 1.7% of conventional captures). All species making up at least 2% of conventional captures were also caught in the latin-square net. Bluegill contributed almost half the chi-square and 6 of 13 negative Tau scores.
- **Size-class distribution:** Mean total length was 9.79 inches (conventional) and 9.76 inches (latin-square), a difference of 0.03 inch with no significance. Using five 3-inch groupings plus a 17-inch-and-above class, χ² = 9.84 (5 df, p ≈ 0.09). Size-class Tau was +0.6798 (z = +4.04; 156 of 180 pairs correctly ordered, 86.5%).
- **Construction and practicality:** Hand construction was labor-intensive and was identified as the net's worst feature. Cost was expected to be relatively high. The net suffered less damage than conventional nets, and individual damaged cells could be replaced.
- **Conclusions and planned use:** The authors judged the latin-square net to contain no emergent biases beyond those already present in conventional gill netting. They planned to use it for small-habitat studies, where it fishes a piece of every mesh size simultaneously in each niche. They also proposed true latin-square designs for depth-distribution studies in larger waters. Further work was recommended to test whether analysis-of-variance methods from field-plot studies apply to fish-sampling data.
