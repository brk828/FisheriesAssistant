# Commercial Freshwater Fisheries of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 reprint from the *California Fish and Game* journal, by Sterling P. Davis of the Marine Resources Operations unit of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], describes the commercial harvest of seven freshwater "rough fish" species in California. It covers the species involved, the history of their introduction and landings, the principal fishing areas, gear and methods, fishing seasons, market channels and 1960–1961 landing and price data. The fishery is dominated by [[Common Carp]] and [[Sacramento Blackfish]], with most of the catch coming from lakes and reservoirs rather than rivers.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Seven freshwater species are taken commercially (Table 1). Five are native to California: [[Hardhead]], [[Hitch]], [[Sacramento Blackfish]], [[Splittail]] and [[Western Sucker]]. Two were introduced: [[Common Carp]] and [[Bigmouth Buffalo]].
- **Families:** Carp, hardhead, hitch, blackfish and splittail belong to the minnow family Cyprinidae. Bigmouth buffalo and western sucker belong to the sucker family Catostomidae.
- **Carp history:** Carp were first introduced from Holstein, Germany, in 1812, when five individuals were placed in private ponds in Sonoma Valley. By 1872 they were promoted as food fish, and ten years after introduction their market price had fallen to 1.5 cents per pound.
- **Bigmouth buffalo history:** The source of introduction is uncertain. The first bigmouth buffalo were noted around 1942 in the San Fernando Reservoir of the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] system. By 1950 they were reported in 11 reservoirs of that system. The source suggests the Roosevelt Dam Impoundment in Arizona as a possible origin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Lakes and reservoirs produced 91 percent of 1960 commercial rough-fish landings. Rivers and irrigation canals supplied the remainder.
- **Key waters:**
  - [[Clear Lake]] (Lake County) is the only significant northern California producer.
  - The [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] and the Sacramento River near Rio Vista supply carp and splittail.
  - The [[San Joaquin Valley]] (Stanislaus, San Joaquin and Merced Counties) accounted for 34 percent of 1960 landings.
  - Southern California reservoirs, including Bouquet, Chatsworth, Fairmont, San Fernando and Haiwee, yield small amounts of carp, bigmouth buffalo and western sucker.
- **Seasonal behavior:** Blackfish feed in shallow water near shore from October to April. In the rest of the year they are believed to move to deeper, cooler water. Carp become semi-dormant in December and January as water temperatures drop, making them less susceptible to trapping.
- **Management context:** The source describes these species as "harmful to sport species" and notes that seining is permitted to remove them. Sport fish caught incidentally are returned to the water or transferred to more desirable waters.

## Key Findings

**Landings and value (1960)**
- Total statewide landings were 494,706 pounds, worth about $55,000 to fishermen (Table 2). Lakes supplied 449,784 pounds and rivers 44,922 pounds.
- Clear Lake contributed 279,835 pounds of the statewide total.
- Carp were first by weight at 318,599 pounds (64 percent) but only 28 percent of value.
- Sacramento blackfish from Clear Lake were 145,010 pounds (29 percent of weight) but 59 percent of value.
- *Source note:* The Table 2 value column does not sum to the stated total of $54,988. The individual values listed add to a larger figure. The reported percentages are used in the text above as given.

**Historical landings**
- Landings fell after World War I and were fairly stable from 1922 to 1931, with the Delta as the main producer.
- A rise in 1941–1942 came from Los Angeles-area netting, which took 211,766 pounds of carp.
- Landings peaked at over one million pounds in 1948, when fishing resumed at Clear Lake and started at [[Lake Almanor]] (Plumas County).
- From 1950 to 1955 Lake Almanor provided most of the catch, when a fishmeal company was permitted to seine carp for animal feed. Clear Lake resumed leadership after operations there ceased in 1955.

**Gear and methods (1961)**
- [[Shore Seine Fishing]] took 394,988 pounds (90 percent of catch). Three special permits were issued. Seines range from 300 to 1,200 feet and are towed by boats, shore crews or truck-mounted winches.
- [[Carp Trap]] fishing took 42,801 pounds of carp (10 percent). Traps measure about 5 feet across at the bottom hoop and 8 feet at the top, cost about $12 to build, and are baited with 5 to 6 pounds of rolled barley. One trap in a new area averaged 150 to 200 pounds per two-night set. The top opening lets down-feeding carp enter while excluding most other species.
- [[Hook and Line Fishing]] took 765 pounds of carp and splittail (under 1 percent).

**Fishing seasons**
- Blackfish: best from October to April. An average of 12,000 pounds of Clear Lake blackfish reached markets monthly through the winter.
- Carp: most readily trapped from August through November.
- Hook and line: no clear seasonal pattern; catch depends more on fishermen than on season.

**Markets and utilization**
- [[Live Fish Transport]]: Blackfish, hardhead, hitch, splittail and small silver carp are trucked alive to San Francisco's Chinatown and held in aquariums. Chinese buyers pay up to 80 cents per pound for live fish, which they consider similar to favored native species. Large goldfish and silver carp are also prized as good-luck symbols.
- Carp are sold in three size classes: large (10 to 20 lb), medium (5 to 7 lb) and small (3 to 5 lb).
- Jewish buyers, mainly in Los Angeles, prefer large carp and suckers for gefilte fish.
- Black consumers of carp and buffalo paid 25 to 30 cents per pound. Fish were packed 200 pounds per box, iced and shipped to several cities.
- About 100,000 pounds of rough fish, mostly carp, are sold for pet food each year at about 1 cent per pound.

**Regulatory and ecological notes**
- Permitted seining requires a commercial license, boat registration and a schedule of operations, with warden supervision to protect incidental sport fish.
- Live fish transport uses aerated tanks, circulating pumps and agitators to keep fish alive during transport.
