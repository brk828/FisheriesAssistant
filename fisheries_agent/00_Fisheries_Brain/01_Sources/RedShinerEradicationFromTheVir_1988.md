## Executive Summary

This 1988 report by W. L. Minckley documents the background, planning failures, and ecological fallout of a rotenone-based eradication campaign targeting the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) in the Virgin River, Arizona-Nevada-Utah. The Virgin River, a desert tributary of the [[Colorado River Basin]] draining into [[Lake Mead]], supports a unique and ancient native fish fauna including the Endangered [[Woundfin]] and candidate-listed [[Virgin River Chub]]. Establishment of a stabilized-flow irrigation reservoir on Quail Creek facilitated red shiner invasion upstream of the "Narrows," prompting a barrier-and-rotenone eradication scheme in September–October 1988. Due to unexpectedly rapid rotenone transport and delayed detoxification, the ichthyocide traveled far beyond intended treatment zones, killing fish as far as Mesquite, Nevada — greatly exceeding the planned ~34-km target reach and impacting 70+ km of river. The report catalogs the actual and potential ecological damage, emphasizes the loss of taxonomically unresolved "mainstream" versus "tributary" forms of several native species, and calls for a structured, long-term post-eradication monitoring and genetics program.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River fauna is notable for presumed intraspecific diversification into distinct **channel ("mainstream") forms** versus **tributary/spring forms**, in at least three native taxa — a pattern common in isolated desert stream systems:

- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): At least three forms/subspecies recognized — mainstream/large-tributary form (*R. o. yarrowi*), and an undescribed spring-tributary form.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*): Exhibits an elongate, thin-bodied "form" in large mainstream habitats (e.g., Virgin River) versus a shorter, thicker-bodied "form" in tributaries (cf. undescribed Little Colorado River sucker, *Catostomus* sp.). Populations in the Grand Canyon reach of the Colorado River may show altered phenotypes due to post-Glen-Canyon-Dam hybridization with Little Colorado River-derived fish.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus* "clarki" complex): Two forms occur in the Virgin River basin — a swift-water form with large fins and thin caudal peduncle in the mainstream, and a thicker-bodied, shorter-finned form in tributaries. Intergradation/hybridization occurs at the mouth of Beaver Dam Creek, AZ. A distinct, geographically isolated population, formerly *Pantosteus intermedius* Tanner (1942), persists in the Pluvial White River remnants (Nevada) and is morphologically divergent from the broader "clarki" complex.
- **[[Virgin River Chub]]** (*Gila robusta seminuda*): Treated taxonomically as a distinct subspecies of *Gila robusta*, restricted to the Virgin River channel downstream of Pah Tempe Spring.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Restricted to Virgin River channel habitat downstream from Pah Tempe Spring; primary focus of Endangered Species Act protections.

Taxonomic resolution of channel-versus-tributary "forms" for dace, suckers, and chub remained unresolved at the time of writing, with the paper calling for allozyme and mitochondrial DNA analysis to clarify species/subspecies status and gene flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System description**: The Virgin River is a hydrologically extreme desert stream — intermittent in lower reaches, prone to violent flash floods and severe drought, with naturally fluctuating native fish populations adapted over millennia to these conditions.
- **Habitat structure**: Native fishes partition habitat into (1) mainstream channel habitats, often associated with spring inputs (e.g., [[Pah Tempe Spring]]), and (2) tributary/spring-fed habitats (e.g., Beaver Dam Wash, Meadow Valley Wash, Moapa River drainage) — the latter now partly disconnected from the mainstream by [[Lake Mead]] inundation.
- **Barriers and connectivity**: The Washington Fields Diversion (WFD) acted as a natural/enhanced barrier limiting upstream red shiner invasion; the "Narrows" segment (often dry in drought, precipitous canyon) historically limited shiner density differences between upstream and downstream reaches.
- **Invasive species dynamics**: [[Red Shiner]] invaded the lower Virgin River from Lake Mead following its 1950s introduction to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. It coexisted with woundfin for ~25+ years at low densities upstream of Mesquite, NV, but flow stabilization from a new Quail Creek reservoir (early 1980s) enabled explosive population expansion upstream to WFD, triggering woundfin decline. Red shiners were previously implicated in decline of [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) in Arizona.
- **Disease vector**: An exotic Asiatic tapeworm, discovered in red shiners, subsequently infected native species including woundfin — compounding competitive/predatory pressure from the invader.
- **Post-treatment recolonization pathways**: Potential native-fish recovery of the affected reach was expected via (a) in situ reproduction of accidental-kill survivors, (b) downstream dispersal from above WFD, (c) tributary/irrigation-diversion refugia, or (d) combinations thereof — each with distinct genetic and community-ecology implications.

## Key Findings

- **Invasion history**: Red shiner was first introduced to the lower Colorado River basin in the 1950s and invaded the lower Virgin River from Lake Mead; population explosion followed flow stabilization from a Quail Creek irrigation reservoir in the early 1980s.
- **Eradication design flaw**: The 1988 plan used two barriers (~10 km and ~34 km below WFD) intended to confine rotenone treatment and detoxification to a defined 34-km reach, assuming native fish recolonization from upstream of WFD.
- **Application failure**: Rotenone traveled far beyond intended detoxification points due to unexpectedly rapid transport and delayed detoxification, causing fish kills at least as far as Mesquite, Nevada, and likely beyond — impacting an estimated 70+ km of river beyond the target zone.
- **Selective mortality**: Rotenone-sensitive native, channel-dwelling species were disproportionately killed, while rotenone-resistant non-natives (red shiner, bullhead catfish *Ameiurus* spp.) persisted — the opposite of the intended eradication outcome.
- **Species-specific impacts**:
  - Losses of Endangered [[Woundfin]] and candidate [[Virgin River Chub]] potentially spanned >100 km of river (34 km target + 70 km unintended downstream impact), sparing only ~15 km upstream of WFD near Pah Tempe Spring.
  - [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] mainstream Virgin River populations represent among the last extant populations in the entire lower Colorado River basin (extirpated elsewhere below Lake Mead), making their loss ecologically significant.
  - [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]] mainstream forms face similar unresolved taxonomic jeopardy from potential extirpation.
- **Conservation/genetic risks identified**:
  1. Possible genetic bottlenecking in surviving channel populations.
  2. Risk of tributary "forms" invading and genetically/ecologically overwhelming depleted channel populations.
  3. Risk of a "worst-case" red shiner resurgence outcompeting recolonizing natives.
- **Salvage efforts**: Prior to treatment, woundfin and Virgin River chub were partially salvaged to Dexter National Fish Hatchery (NM) and a holding pond near Quail Creek Dam; no salvage was attempted for other native species.
- **Recommendations**: Immediate post-eradication mainstream and tributary sampling for population structure, reproduction, and genetics (allozyme and mtDNA) over multiple generations; comparison of hatchery/refugium stocks to original mainstream populations; full documentation of the operation to inform future desert stream fish management.
- **Broader significance**: Despite operational failure, the extensive pre-existing monitoring database for the [[Virgin River]] offers an unparalleled opportunity to study community ecology, genetic recovery processes, and population repopulation/recolonization dynamics in desert stream fish assemblages.
