# Scale-Eating Habits of Terapon jarbua

#ichthyology #marine-biology #terapontidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents scale-eating (lepidophagy) in [[Terapon Jarbua]], a marine teleost up to 25 cm long that is common in the estuaries of the tropical and subtropical Indian Ocean. Fish scales removed from living fish were the most important food item, and the authors state that this niche had not previously been recorded in the marine environment. Feeding experiments showed that the species digests [[Scale-Eating]] material of relatively high energy content, and that scales are taken only from living fish, not from the bottom or from dead fish. The habit is interpreted as a possible modification of predation, or as an extension of [[Cleaning Behaviour]] (removal of [[Ectoparasites]]) from larger fish.

## Background & Description

*Terapon jarbua* is a common tropical Indo-Pacific species that extends south to Port Alfred on the south-east African coast. It is a seasonal migrant, more abundant in the south during summer than in winter (Smith, 1965). It is [[Euryhaline Fish|euryhaline]], with a salinity tolerance of 1–70‰ (Wallace, 1975), which allows it to cope with the salinity extremes of both open and closed estuaries in southern Africa.

Scale-eating is known in several freshwater groups: American characoids (*Roeboides*, *Exodon*, *Roeboexodon*, *Cataprion*, *Probolodus*) and African cichlids (*Plecodus*, *Perissodus*, [[Genyochromis Mento]], *Corematodus*, *Haplochromis*) (Roberts, 1970; Fryer & Iles, 1972). The authors note that *T. jarbua* is the first marine example reported in the paper's framing.

The species has a strongly [[Ctenoid Scales|ctenoid]], rectangular scale type that is relatively small and narrow. Its teeth are conical and arranged in series. The outer series are larger, and the inner series form a broad band. The fish has well-developed fin, preopercular and opercular spines. When captured, it contracts its body into a U shape and erects its spines. The Zulu vernacular name means "thorn-tree".

**Methods overview**

- **Field collection:** 175 fish, 30–211 mm total length, from [[Lake St Lucia]] (March 1975 to March 1976), using gill, seine and cast nets and rotenone. Fish from [[Kosi Estuary]] and [[Sodwana Estuary]] were collected for comparison.
- **Stomach analysis:** Stomachs and hindguts were preserved in 70% ethanol. Food items were identified and analysed by frequency of occurrence. Scales were counted and measured (maximum width).
- **Feeding experiments:** Fish from Sodwana, St Lucia and [[Umlalazi Estuary]] were held in 40 l glass tanks with sea water diluted to 10% with tap water, fitted with air-lift filtration.
  - Experiment 1: nine fish offered 100 loose scales from [[Argyrosomus Hololepidotus]], [[Elops Machnata]] and [[Mugil Cephalus]].
  - Experiments 2 and 3: nine starved fish offered living [[Liza Macrolepis]] or [[Sarotherodon Mossambicus]] (nine prey fish each in Experiment 2; three predator fish fed on living *S. mossambicus* in Experiment 3).
  - Experiment 4: calorific value of scales from living *M. cephalus* (scale weights 6.41–36.24 mg), measured with a micro bomb calorimeter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diet and scale use**

- Stomach contents of 100 fish from Lake St Lucia (Table I) were dominated by fish scales (66% frequency of occurrence). Other items included amphipods (*Grandidierella lignorum*, *Afrochiltonia capensis*, *Melita zeylanica*, 21%), crabs (*Hymenosoma orbiculare*, 19%), insects (11%), and [[Anilocra]] isopods (7%).
- Filamentous algae made up 4% and aquatic macrophytes 7% of the diet. Whole fish (including *Thryssa vitrirostris*, *Syngnathus djarong*, *Gilchristella aestuarius* and *Hyporhamphus knysnaensis*) made up 8%.
- A total of 926 scales was found in the stomachs, with a mean of 13.82 per fish and a scale diameter range of 1–19 mm.
- Scales came from 12 prey species (Table III). Only *Argyrosomus hololepidotus* and *Elops machnata* are piscivorous. Most stomachs contained scales from more than one species, and one fish contained scales from four species.

**Size relationships**

- Jaw width and scale size both increase with body length. Large fish eat a wider range of scale sizes, while juveniles are limited to smaller scales (Fig. 1).
- Juveniles form shoals in shallow estuarine margins, possibly to reduce predation by herons (*Casmerodius albus*) and kingfishers (*Ceryle rudis*). Fish above 100 mm move into deeper water, where scale intake and frequency increase.

**Prey and parasites**

- Scale-eating fish in [[Lake St Lucia]] face a community of potential prey. *T. jarbua* makes up only 1.2% of the St Lucia fish fauna, and over 90% of the remaining fish are considered potential food.
- The species attacks the lateral surface of prey, mainly the posterior body and [[Caudal Peduncle]] region, where it can retreat quickly before retaliation.
- Ectoparasites form part of the diet: 7% *Anilocra* spp., and 1% each of the leech *Ottoniobdella stellata* and the branchiuran *Argulus* sp.

**Predator and prey interactions**

- *T. jarbua* constitutes only 0.2% of the diet of *E. machnata*, a piscivore that also loses scales to it.
- Stripe colour changes are linked to behaviour. Stripes darken in the presence of conspecifics and fade to dull brown when a prey species is present, just before attacks begin.

## Key Findings

1. **Scales are the dominant food.** Fish scales occurred in 66% of stomachs from St Lucia and were the most important food item. The authors describe this as a previously unrecorded marine niche.
2. **Energy value.** Scales of *Mugil cephalus* averaged 2.0353 cal mg⁻¹ (S.D. = 0.2593, n = 10). This is somewhat lower than [[Carbohydrate]] (4.18 cal mg⁻¹) and [[Protein]] (4.32 cal mg⁻¹), but still a highly nutritious resource. The authors also quote 8.5214 J mg⁻¹ for scales.
3. **Digestion.** Scales begin to break down in the stomach and are fully digested before reaching the end of the hindgut. Batches pass through irregularly, causing bulges where scales ≥10 mm diameter are passing.
4. **Living fish only.** Starved fish offered loose scales ate only 9% of them (4% in midwater, 5% from the bottom). Free-falling scales were taken at only 4%. Scales appear to come solely from living fish in natural conditions.
5. **Experimental feeding success.**
   - In Experiment 2, seven of nine fish that had access to living *L. macrolepis* had eaten scales. The other two contained fin rays or were empty.
   - All three fish that fed on *S. mossambicus* in Experiment 3 had eaten scales from that species.
   - Mean removals in 24 h were 6 scales from *L. macrolepis* and 16 from *S. mossambicus*.
   - Scale loss was greatest at the caudal peduncle. Smaller prey lost fewer scales, probably because they are more manoeuvrable.
6. **Diet shifts with size.** Fish below 100 mm (n = 42) and above 100 mm (n = 58) differ in the proportion of scales in their diet. Across all 100 fish, 25% had diets of only scales, 41% had scales plus other food, and 34% had no scales. Larger fish depend more on scales.
7. **Scale size and number.** Maximum scale diameter eaten rises with fish size, both within and among species (Table IV). The authors note that the 118 scales of *T. jarbua* (maximum diameter 19 mm in Table IV, 20 mm in the Discussion) contrast with the 150 scales of *Roeboides prognathus* (maximum diameter 6 mm), showing that smaller scales allow more scales to be eaten.
8. **Possible origins of the habit.** The authors offer four hypotheses:
   - a modification of predation (small size and slow swimming limit whole-fish predation);
   - an extension of parasite removal (cleaning);
   - a modification of herbivory (rejected, because of the short intestine);
   - a fin-feeding link (fin rays were absent in wild fish).
   They conclude that evolution in either direction "cannot be ruled out".
9. **Anti-predator and defence traits.** Spines and the capacity to form a U-shape may deter piscivores. Small scales that are hard to remove may limit [[Autopredation]], and the colour pattern (stripes) may also help.
10. **Sustainability.** Removal of many scales from one fish could harm osmoregulation and survival. Scales regenerate, so scale removal is a renewable resource. Few scales per host is the optimum, and a large scale-eating population could condition prey to evade attacks.
11. **Wider distribution (note added in proof).** Vari (pers. comm.) found that *T. jarbua* is also lepidophagous in Australian waters and the Western Pacific. No other species in the family was found to eat scales.
