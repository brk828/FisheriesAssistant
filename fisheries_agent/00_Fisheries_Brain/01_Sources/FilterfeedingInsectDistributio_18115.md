# Filter-feeding Insect Distribution and Planktonic Food in the Montreal River

#trichoptera #hydropsychidae #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-entomology

## Executive Summary

This study compared benthic insect populations and planktonic food at two physically similar rapids on the [[Montreal River]], Saskatchewan, during summer 1960. The lower station, below a series of productive lakes, held substantially more filter-feeding [[Trichoptera]] larvae, mainly [[Hydropsychidae]]. The author attributes this to a richer supply of suspended food flushed from the lake-stream system, not to differences in water chemistry. Alkalinity was actually higher at the less productive upper station. The paper also reports distribution patterns for [[Ephemeroptera]] and [[Plecoptera]].

## Background & Description

The work was part of a broader investigation of plankton, bottom fauna, and water chemistry in the Montreal River and four associated lakes. It was supported by an NSF research grant and forms part of a PhD thesis from the University of Saskatchewan. The author was Colbert E. Cushing Jr. A full description of the lake-stream system was still in preparation at the time.

Two stations were compared:

- **Upper rapids (MR2):** about 53 km downstream of [[Montreal Lake]]; river about 20 m wide.
- **Lower rapids (MR12):** about 124 km downstream of Montreal Lake; river about 30 m wide.

Both had rubble and boulder substrates and water velocities up to 1 m/s.

**Methods**

- Benthos was sampled with a [[Surber Sampler]] (one square foot), paired with a [[Drift Sampling]] sample of equal duration to separate drifting organisms from resident ones. Lower-rapids samples were collected weekly and upper-rapids samples biweekly.
- Trichoptera were split into campodeiform larvae (mostly filter-feeding Hydropsychidae) and eruciform larvae (active foragers).
- Qualitative samples were hand-picked from stones and debris.
- Plankton was concentrated by filtering 25 or 50 L of water through a No. 20 Wisconsin net (68 meshes/cm). Zooplankton and amorphous organic particles were counted with a Sedgwick-Rafter cell; [[Phytoplankton]] colonies were counted as "units" per liter.
- The author noted that Surber samplers give imprecise numerical estimates and that bottom fauna are contagiously distributed. The paper therefore emphasizes qualitative patterns and large quantitative differences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bottom-fauna production is often linked to [[Alkalinity]]. The author argues that where a stream receives a rich supply of [[Suspended Food]] from upstream lakes, chemically similar reaches can support very different faunas. Lower-rapids temperatures fluctuated more than upper-rapids temperatures, which the author links to the downstream increase in mayfly nymphs, as earlier workers had reported.

Filter-feeders depend on suspended particles and [[Plankton]] (both [[Zooplankton]] and phytoplankton) carried downstream. Lake productivity therefore propagates into the river and shapes the benthic community below.

## Key Findings

**1. Filter-feeding caddisflies were more abundant downstream.**
- Campodeiform Trichoptera (mostly [[Hydropsyche Recurvata]] and [[Cheumatopsyche Campyla]], the most abundant adults collected) were present in greater numbers at the lower rapids. Species richness of both Trichoptera groups was similar at the two stations.
- The late-summer increase at the lower rapids came almost entirely from early-instar Hydropsychidae larvae. No comparable increase occurred upstream.
- Counts are described as minimal estimates, because many larvae likely passed through the net or stayed on stones.

**2. Plankton was richer at the lower rapids.**
- All plankton groups counted were almost invariably higher at the lower rapids, except amorphous organic matter, which was higher upstream during early-summer runoff.
- Counts of amorphous particles were similar at both stations when Hydropsychidae were most abundant.
- Total benthic numbers peaked at the upper rapids in late August at 254 per square foot (2,730 per m²). The lower rapids peaked at 3,484 per square foot (37,440 per m²) in late summer.

**3. Mayflies differed in composition and abundance.**
- The lower rapids had more mayfly larvae, mainly [[Baetis]] spp. and [[Tricorythodes Fallax]].
- Five species of [[Ephemerella]] occurred only at the upper rapids. The author found fewer mayfly species overall downstream.
- Fourfold increases in Baetis below a lake were reported by [[Illies]] (1956), consistent with these results.

**4. Stoneflies were scarce and restricted.**
- Plecoptera were similar at both stations except for [[Pteronarcys Dorsata]], found only at the upper rapids, where it was the commonest stonefly. None were collected in two summers at the lower rapids, possibly because the species lives in deeper, inaccessible water there.

**5. Food supply, not alkalinity, explained the pattern.**
- The author concludes that filter-feeder abundance follows the quantitative distribution of suspended food. This matches earlier European and North American work (Berg, Knopp, Miller, Scott).
- Methyl orange alkalinity was higher at the less productive upper station (133 ppm) than at the lower station (106 ppm), so alkalinity could not explain the greater downstream fauna.
- Montreal Lake contributed less plankton than the more productive lakes immediately above the lower rapids, and plankton leaving Montreal Lake apparently disappeared before reaching the upper rapids.

**6. Broader implication for stream surveys.**
- Two stream reaches with similar chemistry can support very different bottom faunas if one receives a richer upstream food supply. The author suggests this is often overlooked in stream surveys.
