# Procedures for Stocking Nonnative Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft [[Environmental Assessment]], prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Ecological Services, Grand Junction, Colorado; dated April 30, 1996), evaluates procedures that would guide future stocking of nonnative fishes in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The stated goal is to reduce, minimize, or eliminate the impacts of nonnative fishes on four [[Endangered Species Act]]-listed native fishes, while still allowing stocking for recreational fishing and private aquaculture where it is compatible with recovery. The document examines a no-action alternative and five action alternatives, analyzes their effects on aquatic resources, recreation, endangered fish recovery, and the economy, and summarizes the trade-offs in a comparison table. No preferred alternative is selected in this draft; the choice is to be made after public and scientific review.

## Background & Description

**Endangered natives.** Four species are listed: [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] (listed March 11, 1967), [[Bonytail]] (April 23, 1980), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (October 23, 1991). [[Critical Habitat]] was designated March 21, 1994, covering river reaches and the associated 100-year floodplain.

**Basin fauna.** The Basin's fish fauna is depauperate and geographically isolated: 36 native species are recorded, and 64 percent of them occur nowhere else (Miller 1959). Forty-two nonnative species and subspecies have been introduced (Tyus et al. 1982), some through bait-bucket transfers and others through deliberate stocking for sport, forage, vegetation control, and mosquito control.

**Policy context.** The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for these fishes was formalized in January 1988 under the Endangered Species Act. Its five elements include habitat management, habitat development, native fish stocking, nonnative species and sport fishing control, and research and monitoring. This assessment addresses the fourth element. The stocking procedures would be implemented through a Memorandum of Understanding among the Service and the fish and wildlife agencies of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming. Related policy developments include [[Executive Order 12962]] (June 7, 1995) on recreational fisheries and a Colorado-Interior Memorandum of Agreement (November 29, 1995).

**Interim history.** [[Interim Stocking Procedures]] were adopted in October 1994 and expired in December 1995, although the states voluntarily continued to follow them. Under those procedures, Colorado submitted eight lake management plans for floodplain waters; none was approved because all lay within the 40-year floodplain. An April 1995 independent scientific review team advised against stocking outside historic range, especially into critical habitat, and noted that fish in ponds across the 10- to 100-year floodplains would eventually escape to the river. Public meetings in December 1995 drew about 70 attendees, with few expressing clear support or opposition.

**Scope.** The procedures cover the Upper Basin, excluding the San Juan drainage. Little or no warmwater stocking currently occurs in Utah or Wyoming; most ongoing warmwater stocking is by the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] in public waters or by the [[Aquaculture Industry]] in private ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Habitat degradation, caused mainly by dams, water depletions, and floodplain diking, is identified as the major reason for declines of the four endangered fishes. These changes favored nonnative species, which now dominate most altered habitats. Recovery is considered unlikely until nonnative fish problems are addressed.

**Competition and predation.** Nonnative fishes compete for limited habitat and food and prey on eggs, larvae, and juveniles. The document cites Tyus and Saunders (1996) for a long list of documented predation cases, including channel catfish and green sunfish eating [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae and eggs, and northern pike eating [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Recruitment of young endangered fish is described as very low despite documented spawning. Nonnative fishes can also cause harassment, disease, and parasite spread; the parasite *Lernea* sp. is thought to have arrived through nonnative introductions. Stocking can keep nonnative populations above habitat carrying capacity and speeds their recovery after drought or flood.

**Escapement pathways.** Fish escape from standing waters into low-velocity river habitats, the same habitats used by young endangered fish. Escapement is most likely from ponds with outlets to the river and from floodplain ponds that become connected during high flows.

**Nonnative species ranking.** The 1991 ranking by [[Hawkins and Nesler]] of 28 nonnative species (adverse impact) placed [[Channel Catfish]] 1st, [[Red Shiner]] 2nd, [[Northern Pike]] 3rd, [[Common Carp]] 4th, [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Green Sunfish]] tied 5th, [[Largemouth Bass]] 7th, [[Mosquitofish]] 8th, [[Smallmouth Bass]] 10th, and [[Bluegill]] 11th. [[Black Crappie]] was not ranked as a problem in 1991, but recent large captures in Green River nursery areas raised concern. [[White Crappie]] ranked 28th because of its limited Basin distribution.

**Economic and recreational setting.** Colorado anglers fished about 8.2 million days in 1991, with 79 percent in coldwater lakes and streams. Warmwater fishing was about six percent of effort. In a survey of warmwater anglers in four western Colorado counties, 36 percent preferred catfish and 25 percent preferred largemouth bass. Wyoming issued 115,148 resident fishing licenses in 1994. Utah's warmwater stocking was limited to a possible smallmouth bass plan for Strawberry Reservoir.

**Private ponds.** [[Mitchell]] (1995) identified 308 ponds covering 878 surface acres along the Gunnison and Colorado rivers. On the Colorado River, 73 percent of ponds are privately owned. Of 101 ponds examined along the Colorado River from Palisade to Loma, 85 lay below the 10-year floodplain. Warmwater stocking occurred in four percent of Colorado River ponds, representing 20 percent of their surface acreage.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives examined.**
- **No Action:** No formal procedures; states retain discretion; private ponds in the floodplain would largely be unregulated. Ponds within the 10-year floodplain would be reclaimed on a voluntary basis.
- **Alternative 1:** Routine stocking limited to a defined list of waters (for example, trout basin-wide; largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie, and others in isolated waters outside the 100-year floodplain). All other proposals receive case-by-case review. This is described as the simplest approach with the fewest routine stocking opportunities.
- **Alternative 2:** Routine stocking in isolated ponds above the 50-year floodplain and in connected waters only if screened; ponds must be bermed to [[FEMA]] standards five feet above the ordinary high water line (OHWL). Restrictive.
- **Alternative 3:** Most restrictive. Stocking (except trout) confined to isolated waters above the 100-year floodplain; no artificial dikes to remove ponds from the floodplain; no connected-water stocking regardless of screening.
- **Alternative 4:** Allows stocking of largemouth bass, bluegill, and black crappie in isolated ponds outside the 10-year floodplain, including ponds bermed to FEMA standards. Upstream standing waters may be stocked with species already present downstream.
- **Alternative 5:** Similar to Alternative 4 but without berming opportunities; floodplain limits have no exceptions. Case-by-case situations match Alternative 1.

**Common features.**
- Trout stocking is permitted in all alternatives because no trout impacts have been identified in the Upper Basin.
- Utah chub stocking in waters where it already exists is permitted routinely.
- Nonsalmonid stocking directly into occupied endangered fish habitat is prohibited.
- Proposals for new species or hybrids require case-by-case review and must follow the [[American Fisheries Society]] policy on introductions of aquatic species.
- Ten species are prohibited in all action alternatives: northern pike, tiger muskie, common carp, red shiner, black bullhead, wiper, green sunfish, yellow perch, walleye, and white crappie (Alternatives 2 and 3 list them explicitly).

**Case-by-case review.** Proposals must include purpose and location, species and numbers, escapement potential, control measures, impact-remediation plans, and a monitoring plan. The process (Appendix B) runs through a 60-day public and agency comment period, a 30-day informal [[Section 7 Consultation]] step, and an escalation path to the State Wildlife Commission if objections remain unresolved.

**Monitoring.** All alternatives would use the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]]. Alternatives 2 and 3 require a peer-reviewed study of ISMP effectiveness before routine stocking begins. Wyoming has no ISMP monitoring.

**Projected impacts (Table 9 summary).**
- **No Action:** Increased nonnative occurrence, competition, and predation. Endangered fish populations would remain near present levels with little chance of recovery.
- **Alternative 1:** Modest benefit to endangered fish; higher pond-owner costs; localized recreational adjustments.
- **Alternative 2:** Largest reduction in escapement for flows below the 100-year event; significant berming costs; loss of warmwater fishing in private ponds below the 50-year floodplain.
- **Alternative 3:** Greatest reduction in nonnative numbers in the river; largest impact on warmwater fishing and the aquaculture industry.
- **Alternatives 4 and 5:** Allow periodic reseeding of lower ponds from upstream waters, so river numbers could rebound; progress toward recovery would be slower than under Alternatives 2 and 3.

**Economic estimate.** Using 100 fishing days per acre at $40 per day, loss of 20 surface acres of warmwater fishing equals about $80,000 per year in direct impact; loss of 100 acres equals about $400,000 per year. The document notes that overall Colorado fishing days would change minimally because warmwater fishing is a small share of total effort.

**Recovery and water development.** The document states that the Recovery Program serves as a reasonable and prudent alternative for Section 7 compliance in water development. Stocking procedures that do not adequately address nonnative fish issues would not satisfy this role, and failure could affect water development projects valued in billions of dollars.

**Conclusion.** The draft does not select a preferred alternative. It concludes that the only environmental impacts identified are decreases in nonnative fish numbers and increases in endangered fish populations, and that positive and negative impacts must be weighed together before a set of procedures is adopted. The final choice may combine elements of several alternatives.
