# Relative Susceptibilities of Freshwater Fish to Zinc Poisoning (Part II)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 laboratory study by [[Ian R. Ball]], published in *Water Research*, compares the acute toxicity of [[Zinc Sulphate]] to five freshwater fish species: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Perch]], [[Roach]], [[Gudgeon]] and [[Bream]]. It is the second paper in a series following a study on [[Undissociated Ammonia]]. Over a 7-day exposure in hard, well-aerated water, trout were more sensitive than the cyprinids at all times, but by the end of the test perch, roach and bream showed similar sensitivities. The author concludes that short-duration toxicity tests are not reliable for ranking the ultimate sensitivity of fish species to a poison.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a long-standing assumption that [[Salmonidae]] (salmonid fish) are more susceptible to pollutants than [[Coarse Fish]]. The author notes this assumption did not hold for ammonia in well-oxygenated water, where the trout–cyprinid difference largely disappeared at lower concentrations over exposures of 2 days or more. [[Skidmore (1964)]] reviewed zinc toxicity and noted that no truly comparative study of relative resistance between two aquatic species had been produced. [[Pickering and Henderson (1966)]] later provided 24, 48 and 96 hr median tolerance limits for warm-water fish, but without toxicity curves, making it difficult to judge whether values were asymptotic with the time axis.

**Species tested (scientific names as given):**
- *Salmo gairdnerii* (rainbow trout), from the Berkshire Trout Farm, April 1967
- *Perca fluviatilis* (perch), from a pond at Heydon, February 1967
- *Rutilus rutilus* (roach), two stocks: Buckenham Broad (November 1966) and Heydon (February 1967)
- *Gobio gobio* (gudgeon), from a canal at Stapleford, May 1967
- *Abramis brama* (bream), from Buckenham Broad, November 1966

**Methods:** Poison was prepared from hydrated zinc sulphate (ZnSO4·7H2O) dissolved in deionized water. Methods matched the earlier ammonia experiments, except aquaria were aerated with air plus carbon dioxide to keep pH relatively low. Test conditions (Table 1) included dissolved oxygen of 7.2–7.9 mg/l (85–95% saturation) for most species, and temperatures from roughly 10.0 to 15.8 °C depending on species and test date. Groups of 10 fish were used per test, with 1 to 3 tests per species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Zinc is presented as a common pollutant of freshwater systems. The study uses hard, well-aerated water, a condition under which the author treats the results as relevant to typical receiving waters. The gudgeon test was run at a later date and at a slightly higher temperature than the other coarse fish, which the author suggests may partly explain its greater susceptibility. Seasonal effects are also raised as a possible factor. The author stresses that exposure duration and the shape of the toxicity curve (whether it levels off over time) strongly influence the apparent ranking of species, which has practical consequences for setting water-quality standards.

## Key Findings

- **Five-day LC50 values** (mg Zn/l, 95% confidence limits in brackets) and slope functions (S):

| Species | 5-day LC50 (mg Zn/l) | Slope (S) |
|---|---|---|
| Rainbow Trout | 4.6 (3.8–5.5) | 1.34 |
| Bream | 14.3 (12.5–16.3) | 1.56 |
| Perch | 16.0 (14.8–17.3) | 1.09 |
| Roach (Norfolk stock) | 17.3 (15.4–19.4) | 1.26 |
| Roach (Herts stock) | 17.3 (14.5–19.7) | 1.32 |

- **Trout were the most sensitive species.** Bream, roach and perch were roughly four to five times more resistant to zinc than rainbow trout.
- **Gudgeon could not be assigned a 5-day LC50** because the 7-day test was too short for the curve to become asymptotic. The 7-day LC50 was 8.4 mg Zn/l (7.1–10.0), with a slope of 1.2. The author states that gudgeon appear more resistant than trout, but confirmation is needed.
- **The two roach stocks** did not differ significantly in their response to zinc at any time, so only the Buckenham Broad curve was plotted.
- **Short-term rankings are misleading.** Over short exposures, bream appeared more resistant than perch and roach, but after about 2 days this ranking reversed.
- **Conclusion:** Experiments of short duration cannot reliably rank the ultimate sensitivity of fish species to a poison. Adequate test duration is needed to establish asymptotic toxicity values.
