# Rio Grande Silvery Minnow Recovery Plan (Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan, prepared by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2, Albuquerque) with a stakeholder [[Recovery Team]], addresses the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*), federally listed as endangered and also listed as endangered in New Mexico, Texas, and the Republic of Mexico. The species once occurred throughout the Rio Grande basin in New Mexico and Texas. It now occupies only the [[Middle Rio Grande Valley]] between [[Cochiti Dam]] and the headwaters of [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], about 5% of its known historic range.

Decline is attributed to modified discharge patterns and channel desiccation caused by impoundments and diversions, channelization, competition and predation by [[Nonnative Fish Species]], and water quality degradation. The plan sets two goals: stabilize and enhance the middle valley population, and reestablish self-sustaining populations in at least three other reaches of its historic range. Recovery is judged against five consecutive years without biologically significant declines, plus institutional protections. The estimated five-year cost is $9,005.0 thousand (about $9.0 million), excluding capital projects and water acquisition.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was first described as *Algoma amara* by Girard (1856) from specimens near Brownsville, Texas. It was long synonymized with the Mississippi silvery minnow and the [[Plains Minnow]] (*H. placitus*). Bestgen and Propst (1996) confirmed its distinctiveness. It is one of seven recognized *Hybognathus* species in the United States, and no reliable characters support subspecies. Unique alleles at loci Est-1, Est-3, and Sod-2 distinguish it from congeners (Cook et al. 1992).

**Morphology.** It is a small, relatively heavy-bodied minnow that rarely exceeds 100 mm total length. Live fish are light greenish-yellow dorsally and cream to white ventrally. Lateral line scales average 37 (range 34–40), and circumference scales average 28 (range 22–33). The subterminal mouth, pharyngeal filtering apparatus, and short, coiled intestine reflect its herbivorous habits. Sexual dimorphism is slight: breeding males have broadly fanned pectoral fins with thickened rays, and both sexes develop tubercles during spawning.

**Life history (from Platania and Dudley 1997).**
- Spawning occurs over about one month in May–June, coinciding with spring runoff, at 20–24°C, in the water column.
- Age 1 fish dominate spawners. Age 2 fish make up under 10% of spawners.
- Each female may produce 3 to 18 clutches in 12 hours, with a mean clutch size of about 270 eggs. Age 2 females produce about twice as many eggs as Age 1 females.
- Semi-buoyant eggs drift for up to 50 hours. Larvae are about 3.7 mm SL at hatching and move to low-velocity habitats.
- Maximum size is about 87 mm SL. Maximum longevity is about 25 months, but very few fish survive beyond 13 months.
- Gut contents with sand and silt suggest epipsammatic algae are an important food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic range.** The species was formerly among the most widespread and abundant fishes of the Rio Grande basin. It has been extirpated from the Rio Grande upstream of Cochiti Dam (presumed; 62 post-1983 sampling efforts failed to find it), from the reach between Elephant Butte and Caballo reservoirs, from the Caballo-to-Texas-border reach (last collected in the 1940s), and from Big Bend National Park (none since 1960). It is also gone from the Pecos River above Roswell, where the last collection was in 1968, and from the lower Rio Grande, where the last credible collection dates to the late 1950s.

**Hydrology and drying.** The Rio Grande water supply has been fully appropriated for over 100 years. Impoundments, diversions, and channel narrowing have changed the river. The channel width has been reduced by about 50% since 1935. Percent of days with no flow:

| Period | San Acacia | San Marcial |
|---|---|---|
| 1896–1950 | 2% | 8% |
| 1951–1980 | 17% | 42% |
| 1981–present | 2% | 18% |

Extensive drying in 1989, 1990, and April–May 1996 killed fish, including gravid adults. The 1989–1990 dewatering took at least two years to recover from.

**Habitat use.** Fish were abundant in areas with velocities under 10 cm/s (86.5% of individuals). Silt was the substrate for 91.3%. Preferred mesohabitats were debris piles (40.5%), pools (35.9%), and backwaters (13.8%), while main channel runs were avoided (1.3%). Small fish use shallow, slow water, and larger fish shift to faster water. In winter, fish concentrate in [[Instream Debris]]. Over 70% of individuals selected debris piles in a winter study (Dudley and Platania 1996).

**Reach-by-reach status.** The team's analysis identified reaches for reestablishment, in priority order:
1. Sumner Dam to Brantley Reservoir (Pecos River)
2. Red Bluff Reservoir to Amistad Reservoir
3. Conchos to Amistad
4. Amistad to Falcon Reservoir
5. Falcon Reservoir to Gulf of Mexico
6. Brantley to Red Bluff Reservoir

Reach-level threats include [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]] entrainment, dewatering below diversions, salinity, and nonnative predators such as channel catfish, bass, and gar.

**Related Rio Grande species.** Other short-lived pelagic-spawning cyprinids (speckled chub, Rio Grande shiner, phantom shiner, bluntnose shiner) were extirpated from the middle river and share its reproductive strategy. This suggests the silvery minnow's decline reflects a broader loss of this reproductive guild.

## Key Findings

**Current status and monitoring.**
- The 1992 survey took 215 collections (143,040 specimens of 24 species) between Angostura and Elephant Butte. Rio Grande silvery minnow numbered 2,825 in summer and 472 in autumn samples.
- Over 75% of all individuals were taken at or downstream of San Acacia Diversion Dam.
- At Cochiti, 22 individuals (0.3%) were found in 1994 (0.05 fish/10 m²) and none in 1992 or 1993.
- A 1987 vs. 1992 comparison in the Isleta Reach found only 95 specimens in 22 collections in 1992, despite a far larger catch. The 1992 Isleta samples below the dam (4,943 and 6,546 specimens) yielded only four silvery minnows.
- Historically, the species was present in 26 of 28 collections from 1926–1959 and made up 41.1% (N=3,739) of those samples. Abundance then fell sharply, and the seven collections in the 1960s yielded no specimens.

**Recovery objectives and criteria.**
- Middle valley: achieved when no biologically significant declines in reproduction, distribution, or abundance occur for five consecutive years and institutional protections are in place.
- Reestablishment: met when self-sustaining populations are established in at least three designated reaches, with the same five-year standard.

**Prioritized actions (Part II narrative outline).**
1. Restore and protect middle valley habitat. This covers flow requirements, a [[Water Management Strategy]], water acquisition, conjunctive use, reservoir operations, metering, water quality studies, control of nonnative fish, and fish passage and entrainment reduction.
2. Reestablish the species into historic range. This includes building a [[Captive Broodstock]], baseline genetics, spawning and dispersal studies, Pecos River hybridization and competition studies with plains minnow, and stocking and monitoring.
3. Run a public awareness and education program, including a project home page and an aquarium display.
4. Maintain an [[Adaptive Management]] program with long-term monitoring, a centralized database, and periodic review.
5. Develop a long-term reestablishment master plan, including a full-time coordinator.

Annual totals fall from $2,523.0 thousand in 1998 to $828.0 thousand in 2002.

**Water management.** A November 14, 1996 [[Water Management Strategy]] (Appendix C) was developed by federal, state, and district agencies. It identifies options such as water acquisition, conjunctive use, upstream storage changes, and water-use efficiency. In 1996 the minnow was largely protected by leaving native river water undiverted, supplemented by City of Albuquerque [[San Juan-Chama Project]] water. Future availability of that water is uncertain. The plan also notes that de-listing is not expected in the near future.

**Documented data gaps.** The plan notes little is known about the species' population genetics, spawning cues, larval habitat, toxicity of contaminants, and the effects of nonnative competitors. Those gaps are the basis for much of the proposed research.
