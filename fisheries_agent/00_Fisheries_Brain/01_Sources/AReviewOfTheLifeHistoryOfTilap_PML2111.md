# Life History of Tilapia Zillii and Its Desirability in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 administrative report from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Fisheries Branch, authored by Ronald J. Felzmann, reviews the [[Life History]] of [[Tilapia Zillii]] and assesses whether the species should be permitted in California. The author concludes that the species could establish and overpopulate many California waters, compete with native and game fishes, and damage rice and irrigation systems. The report recommends that the species be designated a prohibited species in northern and central California. It notes that the species is already too widespread in southern California to eradicate.

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** The species is a cichlid with a continuous dorsal fin and two incomplete lateral lines. The body is elongate and brown above, with a yellow to white underside. Six to seven dark vertical bars may be visible. The dorsal fin has a large black marking, often bordered in yellow or orange.
- **Size:** Adults reach 27 to 35 cm (10.6 to 13.8 in) in length and up to 800 g (1.8 lb).
- **Age and growth:** In [[Lake Tiberias]], Ben-Tuvia (1960) recorded 1-year-old males and females at 6.6 cm total length. Five-year-old males and females measured 14.9 and 17.6 cm (5.9 and 6.9 in). Fryer and Iles (1972) reported 3-, 5-, and 6-year-old males from the [[Niger River]] at 22.9 to 26.9 cm (9.0 to 10.6 in). The maximum recorded age was over 7 years in Lake Tiberias.
- **Native range:** The species is widely distributed in Africa and the Middle East. It occurs in lakes, ponds, streams, and rivers, and was reported at altitudes up to 2,500 m in East Africa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature tolerance:** The species tolerates winter temperatures as low as about 9°C (48.2°F). It survived two consecutive winters in California ponds, but the report describes the 1972 cold winter as unusual, and ponds near Napa did not survive it. Spawning occurs when water temperatures are above about 18°C.
- **Salinity tolerance:** Tolerance of salinities up to about 41 to 45 ‰ has been reported, which raises the possibility of establishment in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], [[San Francisco Bay]], and [[San Pablo Bay]].
- **Habitat preference:** The species is restricted to shallow water with plant growth, which provides food and shelter. It often overpopulates and stunts in ponds, particularly under crowded conditions.
- **Current distribution in California:** The species was not established in northern California. In central California it was found in Santa Clara and Kern Counties and at a University of California site. Populations at Napa golf course ponds did not survive the 1972 winter. In southern California it occurs at numerous locations, with the greatest concentrations in irrigation systems exceeding 20,000 fish. It has also been stocked in irrigation canals.
- **Diet:** Adults are primarily herbivorous and consume aquatic plants. Stomach studies (Ben-Tuvia) recorded diatoms, rotifers, copepods, cladocerans, ostracods, insect larvae, and chironomids. Other studies noted mosquito larvae consumption, and cannibalism of young cichlids was reported. Young fish feed on Daphnia during their first month.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and early life history**
- Spawning occurs from April to September in the central Niger basin. A single spawning may produce as many as several thousand eggs. Counts for females from about 25 cm long were reported as roughly 1,000 eggs, and Fryer and Iles suggested that some females produce much higher numbers.
- The species is monogamous for at least several broods. Both sexes develop breeding colors and defend the spawning territory.
- The male and female build a circular saucer-shaped nest about 20 to 25 cm in diameter and 5 to 6 cm deep, usually in sandy or muddy substrate, with pits around the perimeter to hold larvae.
- Eggs are small (1.1 to 1.6 mm) and adhesive. Hatching takes about 2 to 4 days, depending on temperature. The larvae are moved to the pits by the parents.
- Larvae begin feeding at 10 to 12 days, at about 7 mm in length. The parents guard the young for about 5 days, and the young may remain near the parents for some time after.
- Females first spawn at about 7 cm total length in overcrowded ponds (Lowe, 1955a). Individuals of 13 to 14 cm mature in Lake Tiberias after two years of growth.

**Competition**
- Fryer (1961) found that the species is more aggressive than [[Tilapia Variabilis]]. In tank studies with young fish of both species, the larger *T. zillii* monopolized food and drove off smaller fish of both species.
- Fryer and Iles (1972) reported that *T. zillii* is competitively superior to *T. variabilis* in shallow habitat.

**Assessment and recommendations**
- The author judges that the species could populate and overpopulate a variety of California waters, including reservoirs, lakes, farm ponds, and canals.
- The species could compete for food with plants and invertebrates, reduce water quality by feeding, and compete with native fishes for spawning habitat. It may also prey on native fish eggs and young.
- The herbivorous habit could make the species undesirable to rice growers, because it may damage rice fields.
- The report recommends that the species be designated a prohibited species in northern and central California.
- Because the species is already too abundant and widely established in southern California to eradicate, the report advises that every effort be made to prevent its establishment in additional waters, especially those supporting the Colorado River [[Game Fish]] and native fishes. It also cites risk to the [[Splittail]] (*Pogonichthys macrolepidotus*) in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system.
- The report recommends that the Department consider a cooperative study on aquatic weed control. It notes that tilapia has been used for biological weed control, and that the Department may disapprove of its use for that purpose.

**Background information**
- The species was introduced to Hawaii, Guam, Arizona, and other U.S. locations. Its use for [[Aquatic Weed Control]] and as a food fish was also reviewed, but the report notes that no significant information on diseases or parasites was available.
