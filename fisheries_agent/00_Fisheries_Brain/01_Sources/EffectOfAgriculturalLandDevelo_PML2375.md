# Effect of Agricultural Land Development on Drainage Waters in the North Carolina Tidewater Region

#water-quality #hydrology #wetland-ecology #agricultural-drainage

## Executive Summary

This EPA Environmental Research Laboratory project summary (EPA-600/S3-80-087, July 1981) reports a three-year study of how [[Agricultural Land Development]] affects hydrology and runoff water quality on high organic soils in the [[North Carolina Tidewater Region]]. The study was carried out by R. W. Skaggs, J. W. Gilliam, T. J. Sheets, and J. S. Barnes, with sponsorship from the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]], the [[North Carolina Water Resources Research Institute]], and the North Carolina Agricultural Research Service.

Key outcomes were:
- A small but significant increase in organic nitrogen in drainage water after development.
- Substantially greater phosphorus loss from true organic soils, identified as the largest potential eutrophication hazard.
- Reduced [[Evapotranspiration]] and a small increase in annual outflow, even in drought years.
- Relatively small changes in dissolved oxygen, biochemical oxygen demand, temperature, pH, and other water quality parameters.
- A significant increase in fecal and total coliform bacteria from pasture land.
- Rapid disappearance of the herbicide [[Alachlor]] from soil, with high drainage-water concentrations linked mainly to direct spraying or spray drift.

## Background & Description

The Tidewater Region is flat, with surface elevations only a few meters above sea level and a water table near the surface. Many soils are organic or have high organic surface layers, collectively called the [[Blacklands]]. Drainage, essential for agriculture here, began in the late 1600s, with alternating periods of clearing and inactivity.

The latest drainage expansion began in the early 1970s, when several large corporations cleared and developed thousands of hectares in eastern North Carolina. This coincided with [[Algal Blooms]] in the [[Chowan River]]. Concern from scientists, agencies, and the public focused on several possible impacts:
- Higher peak runoff exceeding the capacity of outlet canals and rivers.
- Sedimentation, nutrient inputs, and algal blooms in the [[Albemarle and Pamlico Estuaries]], which support commercial and sport fishing.
- Pesticide movement into water and fecal inputs from pasture.
- Increased heavy-metal concentrations and reduced salinity affecting saltwater organisms.
- Soil oxidation and land [[Subsidence]].

Basic data on these effects were lacking, so the project was designed to fill that gap.

**Methods.** Experiments compared paired developed and undeveloped sites on three soil types spanning the Blacklands, for six experimental sites in total. The main methods were:
- Calibrated triangular weirs behind flashboard riser structures, with continuous stage recording for outflow.
- Flow-proportional automatic water samplers, with weekly servicing.
- Continuous rainfall and water table recording.
- Weekly field measurements of conductivity, salinity, dissolved oxygen, temperature, and pH.
- Laboratory analyses of total P, total N, nitrate-nitrogen, total organic carbon, filterable solids, and turbidity.
- Monthly composite analyses for Ca, Mg, Na, Cu, Fe, Mn, Cd, and Zn.
- Fecal and total coliform counts from grab samples every two weeks.
- Alachlor sampling of soil for four weeks after application and runoff sampling during two to five rain events, using XAD-2 resin for field processing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Tidewater drainage system links agricultural fields to estuaries that support commercial and recreational fisheries. Impacts on these waters depend on soil type, distance to outlets, and sediment characteristics in collector ditches.

- **Phosphorus:** Mineral soils bind fertilizer phosphorus, but organic soils release it readily. Mineral sediments in ditches can remove much dissolved orthophosphate, but organic soils with little mineral content located next to estuaries or major streams can significantly increase phosphorus entry.
- **Nitrogen:** Nitrate levels in drainage from these organic soils are very low, so inorganic nitrogen increases are smaller than predicted from other North Carolina agricultural areas.
- **Coliforms:** Grazed land commonly raises coliform levels across the United States with few apparent problems. Risk rises when grazed land sits next to shellfish waters.
- **Atmospheric inputs:** Atmospheric deposition of N and P was three to four times greater than losses in drainage from natural areas. Na, Cl, K, Ca, Mg, Cu, Mn, and Zn inputs roughly matched drainage losses, and there was a net loss of Fe from natural areas.
- **Sensitive areas:** Ecologically sensitive areas lie close to large farms, so careful pesticide application matters.

## Key Findings

**Hydrology**
- Peak runoff occurred earlier and was three to four times higher on developed land than on similar undeveloped land.
- The existing canal network, some with pump-assisted outlets, cannot drain developed fields as fast as they produce runoff, so peak-flow differences may shrink during the largest events.
- Total annual outflow was sometimes higher and sometimes similar on developed sites. Development generally reduces evapotranspiration, so annual runoff rises during drought years followed by runoff-producing rain. The increase is usually small.
- Current drainage is mostly surface rather than subsurface. Average water tables on developed deep organic soils were about 22 cm deeper than on undeveloped sites.

**Nutrients and Water Quality**
- Inorganic nitrogen losses from developed areas exceeded those from natural areas, but the concentration increase was smaller than expected.
- Organic soils lost considerably more phosphorus: weighted average total phosphorus in drainage from two developed organic soils was 1 to 2.5 mg/L, an efflux of 7 to 10 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹.
- Dissolved oxygen, biochemical oxygen demand, and pH were slightly higher in developed drainage. Ca, Mg, Cl, Na, and K changed, but these are not critical for water quality here. Cu, Zn, Mn, and Fe efflux was little affected.
- Sediment load rose modestly, mainly during clearing and land shaping. Erosion is not a major problem on flat Blacklands once development is complete.

**Microbiology**
- Fecal and total coliform counts from the pastured site were considerably higher than from natural areas. The cropped shallow organic soil also showed increased coliforms, although no domestic animals were kept there.

**Pesticides**
- [[Alachlor]] parent compound disappeared rapidly after application to organic soil, but low levels of "apparent" alachlor persisted between seasons in a biologically inactive form. No significant accumulation was detected.
- Transport of alachlor and similar pesticides in runoff from these soils does not appear to pose a significant hazard to aquatic life.
- High early-period concentrations probably came from direct spraying of ditches or spray drift. Greater care during application would reduce these hazards.
- Filter paper discs placed on the soil surface during application were the most effective way to confirm deposition. Reliable residue estimates required at least seven soil samples per plot or a composite of seven sites.

**Soil Properties and Subsidence**
- Development removed the root mat and lowered surface elevation. Bulk density rose, porosity fell, and hydraulic conductivity and drainable porosity declined. Effects grew with the depth of the organic layer.
- [[Subsidence]] was greatest immediately after development, from irreversible drying and shrinkage of the root zone. Afterward, subsidence on the deep organic pasture site was below 1 mm per year over the three-year period.
