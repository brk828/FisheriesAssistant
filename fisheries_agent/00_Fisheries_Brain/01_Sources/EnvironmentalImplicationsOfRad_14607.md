# Environmental Implications of Radioactive Waste Disposal as Related to Stream Environments

#aquatic-ecology #environmental-monitoring #radioactive-contamination #stream-ecology #water-quality #public-health

## Executive Summary

This 1959 document by [[Conrad P. Straub]], [[Abraham S. Goldin]], and [[Albert G. Friend]] of the Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center addresses the environmental implications of radioactive waste disposal in stream environments. The paper examines increasing volumes of radioactive materials entering U.S. streams from laboratories, atomic energy facilities, and fallout, estimating approximately 16 × 10⁴ cubic meters per day of radioactive waste discharge. The authors evaluate multiple stream uses (potable water, recreation, fishing, irrigation, navigation) and propose five approaches for calculating permissible discharge levels. The document emphasizes that current criteria are insufficient without knowledge of specific radionuclides present and their downstream fate, and that permissible levels must be modified based on downstream water uses and population exposures.

## Background & Description

The nuclear industry requires substantial water supplies for ore milling, reactor cooling, moderation/shielding, and waste dilution. Radioactive waste materials originate from all phases of the industry: ore processing, fissionable material production, power generation, and radionuclide use in research, industry, and medicine.

**Discharge Volumes and Activities:**
- Mills: 6.8 × 10⁴ m³/day, ~100 curies/day
- Feed Materials Plants: 0.1 × 10⁴ m³/day
- Reactor Coolant: 8.0 × 10⁴ m³/day (Hanford average since 1944), ~410 curies/day
- Isotope Users: 1.1-1.9 × 10⁴ m³/day, 1-2 curies/day
- Fallout contribution: ~460 curies/day (approximately equal to industrial operations)

Reactor cooling operations at [[Hanford]] represent approximately 48% of total estimated waste volume discharged to streams. The [[U.S. Atomic Energy Commission]] established discharge limits at one-tenth of [[National Committee on Radiation Protection and Measurements]] maximum permissible concentrations (MPC).

**Stream Uses Requiring Evaluation:**
1. Domestic water supply
2. Industrial water supply
3. Irrigation (spray vs. ditch methods)
4. Food supply (fish, wild fowl, shellfish)
5. Waste disposal
6. Recreation (bathing, boating, fishing)
7. Navigation

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Natural agents remove radioactive materials from water through concentration in clays, finely dispersed materials, and aquatic biota. Concentration factors depend on multiple variables including organism metabolism, temperature, feeding habits, and chemical composition of receiving streams.

**Bioconcentration Examples:**
- [[Columbia River]]: Biological organisms concentrated P-32 in excess of 100,000-fold due to stable phosphorus concentration of only 0.01 mg/L, necessitating a 1,000-fold reduction in maximum permissible P-32 concentration for release
- Dead organisms show more general uptake than living ones
- Radioisotopes may be metabolized along with similar non-radioactive elements (e.g., strontium in lieu of calcium)

**Environmental Media Sampling:**
The authors evaluated multiple media for monitoring effectiveness:

*Water:* Advantages include simplicity, quantitative results, legal standard basis, and human consumption relevance. Disadvantages include low activity levels, varying chemical composition, and fallout contamination.

*Fish:* Cumulative intake and human consumption relevance, but complicated by species differences, seasonal variation, temperature effects, and varying food habits.

*Muds:* Cumulative and concentrated, but qualitative, subject to bottom movement, and difficult to sample.

*Algae (sessile):* Cumulative, concentrated, fixed organisms, but semi-specific and non-quantitative with seasonal variations.

*Plankton:* Ease of collection but highly variable depending on species, feeding habits, and temperature; non-quantitative.

*Aquatic Vegetation and Shellfish:* Ease of sampling but contaminated with silt/debris, seasonal, and non-quantitative.

Gamma spectra from [[Knolls Atomic Power Laboratory]] demonstrated that each medium provided unique information not available from other media, emphasizing the need for careful selection of sampling media based on study objectives.

**Radionuclide Dynamics:**
Activity levels in organisms, silts, and bottom muds are affected by stream activity levels. These are dynamic systems with bidirectional exchange between liquid and solid phases. During low stream activity periods, activity returns from solid media where it was stored during high activity periods. Below uranium milling operations, radium associated with particulate matter settles to stream bottoms and releases slowly to surrounding water for extended periods.

**Fallout Contributions:**
In the absence of weapons tests, shorter-lived radionuclides in fallout are relatively minor. The bulk of radioactivity derives from longer-lived isotopes: cerium-144, zirconium-95, niobium-95, promethium-147, strontium-90, and cesium-137.

## Key Findings

**Strontium-90 Contamination Example ([[Cincinnati, Ohio]]):**
Daily intake from multiple environmental sources:
- Air: 0.8 µµc/day (0.04 µµc/m³, 2×10⁴ mL intake)
- Water (cistern): 0.7-44 µµc/day (0.3-20 µµc/L, 2200 mL intake)
- Water (surface): 1.3-2.0 µµc/day (0.6-0.9 µµc/L, 2200 mL intake)
- Milk: 8.5 µµc/day (8.5 µµc/L, 1000 mL intake)
- Food: 7 µµc/day (0.2-60 µµc/kg, 1200 g intake)
- **Total range: 17.0-60.3 µµc/day**

**Five Approaches for Calculating Permissible Discharge:**

**Approach 1 (Simplest):** Use 1/10 of MPC for specific radionuclide. For strontium-90, MPC = 10⁻⁷ µc/mL. Formula: (PwA)plant ≤ (MPC)x. Disadvantage: Does not account for upstream releases or other radionuclides.

**Approach 2 (Upstream Correction):** Accounts for existing radioactivity levels. Formula: (PwA)plant ≤ (MPC)x - [(PwA)upstream + (PwA)fallout]. Example: If upstream contributes 3×10⁻⁸ µc/mL Sr-90 and fallout contributes 2×10⁻⁸ µc/mL, allowable addition = 0.7×10⁻⁷ µc/mL. Disadvantage: Does not consider other exposure sources.

**Approach 3 (Multiple Source Intake):** Considers air, milk, food, and water contributions. For Sr-90 example: if air, milk, and food contribute 16.3 µµc/day and total permissible intake = 220 µµc/day (100 µµc/L × 2.2 L/day), water can contribute difference, yielding maximum concentration of 93 µµc/L or 0.9×10⁻⁷ µc/mL. Formula: kwPwA + kaPaa + kmPmA + kfPfA ≤ (MPC)x. Disadvantage: Does not account for contributions from other radionuclides in each medium.

**Approach 4 (Total Dose Calculation):** Considers radioactive material from each environmental source, calculates dose from each radionuclide intake plus external sources, subtracts from permissible dose level to determine residual allowable release. Formula incorporates dose contributions DA, DB, Dγ, Dn subtracted from permissible dose Ix, with external doses Rγ and Rn. Most comprehensive approach accounting for all exposure pathways.

**Approach 5 (Reconcentration Factors):** Accounts for activity concentration in fish, irrigated plants, and animal products from forage crops. Determines actual intake from each source and reconcentration factors. Formula modifies Approach 4 to include concentration factor (Cwb) for radionuclide A in medium b and daily intake of medium b (kb). Allows adjustment of permissible discharge levels based on total exposure (internal and external) from all sources.

**Critical Operational Findings:**

- Gross activity measurements have limited significance; specific radionuclide identification is necessary to determine actual hazard
- Small amounts of activity released require concentration schemes for analysis; gross activity determination in such samples has little value
- Studies of downstream radionuclide distribution provide information on temporary holdup or storage under given stream flow conditions
- Flow parameter knowledge enables prediction of radionuclide levels at downstream points, critical for emergency planning
- Emergency situations may require avoiding stream water intake for calculated periods; emergency water storage planning is necessary
- Possible diversion of radioactively contaminated waters can prevent entry into reservoirs where diffusion and deposition could cause prolonged contamination

**Regulatory and Management Recommendations:**

- All levels should be considered in terms of long-term intake (50 years), allowing time for operating procedure modifications before excessive exposures occur
- Each plant must obtain detailed information on nuclide composition and quantity of radioactive materials discharged
- Labeling each discharge with identifying material would facilitate environmental studies, particularly with multiple sources
- Where multiple sources discharge to a given stream, allocation of maximum amounts of specific radionuclides must be established according to downstream population exposures from all sources
- Permissible discharge levels must be modified based on downstream water uses and existing radionuclide levels

**Public Relations Requirements:**

- Public in vicinity of nuclear facilities should be completely informed of radioactive material levels released (design or accident) and consequences
- Public satisfaction requires actual data on activity levels in various environmental segments with proper interpretation
- Analyses must include water and food substances developing in or contacting potentially contaminated streams
- Public should be fully advised of precautions for controlling releases during emergencies and potential environmental consequences
- Prior knowledge, confidence in plant supervision, and full understanding of nuclear incident consequences will materially aid public acceptance

**Specific Case Studies:**

- [[Columbia River]] required lowering of P-32 maximum permissible concentration by factor of approximately 1,000 due to extreme bioconcentration
- Below uranium milling operations, radium associated with particulate matter in tailings ponds or direct stream discharge settles to stream bottom and releases slowly to surrounding water for long periods
- Gamma spectra from multiple media at [[Knolls Atomic Power Laboratory]] demonstrated each medium provided unique information not obtainable from other media

The document emphasizes that current municipal water treatment processes will probably not reduce activity levels to useable values during emergencies, necessitating temporary avoidance of stream water intake to prevent excessive downstream population exposure.
