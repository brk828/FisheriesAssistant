# Atlantic Sturgeon Management, Radiotelemetric Habitat Use in Colorado Squawfish, and Accuracy of Angler-Reported Red Drum Sizes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Acipenseridae #Cyprinidae #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This bundle contains three fisheries papers. The first, the closing section of a study on the [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser oxyrhynchus*) in South Carolina, argues that the caviar fishery selectively targets large females and recommends long-term monitoring and protective laws before stocks are depleted. The second, a 1984 study in the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*, shows that [[Radiotelemetry]] yields more representative habitat-preference data for [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) than conventional capture, which is biased by [[Electrofishing]]. The third evaluates whether size data volunteered by anglers and commercial fishers from [[Red Drum]] (*Sciaenops ocellatus*) tag returns are accurate enough for growth-rate estimation.

## Background & Description

**Atlantic sturgeon (Smith, Marchette and Ulrich).** The fish enter the fishery at an advanced age, and part of the population is suspected of being available only at multi-year intervals. Fishing pressure is shifting toward the "caviar"-producing females through larger mesh and increased effort. The reference list (about 14 entries) covers historical catch records, the [[Gulf of Mexico Sturgeon]] life history in the [[Suwannee River]], pectoral-fin-ray ageing, caviar industry reports, and the authors' own final report to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

**Colorado squawfish (Tyus, Burdick and McAda).** This is an endemic cyprinid of the Colorado River system. Its habitat is studied in turbid rivers where visual observation is impossible. The authors (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Vernal, Utah) tested whether implanted radio transmitters could replace capture-based habitat records.

**Red drum (Ferguson, Green and Matlock).** This sciaenid is tagged in Texas bays. Anglers and commercial fishers return tags with lengths and weights, which are used for growth studies. The authors (Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, Coastal Fisheries Branch) tested the reliability of those volunteered data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sturgeon:** Spawning and nursery habitats are not yet identified. The species is vulnerable to incidental capture and mortality in other fisheries, which has not been documented.
- **Squawfish:** Fish were captured by electrofishing in April and May in the Green, White and Yampa rivers. Water is turbid, and the fish are highly mobile. Substrates ranged from sand and silt to rubble, boulder and gravel, depending on season. Radiotelemetry revealed that fish sometimes select drop-offs beside mid-channel sand bars, which conventional gear cannot sample.
- **Red drum:** The tagging program covers all major Texas bays. Study fish were 295–632 mm total length at both release and recapture. Tagging was publicized through media and bait and tackle outlets, and rewards of $1–25 were paid per returned tag.

## Key Findings

**Atlantic sturgeon: management recommendations**
1. Conduct long-term analyses of fishing effort and landings. Documenting the effects of increased effort or gear selectivity will require a long sampling program, and stocks could be depleted before results are in hand.
2. Enact laws protecting sub-adults and first-time spawners.
3. Conduct environmental studies to identify spawning and nursery habitats and areas.
4. Conduct long-term tagging studies of juvenile and adult migration and homing behavior.
5. Document incidental capture and survival of sturgeon in other fisheries.

The authors conclude that effective management may be possible only once this information is available.

**Colorado squawfish: radiotelemetry versus conventional collections**
- Habitat data came from 22 radiotelemetered fish (total length given as 385–707 mm) and 241 fish at point of capture (405–707 mm). Fish were tracked with Sm-1 transmitters (AVM Company) of 11 g, with a theoretical battery life of about 200 days, operating at 40.660–40.700 kHz. Locations were recorded every 15 minutes. A fish that stayed in one spot for 30 minutes was treated as using preferred habitat.
- Radiotelemetry produced 2,329 habitat observations across the three rivers, with 1,405 of these used in the Green River comparison with 128 collected fish.
- Of the 22 tracked fish, 13 were in the Green River, 7 in the White River and 6 in the Yampa River. Thirteen Green River fish were tracked, with 7 in the Yampa and 6 in the White.
- Fish were found mostly along shorelines. Collected fish were over-represented in runs, because fish pushed from shorelines by electrical current moved into runs where they were captured.
- Mean selected conditions were:
  - Green River (13 fish): depth 1.4 m, velocity 0.2 m/s
  - Yampa River (7 fish): depth 0.94 m, velocity 0.12 m/s
  - White River (6 fish): depth 0.72 m, velocity 0.49 m/s
- Depth differed significantly between the Green River and its tributaries, but not between the White and Yampa. Velocity differed among the three rivers. Both methods gave similar results, and the authors concluded that radiotelemetry is more accurate and can reveal diel and seasonal preferences in the same fish.

**Red drum: accuracy of volunteered size data**
- The analysis used tag-recapture data from November 1975 to December 1977 for fish recaptured by Texas Parks and Wildlife Department (TPWD) staff, sport anglers and commercial fishers.
- Length-weight regressions differed significantly between data reported by fishermen and data collected by TPWD biologists (F = 6.312; P < 0.06, as printed in the source).
- Fishermen's lengths were not biased but were more variable, because they were rounded to the nearest whole or partial inch.
- Fishermen's weights were more variable, and they overestimated the weight of small fish. Growth estimates from fishermen's weights are therefore biased.
- TPWD total-length data gave the most precise growth estimates. Earlier work had reported both overestimated and underestimated lengths, and growth-rate changes proved less sensitive to error than absolute sizes.
