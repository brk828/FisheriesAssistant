# Request to Stock Native Fishes at ASU Golf Course Water Features

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #freshwater-conservation

## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from Paul C. Marsh (Associate Professor, ASU Center for Environmental Studies) to the Sun Angel Foundation requests authorization to stock approximately 1,500 juveniles each of three imperiled native southwestern "big river" fishes—[[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]—into newly constructed water features at the Arizona State University Golf Course. The proposal builds on a successful 1986 precedent at the [[ASU Research Park]], where similar rare fishes were stocked for research and educational purposes. The document is not a primary scientific study but a management/research proposal outlining rationale for using artificial urban water features as surrogate habitats for conservation research on declining native fish populations.

## Background & Description
The source text does not provide morphological descriptions, measurements, or anatomical data for the three species. It refers to them collectively as "young" or juvenile animals (~1,500 individuals per species) intended for stocking, sourced as "experimental animals" supplied by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. For detailed morphological data, see species-specific entries: [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native habitat context**: All three species are native "big river" fishes historically abundant in Arizona's major rivers (part of the [[Colorado River Basin]] native fish assemblage), now rare or extirpated in the wild.
- **Proposed surrogate habitat**: ASU Golf Course water features, described as artificial, initially fish-free environments allowing stocked native fish to grow without competitive or predatory pressure from non-natives.
- **Invasion dynamics**: Non-native fishes are expected to invade these water features via connection to the [[Salt River Project]] canal system, eventually establishing reproducing populations. This creates a natural experimental setting for studying **native-nonnative fish interactions**, a process implicated in the historical decline of native southwestern fishes.
- **Comparative research value**: The Golf Course site is intended to complement ongoing parallel research at the [[ASU Research Park]], enabling comparative ecological study across two urban surrogate habitats.
- **Conservation linkage**: Fish surviving to adulthood at this site could serve as broodstock for state and federal native fish reintroduction programs, linking the site's ecological role to broader recovery efforts for the [[Colorado River Basin]] fish fauna.

## Key Findings
- **Species requested for stocking**: [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Colorado pikeminnow), and [[Razorback Sucker]] — approximately 1,500 juveniles each.
- **Timing**: Proposed stocking window was September–October 1988.
- **Institutional support**: Concurrence and animal supply already secured from [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- **Precedent**: A comparable 1986 stocking program at the ASU Research Park was reported as highly successful, supporting research and educational activity.
- **Research rationale**:
  1. Proximity to ASU facilitates frequent scientific and student access to otherwise rare/inaccessible species.
  2. Initial absence of non-native fish allows a controlled growth period before anticipated invasion via canal connectivity.
  3. Potential to generate broodstock for state/federal reintroduction programs.
  4. High public visibility of the Golf Course supports conservation outreach and media engagement.
  5. The site offers comparative data complementing the existing Research Park program.
- **Underlying conservation problem noted**: Causes of native fish decline in Arizona's rivers are described as "not well understood," and wild populations are too scarce or remote for practical study — motivating the use of constructed habitats as research surrogates.
- **Broader significance**: The proposal frames urban/artificial water bodies as viable tools for conservation research, public education, and germplasm (broodstock) preservation for critically imperiled [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes.
