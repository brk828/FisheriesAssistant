## Executive Summary
This 1996 internal memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Parker FRO) documents a policy shift regarding the use of isolated cove habitats as **growout facilities** for native fish repatriation along the Lower Colorado River. Based on three years of operational experience, the author de-emphasizes the creation of *additional* isolated coves — particularly on **[[Lake Havasu]]** — due to recurring biological and anthropogenic failures. The memo establishes new, more stringent site-selection criteria emphasizing water depth and exchange rate, while affirming continued support for existing successful sites such as the **High Levee Pond at [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]** and cove systems on **[[Lake Mohave]]**. The ultimate management goal is increasing survival and eventual repatriation of endangered native fish — the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** and **[[Bonytail Chub]]** — into the Lower Colorado River.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data (e.g., length, weight, meristic counts) are reported in this source document, as it is a programmatic/administrative memo rather than a species morphology study. However, the two focal taxa are notable federally listed Colorado River endemics:

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — family Catostomidae; recognizable by a pronounced dorsal keel behind the head (not described in this text but relevant taxonomic context).
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — family Cyprinidae; a large-bodied Colorado River cyprinid.

Both species were tracked in this report solely via **production counts** from growout coves:
- High Levee Pond (Cibola NWR): **>500 razorback suckers** and **~150 bonytails** produced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper describes an **artificial/managed ecological niche** — isolated cove and pond habitats functioning as protective nursery/growout environments, distinct from the open reservoir or riverine habitat these species would naturally occupy.

**Key habitat/environmental factors identified as critical for growout success:**
- **Depth**: Coves require a "considerable area over six feet in depth."
- **Water exchange**: Essential via percolation or inflow; described as "imperative" regardless of cove size.
- **Water level fluctuation**: Acts as a natural exchange/cleaning mechanism.
  - [[Lake Mohave]]: ~16 ft annual fluctuation → strong reservoir-cove exchange; 8 of 9 coves go dry annually, self-cleaning of debris, effective harvesting.
  - [[Lake Havasu]]: only ~5 ft annual fluctuation → poor exchange, retained debris, harder harvesting.
- **Thermal regime**: Lake Havasu's warmer ambient temperatures increase risk of high water temperatures and **anoxia** in poorly-flushed coves; Lake Mohave's cooler regime avoids this.
- **Successful cove examples**: Office Cove, Bulkhead Cove, and Pittsburgh Point Cove (Lake Havasu) — all exhibit adequate depth/exchange in varying degrees by year.
- **Golf course ponds**: Function well as growout sites due to continuous pumping/water exchange from golf course operations — an anthropogenic analog to natural flushing.

**Threats to growout habitat integrity** (Lake Havasu coves specifically):
- Vandalism
- Exotic fish introductions
- Net breaches (containment failure)
- Parasite loads
- High water temperatures
- Anoxic conditions

## Key Findings
- **Policy change**: The Parker FRO will **de-emphasize development of new isolated coves**, especially on Lake Havasu, due to repeated biological/logistical failures.
- **Primary biological drivers** of the policy shift: high water temperatures and anoxia, both linked to insufficient water exchange in low-fluctuation reservoirs.
- **New site-selection criteria** going forward:
  1. Substantial area >6 ft depth.
  2. Reliable water exchange (percolation or inflow).
- **Lake Mohave vs. Lake Havasu contrast**: Differential reservoir fluctuation (16 ft vs. 5 ft annually) is the central explanatory variable for differing cove success rates.
- **Existing successful facilities are unaffected** — continued partnership and operation with:
  - [[Lower Colorado River Refuges]]
  - [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM)
  - High Levee Pond, Cibola NWR (documented producer of >500 razorback suckers, ~150 bonytails)
- **Future flexibility**: New coves meeting the revised criteria will still be considered, especially large water bodies and sites in other parts of Arizona.
- **Conservation goal**: Continued growout production intended to support repatriation of native [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] populations into the Lower Colorado River.
