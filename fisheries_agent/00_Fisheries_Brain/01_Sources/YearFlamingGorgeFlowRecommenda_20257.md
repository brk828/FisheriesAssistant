# Colorado Squawfish Nursery Habitat and Flow Relationships in the Green River (FY 1993 Annual Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program]] (Recovery Program Project 18-2, dated December 10, 1993) describes the second-to-last field season of a study on how [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) nursery habitat forms and persists in the [[Green River]] below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. Principal investigators were Lee Lentsch of the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR) and Jack Schmidt of Utah State University. The study began in 1990 and is ongoing. It sampled two reaches, [[Ouray Reach]] and [[Mineral Bottom]], over three to four trips in 1993 while pairing fish sampling with geomorphic surveys and flow data. The main conclusion is that baseflows for maximizing nursery habitat should be set relative to the elevation of bar surfaces built by each spring's runoff, not as an absolute discharge.

## Background & Description

The project aims to link flow regime to the availability, formation, and maintenance of Colorado Squawfish nursery habitat. Its objectives include:

- Developing definitions of macro- and meso-habitat features based on [[Fluvial Geomorphology]].
- Relating sediment transport and bar formation to nursery habitat.
- Comparing available habitat with habitat selected by young squawfish.
- Relating [[Degree-Day Accumulation]] in the main channel and in nursery habitats to fish use.
- Testing whether [[Aerial Video Imagery]] can predict year-class strength and identify "quality" habitat.
- Comparing intensive sampling with standardized monitoring data to improve [[Standardized Monitoring Program]] procedures.
- Coordinating with studies of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations to avoid duplicated effort.

The study is presented as a way to evaluate and possibly improve existing monitoring under the [[Integrated Standardized Monitoring Program]] and to clarify how flows affect habitat availability.

FY 93 tasks were to obtain recommended flows, sample habitats and fish populations, evaluate aerial video as a habitat quantifier, and correlate flows with optimum habitat at Ouray and Mineral Bottom.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and physical conditions.** Spring 1993 high flows scoured silt from backwaters, a change larger than any seen earlier in the study. Flows during the sampling trips were:

- Ouray, April: roughly 4,000 cfs, with incessant rain and shoreline silt run-off. Many backwaters held viscous silt muck that likely made them unusable to fish.
- Ouray, August: about 1,400 cfs.
- Ouray, Sept 30–Oct 1: about 1,500 cfs.
- Peak discharge for the year was 20,300 ft³/s.

**Habitat types.** Fish were grouped by habitat: main channel (MC), secondary channel (SC), backwater (BW), and tributary (Tr) habitats. The study distinguished "permanent" habitats (secondary-channel backwaters and flooded tributaries) from those that are seasonal. Tributaries were unavailable as habitat during low-flow months, and by July 1993 they had become isolated pools cut off by sandbars at their mouths.

**Habitat changes after high flows.** The flood produced fewer habitat sites than expected. Rather than many deeply scoured secondary channels behind sandbars, most habitats were embayments formed by migrating bars. Sand substrates were clean, except in isolated tributary-mouth pools. Algae, ephemeropterans, and odonates were still found in shallow, warm margins, which indicated continued productivity.

**Native and non-native fish.** The squawfish sample was mostly young-of-year (YOY) fish, along with some juveniles. Native species included [[Gila Species]] YOY, [[Native Sucker Species]], and [[Speckled Dace]]. Non-native fish dominated many samples, mainly [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]], with fewer [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Northern Pike]] (one 200 mm individual in August).

## Key Findings

### Ouray Reach

- **Trip I (April 4–7):** Sampling was hampered by high water and silt, and low squawfish numbers may bias habitat-quality estimates. Catch rates were down, and variability within and between habitat types increased. Presence/absence analysis would likely misrepresent habitat use, so the authors recommend a [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE) measure alongside presence/absence.
- **Trip II (August 8–11):** Flows were about 1,400 cfs. Large numbers of YOY suckers were collected. Lethal sampling was needed for positive identification because of small fish size and high summer temperatures. Preserved samples are to be processed in fall 1993 or spring 1994.
- **Trip III (Sept 30–Oct 1):** Flows were about 1,500 cfs. Most squawfish measured 30–45 mm. Sixty-seven percent of sampled backwaters held squawfish, and 83% of occupied backwaters had fewer than five. This was a decline from the previous fall, when 590 squawfish were sampled from 61 backwaters.
- **Thermographs:** Hourly temperature loggers were placed in selected backwaters in summer. They will allow calculation of temperature units over daily and longer intervals, so that squawfish distribution can be related to long-term thermal conditions instead of point samples. Backwater abundance peaked in summer 1994 compared with a fall maximum in 1993.

- **March 23:** Squawfish showed slight selectivity for secondary-channel backwaters and flooded tributaries, the deepest and most permanent habitats. Squawfish occurred in 53.3% of available habitats, compared with 33.3% in September 1992. Squawfish often occupied flooded vegetation, which was not present at other flows, so it is unclear whether this habitat was preferred or simply newly available. Length ranged from 26 to 85 mm (mean 59 mm), compared with a mean of 45 mm in September 1992.
- **July 21–22:** Only 14 squawfish were collected, including 2 YOY, compared with 285 YOY in July 1992. The authors attribute the difference mainly to delayed spawning after cool spring temperatures and prolonged high flows rather than to reduced spawning success. Twenty-six Gila YOY were collected, in contrast to very few on earlier trips. Squawfish occurred in 30% of available habitats, but most were juveniles that should be excluded from nursery habitat analyses. YOY were found in only 2 (13%) of sites. Squawfish were collected only in backwaters, with no relation to depth.
- **August 11–12:** A total of 499 YOY squawfish were identified, including released and preserved fish. High air and water temperatures caused mortality, and no juveniles were collected. Only nine Gila YOY were collected. Squawfish occurred in nearly every available habitat, which masked selectivity. Of 38 habitats sampled, 7 secondary-channel habitats held squawfish, with the single exception of one flowing-water site. The number of fish in backwaters shifted sharply between July and August, when juveniles and chubs in July were replaced by YOY squawfish and suckers in August.
- **September 28–30:** Preserved YOY squawfish were collected, and no juveniles were taken. Selectivity for secondary-channel backwaters returned. Squawfish were present in all 10 secondary-channel habitats within the 31 habitats sampled. The authors note that the high August catch may partly reflect sampling timing.
- **Aerial video:** Aerial video prints were most useful for tracking which backwaters persisted over time and which changed location on a sediment feature.

### Geomorphology Assessment

- Ten channel cross-sections, first established by [[Andrews and Nelson Model|Andrews and Nelson (1986)]] and resurveyed in 1987, 1989, and 1990, were reoccupied in November 1992 and in June, August, and November 1993. Topographic maps of the bed were produced for a 1-mile study reach.
- Results showed:
  - Significant bed scour of 2–3 m this year and aggradation of bar surfaces.
  - Aggradation caused a net loss of total available nursery habitat at low discharges but a smaller loss of high-quality habitat.
  - Nursery habitat on bars develops during late recession from peak flow.
- Significant bed scour had not occurred since 1986, the last high peak flow, although aggradation has occurred in some flow years.
- At the same discharge, available habitat in 1993 was about 30% less than in 1992, but the reduction in high-quality habitat was much smaller.
- Maximum available habitat appeared at a discharge of roughly 40–100 m³/s, perhaps near 70 m³/s. The 1993–94 curve will not be known until spring 1994 data are available.
- Habitat suitability curves differ from year to year, but all years may fit a single curve if discharge is converted to relative bar stage. Maximum available habitat is expected at a critical elevation about 1 to 1.3 m below the average height of the highest bar surface. This level is probably equivalent to the average base of scour channels and dune scour pits.

### Management Implications

- Baseflow recommendations for the Green River should not rely on any absolute discharge value.
- If the goal is to maximize available nursery habitat, baseflows should be set relative to the average elevation of bar surfaces built by each spring's runoff.
- Models are needed to estimate the magnitude and duration of peak flows that build bars to specific elevations, and habitat suitability curves expressed in terms of relative stage can then be used to quantify appropriate baseflows.
- Future work will refine habitat suitability curves and apply a model to predict average bar-surface elevation.
