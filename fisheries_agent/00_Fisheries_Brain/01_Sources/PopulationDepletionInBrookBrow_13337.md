# Population Depletion in Brook, Brown, and Rainbow Trout Stocked in the Blackledge River, Connecticut, 1942

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1942 study, published in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]], tested how quickly heavy angling pressure depletes hatchery trout stocked in a 1.7-mile, weir-enclosed section of the [[Blackledge River]] in Connecticut. A total of 4,757 marked [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked in three plantings. Anglers recovered about 80 per cent of the available population, mostly within a few days of each planting. Catch per unit effort was closely tied to remaining population density, except for one brown trout planting. The authors conclude that the Connecticut stocking model, with high initial pressure and rapid depletion, yields only poor fishing between plantings, and that smaller, more frequent stockings would extend good fishing.

## Background & Description

- **Authors:** Lyle M. Thorpe, H. John Rayner, and Dwight A. Webster, Connecticut State Board of Fisheries and Game (Hartford) and the Laboratory of Limnology and Fisheries, Cornell University (Ithaca, NY).
- **Context:** Connecticut provides trout fishing in larger streams mainly by stocking legal-sized yearling and two-year-old trout. A 1933 [[Tagging Experiment]] (Cobb, 1934) found only about one-third of stocked fish were recovered by angling in the current season.
- **Study site:** A State-owned 1.7-mile section of the Blackledge River, believed typical of Connecticut stocked waters. Summer water temperatures often approach the upper tolerance limits of trout; afternoon temperatures of 80–82 °F occurred on five consecutive days during the 1942 experiment. The bed is rubble, boulders, and some sand, with stream improvement structures such as V-dams and current deflectors.
- **Design:** Fish-tight weirs with two-way traps at each end. Fishing by permit only, under constant patrol, with anglers checked in and out and their hours, catch, method (flies or bait), and home distance recorded. State rules applied: six-inch minimum length and a 15-trout daily creel limit. Fishing was closed April 30 to May 6 because of fire hazard.
- **Stocking:** Fish were 8–12 inches total length, marked by fin clip (right ventral, right pectoral, or adipose). Planting I (April 17, 2,185 fish, 1,285 per mile) preceded the season. Planting II (April 30, 1,500 fish, 882 per mile) and Planting III (June 8, 890 fish, 524 per mile) occurred during it. Delivery was by tank truck.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Movement:** Of 79 trout tagged while moving in or out of the enclosure, 52 were leaving, mostly unmarked recent hatchery fish that had entered before the weirs were installed on April 16. Only about 1 per cent of planted marked fish left their planting areas. The farthest recaptured fish travelled 2 miles, and none crossed between weirs.
- **Mortality:** Planting II brook and brown trout developed furunculosis (ulcer disease) about five days after stocking, probably from transfer into warmer river water. Recorded deaths totalled 821 across all plantings, a minimum because carcasses in deep pools, under rocks, or taken by scavengers went undetected.
- **Fate of remainder:** Of the theoretical 761 marked fish not caught, 11 counts from September 1 to 20 averaged 30 fish between the weirs (range 25–87). Predation and unnoticed natural death were presumed.
- **Natural reproduction and wild stock:** Of 97 unmarked trout caught, 23 were judged "wild" by high coloration. Brown trout fry and fingerlings were seined, confirming some natural reproduction.
- **Water temperature and behaviour:** Mean temperatures for the first five fishing days after each planting were 52 °F (Planting I), 59 °F (Planting II), and 68 °F (Planting III). Trout gathered in deep holes in early spring and moved to riffles later.

## Key Findings

- **Total catch and returns:** 3,446 marked trout were taken by 3,152 anglers over 9,746 hours (about 0.4 trout per hour), with an overall recovery of 82 per cent of the available population (brook 83%, brown 79%, rainbow 83%; Plantings I, II, III: 89%, 71%, 79%).
- **Rate of depletion:** Brook trout were depleted fastest, brown trout slowest, and rainbow trout intermediate. About three-quarters of each planting's catch was taken within four days, a pattern the authors attribute to anglers "following the hatchery truck."
- **Catch per hour:** Only five days (April 18; May 7–8; June 8–9) exceeded the one-trout-per-hour benchmark of [[Schuck]] (1942). Catch per unit effort correlated strongly with remaining population in all populations except brown trout Planting II (r = 0.43).
- **Brown trout anomaly:** Newly planted brown trout in Planting II showed no relation to effort (r = 0.24), whereas brown trout from Planting I were significantly related (r = 0.72). The authors could not separate such behavioural differences from population-depletion effects.
- **Seasonal yield increase:** Regression slopes rose progressively across plantings; the slope difference between Plantings I and III was highly significant for brown and rainbow trout. Three explanations were weighed: fewer anglers (722, 456, 238 anglers over the first five days), greater angler skill, and rising water temperature, with the last judged most plausible.
- **Angler catch distribution:** Roughly one-third of anglers took about three-quarters of the catch in the first days after stocking. Over 70 per cent of anglers caught nothing after the second day. A 15-trout limit allowed heavy early removal, and the authors suggest a lower limit (e.g., 5 trout) would help but would not solve the problem.
- **Management recommendations:** Brown trout provided the most sustained fishing and total yield comparable to the other species. Because depletion stems from the distribution of angling pressure, the authors suggest reducing planted numbers (lowering initial yield) and stocking more often (extending good fishing days). The study partly contradicts [[Schuck]] (1942), who recommended brook trout as the more economical choice.
- **Limitations:** The experiment was in one stream, with publicity and wartime population increases likely inflating angling pressure. The authors note that the 1933 tagging results gave far lower yields.
