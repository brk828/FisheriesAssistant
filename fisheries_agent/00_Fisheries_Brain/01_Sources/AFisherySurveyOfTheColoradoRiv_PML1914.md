# Fishery Survey of the Colorado River Below Boulder Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This early fishery survey, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and Stanford University, assesses the [[Colorado River]] below [[Boulder Dam]] after the dam was built. Dam-regulated water from the deep outlet of [[Lake Mead]] is cold and clear, which has turned roughly 25 to 30 miles of river into good [[Rainbow Trout]] water. The survey reports temperatures, physical and chemical conditions, the food base, trout feeding and growth, and past stocking. It concludes that the trout population is food-limited by neither algae nor invertebrates, but that natural reproduction is unproven. It recommends stocking larger fish and keeping regulations in place until spawning is studied.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Colorado River has its headwaters in high mountain areas of Utah, Colorado, Wyoming, New Mexico, Nevada, and Arizona. Its channel runs through arid country, cutting deep, straight-walled canyons. Boulder Dam impounds Lake Mead and traps the river's silt load, so water released downstream is nearly silt-free.
- **Study context:** Interest in the river came from sportsmen's organizations, civic clubs, and state game agencies in Nevada and Arizona. Earlier unpublished reports by [[Davis and Needham]] and others informed the work. The author drew on the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[National Park Service]], and on local wardens and operators.
- **Physical features:** Releases come from outlets at about the 900-foot and 1,050-foot levels of the reservoir. Water from the 900-foot outlet ranged from about 54 to 57°F and from the 1,050-foot outlet from about 55 to 61°F in 1940–1941 (OCR-degraded values).
- **Access:** Canyon walls rise 400 to 500 feet. Road access is limited to two dry washes on the Arizona side and one at [[Eldorado Canyon]]. Flash floods are a summer hazard. Boating is dangerous, especially in underpowered craft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and channel**
- Flows at the time of study were about 27,000 to 35,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), with a normal flow of about 20,000 cfs and a reported range of 4,000 to 12,000 cfs. The river is about 500 feet wide, giving about 1,600 acres of trout water in the section studied.
- The river falls about 3 feet per mile. Rapids are steeper, and quiet stretches may fall only 1 or 2 feet per mile. Average flow speed is about 4 miles per hour.
- Notable rapids include [[Ringbolt Rapids]] and an unnamed rapid between the dam and Eldorado Canyon.
- Water levels fluctuate by no more than about one foot in a day in normal operation. Over six years, flows ranged from roughly 2,500 to 35,000 cfs, and the flow was cut entirely off on one day in February. Such fluctuation affects spawning and egg development.

**Temperature**
- Water temperatures from 1938 to 1941 (USGS data) varied by only one or two degrees Fahrenheit between winter and summer.
- During the August–November 1941 spill, average temperatures at Willow Beach reached about 61–69°F, and [[Eldorado Canyon]] reached about 67°F on October 31, 1941, with discharge at the dam at 64°F. The survey warns that prolonged high temperatures could harm trout and recommends avoiding further spill.

**Chemistry**
- Water is clear and highly alkaline, with pH about 8.2. Dissolved carbonates and bicarbonates total about 121 ppm. Calcium sulfate (gypsum) and magnesium salts occur in small amounts.
- Dissolved oxygen is high because water is aerated through turbulent flow below the dam, rapids, and riffles.

**Substrate and productivity**
- Gravel shoals and riffles are covered by a near-continuous blanket of green algae, mostly [[Cladophora]]. Clarity lets light reach the bed, so algal growth is widespread.
- Silt bars in the lower river are suspended by current, which increases turbidity. Silt and debris reduce productivity, and the survey recommends concentrating management above that point.
- The surrounding terrain is largely barren. Riparian vegetation such as [[Cottonwood]], [[Tamarisk]], and willows grows only where soils allow.

**Aquatic life**
- Aquatic plants are scarce apart from algae. One plant was found in stomach contents, and bulrushes and sedges occur in quiet areas.
- Bottom samples were poor. Dredge samples yielded almost no organisms, but riffle samples held abundant [[Cladophora]] and [[Caddisfly Larvae]], common [[Midge Larvae]], rare stonefly nymphs, and diverse [[Mayfly Nymphs]] (Baetis). Protozoa and diatoms were also present.
- Terrestrial insects are sparse, which limits their contribution to trout diet.
- The river fish fauna recorded includes [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), plus a [[Largemouth Bass]] and a small catfish found in trout stomachs.

## Key Findings

**Trout growth and condition**
- The 37 trout measured ranged up to a 17.5-inch fish, the longest taken during the study, which weighed 1 pound 6 ounces dressed.
- Condition coefficients (based on total length) were about 1.00–1.01 for 10–15 inch fish. The survey notes that coefficients based on standard length would be higher, for example 1.66 instead of 1.09 for 10- to 11-inch fish.
- Scale-based aging proved unreliable, because growth rings were too uniform. Some scales showed possible spawning checks near the edge, and eroded scales had 3 to 6 new circuli added by early November. Rapid growth is indicated by widely spaced circuli, but growth rates cannot be determined without marking and recovery of stocked fish.

**Diet (48 trout collected August 17 – November 16, 1941)**
- Nearly all stomachs (46 of 48) contained algae, which made up 70 to 98 percent of contents, mostly [[Cladophora]].
- [[Mayfly Nymphs]] (Baetis) occurred in 81 percent of stomachs and midge larvae, pupae, and adults in 84 percent.
- [[Daphnia]] appeared in 13 stomachs and [[Diaptomus]] in 9. [[Caddisfly Larvae]] occurred in 50 percent.
- Terrestrial items included spiders (9 stomachs), beetles (4), a grasshopper, and a fly. Anglerworms and salmon eggs were common, probably from bait. Stomachs were generally full.
- Earlier observations by Davis and Needham found six fish feeding on the bottom, mainly on algae, and two feeding near the surface, mainly on terrestrial insects. Their findings suggest trout may be using algae as food, but digestion of algae was not confirmed.

**Natural reproduction**
- No definite evidence shows successful spawning. Fishermen reported small trout in January and February, which might indicate natural reproduction, but these fish could also have come from stocking, since only yearlings were stocked then.
- Gravel is plentiful and fluctuation is limited, so conditions are not considered hostile to spawning. The survey calls for detailed study of reproduction before a long-range management plan is set.

**Stocking history**
- Stocking records (Table 1) show the first rainbow trout planted below Boulder Dam in the early 1930s. Plantings have included four-inch, six-inch, and one-half-pound-size fish, and some were placed at Eldorado Canyon and Willow Beach.
- In December 1941 and January 1942 about 62,000 three-inch rainbows were delivered from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service hatchery at Springville, Utah, for spring planting.
- About 100,000 freshwater shrimp were planted in April, but none were found in trout stomachs.

**Management recommendations (summary)**
1. Keep current trout-fishing seasons and creel limits until spawning is better understood. Present creel limits are considered satisfactory.
2. Harmonize differing Arizona and Nevada fishing regulations where possible, and impose a minimum size limit of 7 inches.
3. No further attempts to introduce food organisms are needed.
4. Annual stocking of about 30,000 six-inch rainbows would meet current fishing demand if angling pressure does not rise. Fish should be held in rearing ponds until 5 to 7 inches long; if not, 60,000 to 75,000 fingerlings are advised. Stocking yearlings avoids heavy predation on young fish.
