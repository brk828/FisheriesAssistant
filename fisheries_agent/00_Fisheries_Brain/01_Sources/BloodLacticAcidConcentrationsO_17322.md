# Blood Lactic Acid Concentration of Unexercised and Exercised Mature Carp in Winter and Summer

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinus-carpio

## Executive Summary

This experimental study measured [[Blood Lactic Acid]] concentrations in mature [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) before and for 16 hours after 15 minutes of [[Forced Exercise]], in two seasons: February-March 1961 (1°C) and July 1961 (24°C). In winter, mean blood lactic acid roughly doubled, from 9 mg % (unexercised) to 22 mg % immediately after exercise, stayed elevated for at least 2.0-2.5 hours, and had returned to baseline by 4.0-4.4 hours. Sex had no detectable effect on mean values in either season. In July, individual variability was greater, no significant treatment differences were detected, and the highest individual values of the study were recorded. The author attributes the summer results to possible temperature effects, an insufficient holding period, and greater uncontrolled activity, and notes that the study design (group exercise, fixed sampling windows) limits comparison with earlier work.

## Background & Description

Lactic acid is a central variable in fish physiology. Its accumulation in blood after muscular activity has been linked to mortality following hyperactivity, rigor mortis, and carbohydrate metabolism. Earlier studies measured blood lactic acid in several species, including [[Kamloops Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Sockeye Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Steelhead Trout]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], and [[Chinook Salmon]], and under stressors such as handling, transportation, trolling, hooking, tagging, and air exposure. Only two earlier studies examined carp: Secondat and Diaz (1942) and Black (1955). Both exercised fish individually, whereas the present study exercised them in groups.

The purpose of this study was to determine the effect of forced muscular exercise on blood lactic acid in mature carp in winter and summer, and to track changes during post-exercise recovery.

**Study design**

- Two collections were made. The winter group came from [[Lower Gar Lake]] and was held 19 days in a hatchery-pond crib near the Spirit Lake Biology Station before testing on February 28 and March 1. The summer group came from [[East Okoboji Lake]] and was held one day in a lake crib before testing on July 21 and 22.
- Each experiment used five treatments of 10 fish each: unexercised; 0.0-0.5 h; 2.0-2.5 h; 4.0-4.4 h; and 16.0-16.4 h after exercise. Fish were not fed.
- Forced exercise was 15 minutes of chasing in the crib with a dip-net, applied to the whole group at once. Each fish was sampled only once.
- Blood was drawn by cardiac puncture after stunning and an incision. Winter samples were 1 ml; summer samples averaged 0.3 ml (range 0.2-0.4 ml). Samples were deproteinized in 10% trichloroacetic acid and stored at -14°C.
- Lactic acid was measured with a modified colorimetric method (Barker and Summerson, as outlined in Hawk, Oser and Summerson, 1954) on a Bausch and Lomb Spectronic 20 spectrophotometer. Duplicate determinations on 18 filtrates differed by a mean of 3 mg % (range 0-12 mg %).
- Sampling a 10-fish group took 18-34 minutes. The average time from netting to completed sampling per fish was 2.5 minutes (range 0.9-6.5) in winter and 1.1 minutes (range 0.2-2.5) in summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Winter conditions (February-March 1961):** Surface water was about 1°C and clear, so fish were visible. They showed only limited voluntary activity.

**Summer conditions (July 1961):** Surface water was about 24°C. Turbidity prevented observation. Voluntary activity was thought to be higher than in winter because most fish had head injuries, probably from attempts to escape the crib.

**Maturity and holding:** Most carp were mature, judged by well-developed gonads. Nine carp were excluded from analysis because of lactic acid determination errors, immaturity, or much larger size. The author notes this makes the retained sample more homogeneous but less random.

**Temperature and physiology:** The author suggests temperature may affect blood lactic acid through differences in muscular activity and diffusion of lactate from muscle to blood. Comparisons across studies were limited because the unexercised means differed only slightly across temperatures, and the 24°C data were not significantly different, so they were of little comparative value.

## Key Findings

**Winter (February-March, 1°C)**

- Unexercised mean blood lactic acid: 9 mg % (range 6-16 mg %, n = 10).
- Immediately after exercise (0.0-0.5 h): mean 22 mg % (range 11-34 mg %, n = 10), an approximately twofold increase.
- Elevated levels persisted for at least 2.0-2.5 h (mean 23 mg %, range 9-44 mg %).
- Levels returned toward baseline by 4.0-4.4 h (mean 12 mg %, range 5-18 mg %) and at 16.0-16.4 h (mean 13 mg %, range 5-23 mg %).
- Treatment means did not differ significantly among unexercised, 4.0-4.4 h, and 16.0-16.4 h groups. The 0.0-0.5 h and 2.0-2.5 h means were significantly higher than those three.
- No significant sex difference was detected.
- No further rise occurred after exercise, though the author notes the chosen sampling times may have missed a later peak.

**Summer (July, 24°C)**

- Variability among individuals was greater than in winter, and the highest individual blood lactic acid values of the study occurred in July.
- No significant differences were detected among unexercised and exercised groups, or between sexes.
- Probable explanations offered: temperature effects, a short holding period, and greater variation in voluntary and experimental activity.

**Sex and size**

- Winter: female and male mean total lengths did not differ significantly, but female mean weight was significantly greater (female 1.74 kg vs. male 1.39 kg in the analysed sample).
- Summer: females were significantly greater than males in both mean length (56 cm vs. 43 cm) and weight (2.30 kg vs. 1.10 kg).
- Sample sizes for analysis: winter 29 males and 19 females; summer 31 males and 11 females.

**Variance and statistical treatment**

- Variability increased with the mean in both seasons, with standard deviation roughly proportional to the mean (constant coefficient of variation).
- Bartlett's test showed heterogeneous variances in both experiments.
- A log10 transformation was applied before factorial analysis of variance. Significant effects appeared only among treatment means in the winter experiment (significance level P = 0.05). Multiple range tests followed [[Kramer Multiple Range Test]].

**Comparison with other studies**

- Black (1955) reported a six- to eightfold increase in carp at 11.5°C, compared with about twofold at 1°C in this study. The author notes these differences are hard to interpret given the methods used.
- Denyes and Joseph (1956) found no consistent relationship between temperature (5-35°C) and blood lactic acid in unexercised [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Black et al. (1962) reported consistently higher mean blood lactic acid in female than male [[Rainbow Trout]]. This study found no such difference in carp.
- The increase in variability with the mean, seen here and in Black (1955), was described as previously unnoted and potentially important for interpreting such data. The author calls for further research.

**Methodological caveats**

- Group exercise may produce a smaller response than individual exercise (Shlaifer, 1938), and the sampling window may allow further change during sampling.
- Smaller blood volumes in summer were judged unlikely to introduce serious error.
- Exclusion of nine fish was not random.
