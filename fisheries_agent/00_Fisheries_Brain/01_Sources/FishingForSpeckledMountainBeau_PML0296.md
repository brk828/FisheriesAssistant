# Fishing for Speckled Mountain Beauties in Old Newspapers and Journals

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of *Wildlife Views* contains two items. The lead article by David E. Brown traces the history of recreational trout fishing for Arizona's native "speckled mountain trout" using 1870s–1910s newspapers, journals and early naturalist accounts. It describes how railroads brought tourists, how a territorial fish commission began stocking non-native trout after 1891, and how native populations declined under stocking, hybridization and heavy harvest. A shorter companion piece profiles the [[Badger]] (*Taxidea taxus*) in Arizona, covering its biology, status and management. A fragment of an unrelated centipede item appears at the end and is not analyzed.

## Background & Description

**Native trout and early anglers.** Early Arizona settlers, and the [[Apache]] and [[Yavapai]] peoples, had little interest in recreational fishing, though Arizona's high streams held abundant native trout known variously as speckled, mountain or yellow-bellied trout. An 1878 *Phoenix Herald* notice touted the upper [[Salt River]], [[White River]] and [[Black River]] as well stocked with speckled mountain trout, and fishing was promoted to out-of-state tourists.

**Railroad-era sportsmen.** After the railroad between Albuquerque and Flagstaff was completed, fly-fishing tourists arrived. Among the earliest was [[J. R. W. Hitchcock]], who fished [[Oak Creek]] in 1885 and published his account in *Outing* magazine. He described the local trout as very dark, nearly black, marked with fine spots, and longer in proportion to weight than Eastern brook trout.

**Key witnesses.**
- [[Edgar A. Mearns]], surgeon-naturalist stationed at Camp Verde (1884–1889), fished native trout and described them in detail. His field notes record a trout of about 2¼ pounds taken in Oak Creek in May 1889 (the figure is partly illegible in the scan), which officers estimated had weighed more when caught. This fish is linked to the [[Gila Trout]], and the article calls it probably the largest Gila trout ever recorded in the wild.
- [[Stanley Sykes]], who arrived in Arizona in 1896, reported native trout as long as 16 inches in Oak Creek. He later observed that introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] were displacing natives through competition and hybridization.
- E. C. Becker recalled White Mountain native trout as so plentiful that a boy could catch 100 in a few hours.
- In 1910, [[Dillon Wallace]] and guide John Lewis fished the West Fork of the White River on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], at an elevation above 9,000 feet, catching fish 8–12 inches long.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Policy and stocking.** The 1891 Territorial Legislature authorized a three-member [[Arizona Fish Commission]] to take advantage of a federal program to cultivate food fish. The commission received $500 for purchasing and releasing fish. Its first trout stocking occurred in October 1891. Flagstaff boosters pushed for trout stocking in Oak Creek, and a 1892 newspaper announced trout in Oak Creek, after a commissioner brought in fish from Leadville, Colorado.

**Harvest and law.** Excessive harvest was a documented problem. A 1894 report described one group catching more than 50 trout in Oak Creek and another party selling fish in Flagstaff. Gunpowder and seines had been outlawed since 1887. A territorial statute of 1886 prohibited taking or selling brook or mountain trout between November 1 and April 1, but it did not place a bag or possession limit. Revised in 1897, the closed season was extended to September 1–June 1. The article notes that stocked fish appeared to compensate for harvest losses.

**Stocking expansion.** Stocking grew after 1900. A 1908 shipment of 10,000 rainbow trout from the federal hatchery at Pueblo, Colorado, was planned for Oak Creek. Local stockings of brook and rainbow trout occurred in the Catalina Mountains, the Pinaleño Mountains, and the Graham Mountains. Plantings increased in the Gila and [[Verde River]] systems after 1907. All fish caught in Oak Creek after 1913 were rainbows or hybrids. Brook trout reached the upper [[Little Colorado River]] by 1917, and the Black River drainages received cutthroat and rainbow trout after 1920. Once hatchery programs were established, these stockings were believed to have nearly eliminated the native trout.

**Current management.** The [[Apache Trout]] is managed on waters within the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests as a renewable natural resource. The [[Gila Trout]] was listed as Endangered, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department recommended downlisting it to Threatened.

**Badger ecology (companion article).** In Arizona, badgers are common statewide and occupy grasslands, farmland, desert scrub and other treeless areas. The state type locality is the San Pedro River, Cochise County. Badgers eat small mammals such as ground squirrels, along with birds, reptiles, eggs, insects and carrion. They are primarily nocturnal and do not hibernate. They are solitary except during breeding, when embryonic diapause delays birth until spring. Litters range from one to five pups. Badgers sometimes forage alongside [[Coyote]]s, which capture rodents flushed from burrows. The species is not listed as a Species of Special Concern in Arizona, and Commission Order 13 allows unlimited take. Arizona has no management needs identified, though western concern over population declines is growing.

## Key Findings

- Arizona's native speckled mountain trout were abundant and prized sport fish in the late 1800s. Anglers reported fish up to 16 inches and a possible Gila trout of about 2¼ pounds.
- Railroad access and tourism created fishing demand. The territorial government responded by creating the [[Arizona Fish Commission]] in 1891 and beginning trout stocking that same year.
- Early harvest was substantial and largely unregulated by bag limits. Laws restricted seasons, gear and sales, but not catch numbers.
- Stocking of rainbow, brook and cutthroat trout led to competition, hybridization and near-loss of native populations. By 1913, Oak Creek catches were all rainbows or hybrids.
- Some native populations survive. [[Apache Trout]] are managed as a renewable resource, and [[Gila Trout]] status was under review for downlisting.
- The companion badger article documents a common, widespread species with no identified management need in Arizona, regulated by an unlimited take order.
