# Endangered and Threatened Wildlife and Plants: Proposed Critical Habitat for the Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #freshwater-conservation #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1999 Federal Register proposed rule from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) responds to a court order (*Southwest Center for Biological Diversity v. Clark*) mandating designation of critical habitat for two imperiled small-bodied cyprinid fishes native to the Gila River Basin: the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Both species were listed as Threatened in 1986, with critical habitat designation deferred and later vacated by litigation (*Catron County Board of Commissioners v. USFWS*, 1994) over NEPA compliance failures. The current proposal designates approximately 1,443 km (894 mi) of stream habitat across the [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Black River]], [[Verde River]], and [[San Pedro River]] systems in Arizona and New Mexico. The designation is organized into seven geographic "complexes" reflecting both occupied habitat and unoccupied areas deemed essential for reestablishment, reflecting the species' collapse to 10–20% of their historical ranges due to habitat degradation and nonnative species invasion.

## Background & Description

### Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)
- Small, slim-bodied fish, **<80 mm (3 in)** total length
- Very silvery lateral coloration
- Distinctive spines present in both the **dorsal fin** and **pelvic fins** (diagnostic feature; Minckley 1973)
- Sole species in the monotypic genus *Meda*
- Type locality: Rio San Pedro, Arizona (collected 1851; described by Girard, 1856)

### Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*, syn. *Rhinichthys cobitis*)
- Small, slender, elongated body form, **<80 mm (3 in)** total length
- **Olivaceous** coloration
- **Oblique (slanting), terminal mouth**
- Eyes markedly directed upward — an adaptation for benthic riffle-dwelling life
- Type locality: Rio San Pedro, Arizona (collected 1851; described by Girard, 1865)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species are specialist inhabitants of **perennial, unregulated riverine systems** in the arid Southwest, exhibiting strong dependence on natural hydrological disturbance regimes.

### Spikedace Habitat Requirements
- Moderate to large perennial streams
- Shallow riffles with **sand, gravel, and rubble** substrates and moderate-to-swift current
- Swift pools over sand/gravel
- Specific microhabitats: **shear zones** (rapid flow bordering slow flow), **sheet flow** at heads of mid-channel bars, and **eddies** at downstream riffle edges
- Elevation range: up to ~2,000 m (6,500 ft)
- Historically distributed throughout the [[Gila River Basin]], including the Verde, Agua Fria, Salt, San Pedro, and San Francisco subbasins

### Loach Minnow Habitat Requirements
- Small to large perennial streams
- Shallow, **turbulent riffles** dominated by **cobble** substrate with swift currents
- Uses interstitial spaces between/behind larger substrate particles for resting and spawning
- Highly sensitive to **fine sediment infilling** of interstitial spaces — considered a key threat indicator
- Elevation range: up to ~2,500 m (8,200 ft)
- Historically ranged through the Gila mainstem, Verde, Salt, San Pedro, and San Francisco subbasins

### Shared Ecological Drivers
- **Recurrent natural flooding** and an intact, natural [[Hydrograph]] are critical for maintaining suitable habitat structure and for allowing native fish to outcompete invading nonnative species (Propst et al. 1986; Minckley and Meffe 1987)
- Both species require **interconnected stream complexes** allowing movement/recolonization between habitat patches
- High **genetic differentiation** documented among remnant populations (Tibbets 1992, 1993), elevating the conservation value of each surviving population
- Both species co-occurred historically in most Gila Basin streams, and East Fork Black River loach minnow presence is used as an indicator of restoration suitability for spikedace

## Key Findings

### Population Status & Decline
- **Spikedace**: Restricted to ~445 km (276 mi) of stream — only **10–15%** of historical range. Currently common only in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and portions of the upper Gila River (NM). No remnant populations in the Agua Fria, Salt, or San Francisco/Blue subbasins.
- **Loach Minnow**: Restricted to ~645 km (400 mi) of stream — only **15–20%** of historical range. Currently common only in Aravaipa Creek, the Blue River, and limited portions of the San Francisco, upper Gila, and Tularosa Rivers. **Completely extirpated from the Verde subbasin.**
- Primary threats: habitat destruction, and competition/predation from **nonnative aquatic species**.

### Proposed Critical Habitat Designation
- Total proposed: **~1,443 km (894 mi)** for both species combined
- **Loach minnow**: entire designation applies (41 reaches across 26 streams, 7 complexes)
- **Spikedace**: ~1,325 km (822 mi) overlapping designation (31 reaches across 26 streams, 7 complexes)
- Seven shared geographic complexes:
  1. **Verde River complex** (AZ) — genetically/morphologically distinct spikedace population; loach minnow extirpated but targeted for reestablishment
  2. **Black River complex** (AZ) — Salt River subbasin remnant; East Fork Black River loach minnow population discovered 1996
  3. **Tonto Creek complex** (AZ) — restoration-focused, high native fish community integrity
  4. **Middle Gila/Lower San Pedro/Aravaipa Creek complex** (AZ) — core stronghold population in Aravaipa Creek
  5. **Middle–Upper San Pedro River complex** (AZ) — type locality region; currently unoccupied but high restoration priority
  6. **Gila Box/San Francisco River complex** (AZ/NM) — largest restoration area; includes Eagle Creek (only remaining spikedace pop. in this complex) and extensive occupied loach minnow habitat in the Blue/San Francisco system
  7. **Upper Gila River complex** (NM) — considered the "core" of remaining populations for both species; low habitat threat due to remoteness

### Primary Constituent Elements (PCEs)
Both species share overlapping PCE criteria essential to conservation, including:
- Permanent, flowing, unpolluted water
- Distinct life-stage-specific habitat requirements (adult/juvenile/larval flow velocity and cover needs)
- Substrate composition: sand/gravel/cobble/rubble with low fine-sediment embeddedness
- Presence of pool-riffle-run-backwater habitat mosaic
- Low to moderate stream gradient
- Water temperature range of **1–30°C (35–85°F)** with natural seasonal/diurnal variation
- Abundant aquatic insect prey base
- Periodic natural flooding and a natural (or native-fish-supporting) hydrograph
- **Few or no nonnative predatory/competitive species**

### Regulatory & Legal Context
- Both species listed as Threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986; critical habitat deferred pending economic analysis
- 1994 critical habitat finalized under settlement (*Greater Gila Biodiversity Project v. USFWS*) but vacated in 1994 (*Catron County Board of Commissioners v. USFWS*) for NEPA non-compliance
- Formally removed from CFR in 1998; re-proposed in 1999 under court order with a Feb. 17, 2000 deadline
- 1994 finding determined **reclassification to Endangered was warranted but precluded** by higher-priority listing actions
- Proposed designation **excludes tribal lands** (e.g., Yavapai Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Reservation), based on a determination these populations can persist without formal designation (per Secretarial Order 3206)
