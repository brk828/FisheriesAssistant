# Crustacea in Eastern Kansas

#crustacea #freshwater-biology #microcrustacea

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the non-crayfish Crustacea of temporary and permanent freshwater bodies within about 2.5 miles of [[Lawrence Kansas]], in [[Eastern Kansas]]. Collections ran from February 1947 to May 1948 at roughly thirty water bodies, mostly temporary. The authors list 29 species in the abstract, though the checklist that follows contains 30 names. Many taxa are marked as new records for Kansas. The paper gives an annotated account of each species, covering recognition characters, local occurrence, physicochemical tolerances, and general distribution. It also includes keys to the Daphniidae and Chydoridae of the region, a historical review of earlier Kansas records, and a glossary.

## Background & Description

The study area is a mix of low, rocky hills dissected by small streams, well-drained slopes, and broad, poorly drained floodplains. Oaks, hickories, black walnut, elms, and cottonwood cover the hills, while slopes and floodplains support mixed grasses and herbs. Annual rainfall is 35 to 45 inches, mostly in spring and fall, and summers are often hot and dry. Roadside ditches, railroad fills, floodplain drainage canals, pastures, and woodland pools are common. These ephemeral pools fill with vegetation during dry periods, creating productive culture media for bacteria, protozoa, algae, and small crustaceans.

Historical records begin in the early 1880s. [[Packard 1883 Phyllopod Monograph]] described North American phyllopods, many of them collected by Dr. L. Watson at Ellis, Kansas, and by Professor Joshua Lindahl at Fort Wallace, Kansas. Hungerford (1922) described a new species of Caecidotea from eastern Kansas, and Underwood (1886) credited Kansas with eleven phyllopod species, all taken from the Packard material.

Methods included dip nets, a silk plankton towing net, a Birge cone net, a minnow seine, and collecting jars. Specimens were preserved in alcohol and some were stained (acid fuchsin) and mounted in polyvinyl alcohol. Water hydrogen ion concentration (pH) was measured with a Coleman potentiometer, and temperature, pool type, and water condition were recorded on 5 by 8 inch cards with photographs. The authors also tried to rear fairy shrimp from eggs in laboratory aquaria and in a campus pond, without success: no Anostraca appeared, which the authors attribute to irregular reappearance of these animals in ponds over several years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fauna is dominated by temporary-pool specialists. Fairy shrimps (Anostraca and Conchostraca) appear suddenly after vernal and autumnal rains and may vanish for years. Cladocerans and copepods are the most abundant groups in most pools, and the isopod and amphipod records reflect bottom and vegetation habitats. Several taxa showed strong responses to season and habitat:

- Many species peaked in spring or autumn rain-pools, and some were absent from open water in summer or winter.
- Several species were found across a wide range of temperature and pH, and the authors often judged these variables to have little effect on presence.
- Vegetation type mattered for some taxa. For example, [[Pleuroxus Denticulatus]] was most common in weedy pools, while [[Kurzia Latissima]] favored pools with little weed growth.
- Feeding ecology was largely planktonic. Diatoms, micro-organisms, and debris were the main foods, and several species preyed on smaller crustaceans (for example, [[Caenestheriella Belfragei]] consumed cyclopoids, diaptomids, daphnids, and ostracods).

Water temperatures recorded across taxa ranged from about 4 °C to 35 °C, and pH values from about 6.8 to 7.9. The paper reports these ranges by species rather than as a single set of values.

## Key Findings

**Faunal inventory.** The authors list 29 species in the abstract, with the checklist containing 30 names. The species fall into 7 groups: Anostraca (4 species), Conchostraca (1), Cladocera (12), Ostracoda (2), Copepoda (7), Isopoda (3), and Amphipoda (1). Counting the asterisks in the list, 26 names are flagged as new records for Kansas. Not flagged are [[Streptocephalus Texanus]], [[Thamnocephalus Platyurus]], [[Diaptomus Clavipes]], and [[Caecidotea Tridentata]], which had earlier Kansas records.

**Fairy shrimps.**
- [[Streptocephalus Texanus]] was collected from a drainage ditch about 2.25 miles northeast of Lawrence from October 9 to November 12, 1947. Water pH stayed near 7.9. Earlier records came from Ellis and Wallace, Kansas.
- [[Streptocephalus Sealii]] was new to Kansas. Adults appeared suddenly: four adults on October 9–10, 1947 in a roadside ditch, and about fifty on October 30 and November 5, 1947 in a drainage ditch. The roadside ditch had been dry from June 7 to September 12, 1947. A large specimen series (42–45 mm) came from a pasture basin in Shelby County, Texas.
- [[Thamnocephalus Platyurus]] was collected only twice in eastern Kansas. The first male, 45 mm long, was taken October 27, 1947, and 11 more specimens on October 29, 1947. Females measured 50 mm.
- [[Eubranchipus Serratus]] was new to Kansas and occurs over a wide range of conditions, with temperatures of 4–23 °C and pH of 6.93–7.6. On February 16, 1948, thousands of dead, mature individuals were found in a flooded field under ice that had covered the water from January 1 to February 12, 1948. The authors infer that these animals hatched in the field, could not reach oxygen under the ice, and died after reaching maturity. Adult length varied from 10 to 35 mm in different pools.
- [[Caenestheriella Belfragei]] was new to Kansas and was found in both drainage and roadside ditches between October 1947 and March 1948. It was present at temperatures from 5 to 35 °C and pH 6.8 to 7.9.

**Cladocerans.**
- [[Daphnia Pulex]] is probably the most common and abundant crustacean in eastern Kansas pools, ponds, streams, and ditches. It is most common in spring and fall, tolerates 5–27 °C and pH 6.9–7.4, and was absent from open water in summer and winter. Laboratory cultures reproduced year-round.
- [[Daphnia Longispina]] was found in open water of [[Lone Star Lake]], about 12 miles southwest of Lawrence. The lake is up to 45 feet deep and covers 195 acres. A Birge cone net dragged for 150–500 yards collected large numbers in April.
- [[Scapholeberis Mucronata]] was common in spring and summer temporary pools and was most abundant in vernal waters.
- [[Simocephalus Vetulus]] and [[Simocephalus Serrulatus]] were often found together and are hard to separate. Both are sporadic, appearing in large numbers for a week or two and then disappearing. Hoff (1943) reported that S. serrulatus occurs in a slightly higher percentage of collections.
- [[Ceriodaphnia Reticulata]] is transparent and hard to see, but it is as abundant as S. mucronata. It prefers deeper water and congregates near shoreline vegetation.
- [[Moina Rectirostris]] was collected from a drainage ditch under a railroad trestle from August 3 to 15, 1947, at 28–32 °C and pH 6.9. It was so abundant in shallow water that individuals were visible to the naked eye.
- [[Bosmina Longirostris]] was taken from open water of the [[Potter's Lake]] pond on the University of Kansas campus and forms surface aggregations, with individuals often clinging end-to-end in chains.
- [[Chydorus Sphaericus]] is common in spring in temporary and permanent pools, with little habitat preference. The authors note it is the commonest of all Cladocera (Birge 1948).
- [[Kurzia Latissima]] was abundant in spring and fall vernal and autumnal pools. Laboratory cultures reproduced sporadically all year, at 6 °C as frequently as at room temperature.
- [[Leydigia Quadrangularis]] was rare. Only two specimens appeared in a roadside ditch sample after about two weeks in the lab, and 25 appeared in an oxbow lake sample from March 16, 1948, over two to three weeks.
- [[Pleuroxus Denticulatus]] is common in vernal and autumnal ponds and ditches along railroad fills and roadsides.

**Ostracods.**
- [[Cypridopsis Vidua]] occurs abundantly in most spring ponds, pools, and lakes, and is present in summer and fall in smaller numbers. It is a bottom feeder and often appears in lab samples after two to three weeks.
- [[Physocypria Pustulosa]] is common in eastern Kansas and was also found in southeastern and western Kansas samples. It is most abundant in temporary waters from April to June.

**Copepods.**
- [[Diaptomus Clavipes]] is common in shallow temporary pools in spring and fall, often coloring the water red. The authors note that the species had only three known localities: West Okoboji Lake, Iowa; Lincoln, Nebraska; and Greeley, Colorado.
- [[Cyclops Bicuspidatus Thomasi]] is common in most eastern Kansas waters and was largely unaffected by depth, temperature, or pH.
- [[Cyclops Vernalis]] is common in temporary pools from spring through autumn, especially in rain-pools. Adults appear in lab samples after one or more weeks, which the authors attribute to hatching of eggs in the samples.
- [[Eucyclops Agilis]] is found in shallow ditches along roadsides and railroad fills. It peaks in May and is absent during severe winter weather.
- [[Attheyella Illinoisensis]] was found at two spring localities: a shallow pool near a drainage ditch and a spring-fed stream about one mile northwest of the campus. It is apparently uncommon in eastern Kansas.

**Isopods and amphipods.**
- [[Mancasellus Sp.]] (identification unresolved) is common in shallow waters and is most abundant in spring. It disappears when pools dry and returns after the first fall rains. The genus is under revision, and the authors note that a new generic name may be needed.
- [[Caecidotea Tridentata]] is fairly common in cisterns, wells, springs, and shallow rocky streams. It was originally described from a Lawrence cistern. It is eyeless but light-sensitive, and it hides under decaying vegetation or rocks.
- [[Armadillidium Vulgare]] is a terrestrial isopod found in damp places, including gardens, basements, and under rocks. It is active in spring through fall.
- [[Hyalella Knickerbockeri]] occurs in pools with Ceratophyllum and was most abundant in spring at the campus pond. It swims with jumping pleopods and feeds on micro-plankton.

**Keys and nomenclature.** The paper provides keys to the genera and species of Daphniidae and Chydoridae in eastern Kansas and a glossary of morphological terms. The authors also treat several historical synonyms. Creaser (1930b) considered Packard's *Streptocephalus floridanus*, Dodds's *S. coloradensis*, and Pesta's *S. americanus* to be synonyms of *S. sealii*.
