# Bird Observations on the Upper Gila River, Arizona (June 1971)

#ornithology #avian-ecology #riparian-birds

## Executive Summary

This field report documents bird observations made on the [[Upper Gila River]] in Arizona on 22 and 23 June 1971. Fieldwork started on the [[San Francisco River]] below the Phelps Dodge plant at [[Morenci]] and continued downstream through the canyon, passing the night of 22 June at the confluence with [[Eagle Creek]]. In total, 54 bird species were recorded, most of which almost certainly nest in the canyon or on adjacent slopes. The author judges the avifauna typical of a [[Sonoran Desert Riparian Zone]], but considers the area exceptional because of its permanent stream flow, which is becoming rare at low elevations in Arizona. The report closes with a recommendation that the reach be protected under the [[Scenic Rivers Act]].

## Background & Description

The survey covered a river canyon with [[Cottonwood Galleries]], [[Mesquite Bosque]] thickets, and surrounding desert slopes. On 22 June observation began in the morning near the [[Phelps Dodge]] picnic area and continued for roughly eight miles downstream (the OCR-degraded distance figure is approximate). On 23 June the observer covered an additional two-mile stretch.

Birds were grouped in the report into three tiers:

- **Very common and conspicuous (17 species):** These include [[Black Hawk]], [[Mourning Dove]], [[Cliff Swallow]], [[Summer Tanager]], and [[Killdeer]].
- **Less abundant but important (29 species):** These include [[Gambel's Quail]], [[Barn Owl]], [[Common Nighthawk]], [[Lucy's Warbler]], and [[Abert's Towhee]].
- **Uncommon or stragglers (8 species):** These include waterfowl, shorebirds, and flycatchers that were seen only briefly. Among them are Spotted Sandpiper, Coot, and Traill's Flycatcher.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The canyon's value rests on the combination of habitat features more than on rare species. None of the recorded birds is considered truly rare in Arizona, but many are regionally restricted to southwestern desert areas. The author stresses that the [[Permanent Stream Flow]] is the key attribute. Permanent streams at low elevations in Arizona are disappearing quickly, so the reach offers a refuge for riparian and desert birds.

The site is also important for [[Migratory Birds]]. Riparian corridors in central and southern Arizona draw many transient species that stop for a day or two before continuing. Because the surrounding desert often lacks adequate food and water, the trees and water along the permanent stream become critical stopover habitat in both spring and fall. The author expects some species to winter in the canyon, including several waterfowl species, though the June survey did not document this directly.

Breeding activity was noted in several places:

- The [[Black Hawk]] population appeared higher than in other riparian sites in Arizona, and the birds were tame.
- A large [[Cliff Swallow]] colony on the bluff contained about 80 active nests.
- [[Mourning Dove]] and [[White-winged Dove]] nested in mesquite thickets, with Mourning Dove much more abundant.
- A Barn Owl adult was seen feeding a fledgling at Eagle Creek.
- Gambel's Quail were common but had less nesting success that year, which the author attributed to extreme dryness.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 54 bird species were recorded along the Gila River reach during mid-June 1971. The author notes that the list is short because it reflects only the midsummer season.
- **Black Hawk abundance:** A minimum of six individuals were seen, including one first-year bird. The author considers this species very common and conspicuous in this habitat, with numbers likely higher than at other Arizona riparian sites.
- **Cliff Swallow colonies:** Several colonies occur on the bluff, and the largest contains about 80 active nests.
- **Summer Tanager:** Described as especially common, more so than the author had seen anywhere else in Arizona.
- **Nocturnal and cryptic species:** Common Nighthawk: one identified plus five unidentified nighthawks. The Elf Owl and Poorwill are probably more common than the observations indicate.
- **Waterfowl:** Nine unidentified ducks flew in one compact group past the Eagle Creek confluence.
- **Non-native species:** House Sparrow was noted only at the picnic area on the San Francisco River where observations began.
- **Migration and wintering:** The riparian corridor is expected to be important for migrants and wintering birds, but this was not confirmed by the June survey.
- **Conservation recommendation:** The author recommends that the reach be set aside under the [[Scenic Rivers Act]]. The argument is that the combination of a permanent stream, a scenic canyon, cottonwood groves, mesquite bosques, and other riparian vegetation is extremely uncommon in the arid Southwest, even though the bird list itself lacks rare species.
- **Recreational value:** Because the Black Hawk is fairly tolerant of people if not disturbed, the author expects the site to attract [[Birdwatching Tourism]] and to offer both scenic and wildlife viewing opportunities.
