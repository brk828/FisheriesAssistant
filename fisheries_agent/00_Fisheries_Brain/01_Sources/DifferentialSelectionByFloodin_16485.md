# Differential Selection by Flooding in Stream-Fish Communities of the Arid American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #stream-fish-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[Gary K. Meffe]] examines why native fishes of the arid American Southwest persist alongside, or are displaced by, [[Nonnative Fishes]] in streams. The authors argue that native fauna evolved under violent, flash-flood hydrology, whereas many nonnative species evolved under the slow, cumulative floods of mesic lowlands. Consequently, large floods differentially remove nonnative fishes while native species often survive. Regulation of rivers by dams removes this selective pressure, favoring lake-adapted introduced species and driving native declines. The chapter concludes that maintaining native southwestern fish faunas requires preserving natural flooding rather than managing flows to exclude it.

## Background & Description

The native ichthyofauna west of the Rocky Mountain axis is depauperate and unique, dominated by geographic relicts, monotypic genera and endemics. The region between the Rockies and the [[Sierra Nevada]], south of the [[Columbia River]] and [[Klamath River]] and north of the U.S.–Mexico border, supports about 75 native species. Of these, 43 (57%) are endemic to a single drainage and 18 (21%) occur at one locality each (Minckley et al., 1986). In Arizona and New Mexico west of the Continental Divide, there are 37 recognized species and subspecies, 4 in monotypic genera, and 14 (38%) endemic.

Survivors fall into three groups: species with generalized traits that resist extinction, specialists of long-lived habitats such as thermal springs or erosive streams, and species preadapted to high temperature or salinity.

Habitat alteration and introduced fishes are reducing this fauna. At least four species and six subspecies in six genera have gone extinct in the last 40 years because of human disturbance. Of 67 U.S. fishes listed as threatened or endangered, 45 (67.2%) live in arid zones (James E. Johnson, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, pers. comm.). Insular distributions with few colonization pathways make these fishes especially vulnerable to local extinction.

More than 140 fish species have been established outside their native North American ranges for sport, forage, biological control, aquaculture, hobby use and accident. Most nonnative fishes in the Southwest are transplants from the Mississippi or other mesic lowland drainages, while others are exotics from Eurasia, Central America and Africa. Humans have dispersed these fishes widely, so they are less constrained by natural or artificial barriers than native forms. Southwestern stream-fish communities have shifted from native faunas to mixed native–nonnative associations, or in some cases to wholly introduced assemblages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impoundments and regulation.** Successful introductions are usually linked to artificial habitats. Dams convert flood-prone rivers into calm, mildly fluctuating lakes with stabilized downstream flows. Regulation of the [[Colorado River]] and other western rivers reduces fluvial habitat for natives and favors introduced clupeids, salmonids, cyprinids, catostomids, ictalurids, poeciliids, centrarchids, percichthyids, percids and cichlids. Reservoirs are especially harmful: [[Sacramento Squawfish]] and [[Hardhead]] in California often disappear from reservoirs after an initial five to ten years of abundance. In the lower Colorado system, [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] can persist for 30 or more years without successful recruitment before declining toward local extinction.

**Variation in replacement.** Environmental extremes can exclude nonnative fishes. In some Nevada springs, temperatures above 36 °C and dissolved oxygen below 1 mg/l exclude introduced forms but are tolerated by [[Desert Springfishes]] (*Crenichthys baileyi*, *C. nevadae*). [[Moapa]] and [[Desert Dace]] inhabit water above 34 °C that most introduced fishes avoid. The [[Desert Pupfish]] of the [[Salton Sea]] tolerated salinities of 80 to 90 g/l, and its eggs hatch in salinities up to 70 g/l. Daily temperature swings above 20 °C and maxima above 38 °C also shielded it, but tolerant [[Sailfin Molly]] and exotic cichlids are now abundant, and the pupfish has nearly disappeared and is proposed for endangered listing. Native fishes persist longest in canyon-bound reaches of both regulated and unregulated systems.

**Flood hydrology.** Southwestern floods differ qualitatively and quantitatively from those of mesic lowlands because of geology, physiography and climate. Southwestern gradients are one to two orders of magnitude steeper, and relief is far greater (Table 12.1). Arid-zone relief ranged from 541.5 to 1,156.9 m and gradients from 3.09 to 14.58 m/km. Mesic relief ranged from 4.6 to 360.0 m and gradients from 0.03 to 0.84 m/km.

Summer monsoon storms can increase discharge by three or more orders of magnitude within seconds or minutes. Flash floods rapidly convert dry washes into torrents and recede just as fast. Winter floods are less common and scour only when unusually large. Canyon-constrained floods on bedrock cannot dissipate energy through overflow, so they move boulders, strip terraces and transport riparian trees. Water temperatures can drop by 15 °C or more.

Mesic watersheds, by contrast, have low relief, deep soils and broad floodplains. Their floods take hours to days to peak and recede slowly. Floodplain lakes and oxbows serve as refugia.

**Discharge patterns.** Discharge records from five Arizona streams and three Florida Suwannee drainage streams showed sharp contrasts. In the [[Santa Cruz River]], discharge was below 0.03 m/s 38% of the time but carried only about 0.5% of total water yield. Discharge below 0.3 m/s occurred nearly 84% of the time yet accounted for only 9% of yield. The upper 50% of water yield occurred at flows above 7.5 m/s in just 0.6% of the time. In the [[Santa Fe River]] of Florida, the upper 50% of yield occurred in 9% of the time, about 15 times more than in the arid system.

**Selective pressures.** Mesic fishes can reach floodplains to avoid high flows, so selection to hold position during floods should be weak. Arid-zone fishes often cannot escape by moving onto floodplains. Native fishes concentrate in canyons during summer to avoid hot, shallow, unshaded reaches and exploit permanent bedrock pools. Being carried downstream is often fatal, because many arid channels desiccate before reaching master streams. Floodwaters infiltrate, spread and evaporate (evapotranspiration may exceed 2 m/y), stranding transported fishes. Morphological traits such as depressed skulls, keeled napes, buttressed fins and narrow caudal peduncles, found in cyprinids and catostomids of flood-prone waters, likely reduce drag and aid swimming in turbulent currents.

## Key Findings

**Flood magnitude determines nonnative removal.** Fish faunas of canyon-bound reaches in unregulated streams shifted from mixed native–nonnative assemblages to predominantly or exclusively native ones after floods approaching or exceeding two orders of magnitude above mean discharge. Floods near one order of magnitude depleted but rarely destroyed nonnative populations. Floods below one order of magnitude had no discernible effect. Regression analyses showed that declines in nonnative species were strongly correlated with increasing flood magnitude (r = −0.873, P < 0.01), whereas changes in native species were not significant (P > 0.05).

**Native fishes survive floods better than nonnatives.** No native species was extirpated by high discharge in the study, although young-of-the-year losses were typically greater than adult losses. A massive flood in [[Sycamore Creek]] destroyed young and reduced adult [[Longfin Dace]] while eliminating nonnative fishes. An Eagle Creek flood in 1984–85 numerically reduced the entire native fauna of six species. A flood in [[Aravaipa Creek]] in 1983, almost 2,000 times mean discharge, had no discernible effect on seven native species.

**Nonnative fishes vary in flood vulnerability.** [[Common Carp]] were reduced by high-intensity floods. [[Red Shiner]] declines were rapidly reversed through survivors, reinvasion or reintroduction. [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] were typically removed and rarely returned. Flathead catfish, a stream-adapted species, expanded despite flooding. Poeciliids were decimated but recovered quickly. Centrarchids, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Bluegill]], were highly susceptible to displacement, although smallmouth bass and green sunfish reappeared within weeks. Exotic cichlids disappeared from unregulated streams.

**Regulated systems respond differently.** Regulated reaches with low, controlled releases showed few compositional changes. Emergency releases, however, destroyed most nonnative fishes in canyon-bound reaches of the Salt and Verde rivers, while native catostomids and some longfin dace persisted. A similar release into the lower Colorado River had little influence on nonnative or native fishes.

**Sharp Spring experiment.** At [[Sharp Spring]], Arizona (1979–1983), native [[Sonoran Topminnow]] coexisted with transplanted [[Western Mosquitofish]] after prolonged low discharge. Both species were sampled before and after a series of flash floods. Mosquitofish populations declined by more than 98%, while topminnows declined by 75%. Thousands of topminnows were later found in newly filled pools a few hundred meters downstream, allowing recolonization. Laboratory flume tests showed topminnows held position in surging currents by moving against flume walls, a behavior present even in one-day-old fish. Mosquitofish repeatedly failed to hold position, and young fish were killed.

**Distributional patterns.** Native fishes persist where streams are remote from population centers, serve as unmodified water-delivery systems, or lie in rugged, canyon-bound terrain. In the [[Virgin River]], endangered [[Woundfin]] and other native cyprinoids were absent from or rare in modified downstream reaches. Canyon reaches of the upper Colorado system retained native species, and [[Humpback Chub]] persisted in deep canyon areas too harsh for exotics. Miller et al. (1982) reported 10 native and 21 nonnative species upstream of Lake Powell. Native fishes made up 88% of catches in the [[Gunnison River]] but only 27% in the [[Dolores River]].

**Dynamic equilibrium model.** The authors propose that frequent, strong floods allow native species to recover between floods and maintain long-term coexistence with nonnatives (Fig. 12.7). Without floods, nonnative fishes increase to approach 100% of the fauna.

**Implications for management.** Arid-land water demands make maintaining a natural flow regime difficult. Instream-flow methods developed mainly for salmonids may not suit warmwater arid-zone species. The authors conclude that regulation which ends flooding will extirpate native fishes and recommend preserving "naturalness" in southwestern streams rather than manipulating discharge.
