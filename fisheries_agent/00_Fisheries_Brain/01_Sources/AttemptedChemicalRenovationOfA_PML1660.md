# Renovation of an Arizona Springbrook for the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the decline of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), an endangered livebearing poeciliid of lowland springs in the American Southwest, after colonization by the non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) at Bylas Springs, Arizona. The author attempted to remove mosquitofish from First Spring by poisoning the system with [[Antimycin A]] and then re-introduced the topminnows he had removed beforehand. Although mosquitofish seemed eliminated and topminnow numbers rose into the hundreds, mosquitofish reappeared within 15 weeks. The paper concludes that repeated chemical treatments combined with downstream barriers may be necessary to manage the species at this site.

## Background & Description

The Sonoran topminnow is a small (<50 mm) viviparous poeciliid native to lowland streams, springs and marshes of the American Southwest. It was formerly widespread and abundant in the [[Gila River]] drainage of Arizona and New Mexico, but by the early 1980s survived in the United States only in a few isolated populations in southeastern Arizona, and was listed as endangered (U.S. Department of the Interior 1980). The author notes that the decline occurred in less than four decades and attributes it to habitat alteration and interactions with introduced fishes, particularly mosquitofish. Mosquitofish reportedly can eliminate topminnows from a habitat within one to two years of introduction.

The study site is three small springbrooks, collectively called Bylas Springs, which rise from alluvial fill and flow toward the Gila River near 800 m elevation within the [[Sonoran Desert]] scrub zone. The author's focus is [[First Spring]], which has two springheads ("Bos Spring" and "Medicine Spring") discharging about one liter per second at average current velocities of about 0.2 m/s. Water sinks into alluvium about 800 m from Bos Spring and 600 m from the Gila River channel.

Note: the editor's note in the source states that the correct common name is Gila topminnow; the author used "Sonoran topminnow" to cover both subspecies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** The springs are characterized by low pH, high dissolved oxygen and a constant temperature of 28–29 °C at the sources. Conditions vary more downstream. In August 1981, a 12-site profile of First Spring showed conductivity ranging from about 4,000 to 4,400 µmhos/cm at upstream and mid sites and about 1,350–1,700 µmhos/cm at Salt Creek-associated sites, with pH rising downstream from 7.6 to 9.0.
- **Vegetation:** Dominant aquatic plants include sedges (*Eleocharis* spp., *Scirpus americana*), pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), naiad (*Najas guadalupensis*), algae (*Vaucheria* sp.) and stonewort (*Chara* sp.), which periodically chokes the channel. Riparian vegetation includes velvet mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*) and introduced saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*).
- **Invertebrates:** Aquatic invertebrates include endemic hydrobiid snails, amphipods (*Hyalella azteca*) and zygopteran damselflies.
- **Refugia:** Low pH, low dissolved oxygen and high CO₂ at the springheads appear to inhibit mosquitofish, so these sources serve as refugia for topminnow populations.
- **Flood history:** A flood in winter 1978–1979 breached an alluvial dam that had isolated the springs from the Gila River since at least 1917, which allowed mosquitofish to enter.

## Key Findings

- **Decline and colonization:** Sonoran topminnows were the only fish in Bylas Springs from 1968 to 1978. Mosquitofish were found in large numbers in First Spring and Salt Creek in July 1980. Within 1.5 years, topminnows had disappeared from lower Salt Creek and were outnumbered by mosquitofish in First Spring.
- **Fin damage:** Of 121 juvenile and adult topminnows examined from Medicine and Bos springs, 79 (65.3%) had moderate to severe caudal fin damage. Virtually no fin damage was found in topminnows from Bylas Springs in 1968 or from Second Spring in 1981–1982, habitats without mosquitofish.
- **Second Spring:** Topminnows persist throughout Second Spring, the only system not colonized by mosquitofish, and comprised 12% of fishes there.
- **Causal inference:** The author rejects habitat alteration and water chemistry as causes of decline, since these remained unchanged, and attributes the decline to mosquitofish colonization.
- **Poisoning trial (27 March 1982):** 157 topminnows were removed from First Spring and held. Antimycin A was applied at about 10 µg/L. Application took 2 hours. Mosquitofish were affected earlier than topminnows: in the first half hour, only 9.3% of collected fish were topminnows, but they made up 33.0% of fish collected in the second half hour. One hour after application, essentially all fish were dead.
- **Invertebrate effects:** Some amphipods and zygopterans died during treatment, but three weeks later all major invertebrate groups were present and abundant.
- **Re-introduction:** On 16 April 1982, no living fish were found in First Spring. The 26 males, 37 females and 4 juveniles of topminnow were re-introduced. Mosquitofish were absent on 16 April and 29 May 1982. On 10 July 1982, several mosquitofish were collected at Station 8, showing that the original population had not been fully removed. Re-stocked topminnows expanded into the hundreds, and juveniles became much more abundant than previously observed.
- **Renovation failure:** Reproducing mosquitofish were collected 15 weeks after treatment. The author suggests a single gravid female may have survived in an isolated pocket of water.
- **Comparable cases:** A pond in Cochise County failed after 3 months. Ord Creek (brown and brook trout) failed on the first antimycin treatment but succeeded after a second.
- **Recommendations:** Repeated antimycin treatments combined with downstream barriers (2 or 3 barriers, about 1 m high, near stations 8–10 on First Spring) may be needed. Laboratory tests of relative toxicity between mosquitofish and topminnows are recommended, as mosquitofish appeared more susceptible.
