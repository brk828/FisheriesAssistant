# Overwinter Survival of Age-0 Colorado Squawfish: Role of Size, Condition and Lipid Content

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science thesis from [[Colorado State University]] (Fort Collins, Fall 1989) by Jay M. Thompson examines why age-0 [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered [[Cyprinidae]] piscivore, may suffer high mortality during its first winter. Hatchery-reared fish in three size classes (small ~30 mm, medium ~36 mm, large ~44 mm total length) were held at simulated winter temperatures of 3.5 ± 1.5 °C for 210 days under three feeding regimes: dry pellets, starvation, and live brine shrimp. Survival, length, condition factor and lipid content were tracked. Starvation was strongly linked to death in small and medium fish, and dying fish had lipid levels near 3.6% of dry weight. The results were used to propose conceptual size-versus-survival curves for wild populations.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Colorado squawfish, a large piscivorous cyprinid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is now restricted to the northern part of its historical range (see Figure 1 of the thesis) and was listed as endangered in 1973.
- **Causes of decline:** Attributed to dam construction, introduction of non-native species and poor land use (Miller 1961; Minckley and Deacon 1968; Molles 1980; Behnke and Benson 1983; Carlson and Muth 1989).
- **Recruitment concern:** Small size of age-0 fish entering winter may limit recruitment to the adult stock (Kaeding and Osmundson 1988).
- **Late spawning:** Spawning at 20–22 °C occurs around July or August in the Upper Colorado Basin, leaving age-0 fish a short growth period before winter. In the Lower Basin, the threshold is reached in early May.
- **Growth cessation:** Growth stops below about 13 °C, usually reached in October in the Upper Basin.
- **Prior research gap:** Overwinter survival studies of age-0 Colorado squawfish were "conspicuously lacking." Osmundson (1987) found no size-selective mortality, but his smallest fish (54 mm TL) were much larger than wild fish at the same time of year.
- **Preliminary study:** Thompson held 331 hatchery fish (39–87 mm TL) from January to July 1989; survival was 100% for all sizes, but fish were too large to represent wild age-0 fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Winter mortality mechanisms in temperate fishes** include hypothermia, predation, flood displacement, inadequate lipid reserves leading to starvation, and ice damage (Seelbach 1987). Energy depletion is widely reported, and smaller fish have higher weight-specific basal metabolism, so they exhaust energy reserves faster (Shuter et al. 1980; Shuter and Post 1989).

**Lipid-size relationship:** Larger fish tend to carry more lipid. This pattern has been documented in [[Yellow Perch]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Sand Smelt]].

**Feeding behavior:** Many warmwater and coolwater fishes restrict feeding in cold water. Whether Colorado squawfish feed in winter was previously unknown.

**Laboratory setting:** The aquarium system was a closed recirculating system using dechlorinated city water, a biological filter and a 30-watt UV steriliser. Water temperature was held at 3.5 ± 1.5 °C. Photoperiod was 10 L:14 D.

**Field context:** Wild age-0 fish were collected from the Colorado River (river km 74–159) and the Green River, using 4.6 m beach seines with 0.32 cm mesh in backwater habitats. Green River populations are generally larger than Upper Colorado River fish, which spawn later.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth and condition over winter**
- Fish grew little at winter temperatures. Condition factor (K) declined significantly for small and large fish between day 0 and day 210, and also declined for medium fish, though those results were hard to interpret.
- Starved fish lost condition faster than fed fish in all size classes.
- Mean condition factor of dying fish was 0.36, lower than that of all surviving fish except starved medium fish on day 140.

**2. Lipid depletion**
- Lipid content was inversely related to winter duration for all size classes and feeding regimes, with one exception: a non-significant increase in pellet-fed medium fish between days 70 and 140.
- Lipid reserves declined faster in starved small and medium fish than in their fed counterparts.
- Dying fish had lipid levels of 3.6% dry weight, significantly lower than in fish removed alive. This is treated as the critical lipid threshold for survival.
- Initial hatchery lipid averaged 28.9% dry weight, compared with 14.9% for wild fish sampled in October 1988 and 13.6% for wild fish sampled in March 1989.

**3. Survival**
- **Small fish:** Only 2 of 61 starved small fish survived to day 210, compared with 98 of 103 pellet-fed fish (5 died), and 33 of 40 brine-shrimp-fed fish. Starvation was significantly associated with death.
- **Medium fish:** Only 6 of 90 starved medium fish were alive at day 210. Pellet-fed medium fish showed 2 deaths out of 125 original fish. Nitrite spikes caused high early mortality in medium fish, so their day 0 data are not comparable.
- **Large fish:** All pellet-fed and starved large fish survived, despite an ichthyobodiasis outbreak in October–November. Brine-shrimp-fed large fish suffered high mortality from Ichthyobodo in December.
- Survival of starved small and medium fish was significantly lower than fed fish of the same sizes.

**4. Feeding behavior**
- Fish fed readily after a 2–3 week acclimation phase. Feeding activity was inversely related to fish size, with small fish most active.
- At ~4 °C, captive fish fed readily and stayed active. Wild stomach analysis in the [[Green River]] showed food in 15 of 19 fish collected in November 1987, and one December fish was feeding.

**5. Size-survival relationship**
- Lipid content increased with length in hatchery fish. Length was positively but weakly correlated with time to death in starved fish (Figure 2: time to starvation = 28.06 + 0.03 × length; r² = 0.40).
- Body water content rose linearly as lipid content fell (lipid = 220.79 − 2.54 × body water; r² = 0.92).
- Newsome and Leduc (1975) reported a critical lipid level of about 2.2% in yellow perch. The thesis's measured threshold was about 3.6%.

**6. Conceptual model**
- Five curves were proposed (Figure 5). Line A (direct size-starvation relation) and Line B (fed fish survive) bracket the range. Curve D, a non-linear relation, was considered most likely, with a size threshold of about 50 mm TL for piscivory.
- The model suggests the critical size for piscivory (~30–50 mm TL, USFWS unpublished) may define the survival threshold.

**7. Wild population comparisons**
- Lipid content of wild fish did not decline significantly over winter, which could reflect survivor bias, pre-winter lipid accumulation, or winter feeding.
- Wild condition factor declined significantly from fall (0.75 ± 0.15, n = 30) to spring (0.60 ± 0.07, n = 30). Spring values were still well above 0.36.

**8. Live cage experiments**
- Cages in the Colorado River silted up and all fish died in both 1988 and 1989. In a riverside gravel-pit pond, 15 of 25 large fish survived (condition 0.72 → 0.62) and 1 of 25 medium fish survived.

**9. Recommendations for future work**
- Simulate gradual autumn cooling and spring warming.
- Conduct proximate analysis (lipid, water, protein, ash).
- Pair the lab study with winter field collection in December and March, across the Upper Basin.
- Use a lipid extraction method requiring less tissue than Soxhlet extraction, or hold more fish.
