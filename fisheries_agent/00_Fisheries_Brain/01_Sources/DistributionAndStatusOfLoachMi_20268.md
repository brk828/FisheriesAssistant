# Distribution and Status of Loach Minnow (*Rhinichthys cobitis*) on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This 1993 proposal, submitted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Arizona Fishery Resources Office to the White Mountain Apache Tribal Council, outlines a survey plan to determine the distribution and status of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*, formerly *Tiaroga cobitis*) on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation. The species, a specialized benthic riffle-dweller of the [[Gila River Drainage]], has suffered severe range fragmentation due to habitat degradation and non-native species introductions. The document was prompted by an August 1992 controversy in which a proposed [[Indian Health Service]] water diversion and treatment facility on the North Fork White River was deemed likely to jeopardize the resident White River population. The proposal details specific "high-probability" stream segments (Black River, Carrizo Creek, and Cibecue Creek systems) targeted for systematic electrofishing, seining, and observational surveys to locate additional populations, which would improve the species' conservation status and reduce regulatory constraints on tribal water projects.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomic Note:** The species was reclassified from *Tiaroga cobitis* to *Rhinichthys cobitis*, placing it within the genus [[Rhinichthys]] per Woodman (1993), based on systematic relationships within the Cyprinid genus *Rhinichthys*.
- **Family:** Cyprinidae.
- **Functional Morphology:** Described as a "specialized bottom-dwelling" (benthic) species, morphologically and behaviorally adapted to maintain position in cobble-bottomed riffle habitats with swift current — a niche shared with other benthic riffle specialists such as the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*).
- **Field Measurement Protocol:** Specimens collected during proposed surveys were to be measured to the nearest millimeter and weighed to the nearest gram before release (except voucher specimens).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Preferred Habitat:** Cobble-bottomed riffle habitats within low- to mid-elevation stream segments of the [[Gila River]] drainage in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Elevation Range:** No New Mexico populations were found above 1,980 m (~6,500 ft.); upper elevational limits varied by stream (Propst and Bestgen 1991).
- **Stream Gradient:** Most large populations occurred in reaches with gradients of 3–6 m/km (~16–32 ft./mi.), though some populations persisted in higher-gradient reaches.
- **Habitat Specificity:** Not all riffles are suitable — substrate embeddedness (fine sediment infilling gravel/cobble matrices) was noted to preclude optimal use by this benthic species, particularly in lower East Fork and mainstem White River reaches.
- **Historic Range:** Formerly widespread in the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]] systems.
- **Current Range Persistence:** Populations persist in [[Aravaipa Creek]], the [[Blue River]] and [[White River (Arizona)]] in Arizona, and portions of the Gila and San Francisco drainages in New Mexico.
- **White River Population:** Distributed across ~22.5 km (14 mi.) of the East Fork and mainstem White River, from downstream of Geronimo Cave (East Fork) to downstream of Canyon Day (mainstem). Habitat quality declines longitudinally downstream, correlating with increased substrate embeddedness.
- **Threats:** Habitat disturbance/destruction (especially dewatering of riffles), altered flow regimes, and competition/predation from introduced non-native species (Minckley 1973; Propst et al. 1988).
- **Co-occurring Species of Interest:** Surveys also aimed to document other resident fishes via mini-hoop netting, though these methods were "not likely" to capture loach minnow itself.

## Key Findings
- **Regulatory Status:** Listed as a **threatened species** under the Endangered Species Act on October 28, 1986 (Federal Register 51(208):39468–39478).
- **Conservation Conflict (1992):** USFWS Ecological Services (Phoenix) issued a jeopardy opinion against a proposed IHS water diversion/treatment facility on the North Fork White River, citing likely dewatering impacts on riffle habitat critical to the White River loach minnow population.
- **Genetic Significance:** The White River population was hypothesized to represent a distinct genetic component of the species, elevating the stakes of the jeopardy determination.
- **Survey Gaps:** Prior surveys (Propst et al. 1985) were biased toward easily accessible stream segments, not necessarily those with suitable habitat, leaving many potentially suitable streams unsurveyed on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation.
- **Proposed Survey Localities (1993):**
  1. **Black River System** — Tonto Creek, Big Bonito Creek, Black River mainstem
  2. **Carrizo Creek System** — Corduroy Creek, Cedar Creek, Carrizo Creek mainstem
  3. **Cibecue Creek System** — Cibecue Creek mainstem
- **Methodology:**
  - Primary technique: 1/8" mesh block nets + pulsed DC electrofishing (with substrate "raking" to dislodge benthic fish).
  - Black River exception: Due to low water conductivity rendering electrofishing unreliable, alternative methods (seining with 4.6 m [15 ft.] 1/8" mesh seines, underwater observation, and 1/4" mesh mini-hoop netting) were proposed.
  - Habitat characterization: Substrate, depth, and current velocity measured at 1 m intervals along transects perpendicular to streamflow at loach minnow detection sites.
  - GPS (Sony Pyxis) used to record sampling coordinates where possible.
- **Schedule:** Surveys planned from July 12, 1993 to no later than October 30, 1993.
- **Data Governance:** Data retained by USFWS Arizona Fishery Resources Office (Pinetop, AZ); copies to be shared with the White Mountain Apache Tribal Attorney and Game and Fish Department. Voucher specimens deposited at the [[Arizona State University]] vertebrate collection. Publication contingent on tribal consent.
- **Management Implication:** Discovery of new populations on the Reservation would improve the species' overall conservation status, potentially easing restrictions on future water resource development projects.
