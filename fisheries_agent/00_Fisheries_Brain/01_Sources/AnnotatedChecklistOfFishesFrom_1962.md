## Executive Summary

This 1962 paper by Robert A. Kuehne presents an annotated checklist of fishes collected from Clemons Fork, a small tributary of Buckhorn Creek in Breathitt County, Kentucky, located within the [[Robinson Forest]] research tract. Based on seine collections from summer 1959 and spring 1961 across seven collection stations, the study documents **17 fish species from 4 families** inhabiting this small (~6000 acre), undisturbed, forested watershed. Despite its small size and intermittent flow during drought, Clemons Fork supports a surprisingly rich and stable fish fauna, attributed to good forest cover, minimal watershed disturbance, and permanent pool habitats maintained by sandstone and gravel substrates. The paper is part of a broader biological survey effort on the site that also included birds ([[Barbour 1956]]) and herpetofauna ([[Bush 1959]]).

## Background & Description

The paper is taxonomic/ecological in scope rather than morphometric; no detailed morphological measurements are provided. Species are identified only by common diagnostic names and habitat associations:

- **[[White Sucker]]** (*Catostomus commersoni*) — single specimen, downstream reaches
- **[[Hog Sucker]]** (*Hypentelium nigricans*) — typical sucker of the stream, found in larger pools
- **[[Creek Chub]]** (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) — dominant headwater species, excludes other forms in extreme headwaters
- **[[Rosefin Shiner]]** (*Notropis ardens*) — found in trunk stream pools
- **[[Common Shiner]]** (*Notropis cornutus*) — abundant in lower half of main stream
- **[[Spotfin Shiner]]** (*Notropis spilopterus*) — rare in this system, common regionally
- **[[Silverjaw Minnow]]** (*Ericymba buccata*) — frequents exposed, shallow pools
- **[[Bluntnose Minnow]]** (*Pimephales notatus*) — found in large, silted pools
- **[[Stoneroller]]** (*Campostoma anomalum*) — common but does not invade extreme headwaters
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieui*) — apex carnivore, restricted to deep pools
- **[[Longear Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis megalotis*) — rare, single specimen
- **[[Rock Bass]]** (*Ambloplites rupestris*) — typical of swift permanent streams, single adult recorded
- **[[Greenside Darter]]** (*Etheostoma blennioides*) — found in larger riffles, lower reaches
- **[[Rainbow Darter]]** (*Etheostoma caeruleum*) — widespread in riffles except extreme headwaters
- **[[Johnny Darter]]** (*Etheostoma nigrum*) — common pool inhabitant, most abundant darter below headwaters
- **[[Arrow Darter]]** (*Etheostoma sagitta*) — headwater specialist, taxonomically notable (see [[Kuehne and Bailey 1961]])

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Clemons Fork is characterized by a **headwater-to-downstream gradient** in species composition and abundance, reflecting habitat stability and stream size:

- **Headwater specialists**: [[Creek Chub]], [[Stoneroller]], and [[Arrow Darter]] typify the extreme upper reaches, with the Creek Chub excluding other species from the smallest tributaries.
- **Pool-dependent predators**: [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]] are restricted to the few deep pools capable of supporting adult predatory fish, indicating habitat-limited distribution of top carnivores.
- **Downstream-associated rarities**: [[White Sucker]], [[Spotfin Shiner]], and [[Greenside Darter]] occur only rarely in lower Clemons Fork, representing the upstream limit of their distribution, but are common in larger downstream systems.
- **Riffle vs. pool specialists**: [[Rainbow Darter]] and [[Greenside Darter]] occupy riffle habitats, while [[Johnny Darter]] and [[Bluntnose Minnow]] favor pool habitats, illustrating microhabitat partitioning within the stream.
- **Habitat stability hypothesis**: The author links the surprising diversity and predictable occurrence of species to the stable stream conditions afforded by intact forest cover and lack of watershed disturbance — a stark contrast to more disturbed streams elsewhere in eastern Kentucky.

The basin's small size (~6000 acres) causes intermittent flow during droughts, but permanent sandstone/gravel-bedded pools provide refugia sustaining year-round fish populations.

## Key Findings

- **17 species from 4 families** (Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae, Percidae) were documented from seven collection stations sampled in 1959 and 1961.
- Species richness and abundance were **unexpectedly high** given the small watershed size, suggesting a link between **habitat stability** and community richness/predictability.
- **Predatory fish** ([[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Rock Bass]]) are habitat-limited, occurring only in the rare deep pools of the system.
- A clear **upstream-downstream distributional gradient** exists, with headwater specialists ([[Creek Chub]], [[Stoneroller]], [[Arrow Darter]]) contrasting with species reaching only their upstream range limits in lower Clemons Fork ([[White Sucker]], [[Spotfin Shiner]], [[Greenside Darter]]).
- The **[[Arrow Darter]]** (*Etheostoma sagitta*) is highlighted as taxonomically and biogeographically significant, historically considered rare, with its peculiar distribution linked to **stream capture events** as detailed in a companion paper ([[Kuehne and Bailey 1961]]).
- The study reinforces the value of **intact forested watersheds** (as in [[Robinson Forest]]) for maintaining diverse, stable stream fish communities, serving as a baseline/reference condition compared to more disturbed eastern Kentucky streams.
- This checklist complements parallel faunal surveys of the same site covering birds ([[Barbour 1956]]) and herpetofauna ([[Bush 1959]]), contributing to a multi-taxon baseline dataset for [[Robinson Forest]].
