# A Climatology of Temperature and Precipitation Variability in the United States

#climatology #environmental-science #climate-variability

## Executive Summary

This [[Environmental Protection Agency]] Project Summary, prepared by the [[Atmospheric Research and Exposure Assessment Laboratory]] (Research Triangle Park, NC), describes the seasonal and annual variability of temperature and precipitation across the contiguous United States for 1895–1985. It is a climate-science document, not an aquatic or ichthyological study, and it contains no fish, invertebrate, or water-chemistry data. Its purpose is to delineate regions of differing climatic variability so that [[Climatic Scenarios]] and ecological monitoring networks can be designed to assess ecosystem sensitivity to climate change.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Scientists and planners lack consensus on climate change and its ecological impacts. Usable climatic scenarios for ecological impact assessment remain rudimentary.
- **Rationale:** Ecosystems in regions with low temperature and precipitation variance may be more sensitive to climate change than those in highly variable regions. Variance and range, not only mean conditions, therefore matter for ecological response.
- **Data:** Monthly temperature and precipitation records came from the [[National Climatic Data Center]]. The contiguous US contains 344 [[Climatic Division]]s, which vary greatly in area. Temperature was recorded to the nearest tenth of a degree F and precipitation to the nearest hundredth of an inch.
- **Methods:** Temperature variance and standardized range were calculated by season and year. Because variance is biased toward wet areas, precipitation was analyzed with the [[Coefficient of Variation]] and a normalized standardized range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document frames its results as inputs for ecology more broadly. It notes that derived variables relevant to local ecosystems, such as surface moisture stress, duration of rainless periods, and length of growing season, can be calculated from temperature and precipitation. It also states that monitoring networks should be established in both high- and low-variability regions, including base-line stations that require climatic stability and stations that must capture a range of conditions. No aquatic habitats, species, or ecosystems are studied directly.

## Key Findings

**Temperature**
- Annual temperature variance is largest in the upper Midwest, especially North and South Dakota and eastern Montana, where it exceeds 30 °F. This pattern reflects a [[Continentality]] effect.
- Variance decreases toward coastal regions, including the West Coast, the Gulf Coast, and the southeastern states. The minimum, below 0.5, occurs on the southern Florida peninsula.
- Winter variance is roughly twice spring variance and roughly four times summer or autumn variance.
- Standardized temperature range is largest in the upper Midwest (North and South Dakota, Minnesota) and smallest near the coasts. Spring and autumn show the greatest within-season variability, which is expected because monthly temperature ranges are wider in transitional seasons.

**Precipitation**
- The [[Coefficient of Variation]] of annual precipitation shows an east–west gradient. It is largest over the southwestern states from Texas to California, where it exceeds 25.0%.
- The smallest coefficients occur in the eastern sections, from the mid-Atlantic and Great Lakes states into New England, where they are below 14.0%.
- Seasonal coefficient patterns resemble the annual pattern, with exceptions: high variation shifts into the lower Midwest in winter and into the Pacific Northwest in summer.
- Standardized precipitation range follows the coefficient-of-variation pattern but is smoother, showing less gradient.
- Greater-than-normal annual precipitation ranges occur from New Mexico to California and in the upper Midwest. Smaller-than-normal ranges occur in New England, the Appalachian states, and the Ohio River Valley.

**Conclusions**
- Successful [[Global Climate Models]] or analogue-based scenarios should reproduce these variability patterns as well as mean patterns. The authors state that current models are, for the most part, unable to do so.
- Ecological monitoring networks should account for these regional variability differences in their design.
