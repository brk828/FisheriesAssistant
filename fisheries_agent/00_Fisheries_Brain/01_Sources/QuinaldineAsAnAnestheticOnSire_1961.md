# Quinaldine as an Anesthetic on Siredon mexicanum (Shaw)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ambystomatidae #axolotl #anesthesiology #herpetology

> **Note:** Although tagged with ichthyology conventions per instructions, the study subject is the axolotl (*Siredon mexicanum*), a neotenic salamander, not a fish. ## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Roger M. Katz (University of Louisville) evaluates **quinaldine (2-methylquinoline)** as a chemical anesthetic for the axolotl, *[[Siredon Mexicanum]]* (=*Ambystoma mexicanum*), a neotenic salamander commonly used in laboratory research. The work is explicitly positioned within the fisheries anesthesiology literature, comparing axolotl responses to previously documented effects of quinaldine and other anesthetics (e.g., chloretone) on fish and amphibian embryos. The study establishes dose-response relationships between quinaldine concentration, induction time, anesthesia duration, and recovery time, identifying **96 mg/l** as the optimal concentration for safe, prolonged anesthesia (up to 15 hours) without lethal or lasting adverse effects. Higher concentrations (264–528 mg/l) induced anesthesia rapidly (9–10 minutes) but risked lethality or tissue damage (mandibular tetany, gastrointestinal disturbance) if exposure was prolonged.

## Background & Description

*Note: This paper is pharmacological/behavioral rather than morphological, but the following organismal and chemical descriptive details are reported:*

- **Study organism:** *Siredon mexicanum* (axolotl), a neotenic (permanently larval) salamander, historically first described as *Gyrinus mexicanus* by Shaw (1789).
- **Color morphs used:** Both **albinistic** and **normally pigmented** individuals.
  - Albino stock traced to lines brought from Poland in the 1930s, originally descended from 1868 Paris crosses (Dumeril, Jardin des Plantes) of spotted and black axolotls; albinism is an autosomal recessive trait.
  - Normally pigmented stock bred in Germany prior to importation to the U.S.
- **Body size:** Average weight 81.4 g (range 70.1–90.6 g); 13 individuals used across 38 experimental trials.
- **Observed physiological/anatomical anesthesia effects:**
  - Loss of motility, muscle tone, equilibrium, and reflex activity under anesthesia (consistent with general vertebrate anesthetic response, McFarland 1960).
  - At sub-lethal but high concentrations (264 mg/l, 5-hr exposure): swelling of the **mandibular region** and apparent **tetanus** of associated musculature, preventing mouth opening despite intact stimulus response — suggesting damage to the branchial region, its innervation, and/or the gastrointestinal tract.
  - Recovery from prolonged anesthesia occurred **asymmetrically**, with one side of the body recovering before the other in no consistent pattern, indicating transient unilateral paralysis with no permanent sensory nervous system effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*[[Siredon Mexicanum]]* is a neotenic salamander native to the lake/canal systems of the Valley of Mexico, retaining larval (aquatic, gilled) morphology into sexual maturity — a classic example of **paedomorphosis** in amphibians. While the paper does not address wild habitat or ecology directly, it notes:

- All experimental specimens were laboratory-reared/captive-bred stock (obtained from Dr. R. R. Humphrey, Indiana University), not wild-caught, reflecting the axolotl's long history as a model laboratory organism.
- Experiments were conducted in dechlorinated/treated city tap water at **room temperature**; the species is described as an **aquatic heterothermous animal**, tolerant of standard laboratory aquatic conditions.
- **Reproductive capacity** was unaffected by repeated anesthetic exposure — animals successfully reproduced one month after experimentation ceased, indicating no lasting reproductive toxicity from quinaldine.
- Comparative ecological context is drawn to fish anesthesia practice generally used in **fisheries handling, transport, and surgical/tagging procedures** (McFarland 1960; Muench 1958), situating quinaldine's utility within broader aquatic animal husbandry.

## Key Findings

1. **Dose-response relationship:** Induction time to total anesthesia is inversely related to quinaldine concentration across the tested range of **82–528 mg/l** (Figure 1).
2. **Lethal threshold:** Concentrations **>528 mg/l were uniformly lethal**; concentrations of 264–396 mg/l were lethal after ~5 hours of continuous exposure.
3. **Optimal concentration:** **96 mg/l** produced the most desirable prolonged anesthesia, sustainable for up to **15 hours** without lethality; a "plateau" recovery time occurred for exposures between ~2–15 hours.
4. **Sub-lethal damage:** Exposure to 264 mg/l for 5 hours followed by transfer to fresh water caused **mandibular swelling and tetany**, feeding regurgitation, and eventual death after ~2 weeks of induced "starvation" — implicating branchial/innervation or GI tract damage.
5. **Regurgitation rates:** 45% of animals regurgitated when held under varying anesthetic concentrations for extended periods vs. 30% when recovery was immediate post-induction; regurgitation showed no significant correlation with mortality and appeared to be an individual trait, more common at lower concentrations.
6. **Rapid induction protocol:** Initial induction at high concentration (396 mg/l) followed by immediate transfer to 96 mg/l reduced total experimental time by ~40 minutes with no observed adverse difference compared to direct induction at 96 mg/l.
7. **Recovery time relationship:** Recovery time increased with concentration and length of exposure (Figures 2–3), with the largest jump in recovery time occurring between 182 and 264 mg/l.
8. **Sub-anesthetic threshold:** Below 80 mg/l, anesthesia was only partial (activity/sensitivity retained); prolonged exposure (4–5 hrs) at this level sometimes caused death despite lack of full anesthesia.
9. **No long-term/reproductive harm:** Repeated anesthetic exposures across multiple trials on the same individuals produced no cumulative adverse effects; normal reproduction occurred one month post-experiment.
10. **Comparative agreement with fish anesthesia literature:** Findings align with [[McFarland 1960]] and [[Muench 1958]] regarding proportionality of recovery time to exposure/concentration and full recovery without long-term effects — but **diverge** from McFarland's fish data showing 12-hour sustained anesthesia tolerance near-lethal doses; axolotls showed lower tolerance ceiling (~15 hrs at 96 mg/l vs. fish tolerance patterns).
11. **Comparison to chloretone toxicity:** Adverse effects (bloating, death, impaired movement) parallel those reported by [[Carmichael 1926]] for chloretone-treated *Ambystoma punctatum* embryos, suggesting shared toxicological mechanisms among amphibian anesthetics at excessive doses.
12. **Practical recommendation:** Quinaldine is endorsed as a low-cost, effective anesthetic for axolotls due to easy suspension in water, wide effective concentration range (80–528 mg/l), rapid induction at high doses (9–10 min), and safe prolonged use at 96 mg/l with no permanent adverse effects when protocols are followed.
