# Monitoring of Humpback Chub and Sympatric Native Fish Populations in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona, 1991–1994

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), prepared by Anthony T. Robinson and funded by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] through the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, documents four years (1991–1994) of spring monitoring of the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes in the lowest 21 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). The study continues a standardized program begun in 1987 and aims to track abundance, age-class structure and health, with the long-term goal of setting threshold criteria for a management action plan. Native fishes still dominated the catch each year, although the native share fell to 89% in 1994. Adult humpback chub catch rates declined in 1994, and the largest individuals were scarce. Population estimates did not differ significantly among years, and parasite infestation by [[Lernaea cyprinacea]] remained low. The authors conclude that the population appears relatively stable but recommend continued monitoring, especially before any flow or temperature regime change arising from the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries historically supported eight native fish species in the Grand Canyon region. Dam construction at Hoover and Glen Canyon fragmented populations, disrupted reproduction through cold hypolimnetic releases, and destabilized rearing habitat through daily stage fluctuations. Non-native predators and competitors, and land-use changes that turned the lower LCR from perennial to ephemeral in its upper reaches, added further pressure. Three species were extirpated (Colorado Squawfish, Bonytail Chub, Roundtail Chub), Razorback Sucker was nearly extirpated, and Humpback Chub's range contracted. The remaining species persist largely by spawning in unregulated tributaries, and the LCR is considered the major spawning site for them. A 1995 [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion found that current Glen Canyon Dam operations jeopardized Humpback Chub and named long-term population monitoring as a Reasonable and Prudent Alternative.

The monitoring design follows [[Hendrickson and Kubly]] (1990), who established repeatable methods and recommended monitoring all native species and their interactions with non-natives. Objectives were to continue this program, refine techniques, and analyze abundance, age structure and health trends.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study reach is a 21 km perennial section of the LCR above its mouth, confined in a steep gorge. [[Blue Spring]] and downstream springs supply about 6.31 m³/s at the mouth. Spring water is about 20 °C, salty, rich in dissolved carbon dioxide and supersaturated with calcium carbonate. Below [[Atomizer Falls]] (14.2 km above the mouth), CO₂ degasses and calcite precipitates, producing a milky blue river with white calcite beds and tufaceous limestone. Mouth temperatures average 9 °C warmer than the mainstem.

Discharge is highly variable: the upper reach alternates between dryness and large floods, and a flood exceeding 2,830 m³/s was estimated for September 1923. Winter snowmelt and summer convective storms drive large seasonal temperature swings. Discharge is recorded at the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Cameron gauge.

Water chemistry (high salinity and CO₂) and periodic floods are thought to restrict non-native populations in the LCR, which are sourced mainly from the mainstem where non-natives are far more abundant. The parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* was also tracked, since warmer water can increase infestation.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling**
- Hoop nets (6.4 mm mesh) were fished continuously for at least 31 days each spring (late April–June) at 13 sites within 1,000–1,200 m of the confluence, plus 6 upstream sites (8,300–10,600 m) in 1991.
- Catch per unit effort (CPE) was expressed as fish per net per 12 h.
- [[Trammel Nets]] were used in 1991 only and were discontinued after several Humpback Chub mortalities.
- All native fish over 150 mm TL received [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags.
- Modified [[Schnabel Estimate]] population estimates were calculated for Humpback Chub over 150 mm.

**Species composition**
- Ten species were captured in 1991–1994: four natives (Humpback Chub, Speckled Dace, Bluehead Sucker, Flannelmouth Sucker) and six non-natives (Fathead Minnow, Common Carp, Rainbow Trout, Channel Catfish, Yellow Bullhead, Plains Killifish).
- Native fish made up 84.2–99.1% of the catch each year; in 1994 the native share fell to 89%, attributed to a strong fathead minnow reproduction year.
- Speckled Dace and Humpback Chub dominated hoop-net catches; Flannelmouth Sucker were most common in trammel nets.

**Size structure**
- Adult Humpback Chub lengths differed among years (N = 256, K-W H = 228.18, p < 0.001), with larger fish in 1988–1991 than in 1987 or 1992–1994.
- No consistent annual trend was found in the percentage of adult Humpback Chub over 400 mm, which was 3.3%, 3.1%, 1.7%, 1.5%, 1.7%, 4.7%, 7.0%, 6.0% and 0% across the listed years (1987–1994, with the 1992–1994 values lower than earlier years).
- Adult Flannelmouth Sucker and Bluehead Sucker lengths also differed among years, but no annual pattern was clear.

**Abundance and catch rates**
- Adult Humpback Chub CPE was similar from 1991 to 1993 but decreased in 1994.
- Speckled Dace adult CPE was highest in 1991.
- Non-zero catches of adult native species were significantly lower in 1992 than in other years.
- The proportion of zero catches for adult Humpback Chub rose from 0.71 (1991) to 0.90 (1994) (G = 91.43, p < 0.001).
- Juvenile Humpback Chub CPE declined from 1991 to 1992, increased in 1993, and then fell again in 1994.

**Population estimates**
- Humpback Chub estimates for the lower 1,200 m were 1991: 719 (CI 321–1,799); 1992: 452 (CI 272–902); 1993: 788 (CI 536–1,226); 1994: 648 (CI 463–1,159), although 1991 was reported as 719 in the source.
- Confidence intervals overlapped, so estimates did not differ significantly among years, and the correlation with year was not significant (r = −0.33, p = 0.66).
- Estimates were much smaller than those of earlier researchers (over 5,000 individuals), though methods and areas differed.

**Discharge effects**
- Adult and juvenile Humpback Chub and adult Speckled Dace catch rates were higher at base flow than during runoff.
- Non-zero catches of adult Flannelmouth Sucker and adult and juvenile Bluehead Sucker were higher during elevated discharge.
- Daily catch rates declined during the May–June 1992 and May 1994 floods.

**Health and reproduction**
- Mean Fulton's condition factor for adult Humpback Chub was 0.80 (1991), 0.84 (1992), 0.80 (1993) and 0.76 (1994); condition differed among years (H = 11.80, p < 0.05) without a progressive trend.
- Female condition was higher than male in all four years, significantly so only in 1991 and 1992.
- Males matured at shorter lengths than females. Both sexes may reach maturity by age 2, though females expressing gametes were rare.
- Fewer than 2% of female chub expressed gametes, versus more than 8% of males, and no females with extrudable gametes were captured in 1994.

**Parasites**
- Fewer than 5% of Humpback Chub were infected with *Lernaea cyprinacea*. In 1994, 39 chub were infected, with a mean intensity of 1.1 parasites per fish and a maximum of 10 on one fish.
- Infestation was lower than that reported by Kaeding and Zimmerman (1983), suggesting a decline since then.

**Channel catfish diet**
- Eleven channel catfish were captured. Only the one caught by angling contained fish remains (a 175 mm sucker and an unidentified fish of about 190 mm); the others contained mainly insects.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The Humpback Chub population appears relatively stable since 1987, but the decline in the largest adult chub in 1994 is a concern.
- Decreased catch rates during high-discharge years should not automatically be read as population decline.
- Several consecutive low-flow years could let non-native populations increase and harm native fishes, especially larvae.
- Continued annual monitoring is needed, particularly if a new flow regime producing warmer releases is implemented under the Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement.
- Future work should use active gear such as trotlines or angling to better assess channel catfish predation, and should record viscera.
