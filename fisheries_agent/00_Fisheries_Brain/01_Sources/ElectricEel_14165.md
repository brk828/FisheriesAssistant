# Electric Eel (Electrophorus electricus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gymnotidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1963 Encyclopaedia Britannica entry describes the [[Electric Eel]] (*Electrophorus electricus*), a South American fresh-water fish with an elongated, nearly round-in-cross-section body. The text covers its taxonomic placement, external anatomy, the structure and output of its [[Electric Organ]], its use of electric shocks to immobilize prey, and its place among other [[Electric Fishes]]. The document is a general reference article, not a research report, and it contains no sample sizes or field data.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The article states that the electric eel is not closely related to true eels (order [[Anguilliformes]]). It places the species in the largest order of fresh-water fishes, the Cypriniformes or Ostariophysi, the group that includes minnows, carps and catfishes. It also places the species in the family [[Gymnotidae]], which the text notes includes the knifefishes kept by home aquarists.

*Editorial note:* Current classification places the family Gymnotidae in the order [[Gymnotiformes]], which is a separate order within the superorder [[Ostariophysi]], not within Cypriniformes. The 1963 text predates this arrangement, so the order assignment above should be read with that in mind.

**Size.** Electric eels commonly exceed three feet in length. The article says they are reported to reach eight to nine feet, with a body thickness comparable to a man's thigh.

**External morphology.**
- Pelvic fins are absent.
- Dorsal and caudal fins are rudimentary.
- The anal fin runs the full length of the body, extending forward to the anus, which lies under the throat.
- The tail region makes up about four-fifths of the total body length and contains the electric organ.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Electric organ and discharge.** The electric organ consists of longitudinal columns, each functioning as a battery built from a very large number of electric plates. Current flows from the electrically positive tail end to the negative front end. The strongest shock occurs when the head and tail contact well-separated points on a victim's body. Shock severity depends on factors including the fish's size and health. For a very brief duration, the article reports more than 500 volts at about 2 amperes (about 1,000 watts) measured in some cases.

**Function.** The main use of the discharge appears to be immobilizing prey. The article states the shock is strong enough to stun a man and perhaps kill a horse. The electric organs are innervated by numerous branches of the [[Spinal Nerves]], so the fish can discharge them at will.

**Behavior.** Electric eels are described as rather sluggish, as are other electric fishes.

**Comparison with other electric fishes.** The article compares the species with:
- [[Electric Ray]] (*Torpedo*, etc.) of warm and temperate seas
- [[Electric Stargazer]] (*Astroscopus*, etc.) of warm American shores
- [[Electric Catfish]] (*Malapterurus electricus*) of the [[Nile River]]

According to the text, electric capacity evolved independently in each of these groups. In all of them, the electric organs are modified muscle tissue.

## Key Findings

- The electric eel is a large, eel-like South American fresh-water fish in the family Gymnotidae. Its body is greatly elongated and nearly round in cross section.
- Typical length is above three feet, with reports of eight to nine feet and a thigh-sized body thickness.
- About four-fifths of the body length is tail, and this region houses the electric organ.
- The electric organ is built from columns of electric plates. It can produce more than 500 volts at about 2 amperes (about 1,000 watts) for a very brief moment.
- Discharge serves mainly to immobilize prey, and it is strong enough to stun a human.
- Electric fishes in different lineages (eels, rays, stargazers, catfish) evolved electricity independently, and in each case the organ derives from muscle tissue.
- The article contains no quantitative field, population or sample-size data. Its content is descriptive and general.
