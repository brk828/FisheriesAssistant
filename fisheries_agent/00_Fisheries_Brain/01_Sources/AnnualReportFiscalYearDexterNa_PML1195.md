# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center: FY 1992 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (Dexter NFH & TC), operated by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), covers fiscal year 1992 (FY 92). The station is a federal refuge, study and production facility for imperiled native fishes of the American Southwest, including northern Mexico. In FY 92 it held or cultured 16 listed species (the source text states 17), and its work covered spawning, rearing, stocking, disease management, genetic and taxonomic study, public outreach and staff training. Major events included the loss of six of nine upper basin wild [[Razorback Sucker]] after a chlorinated water release, the FDA ban on [[Malachite Green]], and a temperature-based trial against [[Ich Parasite]] in [[Woundfin]]. The report closes with budget, facility and genetic-planning priorities for coming years.

## Background & Description

Dexter NFH sits on 640 acres in Chaves County, about 20 miles southeast of Roswell and 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque. The federal government acquired the land on August 31, 1931, and operations began in 1932 under the White Act of 1931. Construction was supported by the Public Works Administration and Works Progress Administration.

The station's mission shifted after the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The station joined the endangered species program in 1978 and established three core goals: refuge, study and production. Its seven objectives include:

- Maintaining a viable, protected gene pool of imperiled Southwestern fishes.
- Developing culture techniques for these native fishes where possible.
- Studying their biological requirements.
- Supplying live and preserved specimens to authorized agencies and institutions.
- Exchanging expertise and data on holding, culture and management.
- Publishing findings.
- Running a public information program on the plight of, and recovery efforts for, imperiled fishes.

The report notes that, of about 600 freshwater fish species in the United States, 85 are listed as threatened or endangered. Most of the species held at Dexter come from New Mexico or contiguous states (Arizona, Utah, Colorado, Texas, Chihuahua and Sonora). The text states that no other place in the world holds such a concentration of rare and endangered fishes at one locality. Dexter was designated a Technology Center in FY 91, one of only six in the Fish and Wildlife Service.

Table 1 of the source lists the species held or cultured in FY 92. The OCR'd table gives 16 entries across five families:

- **Catostomidae:** Razorback, Lost River, Shortnose and Warner suckers.
- **Cyprinidae:** Colorado Pikeminnow, Bonytail Chub, Chihuahua Chub, Pahranagat and Virgin River Roundtail Chubs, Woundfin, and Beautiful Shiner.
- **Ictaluridae:** Yaqui Catfish.
- **Cyprinodontidae:** Leon Springs and Desert Pupfish.
- **Poeciliidae:** Gila Topminnow and Big Bend Gambusia.

Most are federally listed as endangered (E); the Virgin River Roundtail Chub and Woundfin are threatened (T).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The narrative frames the Southwest's freshwater decline as a result of damming and diversion for settlement and development. The text says many unique freshwater ecosystems have been altered or destroyed and that native fishes have declined in numbers and historic range, often unintentionally.

Several environmental and biological threats appear throughout the report:

- **Water supply:** Dexter's water rights are described as fairly secure, though junior rights with priority dates after the 1940s could be at risk if New Mexico lost pending lawsuits. The station's shallow water rights date from 1931 to 1933. Low water conditions on the [[Colorado River]] affected reintroduction timing, and the artesian well failed.
- **Water quality:** A chlorinated water release in March 1992 killed six of nine upper basin wild Razorback Suckers. [[Nitrogen Supersaturation]] episodes caused periodic losses of pond-reared fish, particularly Colorado Pikeminnow.
- **Parasites and disease:** [[Anchor Worm]] (*Lernaea* sp.), introduced with wild-caught fish, was a long-term problem. [[Tapeworm]] infected Virgin River Roundtail Chub and Beautiful Shiner. Yellow grub was present at low incidence in Yaqui Catfish, Lost River Sucker and Shortnose Sucker.
- **Chemical restrictions:** The FDA banned [[Malachite Green]] at federal hatcheries in fall 1991 and restricted [[Formalin]] to a few species and uses. Alternatives were needed for disease control.

The report also describes a 1988 survey in which the Woundfin population had declined significantly. Decreased water flows and other habitat changes were blamed.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker**
- 20 female domestic razorbacks (average 5.52 pounds) were spawned in February 1992 using chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). The text also mentions a second spawning in February.
- An imprinting study used morpholine and phenethyl alcohol on one female's eggs, with one control lot. Fish were reared for eight months.
- Of 100,284 fry stocked into a hatchery production pond, 27,240 fingerlings were harvested (27.22% return). A later harvest of 25,342 fish gave a 92.9% return.
- The 1991 Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) meeting set new stocking sizes: 15 inches for razorbacks and 8 inches for Colorado Pikeminnow. Roughly 30 and 40 months of grow-out were expected, respectively. Annual stocking goals of 3,000 to 5,000 razorbacks and 10,000 to 12,000 squawfish were anticipated in Arizona.
- Six of nine upper basin wild broodfish were lost in March 1992 when a private contractor released chlorinated water. The three survivors went to a Colorado facility.
- The station held 270 F1 domestic lower basin broodfish from [[Lake Mohave]] stock, plus about 900 1992 year-class, 11,900 1991 and 14,000 1992 juveniles.

**Colorado Pikeminnow**
- Fish were distributed to several facilities, including 32,460 eggs to Colorado State University and 9,000 fry to Vernal, Utah.
- Upper basin wild fish produced 21 separate genetic crosses. As of spring 1992, 15 of the 21 groups survived.
- Two were shipped to the Yuma County/Colorado River Indian Tribes Fair.

**Woundfin: Heat Trial Against Ich**
- Woundfin were traditionally held in 12 m (40 ft) raceways. Ich outbreaks had been treated with a daily Malachite Green/Formalin bath for 14 days, but Ich kept returning.
- In a 1992 trial, fish were heated by 1°C every 30 minutes to at least 31°C. Thirteen fish died in the heated tanks. Mean maximum temperature was 37°C for fish that died and 31.5°C for survivors (t = -6.577, p < 0.01, df = 13 as given).
- No treated fish contracted Ich, but no control fish were infected either, so the outcome was inconclusive.
- Spawning was delayed by about 15 weeks. Treated fish produced 257 fry, compared with one fry in the untreated raceway. The report notes that the experiment supports the hypothesis that Woundfin spawning is temperature-dependent.
- Thirteen adult tapeworms were removed from treated tanks, and the report suggests temperature extremes may be worth testing as a non-chemical treatment.

**Chihuahua Chub**
- 19 females were spawned on May 8, 1991, producing 128,152 eggs (an average of 6,745 per female). Swim-up fry were stocked in a rearing pond.
- Reintroduction into historic range in southwestern New Mexico was delayed until late October 1992 (FY 93).
- The captive population descends from ten fish collected in 1979. The station recommends adding wild fish for genetic diversity. 190 adults were held at year end.

**Bonytail Chub**
- Five females produced eggs for contaminant research (about 30,000 eggs were shipped to each of two facilities).
- Three females were spawned for an otolith daily growth increment study by Dr. Dean Hendrickson. Growth was greatly reduced at 10°C, and similar at 21°C and 27°C.
- The station held 466 adults from 1981-year-class broodstock.

**Pahranagat Roundtail Chub**
- This species, held for Region 1, was spawned on May 8, 1991, to increase captive numbers. Seventy-one adults were brought in for spawning, and 30 females were used.
- A leaking pond problem was later repaired.

**Yaqui Catfish**
- 31 fish were returned to Mexico to meet Mexican permit terms requiring 10% of fish to be returned once purity was established.
- Genetic analysis was run at Texas State University. The University of Texas (Dr. Don Morizot) completed a second study.

**Cyprinodontids and Poeciliids**
- Gila Topminnow: over 5,000 fish were moved to an overwinter pond, and none were shipped this year.
- Big Bend Gambusia: 60 adults went to the University of Texas at Austin and 50 to a New York zoo. Maintenance responsibility for this species was proposed to be moved to Uvalde NFH.
- 50 Desert Pupfish were sent to the Hogle Zoo in Salt Lake City. No Leon Springs Pupfish were distributed.

**Public Outreach, Training and Visitors**
- Just over 2,400 people visited the hatchery. Seven guided tours were given.
- Staff attended several training courses, and Buddy Jensen gave a keynote speech at India's first National Seminar on the Endangered Fishes of India.
- Two publications were co-authored, including two chapters in *Battle Against Extinction*.

**Future Outlook**
- Congress appropriated $1.428 million for a new office complex, wet lab, genetics lab and water quality lab in FY 93.
- Pond lining was funded at about $324,000 in FY 92, with roughly $250,000 expected for FY 93 under the WMS New Mexico funding.
- The report recommends a formal "Genetic Plan" for all hatchery operations to maximize genetic diversity. It builds on a report by Dr. Tony Echelle on conservation genetics in Region 2 fishes.
- Additional FTEs and operating funds are needed to carry out this work.
