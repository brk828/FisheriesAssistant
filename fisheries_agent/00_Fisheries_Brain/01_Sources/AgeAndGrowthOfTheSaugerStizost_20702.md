# Age and Growth of the Eastern Sauger (*Stizostedion canadense canadense*) in Norris Reservoir, Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by William W. Hassler of the Division of Biological Sciences, North Carolina State College, examines the [[Age and Growth]], [[Length-Weight Relationship]], and condition of the [[Eastern Sauger]] in [[Norris Reservoir]], a [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] storage impoundment in Tennessee. Scale analysis of 3,393 fish collected between 1937 and 1949 year classes shows that Norris sauger grew rapidly, reaching an average total length of 20.3 inches by age seven, with no progressive decline in growth as the reservoir aged. Females grew faster and were larger than males from age two onward. The average condition factor (K) was 1.42.

## Background & Description

The eastern sauger (*Stizostedion canadense canadense*) is a percid fish native to the Tennessee Valley and wider North American freshwaters. Before this study, published data on its growth in Norris Reservoir were limited to an 84-fish sample (Eschmeyer and Jones, 1941) and a few individual records. Comparable growth data existed for [[Chickamauga Reservoir]], [[Watts Bar Reservoir]], [[Cherokee Reservoir]], and [[Douglas Reservoir]] (Stroud, 1949).

Norris Reservoir was created by [[Norris Dam]] on the [[Clinch River]], closed July 28, 1936. It is the oldest TVA storage reservoir, with a shoreline of up to 800 miles, a maximum flood area of 34,200 acres at 1,020 feet elevation, controlled storage of 2,281,000 acre-feet (minimum 281,000 acre-feet), and a maximum depth near the dam of about 210 feet. Winter drawdown of 60 to 80 feet for flood control retards shoreline plant growth and limits benthos in the littoral zone.

Specimens were collected mainly by TVA Fish and Game Branch staff from 1940 to 1951, with the author's own collections from 1949 to 1952. Gill nets accounted for 3,203 fish, angling for 68, and rotenone sampling for 122.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Scale ageing:** Fish were captured chiefly in winter, so the scale margin was counted as an annulus; age was recorded as annuli plus one.
- **Body-scale relationship:** Scale radius was linearly related to standard length (735 fish); the intercept (-1.38) was judged negligible.
- **Length conversion:** A weighted regression was fitted to 388 fish (total length 212 to 553 mm). Equations: standard length = -11.04 + 0.87 × total length, and total length = 1.149 × standard length + 12.64.
- **Management history:** Earlier studies addressed a year-round open season ([[Eschmeyer and Manges]], 1945), proposed winter netting ([[Eschmeyer and Haslbauer]], 1946), food habits ([[Dendy]], 1946a), depth distribution ([[Cady]], 1945; Dendy, 1945, 1946b, 1948), movement ([[Haslbauer and Manges]], 1947), and tagging-based harvest rates (Manges, 1950).
- **Historical catch:** Sauger were only 2 to 3 percent of game fish catch in 1939 and 1940, rising to about 15 percent in 1948 and 1950.
- **Comparative growth:** Northern populations such as [[Lake Nipigon]] (Hart, 1928), [[Lake of the Woods]] (Carlander, 1950), [[Lake Erie]] (Deason, 1933), and [[Lake Winnipeg]] (Bajkov, 1930) grew more slowly and lived longer.

## Key Findings

**Growth**
- Sexes combined, 13-year average total lengths by age 1 to 7 were 8.4, 13.3, 15.6, 17.2, 18.6, 19.6, and 20.3 inches (based on 3,393 age determinations).
- Growth was fastest in the first year (annual increment 8.4 inches), then 4.9, 2.3, 1.6, 1.4, 1.0, and 0.7 inches.
- No progressive decline in growth occurred across year classes 1937 to 1949; the 1949 year class grew most rapidly, and the 1940 year class recorded the lowest one-year length (155 mm).
- Above-average growing seasons were 1938, 1941, 1942, and 1949; poorest were 1939, 1940, 1944, 1947, and 1948.

**Age structure**
- The oldest fish was seven years old; only three of 3,393 fish reached this age, and few survived beyond five years.

**Sexual differences**
- Sexes grew at similar rates in the first year. Differences in standard length were significant for age groups two through six (Hoel's test; age 6 by Student's t test, highly significant).
- Females averaged 2 mm more than males at age one (not significant, P < 0.13).

**Length-weight relationship**
- Females: W = 2.9775 × 10⁻⁵ × L^3.2458 (n = 1,680).
- Males: W = 1.1752 × 10⁻⁵ × L^3.088 (n = 1,412).
- Sexes combined: W = 2.6095 × 10⁻⁵ × L^3.2259 (n = 3,145).
- Weight increased faster than the cube of length.

**Condition (K)**
- Mean K was 1.43 for females, 1.41 for males, and 1.42 overall. Males had higher K at age one; females exceeded males after age three.
- The K value was similar to other Tennessee Valley reservoirs, though Watts Bar sauger reportedly reached 1.6 (Eschmeyer et al., 1944).

**Record sizes**
- About 5,500 sauger were examined; only one exceeded four pounds (a six-year-old female, 4 lb 2 oz, 21.5 inches). The longest fish measured about 21.8 inches (3 lb 8 oz, age six).
- Only 10 fish (nine females, one male) weighed three pounds or more.
- The heaviest male weighed 3 lb (age seven, 20 inches).
- The western sauger (*Stizostedion canadense boreum*) reached 6 lb 8 oz in the Wind River, Wyoming (Simon, 1946).

**Comparative conclusions**
- Norris sauger attained the largest sizes and fastest growth recorded for the eastern subspecies, which the author attributed to an earlier spawning season, longer growing season, and abundant food.
- Northern populations grew more slowly and lived longer, which the author linked to a reduced growth rate.
