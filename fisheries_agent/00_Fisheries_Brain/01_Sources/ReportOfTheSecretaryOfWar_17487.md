# Report of an Expedition Down the Zuni and Colorado Rivers (1853)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This 1853 United States War Department report, submitted by [[Colonel J. J. Abert]] of the Corps of Topographical Engineers, documents the expedition led by [[Captain Sitgreaves]] from the Zuni pueblo in New Mexico down the [[Zuni River]] to its junction with the [[Colorado River]], and onward to Camp Yuma near the mouth of the [[Gila River]]. The expedition was tasked with determining the course, navigability, and adjacent land of the Zuni and Colorado rivers. Beyond the route survey, the report includes a daily journal, an appended natural history account by [[Dr. Woodhouse]], and an ichthyological appendix by [[Spencer F. Baird]] and [[Charles Girard]] describing three new species of the genus [[Gila]].

## Background & Description

The expedition was organized at Santa Fé with a party that included Lieutenant J. G. Parke (Topographical Engineers), Dr. W. W. Woodhouse (surgeon and naturalist), Mr. R. H. Kern (draughtsman), [[Antoine Leroux]] (guide), five Americans, and ten Mexicans serving as packers and arrieros. The escort, under Brevet Major H. L. Kendrick of the 2nd Artillery, was delayed by the Navajo country, forcing Sitgreaves to wait at Zuni until 24 September before departure.

Sitgreaves' instructions, quoted in the report, directed the party to explore the Zuni from the pueblo of Zuni to its junction with the Colorado, then follow the Colorado to the Gulf of California. The party was limited in mules, provisions, and a chronometer that stopped during rough travel. The report also notes that the [[Army Pack-Saddle]] was poorly shaped for animals in changing condition, and that meat-biscuit proved an excellent provision for future expeditions.

Woodhouse's natural history appendix records collections of birds, reptiles, plants, and mammals gathered along the route from El Paso through Santa Fé and the Zuni region. Notable entries include [[Gambel's Quail]], the [[Rock Swift]], the [[Pinon Pine]], and an unnamed rattlesnake species, *Crotalus lecontei*, which bit Woodhouse at Zuni.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Zuni River is described as a mere rivulet, "not entitled to the name of river," with corn fields and orchards supported by irrigation and seasonal rain. Much of the country traversed was barren, with volcanic basalt and lava, sandstone canyons, and stretches of drought-stressed grassland. Water was the dominant constraint: the mules went without water for four days in October, and the party survived largely by digging at springs and by bringing water from distant seeps.

Temperature varied strongly; the average daily range was given as about 55°F, from 10°F to 65°F. Vegetation shifted from cedar and pinon at the mountain margins to cottonwood, willow, mesquite, creosote, and cacti along the lower Colorado. Ruins of a large prehistoric town were noted near the Little Colorado, with stone and adobe-free houses up to three stories, and pottery resembling that of modern New Mexico pueblos.

Human contact was frequent. Yampai and Tonto Apache groups, the Coyotero Apache, Cojninos, Mohaves, and Yumas were encountered. The Mohave people were described as unusually tall, with men averaging at least six feet. Several confrontations occurred, including arrow attacks at camps on 27 October, 1 November, 3 November, and 9 November, one of which killed a soldier of the escort.

## Key Findings

- **Route and survey:** The expedition traced the Zuni River to the Little Colorado, then to the Colorado, and reached Camp Yuma on 30 November. Sitgreaves reported that the planned Rio Virgin junction was abandoned because of the poor condition of the animals and reduced rations.
- **Navigability:** The Colorado was described as obstructed by shifting sand bars, with rocky promontories and defiles in which navigation was difficult except at high water.
- **Logistics:** Mules were killed for food from late November; the party destroyed spare gear to lighten loads. Camp Yuma provided rations for the onward journey to San Diego.
- **Natural history:** Woodhouse's appendix documented new or notable specimens, including a new swift, a new pouched rat, a new squirrel, and a number of birds and reptiles, several later described by Doctors Torrey and Gray and others.
- **Fishes:** Baird and Girard described three new species of [[Gila]] (published in *Proceedings of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia*, vol. VI, 1853), all collected by Woodhouse in the Zuni River:
  - ***Gila robusta*** - Body very stout anteriorly, tapering suddenly to the tail; eyes near the upper margin of the head; scales sub-elliptical. Upper jaw overlaps the lower; no barbels; six branchiostegal rays; dorsal with nine soft rays and a rudimentary spine. Colour grayish brown above, yellowish beneath.
  - ***Gila elegans*** - Body very slender, tail strongly attenuated, fins well developed, caudal deeply emarginated. Specimen nearly 12 inches long. Dorsal with nine soft rays; colour uniformly reddish with yellow fins; lateral line distinct.
  - ***Gila gracilis*** - Body nearly fusiform, head nearly conical, eyes proportionally large, scales sub-circular. Total length four and a quarter inches; dorsal with eight soft rays and three spines; colour yellowish-brown dorsally, silvery white on sides and belly.
- **Genus diagnosis:** *Gila* is defined by a subfusiform, compressed body; a concave upper head outline; no barbels; two rows of pharyngeal teeth with slightly hooked tips; four branchial arches; and a lateral line that forms an open curve on the abdomen.
- **Expedition conclusions:** The country was judged to be of little interest for settlement, with most of the traversed land "barren, and without general interest." The report notes that the Colorado and its tributaries lacked any freshwater mussel (*Unio*) shells, though fossil oysters and *Anodon* shells were found on the desert.
