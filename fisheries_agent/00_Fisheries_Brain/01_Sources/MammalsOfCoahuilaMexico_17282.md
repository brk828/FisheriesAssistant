# Mammals of Coahuila, México

#mammalogy #biogeography #mammals #rodents #bats #carnivores

## Executive Summary

This is a 1956 monograph by Rollin H. Baker, published as Volume 9, No. 7 of the University of Kansas Publications, Museum of Natural History. It presents the accumulated knowledge of the kinds and distribution of mammals in [[Coahuila]], a largely arid northeastern Mexican state. The report covers the State's topography, climate, and [[Life Zones]] of Coahuila; the relationships between mammals and plant communities; the faunal composition and derivation; the role of physical [[Filter Barriers]] in shaping distributions; the history of mammalogical exploration; and conservation. It closes with a gazetteer, a species-level check-list, and detailed species accounts.

The check-list records 144 kinds of native mammals, representing 103 full species, in 60 genera, 24 families and 8 orders. The author also states that most of these mammals must be able to live without access to surface water, since most of the State's 58,052 square miles are markedly arid.

## Background & Description

The monograph was motivated by interest in the proposed [[Big Bend International Peace Park]], which would include the Chisos and Carmen mountains. The author's stated aim is to place on record the known mammal fauna of Coahuila as a basis for further study and conservation.

The mammal accounts give standard measurements, weights, specimen lists with locality data, and taxonomic notes for each taxon. Most accounts are for subspecies, reflecting the author's treatment of geographic variation. Taxa treated in detail include bats such as [[Choeronycteris mexicana]], [[Leptonycteris nivalis]], [[Myotis planiceps]] and [[Antrozous pallidus]]; shrews such as [[Sorex milleri]]; pocket gophers of the genus [[Thomomys]]; kangaroo rats such as [[Dipodomys nelsoni]]; the endemic [[Mexican Prairie Dog]] (*Cynomys mexicanus*); and carnivores such as the [[Gray Wolf]] and [[Black Bear]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Topography and climate.** Coahuila lies in northern Mexico between the Central Plateau (Mesa del Norte) and the Gulf Coastal Plain. Mountain ranges trend northwest-southeast and are mostly anticlines or domes of Cretaceous limestone. Most mountains are below 6000 ft, and the highest peaks exceed 9000–10,000 ft. Rainfall is low: the author cites an average of 23 inches at Rancho La Mariposa on the east side of the mountains, under 17 inches on a ranch to the west, and probably under 10 inches over much of western Coahuila. Summer rains fall mainly on the eastern mountains, and winters are cool to cold, with northerly "nortes" winds. Permanent streams are few, chiefly the [[Rio Grande]] (northern border), Rio Nazas and Rio Aguanaval; surface water is scarce west of the mountains.

**Life zones and vegetation.** Following [[Merriam Life Zones]] and Muller's vegetation types, the author recognizes the Lower Sonoran (over three-fourths of the State), Upper Sonoran (less than one-fourth), Transition (less than one-fifth) and Canadian life zones (in the summit areas of about four mountains). Major vegetation types include Tamaulipan Thorn Shrub, Chihuahuan Desert Shrub, Piedmont Shrub, Montane Low Forest (oak-pinyon-juniper), Montane Chaparral, Grassland, Montane Mesic Forest, and Subalpine Humid Forest.

**Faunal composition.** Genera were assigned to Tropical, Sonoran and Boreal affinities. Of 56 native genera (43 non-flying, 13 bats), most have Sonoran or Boreal affinities, and most occur at lower elevations; 79 per cent of genera occur in the Lower Sonoran life zone. Montane mammals show affinities with mountains to the northwest (north of latitude 26° N), to the west, and to the south.

**Filter barriers.** Three barriers restrict small mammal dispersal: the Sierra del Carmen–Sierra Madre Oriental Axis, the Rio Grande, and the Southern Coahuila Filter-Barrier. Populations on either side often differ enough to be classed as separate subspecies.

**Conservation.** Hunting, livestock competition, trapping and poisoning have reduced many large mammals. The [[Desert Bighorn]] has disappeared from most desert ranges. The grizzly bear was gone before 1900, and bison and wapiti disappeared before about 1850. Beavers survive mainly along the Rio Grande, with a slow decline elsewhere. The Mexican prairie dog is endangered in parts of its range where it occurs on agricultural land. The author notes that game laws were hard to enforce, and that a Presidential Decree in October 1950 closed seasons for all game in Coahuila.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** 144 kinds of native mammals (103 full species) in 60 genera, 24 families, and 8 orders are recorded for Coahuila. Dedicated accounts cover most taxa, with specimen counts and localities.
- **Aridity:** Most mammals must live without drinking surface water, and mammals are generally uncommon, so trap yields are often poor.
- **Endemism:** Six species occur only in Coahuila or in Coahuila and adjacent Mexican states: [[Sorex milleri]], [[Scalopus montanus]], [[Sciurus alleni]], [[Cynomys mexicanus]], [[Dipodomys nelsoni]], and [[Neotoma goldmani]].
- **Relict populations:** Sorex milleri is considered a relict of the Sorex cinereus group. Scalopus montanus may also be a relict in the Sierra del Carmen. Cynomys mexicanus is considered a relict of northward faunal shifts after the last ice sheet.
- **Biogeographic barriers:** The Sierra del Carmen–Sierra Madre Oriental Axis, Rio Grande and Southern Coahuila Filter-Barrier structure subspecies boundaries, several of which are documented with intergradation evidence.
- **Montane routes:** Montane mammals likely reached Coahuila by northern, trans-plateau and southern routes. The present montane fauna is considered impoverished relative to what might have arrived.
- **Sierra de la Madera transect:** Field parties took 74 small mammals of 15 species at 12 collecting stations in 1952–1954. Most species are restricted to one or two vegetation types.
- **Bats:** Notable records include a colony of about 100 [[Lappet-chinned Bat]] (*Aéllo megalophylla senicula*) in a cave at Puerta Santana, a single known locality for [[Leptonycteris nivalis]] near Arteaga (24 specimens), and a single known specimen of [[Myotis planiceps]].
- **Conservation status:** The grizzly bear is extinct in the State. Beaver survive mainly along the Rio Grande. Wolves now seem restricted to western Coahuila. Desert bighorn, pronghorn and bison have declined sharply.
- **Historical exploration:** Collections were made from the 1850s to the 1950s by researchers including Baird, Palmer, Nelson, Goldman, Marsh, and Baker's own University of Kansas parties, which began fieldwork on 8 July 1949 and involved 80 students and staff.
