## Executive Summary

This document is primarily a **bibliographic index** (publications 1–58, spanning 1940–1957) from the *Instituto de Zoologia "Dr. Augusto Nobre"* in Porto, Portugal, covering a wide range of zoological topics (arachnids, isopods, myriapods, lepidoptera, birds, cetaceans, and fish). Embedded within this bibliography is a full popular-science article, **"Anfipodes Troglóbios"** ("Troglobitic Amphipods") by **Amílcar Mateus** (1957), which is the substantive scientific content of the source. The article addresses **subterranean (phreatobitic) amphipod crustaceans** found in Portuguese wells, springs, and caves, discussing their morphology, physiology, ecology, collection methods, and the paucity of knowledge on Portuguese groundwater amphipod fauna — most notably **Pseudoniphargus africanus** and an undescribed new species/subgenus of **[[Niphargus]]**.

Although the surrounding bibliography references fish research — notably **Machado Cruz, J. A.**'s studies (1954, 1955, 1956) on the biology of the **Sardinha do Norte de Portugal** (*[[Sardina Pilchardus]]*), covering vertebral counts, age structure, reproduction, and generational succession in the sardine fishery — the featured article itself concerns non-fish invertebrate fauna of subterranean freshwater habitats.

## Background & Description

**Troglobitic/Phreatobitic Amphipods (general, e.g. *[[Niphargus]]*, *Pseudoniphargus africanus*):**
- Body laterally compressed and slightly curved (diagnostic of the order Amphipoda within Crustacea).
- Chitinous exoskeleton impregnated with calcareous salts; segmented body with jointed appendages (typical Arthropoda/Crustacea traits).
- Two pairs of antennae (cephalic appendages), numerous paired appendages.
- Eyes, when present, are **sessile** (not stalked, unlike Decapoda such as crabs, lobsters, shrimp).
- Adaptive troglomorphic traits: **depigmentation**, **eye atrophy or complete loss**, compensatory hypertrophy of chemosensory/tactile senses (olfaction and touch), concentrated in the antennae.
- Small body size: typically under 30 mm; some species as small as 1.5 mm; Portuguese specimens collected reach up to 9 mm.
- Respiration via branchial lamellae/sacs attached at the base of certain appendages — requiring water or sufficient humidity to function.
- Females possess ventral **oostegites (lamelas incubadoras)** forming a brood pouch (**marsúpio**) where fertilized eggs and juveniles are retained post-hatching.
- Periodic **molting (ecdysis)**, producing discontinuous growth with vulnerable intermolt periods.

**Reference species:**
- *[[Gammarus]]* sp. — illustrated as a representative (non-troglobitic) amphipod genus (~10x magnification in original figure).
- *Pseudoniphargus africanus* — the only formally recorded phreatobitic amphipod species from Portuguese groundwater at time of writing; originally described by **Chevreux** from wells near Béne and a spring at Kef-Djemel (Beni-Salah mountains), Algeria.
- An undescribed new species of *[[Niphargus]]*, representing a **new subgenus**, collected by the author and Dr. José Braga — description pending publication at time of writing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Groundwater systems — wells (poços), springs (nascentes/fontes), underground water courses, and cave waters (fauna cavernícola). Some species tolerate brackish coastal groundwater, supporting hypotheses of marine ancestral origin for cavernicolous amphipods.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Adapted to stable, generally low temperatures; recorded at altitudes up to 2,150 m (Caucasus). Some populations tolerate thermal springs (e.g., Budapest spring >24°C) or near-freezing/ice-covered water. Experiments by speleologist **A. Viré** showed cavernicolous amphipods tolerate gradual warming up to ~23°C but suffer near-instant mortality with abrupt thermal shifts; surface-dwelling amphipods tolerated up to 30°C. More recent observations suggest cavernicolous amphipods can tolerate slow temperature changes exceeding 20°C if gradual.
- **Photic sensitivity:** Avoid intense light even when eyeless, indicating extra-ocular photoreception.
- **Water chemistry tolerance:** Found in potable water, mineralized thermal/sulfurous springs, and brackish coastal groundwater; apparently avoid ferruginous and highly acidic waters (exact tolerance limits not yet established).
- **Trophic ecology:** Low metabolism enables long fasting (one American cavernicolous specimen reportedly survived 2 years without food); opportunistically voracious when food is available — consuming live prey (protozoans, annelids, other crustaceans, insects), carrion, bat guano, beef bait, bread, and even plant matter (elm leaves, grains, per researcher **Husson**); some species considered strictly **geophagous**; cannibalism observed in captivity.
- **Reproduction:** No fixed breeding season identified — a trait considered characteristic of troglobitic organisms generally.
- **Distribution (Pseudoniphargus africanus):** Algeria (type locality), France (Banyuls-sur-Mer, Corsica), Spain (caves of Guipúzcoa and Santander), Yugoslavia (sulfurous spring, Split, Adriatic coast), and Portugal (a cistern at the Seminário do Funchal, Madeira; Lapa dos Morcegos cave, Arrábida coast, Setúbal district).
- **Related fauna:** Contrasted with Isopoda (e.g., *[[Asellus Lusitanicus]]*, described by Prof. Fernando Frade from the Alviela aqueduct), a separate crustacean order also inhabiting Portuguese subterranean waters, more extensively studied by **Dr. José Braga** at the same institute, who described several new isopod species and a new genus (*Stenasellus Nobrei*, *Synasellus* n. gen., among others cited in the bibliography).

## Key Findings

- **Portuguese groundwater amphipod fauna remains poorly documented**, with only one confirmed species (*Pseudoniphargus africanus*) formally recorded at time of publication, versus a much richer known record for subterranean Isopoda in the same region.
- A **new species and new subgenus of *[[Niphargus]]*** was identified from Portuguese material (collected with Dr. José Braga), with formal description forthcoming (not included in this article).
- Thermal physiology experiments (Viré) demonstrate that **cavernicolous amphipods are markedly more thermally sensitive** than epigean (surface-water) relatives, with abrupt temperature shifts being lethal even within otherwise tolerable ranges — suggesting a mechanism for seasonal disappearance of populations following heavy rainfall events that alter groundwater temperature via infiltration.
- Amphipods exhibit **light avoidance independent of eye presence**, implying non-ocular photoreceptive capacity, correlating with sensory compensation via antennal chemo/mechanoreception.
- A practical **field collection protocol** for well-dwelling amphipods is detailed: a conical fine-mesh net (~40 cm diameter opening, ~50 cm length) suspended on a metal or wooden frame, weighted with lead or stone, lowered to the well bottom for several hours, then retrieved and hand-picked using a brush or toothpick; specimens preserved in 70% alcohol (diluted from 90%) with labeled collection data.
- The article functions as a **citizen-science call to action**, inviting well owners across Portugal to collaborate in specimen collection to expand knowledge of the country's phreatobitic amphipod fauna.
- Contextually (from the bibliography), the same research institute produced significant contemporaneous **fisheries science** on the North Portuguese sardine (*[[Sardina Pilchardus]]*), including vertebral count variability (Machado Cruz 1954), age/reproductive characteristics (1955), and generational succession with economic implications for the sardine fishery (1956) — indicating the institute's dual focus on both invertebrate systematics and applied fisheries biology.
