# Status of Introduced Fishes in Southern Nevada Spring Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This survey updates the status of non-native fishes in spring systems of [[Clark County]], [[Lincoln County]], and [[Nye County]], Nevada. The authors document eight exotic species as established, reproducing populations. Two tilapias, [[Spotted Tilapia]] and [[Redbelly Tilapia]], are recorded for the first time from Nevada waters. Several earlier introductions have disappeared, while others persist or have shifted in dominance. The authors argue that introduced fishes threaten the small, highly endemic fish fauna of the desert southwest and recommend immediate eradication of the spotted tilapia.

## Background & Description

Earlier surveys by [[Miller]] and [[Hubbs]], [[Deacon]], and others had recorded non-native fishes in Nevada. These studies stressed that both exotic species and transplanted native fishes can cause serious harm to the depauperate, often endemic, fauna of the southwestern United States. [[Deacon]] et al. (1964) noted that most endemic fishes are small and therefore vulnerable to introductions of small bait and ornamental fishes.

The paper reports field collections and observations made in 1980 and 1981, compared against earlier records. The study sites are:

- **[[Indian Spring]]**: a warm spring about 62 km northwest of Las Vegas, with a *Hypostomus* population first recorded in 1966.
- **[[Blue Point Spring]]**: in the [[Lake Mead National Recreational Area]], above the Overton Arm of Lake Mead.
- **[[Rogers Spring]]**: 2 km southwest of Blue Point Spring, the most intensively studied site.
- **[[Ash Springs]]** and **[[Crystal Spring]]**: in Pahranagat Valley, Lincoln County.
- **[[Manse Ranch Spring]]** and **[[Cottonwood Park Pond]]**: in Pahrump Valley, Nye County. The pond was built from groundwater near the former [[Pahrump Spring]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biological pollution.** The authors, following [[Courtenay]] et al. (1974), describe the introduction of exotic fishes as [[Biological Pollution]]. Unlike chemical or thermal pollutants, introduced organisms reproduce and expand their ranges. Many exotic fishes are trophic specialists in their native range but become generalists in new environments that lack their natural constraints.

**Lack of predators.** Invasion and dominance by non-native fishes in the desert southwest is facilitated by the absence of native piscivores and by small endemic fishes with no experience of such predators. In Florida, ornamental cichlids dominate waters that also hold native piscivores such as [[Largemouth Bass]].

**Habitat refuges.** Deeply undercut banks, tree roots, and holes provide refuge for the cryptic *Hypostomus* at Indian Spring. A small spotted tilapia population survives winter because inflowing water at Cottonwood Park keeps the temperature near 21 °C (70 °F). Water temperature of 14 °C (58 °F) caused large tilapia die-offs there.

**Parasites.** The authors note that exotic fishes can introduce parasites. This may have occurred in southern Nevada, as reported by [[Deacon]] (1979).

**Regional threat.** The authors conclude that fish introductions threaten the depauperate fauna of southern Nevada as much as water withdrawal for agricultural, domestic, military, and industrial uses, or other habitat modifications.

## Key Findings

**1. Eight exotic species are established and reproducing.** These are *Hypostomus* sp. (a third established species of *Hypostomus* in the U.S.), [[Common Carp]], [[Shortfin Molly]], [[Guppy]], a [[Green Swordtail]] hybrid, [[Convict Cichlid]], [[Spotted Tilapia]], and [[Redbelly Tilapia]]. The authors list [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Goldfish]] as established in other sites, and [[Sailfin Molly]] as still present in Ash Springs.

**2. Two tilapias are new records for Nevada.** The [[Spotted Tilapia]] was established in a spring near the Overton Arm of Lake Mead. The [[Redbelly Tilapia]] was established in a golf course pond in Pahrump Valley. Before this study, the spotted tilapia was known to be established only in Florida, and the redbelly tilapia had been recorded in Arizona, California, and Texas.

**3. Local extirpations and changes in dominance.**
- **Indian Spring:** the green swordtail population is gone, replaced by a yellow to pale orange hybrid swordtail. Only one *Hypostomus* was collected on 18 October 1980 despite repeated seining, although a local resident reported that about 500 individuals had been removed in recent months. A face-mask survey on 25 June 1981 found the species in relatively high density.
- **Blue Point Spring:** no guppies or southern platyfish were found in October 1980. The sailfin molly is assumed to have died out. Shortfin mollies, convict cichlids, and a single spotted tilapia were captured.
- **Rogers Spring:** the spotted tilapia is now dominant by numbers and biomass. Shortfin mollies were very abundant, and convict cichlids were rare. Banded cichlids, sailfin mollies, golden shiners, and mosquitofish are considered no longer extant. The 1963 removal effort likely eliminated the banded cichlid.
- **Ash Springs:** convict cichlids were dominant by biomass and shortfin mollies by number. Mosquitofish were common, and no sailfin mollies were seen.

**4. Spotted tilapia behaviour and population pattern at Rogers Spring.** Most tilapias, including large individuals over 100 mm SL, showed the juvenile banded pattern. Many lacked part or all of their pelvic fins, and other fins were often damaged. The authors attribute this damage to aggressive adult tilapias. Densities at Rogers Spring have never been observed in Florida. The authors suggest the spring's carrying capacity has been reached, and a dominance hierarchy led by a few aggressive individuals limits further growth.

**5. Ash Springs spread.** Convict cichlids, shortfin mollies, and transplanted sailfin mollies were found at Ash Springs in June 1964. The probable source was Rogers Spring. The same three species later spread to Crystal Spring, about 8 km to the north-northwest. Introduced fishes were uncommon in a pool holding 35 to 40 individuals of the [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]].

**6. Recommendations.**
- Eradicate the spotted tilapia immediately, because of its omnivory and its preference for green algae. Rogers Spring bottoms were devoid of green algae, which the authors attribute to tilapia grazing. Other tilapias and *Hypostomus* could cause similar harm.
- Monitor introduced populations more often, especially at likely release sites for unwanted pet fishes.
- Study the mechanisms of decline for native fishes, which have been suggested but not examined. Cause-and-effect evidence requires in situ observation.

**7. Unverified reports.** A verbal report from [[Charles Orr]] described "marmalade" form *Pseudotropheus zebra* seen at Rogers Spring in July 1981. Its status is not confirmed in this study.
