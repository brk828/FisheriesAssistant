# Dynamics in Stream Ecology: Juvenile Atlantic Salmon Production

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted chapter from the Canadian Special Publication of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 118, *Production of Juvenile Atlantic Salmon, Salmo salar, in Natural Waters* (edited by R. J. Gibson and R. E. Cutting), is authored by Thomas E. Waters of the [[University of Minnesota]] Department of Fisheries and Wildlife. The paper reviews the concepts of [[Stream Ecology]] and identifies a major gap in knowledge: very little is known about the productivity of North American streams used by juvenile [[Atlantic Salmon]], or about the invertebrate food resources that support them. Waters argues that quantifying [[Secondary Production]] of benthos, [[Drift Invertebrates]] and terrestrial inputs is essential for improving management of this highly valued fishery.

## Background & Description

Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) juveniles (parr and smolts) are the focus of the paper. Waters frames the subject within broader stream ecology, including holistic concepts such as the [[River Continuum Concept]], [[Resource Spiraling]] and the flow of energy through [[Autochthonous Production]] (in-stream primary production) and [[Allochthonous Inputs]] (such as leaf litter and [[Terrestrial Invertebrates]]).

The paper is partly illegible in its OCR form, so its full sequence of arguments cannot be reconstructed with confidence. Its explicit aims are to:

- Summarize the state of knowledge on juvenile Atlantic salmon production in streams.
- Highlight the link between fish production and invertebrate food supply.
- Point to research needs for management.

The reference list is extensive (roughly 150 entries) and covers stream drift, secondary production methods (including the [[Hynes Method]] and [[Benke Method]]), feeding ecology and acid rain effects on salmon rivers. Acknowledgments credit Minnesota Agricultural Experiment Station Paper 19,228 (Project 72).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and productivity.** Waters notes that soft, low-alkalinity waters are common in the igneous-based streams of eastern Canada and the northeastern United States (Farmer et al. 1981; Haines 1981). He expects juvenile salmon production in these North American streams to be relatively low, probably less than 50 kg·ha⁻¹·yr⁻¹ (after Waters 1977).

**Reported production values.**
- Restigouche and Miramichi river systems in [[New Brunswick]]: about 3 to 50 kg·ha⁻¹·yr⁻¹ (Randall and Paim 1982; Randall and Chadwick 1986).
- Newfoundland watershed dominated by lakes: estimated by Chadwick and Green (1985); most production occurred in lakes.
- European streams: a few estimates (Mann 1971; Power 1973; Bergheim and Hesthagen 1990).

**Enrichment effects.** Gibson and Haedrich (1988) documented exceptionally high densities and growth rates of juvenile salmon in a [[St. John's]] city stream receiving domestic effluents, with elevated phosphorus and nitrogen. The authors suggested that enrichment could be a management tool, though the invertebrate food response was not measured.

**Feeding behavior and competition.**
- Parr feed mainly on drift rather than bottom foraging and establish feeding territories or stations.
- They take fewer surface terrestrials than other stream salmonids, but feeding shifts toward the surface as parr grow larger and older.
- Niche separation with [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] (sympatric, possibly competing species) allows greater use of food resources.
- Brook trout feed less on terrestrials than salmon do, and larger parr feed on terrestrials more than smaller parr. Competition is therefore weaker between brook trout and small salmon than between brook trout and larger parr (Thonney and Gibson 1989; Williams 1981).

**Invertebrate data.** Most studies list invertebrate taxa in the streambed or drift, but few give quantitative data beyond density. Exceptions with biomass data include Gibson and Galbraith (1975) on the [[Matamek River]], Quebec, and Gibson et al. (1987) on basket-sampler colonization in several [[Newfoundland]] rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Knowledge gap:** No estimates of benthic production or other fish-food resource production have been published for North American Atlantic salmon streams. A broad dataset on juvenile salmon biological production in streams "remains yet to be acquired."
- **Expected low productivity:** North American juvenile salmon production is likely under 50 kg·ha⁻¹·yr⁻¹ because of soft, low-alkalinity waters. Measured values in the Restigouche and Miramichi systems range from about 3 to 50 kg·ha⁻¹·yr⁻¹.
- **Enrichment potential:** Nutrient enrichment from domestic effluent produced high juvenile densities and growth in St. John's, suggesting a possible management strategy, but the invertebrate mechanism remains untested.
- **Feeding niche:** Drift feeding, territoriality and an ontogenetic shift to surface feeding define parr ecology, and niche differences reduce competition with brook trout.
- **Research priorities:** Waters calls for measuring productivity of benthos, drift, terrestrial inputs and other allochthonous inputs, their dependence on autochthonous and allochthonous primary materials, and their use by juvenile salmon. He also recommends experimental fertilization studies linking fish food, survival and growth. These data would strengthen management of this valued fishery.
