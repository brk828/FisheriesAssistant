# Concurrent Assessment of Fish and Habitat in Warmwater Streams in Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper presents the [[Warmwater Stream Assessment]] (WSA), a process developed for the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]] (WGFD) to evaluate fish and habitat together in warmwater streams. The WSA has three components: reach selection with accumulation of existing information, fish and habitat sampling, and summarisation and evaluation of the data. It combines a [[Summary Table]] with [[Hierarchical Decision Trees]] built on [[Faunal Filters]], so that the expected native fish assemblage can be compared with the fish actually observed. The authors argue that index-based tools such as the [[Index of Biotic Integrity]] and the [[Fish Habitat Rating]] are simple but do not integrate fish and habitat information or describe individual species. The WSA is presented as a framework that other regions may adapt. Two case studies, [[Horse Creek]] and the [[Laramie River]], illustrate its use. Its main limitation is reduced utility in species-rich systems.

## Background & Description

Many native North American fishes have declined or been extirpated, particularly in the Great Plains and Rocky Mountain regions. Patton et al. (1998) found that over 50% of native fish species in the Missouri River drainage of Wyoming have experienced reduced distributions. Management in the western United States has historically focused on sport fishes, especially coldwater salmonids, so native non-game fishes have received less attention. The WGFD is responsible for all aquatic wildlife and must inventory, evaluate and enhance habitat for them.

Warmwater streams are difficult to assess. They have high [[Fish Species Richness]], diverse life histories and complex abiotic and biotic interactions. Many existing tools are sampling protocols rather than evaluation methods, and the American Fisheries Society manual *Aquatic Habitat Assessment: Common Methods* (Bain & Stevenson 1999) is one example. The authors note that a few index-based methods exist, but each has limits: the IBI needs pristine reference sites and does not describe individual species, and habitat indices hide the requirements of particular species.

The WSA is built on a "synergistic" view in which collection, analysis and interpretation of fish and habitat data form one integrated process. Its hierarchical faunal filters follow Simpson (1953) and Smith & Powell (1971). Species pass through a physiological filter (freshwater versus marine), then geographical filters (glaciation, zoogeography) that define continental and regional faunas, and finally local habitat and biotic filters.

**Sampling methods**

- **Reach selection:** Reaches are chosen in advance, and watershed disturbances are documented using WGFD protocols (for example impoundments, irrigation diversions, grazing, oil and gas development, fire).
- **Reach length:** Reaches are at least 200 m long in streams with mean wetted widths up to 11 m. In rivers wider than 11 m, reach length is 20 times the mean wetted width.
- **Fish gear:** [[Pulsed-DC Electric Fishing]] is the primary method. Backpack units suit streams narrower than 5 m, bank or towed units suit larger streams, and boat-mounted gear suits large rivers. [[Seining]] is used where turbidity or conductivity makes electric fishing ineffective. Fish are identified to species and counted.
- **Habitat measurements:** Variables are recorded at reach scale and [[Channel Unit]] scale (pools, riffles, runs, side channels, backwaters). They include [[Elevation]], [[Turbidity]], [[Intermittence]], channel morphology, maximum depth, mean wetted width, substrate composition, embeddedness, instream cover and [[Habitat Combinations]] at 0.5 m depth intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Warmwater fishes depend on combinations of habitat features, such as deep pools with woody debris, rather than on single variables. The WSA therefore records habitat patches as combinations within each channel unit, which the authors say sets it apart from other methods.

Species absence does not automatically indicate disturbance. Causes can include insufficient sampling, barriers to movement or rarity. Evaluation therefore requires professional judgment about the historic condition of a reach. Information gathered before sampling about the watershed is central to this judgment.

The [[Horse Creek]] headwaters are mountain streams with narrow, steep channels and fish dominated by introduced [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]]. Downstream the stream widens and warms, and its fish assemblage shifts toward cyprinids and catostomids. Irrigation withdrawals and return flows strongly influence the study reach, and upstream and downstream areas are often intermittent or dewatered in summer.

The [[Laramie River]] was affected by [[Grayrocks Reservoir]], built in 1980, which changed channel form and flow. Non-native piscivores were introduced to the reservoir to create sport fisheries. Downstream, those predators and altered habitat appear to have eliminated several species. Historically, Wyoming streams lacked these piscivores because dynamic flows and unstable substrates gave poor habitat for nest-building centrarchids.

## Key Findings

**Methodological findings**

- The WSA integrates fish and habitat data through hierarchical decision trees and a summary table, rather than reducing them to a single index.
- Decision trees test native distribution, elevation, stream width, turbidity, substrate, deep-water habitat and the presence of piscivorous fish. A negative answer at any step points to a likely limiting factor.
- Native ranges and elevation thresholds were taken from the literature and calibrated for Wyoming with empirical data. For example, [[Suckermouth Minnow]] occur at elevations below 1341 m in Wyoming.
- The summary table sorts species into three groups: expected and present, expected and absent, and unexpected or non-native.
- The authors judge the WSA useful for management but caution that it is less suited to systems with high species richness, where distribution maps and decision trees may be too cumbersome.

**Horse Creek case study**

- Sixteen native species were expected in the reach, about 50 km south of Torrington, Wyoming, at 1319 m elevation.
- All expected species except [[Longnose Sucker]] were collected. [[Common Carp]] was the only non-native species sampled.
- The reach had a maximum depth of 1.0 m, with silt and gravel substrate, and aquatic vegetation was the most abundant cover.
- Suckermouth minnow was present, and its decision tree showed suitable conditions. The reach lay within native range, was under 24 m wide, had low turbidity, had riffle and run habitats with gravel and cobble, had deep-water habitat, and lacked piscivorous fish.
- The authors judged the reach only lightly affected by disturbance. Longnose sucker, which normally occurs at higher elevations, may have been blocked from reaching the reach by upstream dewatering.

**Laramie River case study**

- The sampled reach lies 5 km downstream of Grayrocks Reservoir. The expected native assemblage included 20 species.
- The reach was entirely pool habitat. Fish were collected by electric fishing and seining, with several non-native species collected and several expected native species absent.
- Silt, sand and gravel, including woody debris, appear to explain the presence of several native cyprinids and catostomids. Run habitat upstream and downstream, drawn from field notes and photographs, accounts for Iowa and johnny darters.
- Riffle, backwater and side-channel species were absent because reservoir operation changed channel conditions. Species sensitive to non-native piscivores were also absent.

**Conclusions and management applications**

- The WSA is cumulative, with each component building on the last. The authors argue that many agency protocols stop at data collection, which can trap managers in "the activity trap" (Hubert & Bergersen 1998), and that evaluation is needed to support decisions.
- Faunal filters and decision trees, long discussed in ecological theory and mostly for aquatic systems, are proposed as useful for natural resource management more broadly.
- The WSA is one part of a larger Wyoming framework for assessing natural resources, including the Wyoming Habitat Assessment Methodology (WHAM).
- Manuals for the method are available as Quist et al. (2004a) and Quist & Hubert (2004).
