# Effects of Artificial Structure Interstitial Size and Depth on Fish Attraction and Angling Success in C.J. Brown Reservoir, Ohio

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science thesis by Richard Scot Stewart (The Ohio State University, 1982) tests whether artificial structure built from the same material but with different interstitial sizes, and placed at different depths, attracts fish and anglers differently in a flood-control and recreational reservoir. Chapter 1 used a new sampling device, the [[Popnet]], to sample fish inside internal structures with 75 mm and 300 mm interstices. Chapter 2 compared angler harvest from [[Piling Units]] on the 3.0 m and 4.6 m contours with open-water harvest. The main conclusions are that [[Bluegill]] selected small-interstice structure, that deep-water structure supplied high harvest rates late in the season, and that structures with small interstices and deep placement are the most beneficial additions for this reservoir.

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The thesis has two chapters, the second of which was written as a follow-on to the first:

- **Chapter 1: Fish selection of interstitial size within artificial structure.** Tests whether fish species or size classes select structure by interstice size.
- **Chapter 2: The effect of artificial structure depth on angling success.** Compares angler effort, harvest and fish length at piling units with open water.

**Acknowledgements.** Support came from the Division of Fisheries and Wildlife Management and the Ohio Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit (The Ohio State University), Dingell-Johnson Federal Aid Project F-57-R, and the Ohio Department of Natural Resources, Division of Wildlife. The advisor was Dr. David L. Johnson.

**Study site.** [[C.J. Brown Reservoir]] is a flood-control and recreational reservoir in Clark County, Ohio. Reported lake statistics include a surface area of 856.5 hectares, a shoreline length of 16.9 km and a maximum depth of 15.2 m. Most existing structure was removed before filling to seasonal pool in spring 1975. Remaining structure included brush in shallow western bays, riprap on the dam face, breakwaters and docks at the marina, and the original creek channel. The creek channel lay beneath the thermocline and was without dissolved oxygen in summer.

**Piling units.** Between September 1979 and May 1980, 16 units of 25 wooden pilings (0.3 m diameter) were driven into the lake bottom to build a marina. The units were split equally between the 3.0 m and 4.6 m contours, were spaced at least 137 m apart, and were built in a no-wake zone on the eastern shoreline. Each unit was square with five pilings per side, spaced 1.2 m between centers. Pilings extended 1.2 m above seasonal pool. Units G and H carried additional pipes for another study.

**Popnet design.** The popnet is a cylindrical, buoyant, bottomed net (4.25 m diameter, 5.5 m tall, 5.25 mm mesh). Four gaskets fitted around the pilings, and the net was released electrically from waterproof solenoids. The gaskets allowed some fish to escape, which was tested in a retention study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dissolved oxygen and temperature.** During the sampling period, water temperatures ranged from 22.0–27.0 °C at 1 m and 22.0–25.5 °C at the bottom (about 3 m). Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.2–13.4 ppm at 1 m and 4.3–8.0 ppm at 3 m. On one day, dissolved oxygen at 3 m was 1.6 ppm, and catches that day fell within the range of catches on higher-oxygen days.
- **Light.** Light penetration ranged from 3,440–7,700 lux at 1 m and 225–550 lux at 3 m. Incident light ranged from 35,500–77,000 lux.
- **Sampling limits.** Maximum horizontal visibility was only 0.5 m at 3 m depth, which ruled out SCUBA counts.
- **Gear limitations discussed.** Gillnets and trapnets can produce type-two errors when comparing structure types. Electrofishing and angling are biased by species and length. Rotenone was rejected because repeated sampling was needed.
- **Prior work.** Earlier studies of artificial structure compared brush, tire, stake, rock, pipe and car-body materials. Results were mixed, and differences were seldom significant. Depth studies found that centrarchid basses preferred high-profile structure at 4.6–6.1 m, while sunfishes preferred low-profile structure in shallow water (see [[Prince et al. 1975]]).

## Key Findings

### Chapter 1: Interstitial Size Selection

**Sampling effort.** Fish were collected 27 times from small-interstice structures and 28 times from large-interstice structures (N = 55). Nine nets failed to release. Eight internal structures were sampled on 8 days between August 20 and September 18, 1980 (N = 64 structure-samples planned).

**Species captured.** Bluegill, largemouth bass, white crappie, pumpkinseed, black bullhead, carp, gizzard shad, one yellow perch and one channel catfish were captured. Bluegill and gizzard shad were the most common.

**Escapement.** Escapement through the gaskets was small. Of released bluegills, 172 (94%) were recaptured after lifting. Of released largemouth bass, 44 (85%) were recaptured. No size bias in escapement was found (Binomial Test, p > 0.05). The scan does not clearly show how many fish were placed in each net.

**Selection results.**
- No species selected small or large interstices when total numbers were compared (Median Test, p > 0.05).
- Medium and large bluegill (20–140 mm and 140–220 mm) selected small-interstice structure (Table 1).
- Large carp (60–420 mm) selected large-interstice structure.
- Largemouth bass, white crappie, pumpkinseed, black bullhead and gizzard shad did not select structure by interstitial size.

**Light relationships.** Spearman rank correlations showed that numbers of bluegill and white crappie were negatively correlated with incident light.
- Bluegill, both sizes combined: r = -0.35 (p = 0.01).
- White crappie, both sizes combined: r = -0.39 (p = 0.003).
- Bluegill on small interstices: r = -0.41 (p = 0.03).
- White crappie on large interstices: r = -0.42 (p = 0.02).
- Other species were not significantly related to incident light.

**Interpretation.** Stewart suggests that small bluegill may use individual pipes as lateral cover, that larger bluegill may perceive closely spaced pipes as a continuous lateral surface, and that bluegill and white crappie move to deep water for shade or protection when light is high. Predation by [[Largemouth Bass]] may explain the low numbers of small black bullhead on large-interstice structure.

### Chapter 1 Conclusion

Small-interstice structures are the most beneficial for this reservoir because bluegill, which supply a large share of angler harvest, select them.

### Chapter 2: Depth and Angling Success

**Overall use.** Between May 2 and November 30, 1979, and May 5 to September 14, 1980, complete trip interviews were conducted at boat ramps and, from August, at the new marina. Shore anglers were not surveyed.
- 1979 boat anglers: estimated 32,176 angler-hours and 15,036 fish harvested (scan partly illegible).
- 1980 anglers: estimated 22,192 angler-hours and 11,647 fish harvested, giving harvest rates of 0.46 and 0.53 fish per hour for the two periods.
- Bluegill and white crappie made up 93% of harvest in 1979 and 97% in 1980.

**Structure share.** Piling units covered less than 1% of the surface area but received about 19% of angler pressure. They provided 19% of the white crappie and 36% of the bluegill harvested in 1980. Pressure on piling units stayed fairly steady at 21–24% in every month except June, when it was 11%.

**Depth effects.**
- Bluegill harvest rates did not differ between piling units and open water in 1980 (Friedman's ANOVA by ranks, p > 0.05). Some 4.6 m units gave high rates in July, August and September.
- White crappie harvest rates were significantly higher on 4.6 m piling units and in open water than on 3.0 m units (p < 0.05).
- Piling units in Areas 1 and 2 (containing units A–L) gave high harvest in August and September 1980, when harvest was poor elsewhere. In August and September 1980, 90% of bluegill and 15% of white crappie harvested in those areas came from piling units.

**Fish length.** In 1979, bluegill and white crappie caught in open water were significantly longer than those from piling units (Median Test, p < 0.05). The differences were small and were judged unimportant for management.

### Management Conclusions

- Deep-water (4.6 m) structure provided the highest harvest rates for bluegill and white crappie in late summer, when other areas were unproductive.
- Deep structure may also lead anglers unfamiliar with offshore fishing to try those areas.
- Structures should be sited near access points and clearly marked, because some anglers did not recognize them as fishing structures.
- Stewart recommends that future structure be placed with attention to seasonal fish movements, prey location, water chemistry, and existing structure.
