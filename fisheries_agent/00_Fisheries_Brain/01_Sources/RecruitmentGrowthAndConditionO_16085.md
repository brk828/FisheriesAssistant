# Recruitment, Growth and Condition of White Suckers in Experimentally Acidified Lake 223

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomus

## Executive Summary

This Canadian Technical Report (Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 1396, 1985) by S. Chalanchuk, Department of Fisheries and Oceans (Winnipeg), examines a [[White Sucker]] population in [[Lake 223]], a small lake in the [[Experimental Lakes Area]] of northwestern Ontario. The lake was experimentally acidified, and the population was monitored from 1975 to 1983 to track its responses. Recruitment failed in 1981, 1982 and 1983, when pH was 5.0–5.2. Growth and condition first rose, apparently because of more food, then declined as the population increased and food supply fell. The author concludes that the population is stressed and has a poor outlook if pH stays at 5.0 or below.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a gap in acidification research. Most earlier pH evidence came from laboratory experiments or from presence/absence surveys of lakes at different pH values. These approaches show toxic thresholds but not the mechanisms behind population losses. A whole-lake acidification experiment offered a chance to monitor a natural population directly.

Fish losses during acidification are often linked to physiological failures, such as disrupted salt balance and gill function, which are most severe during acid episodes like spring run-off. Population extinction, however, is usually attributed to [[Recruitment Failure]], which can result from failed spawning, hatching failure or embryonic mortality.

**Study lake:** Lake 223 is a small oligotrophic lake (area 27.3 ha, mean depth 7.2 m). Its pH was 6.5–6.7 in 1976. It was lowered by about 0.25 pH units per year to 5.0–5.2 in 1981, then held there in 1982 and 1983.

**Sampling methods:**
- Fish were collected from late April to late October, using modified [[Beamish Trap Net]]s and small-mesh multifilament gillnets (bar mesh 25–45 mm).
- Gillnet-caught fish were held overnight to assess sampling mortality.
- Each fish was measured for fork length (mm) and weight (g), sexed where possible, and checked for prior capture history through tags and fin scarring.
- Fish over 280 mm fork length were tagged with modified Carlin tags. All fish were batch-marked by fin-ray scarring.
- Age was determined by the [[Fin-Ray Aging]] method, which had been validated earlier.

**Analytical approaches:**
- Seasonal length-frequency histograms and a composite age-frequency distribution for 1976.
- Growth curves from mean fork length and weight at age, with [[Back-Calculation]] of lengths for 255 fish to reconstruct growth in 1971–1975. The back-calculation used a geometric mean functional regression.
- Condition assessed with [[Fulton's Condition Factor]] across nine fork-length groups, split into spring (April–June), summer (July–August) and fall (September to early November) intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report links population changes to food supply and density. Dipteran (midge) populations were the main food source for white suckers. Their biomass peaked in 1979 and their numbers in 1980, then declined during 1981–1983. The author could not tell whether the decline was caused by acidification or by predation from the growing sucker population. The white sucker population peaked in abundance in 1981 and then declined.

Growth responses in acidified lakes have been mixed. Some studies found faster growth in acid lakes because of lower densities, and others found slower growth. In Lake 223, growth of younger fish rose as pH fell, while older fish grew at roughly constant rates.

Density dependence was considered as a possible explanation for the 1982 growth slowdown. Earlier work on bluegill in Wisconsin lakes found an inverse relationship between growth and population density. [[Lake 302]], an unmanipulated control lake with pH above 6.25 from 1973 to 1982, showed no change in growth or recruitment during the same period.

Sexual maturity also shaped the growth pattern. Growth slowed after maturity, which in Lake 223 began at about age two for males and three or four for females. Most males were mature by age five and most females by age seven. This matched a decline in growth around age six. The author notes that the age of maturity was about two years later in [[Muskellunge Lake]] in Wisconsin, a population studied by Spoor (1938).

## Key Findings

**1. Recruitment failure coincided with low pH.**
- Recruitment failed in 1981, 1982 and 1983. No fish under 150 mm fork length (age 0+ and 1+) were caught in 1982 or 1983.
- Normal recruitment occurred in Lake 223 in all other years from 1971 to 1980, when pH was 5.6–6.5.
- Other non-acidic lakes in the area showed no year-class failures from 1971 to 1983.
- Young-of-the-year catches (June–October, 1977–1983) confirmed the failures. Catches were low in 1981–1983 even though sampling effort was greater. This ruled out sampling bias.
- The composite age-frequency distribution showed no recruitment failures from 1964 to 1976. Good year-classes occurred in 1972, 1973 and 1975.
- The author concluded that the failures were caused by acidification and not by natural population cycles.
- Trojnar (1977) reported deformities and poor swim-up of sucker prolarvae at pH 5.02, but no physical deformities were observed in Lake 223.

**2. Growth of young fish increased, then slowed.**
- Fish aged two to six grew faster in 1980–1982 than in 1976–1978 or 1971–1975. Growth rates in 1980–1982 approached the upper limit of the range reported by Beamish (1973).
- Fish older than six grew at similar rates throughout 1971–1982. Their error bars were large and overlapped.
- A slight decline in growth may have begun in 1982. The author could not determine whether this was a poor year, acidification effects, or density dependence.

**3. Condition factors rose, then fell.**
- Condition factors increased to a peak in 1978. The highest mean spring condition factor for each fork-length group occurred during 1977–1979.
- The 1978 overall spring mean was 1.39 ± 0.02 (n = 246).
- Condition declined in 1979. In 1982 and 1983, values were lower than in any year from 1976 to 1981.
- Lake 223 had higher condition factors than the non-acid lakes studied by Harvey and Lee (1981), and higher than control Lake 302.
- Spring values were the most reliable for showing population trends, because summer and fall sample sizes were smaller and more variable.

**4. Sex differences in growth.**
- Females grew faster than males in 1978–1979. From 1980 to 1982, both sexes grew at similar rates up to age four or five.
- Females older than five were generally longer and heavier than males of the same age. The author notes that this pattern matches other *Catostomus* species.

**5. Food supply and population density drove the boom-and-bust pattern.**
- Increased food (dipteran biomass) allowed the initial rise in growth and condition.
- The sucker population increased at the same time. Its peak in 1981 was followed by declines in growth and condition as food supply fell.

**6. Overall conclusion.**
- The combined recruitment failures, reduced growth and reduced condition indicate a stressed population.
- The author states that a poor future for the Lake 223 white sucker population is very likely if pH remains at 5.0 or below.
