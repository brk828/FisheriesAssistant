# Research Proposal Correspondence: Assessing Impacts of Notropis lutrensis on Native Meda

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 letter from [[Bill Matthews]] to [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University) transmits a draft methodological framework for assessing how the introduced [[Notropis Lutrensis]] affects native [[Meda]] and [[Tiaroga]] in Arizona streams. It also covers staffing and time commitments for the project. The framework focuses on two potential effects: competition for resources (diet and microhabitat) and lowered reproductive success. Community-level shifts are treated as a secondary concern, and agonistic interference is to be observed opportunistically. The author proposes a sequence of field comparisons across 12 sites, followed by field and laboratory manipulations to test causality. The letter is an unpublished working document, not a results report.

## Background & Description

- **Correspondence context:** Written from the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Station]] in Kingston, Oklahoma, dated 19 September 1985. The author's outline was described as unfinished and not in narrative form, and was sent because of an agency deadline.
- **Personnel:** [[Bret Harvey]] was identified as a prospective postdoctoral researcher. He is experienced in larval fish work, stream manipulations, split-pool fencing, underwater observation, and remote fieldwork. Matthews stated that his own time would be limited by commitments to a separate striped bass research program with Oklahoma, Texas, and Louisiana fisheries agencies. He planned a 7-10 day visit in early 1986 for planning, a similar spring field trip, and a 7-10 day autumn visit, but could not commit to a 2-3 week multi-site effort.
- **Focal species:** The source treats *Notropis lutrensis* as the introduced species and *Meda* (and, by analogy, *Tiaroga*) as native species of concern. *Notropis boops* is mentioned as a comparison, noted as more of a drift-feeder. The source does not give common names or full scientific names for *Meda* or *Tiaroga*.
- **Scope decision:** The author argued that a comprehensive study of all possible effects would exceed the project's scope, so effort should concentrate on resource competition and reproductive success.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Potential impact pathways identified:**
1. Competition for resources (displacement of microhabitat or trophic niche; narrowed food or habitat breadth; lower feeding success; reduced growth).
2. Interference with non-reproductive activities (agonistic encounters).
3. Lowered reproductive success (interference with spawning; predation on eggs and larvae by *N. lutrensis*; reduced fecundity via food limitation).
4. Community-level shifts, where native species crowd into a shared "refuge niche" because of *N. lutrensis* dominance. The author cautioned that the community is taxonomically limited, so [[Diffuse Competition]] theory is not needed.

**Environmental and methodological considerations:**
- Visibility limits snorkel observation. Seining and electrofishing cannot reveal vertical segregation or feeding behaviour (e.g., drift-feeding versus bottom-browsing).
- Depth, current speed, substrate, and turbidity were recommended for habitat characterisation. Temperature and oxygen were considered unnecessary within homogeneous sites.
- Larval *Meda* drift appears to occur after dark and overnight, whereas *N. lutrensis* is described as primarily a visual, daytime feeder. This could limit predation on larvae in the field.
- Field logistics include screen fouling by algae or drift, and the need for low-flow, clear-water conditions with minimal rainfall risk.

## Key Findings

**Proposed field design:**
- Four circumstance types, with three replicate sites each (12 sites total):
  1. *Meda* alone (e.g., Aravaipa Creek, upper Gila River).
  2. Stable co-existence (e.g., upper Verde River).
  3. Unstable active contact zone (Gila River).
  4. *N. lutrensis* alone, to estimate potential resource use after native competitors are gone.
- Stomach samples at each site, with niche breadth, percentage use of trophic categories, [[Niche Overlap]] indices, and [[Principal Components Analysis]] of diet by taxon or prey size.
- Collection of 3-4 additional native species at each site for community-level diet analysis.
- Scale ageing for growth (feasibility was questioned), and seasonal fecundity.

**Microhabitat sampling:**
- Preferred method: visual snorkel observation, with the observer moving upstream and recording position, distance to cover, and conditions at fish location. Schooling fish were measured at the school centre.
- Fallback method: [[Seining]] or [[Electrofishing]] at about 100 random points per site (or 50 if logistics require). Matthews' earlier South Canadian River work used 5 m seine hauls at 100 points, which took about two days, and included 10 environmental parameters per point.
- Analysis: overlap and breadth indices, plus multivariate analyses. A joint PCA of species and populations was suggested, along with combined diet-and-microhabitat analyses.

**Manipulative experiments:**
- **[[Split-Pool Experiments]]:** At least three pools per experiment, divided by Vexar screen fences buried in gravel. One side contains *Meda* alone, the other *Meda* plus *N. lutrensis* at natural densities. Stomach analysis at set intervals. The author estimated the run could take a week or less once established. Whole-pool manipulations were preferred for microhabitat shifts, with a sequence of baseline observation, addition of *N. lutrensis*, and then removal to test for reversal.
- **Gila River addition:** Introducing *N. lutrensis* into an open reach, or into a blocked pool followed by rotenone removal, was considered. The author stressed that this should occur only after first-year field results predict the direction of change, and that the value of the large-scale approach needed judgement.

**Reproductive success:**
- Measure standing crops of *Meda* larvae in pools (nets, visual counts, and larval drift sampling during flow increases), comparing sites with and without *N. lutrensis*.
- Test egg and larval predation in laboratory tanks of adequate size, or in split pools with spawning *Meda* on both sides.
- Assess the effect of nocturnal larval drift on predation risk.

**Scope and recommendations:**
- Priority: resource competition (diet and microhabitat) and reproductive success.
- Secondary: community-level effects.
- Opportunistic: agonistic interference, unless early evidence suggests it matters.
- The letter explicitly declines to state outcomes or results. It is a planning document with no reported data.
