# American Fisheries Society Guidelines for Introductions of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-genetics #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This American Fisheries Society (AFS) document, prepared by the Western Division Endangered Species Committee and a group of co-authors led by [[Jack E. Williams]], presents standardized guidelines for translocating threatened and endangered fishes. More than 90% of listed fishes have been subject to introductions, which are used to establish new populations, supplement existing ones, support educational exhibits or begin artificial propagation. Many introductions have been poorly planned or implemented, producing mixed results and, in at least one case, the extinction of the introduced species' own population. The guidelines are organized into three components: (1) selecting the introduction site, (2) conducting the introduction, and (3) post-introduction monitoring, reporting and analysis. The text was circulated for reader comment before presentation for AFS Executive Committee approval at the 1989 Spring meeting.

## Background & Description

Introductions are an integral recovery tool under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, which requires the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to develop recovery plans for listed species under Section 4(f). Results of introductions are rarely published, and few have been analyzed to determine causes of success or failure.

The document illustrates the risks with the case of the [[Amistad Gambusia]] (*Gambusia amistadensis*). Collected hastily in August 1968 as its spring habitat was being flooded, the original stock of 30 to 40 individuals was moved to the Brackenridge Field Laboratory in Austin, Texas, and portions were transferred to Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico, in 1974. In 1979 all stocks were found to consist solely of [[Mosquitofish]], which had contaminated the populations. The species was declared extinct.

Recovery plans for 39 endangered or threatened U.S. fishes with approved plans were reviewed. Introduction provisions appear in the plans of 32 of the 39 species (82%), in addition to habitat protection, research and monitoring. Table 1 of the source lists each taxon against categories including supplementing existing populations, establishing new populations, genetic reserves, artificial propagation, information and education, and biological control agents. Recovery plans for species without proposed introductions, such as the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] and other restricted-range taxa, emphasize habitat protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Restricted-range species:** Many listed fishes occupy small, unusual habitats (desert springs, caves) that are hard to duplicate, so the document recommends protecting existing habitat first.
- **Genetic limits:** Fish from homogeneous habitats, such as warm springs, may hold little genetic variability needed to adapt to new environments.
- **Habitat mismatch:** The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] was moved from its limestone cavern to a cement refuge below [[Hoover Dam]], where phenotypic changes occurred because the environment differed greatly from its natural habitat.
- **Hybridization and mixing:** Introductions can cause genetic contamination or hybridization, as with the Gambusia case and with [[Razorback Sucker]] stocks, where reintroduction efforts in Arizona (begun in 1982, involving millions of young fish) had not yet produced established populations at the time of writing.
- **Invasive and competing species:** Introduced fish can eliminate other native species; the document notes one instance of this, and cautions about [[Biological Control Agents]] used to remove exotics, such as chemical renovation of springbrooks.
- **Pathogens and parasites:** Unwanted parasites and diseases are frequently introduced with fish, stock, and containers, and crowding or stress can increase their impact.

## Key Findings

**Guidelines for site selection**
- Introduction sites should be secure and protected from habitat destruction, with agreements in place, and should be checked for predators, competitors and parasites.
- Sites should be similar to the species' native or historic habitat, and their hydrology, temperature and water quality should be documented.
- Pre-introduction baseline monitoring should establish the presence or absence of the species and related fauna.
- Taxonomic status and genetic composition of the source stock should be verified, and stock should come from a source population that maintains adequate genetic variability (for example, a number of individuals sufficient to limit inbreeding and drift).

**Guidelines for conducting introductions**
- Stock should be transported carefully and quickly, under favorable weather and water conditions, with quarantine and disease screening where required.
- Introductions should be documented, including source, numbers and genetic composition of individuals released.
- Sex ratios, age structure and stocking size should be chosen to limit the effects of demographic and environmental stochasticity.

**Post-introduction monitoring and analysis**
- Systematic monitoring should be conducted for a defined period, with population size, reproduction and survival recorded.
- Genetic studies of introduced populations are an underused management tool; supplemental stock should come from the same source as the original introduction.
- If an introduction fails, the cause should be identified and eliminated before restocking. The authors argue that understanding failures may be more valuable for recovery than some successes.
- Results of monitoring and causes of failure should be published in the scientific literature or administrative reports and widely distributed.

**Overall conclusion**
- Conservation genetics offers important new tools for recovering rare fishes, but application to fishery science is only beginning. The authors hope the guidelines will assist managers in preserving native fish diversity.
