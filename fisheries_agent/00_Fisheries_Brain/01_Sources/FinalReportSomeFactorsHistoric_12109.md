# Factors Affecting the Distribution and Abundance of Fishes in the Gunnison River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 final report by [[William J Wiltzius]], prepared for the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and financed under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] through the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], examines how dams and diversions have shaped the Gunnison River's fishes. It covers the [[Curecanti Unit]] reservoirs ([[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], [[Morrow Point Reservoir]], [[Crystal Reservoir]]), the [[Gunnison Tunnel]], and [[Taylor Park Reservoir]]. Findings show that cold hypolimnetic releases changed tailwater temperatures and flows, altered macroinvertebrate occurrence, and shifted fishing use. The lower river's rainbow trout fishery expanded, while the native and endemic non-salmonid community, including [[Humpback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], continued to decline.

## Background & Description

The [[Gunnison River]] is a major tributary of the upper [[Colorado River]], rising at Almont, Colorado, and flowing about 150 mi to Grand Junction. Its drainage yields roughly 2 million acre-ft annually, about 14 percent of Colorado River runoff at Lees Ferry. Before 1965 the 60-mi reach above the Gunnison Tunnel was a renowned trout fishery; the report cites a 1946 claim that it was rated the best trout stream in the United States.

Key infrastructure includes:
- **Taylor Dam** (completed 1937), storing water for the [[Uncompahgre Valley Project]] (storage capacity 106,200 acre-ft).
- **Gunnison Tunnel** (completed 1910), diverting up to about 1,000 cfs into the Uncompahgre Valley, sometimes nearly the entire summer flow.
- **Redlands Diversion Dam** (1907), about 1.5 mi above Grand Junction, a potential barrier to upstream fish migration.
- **Curecanti Unit**: Blue Mesa (began storing October 1965), Morrow Point (1968), and Crystal (1977). The unit occupies about 40 mi of prime trout habitat.

The [[Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Monument]] is a steep, 12-mi gorge with depths of 1,730 to 2,725 ft. Kinnear and Vincent (1967) described it as an ecological threshold between upper and lower river reaches.

Study methods included:
- Thermographs and water sampling, plus Surber bottom-fauna samples (three per station).
- Over 11,000 fish collected by hook-and-line, seining, shocking and standard 125-ft experimental gillnets.
- Marking: fluorescent pigment on rainbow trout, tetracycline on kokanee, and fin-clipping for creel tracking.
- A 1977 random creel survey using a 2-2-2 design, with helicopter counts.
- Scale-based ageing and length-weight regressions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flows.** Taylor Dam reduced Taylor River flows at Almont from November to June (by 10.3 to 41.8 percent) and increased them in July to October (up to 211.6 percent in September). Tunnel diversions rose sharply: about 66 percent of August flow and 77.2 percent of September flow were diverted in 1938 to 1965. Mean annual discharge above the Tunnel was 1,895 cfs (1910-1937), 1,613 cfs (1938-1965), and 1,510 cfs (1966-1975).

**Temperature.** Blue Mesa releases were cold (about 38 to 50°F in 1973). Reductions of 0.6 to 9°F occurred at the Tunnel from April to September, and increases of 3.3 to 7.2°F from October to March. At Grand Junction, August, September and October temperatures were significantly lowered (3.5, 4.8 and 3.7°F).

**Water quality.** Alkalinity above the North Fork rose from 78.3 ppm (pre-dam) to 113.7 ppm (post-dam), close to but not reaching statistical significance at the 5 percent level (t = 2.21, 10 df).

**Macroinvertebrates.** The giant stonefly [[Pteronarcys Californica]] was historically abundant (over 90 percent of June stoneflies in the early 1960s between Sapinero and Gunnison) but has largely disappeared with reservoir inundation. Post-dam samples show changes in mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies and dipterans, though the author cautions that seasonal variation and sampling differences may explain many of them.

**Fish community.** Non-salmonids historically dominated the catch below the North Fork. Introduced species include [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]]. Native and endemic species include [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Multiple sucker hybrids were documented, with white sucker hybrids increasing.

## Key Findings

- **Historic trout fishery:** Colorado cutthroat trout were the only trout before 1883. Brook, rainbow and brown trout were introduced in 1883, 1888 and 1893. By 1908, rainbow and brown trout dominated, with rainbows sustained mainly by stocking.
- **Trophy records:** The largest rainbow trout was reported at 13 lb (1903 seine). The hook-and-line record is shared by T. C. Brown and M. W. Staniforth at 12 lb, both from 1897 to 1903. The largest brown trout was 15.5 lb (1959).
- **Creel size:** Average trout in creels were about 1 lb around 1901. In 1952 to 1965 the average was 9.6 in. (about 0.32 lb).
- **Pre-dam use:** The 36-mi area above Black Canyon held 80 percent of 49,100 fisherman-days in 1956, in the 80 mi above the North Fork.
- **Post-dam yield (1977):** Overall, the modified river provided 83,660 man-days (anticipated 78,800), 195,263 salmonids (anticipated 342,600), and 1.53 lb per man-day (anticipated 1.40).
- **Morrow Point to Crystal section:** Use reached only 14 percent of expectation (2,000 man-days). Access loss and no boat launch were cited.
- **Tunnel to North Fork creel (1977):** 14,334 trout were harvested in 6,759 man-days. Of 4,260 fin-clipped catchables, 91.4 percent (3,895) were estimated caught before mid-October (95% CI +/- 1,154).
- **Rainbow origin:** Marked fish in [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]] indicated that natural reproduction contributed less than 8 percent of harvested rainbows since 1975. The author suggested natural reproduction below Crystal Dam may supply much of the rainbow fishery above the Ute Trail.
- **Kokanee:** Blue Mesa kokanee were stocked at 600/lb; about 4 percent of 870,000 returned, at $0.63 per fish. Only under 2 percent of spawners returning to Roaring Judy Hatchery were marked.
- **Growth in the Monument:** Rainbow trout grew faster after the dams (1975 means for ages 2 to 4 exceeded the 1965 maxima). Mean length rose from 9.7 in. (1965) to 11.2 in. (1975).
- **Colorado Squawfish:** No squawfish were reported above Escalante. The last reported specimen was an 8-lb fish in 1965. Historical decline was linked to low flows restricting access from the Colorado River and to Redlands Dam. The author suggested backwaters lacking catfish and bullheads as priority habitat for stocking.
- **Humpback Sucker:** Only one authenticated specimen was taken in the 1970s (a male, 19.5 in., November 1975, below Delta). Its decline is attributed to competition from introduced species and hybridization, not to the Curecanti Unit alone.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids with white suckers became more common. White sucker hybrids with bluehead dominated the hybrid catch in some areas, and flannelmouth x white hybrids were nearly as abundant as flannelmouth suckers in one section in 1977.
- **Management recommendations:** The report proposed public easements (the Bill Overman Ranch road, the McClusky property) and a foot bridge across the North Fork. Stocking of catchable rainbow should rise to about 5,000 annually if access improves. The author recommended against introducing additional exotic fish.
- **Reintroduction:** Trout Unlimited planned to reintroduce [[Pteronarcys Californica]] nymphs above Blue Mesa in fall 1978.
