# Bilateral Asymmetry in Wild-Caught Endangered Gila Topminnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether wild-caught [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) from two isolated Arizona sites show the bilateral asymmetry reported in earlier laboratory work by [[Quattro and Vrijenhoek 1989]]. Fluctuating asymmetry was measured for lateral-line scales, pectoral-fin rays and pelvic-fin rays in fish from [[Monkey Spring]] (MS) and [[Sharp Spring]] (SS). No significant differences between populations were found for any trait. The authors conclude that the earlier asymmetry differences most likely reflected an unknown, possibly unnatural, laboratory stressor rather than a genetic or natural environmental effect.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small, live-bearing, federally and state-listed endangered fish that was once abundant throughout the Gila River drainage of Arizona. Natural populations are now restricted to at most nine isolated locations, mainly springs and stream headwaters.

- **Monkey Spring:** a thermally and chemically stable tributary to Sonoita Creek, isolated from that stream by a 10 m high travertine barrier.
- **Sharp Spring:** a thermally fluctuating, spring-fed tributary to the upper Santa Cruz River. Sharp Spring is highly infested with the introduced mosquitofish *Gambusia* sp.
- The two sites are about 100 km apart by water, and the connecting channels are generally dry.

Earlier work reported that MS fish were monomorphic for 25 allozyme loci while SS fish were polymorphic for two, and that laboratory-raised MS fish had lower survival, growth and fecundity and higher bilateral asymmetry. This was cited as a positive link between genetic variation and fitness in endangered species. Later studies did not confirm these patterns, including [[Mitochondrial DNA]] analysis showing no difference between sites and [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] variation found in both populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling:** In summer 1993, 20 fish were captured per site under a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service endangered species subpermit. Preserved and usable samples were 13 MS and 10 SS fish from 1993, but the sample was supplemented by 25 MS fish collected in 1964 and 22 SS fish collected in 1979, all held at the Arizona State University Museum.
- **Fluctuating asymmetry index:** Following [[Palmer and Strobeck 1986]], FA was calculated as the mean squared left-right difference divided by N, tested with two-tailed t-tests and checked for antisymmetry via kurtosis.
- **Mortality contrast:** Laboratory mortality was about 5.5% over 12 weeks in the authors' own [[Laboratory Rearing]] study (7% MS, 4% SS, per the discussion), versus about 50% (55% MS, 44% SS) in the Quattro and Vrijenhoek study.

## Key Findings

- **Temporal homogeneity:** Samples from different collection years were homogeneous within each site (mean per-trait difference 2.8%; probabilities 0.21 to 0.99, mean 0.62), so they were pooled.
- **No population differences:** No significant difference in bilateral asymmetry between MS and SS was found for any of the three traits.
- **Trait-level results:**
  - Lateral-line scales (mean 29.3 per side): asymmetry significantly different from zero for both sites (P < 0.005).
  - Pectoral-fin rays (mean 14.2): not significantly different from zero.
  - Pelvic-fin rays (mean 5.8): MS fish were fully symmetrical; SS had FA = 0.09 (3 of 32 fish differed by one ray).
- **Symmetry rate:** 68% of all sampled topminnows were symmetrical for all three traits (69% MS, 67% SS).
- **No antisymmetry:** All kurtosis values were positive.
- **Power:** Sample sizes were comparable to earlier studies, so the design could detect asymmetry differences if present.
- **Laboratory versus wild:** Quattro and Vrijenhoek's laboratory fish showed higher asymmetry in MS than SS for all traits, while the authors' laboratory fish and wild fish showed no such difference. Two MS and two SS fish in the earlier study differed by two or three pelvic-fin rays (about 30% left-right difference), suggesting extreme stress in the lab.
- **Conservation implication:** The link between allozyme heterozygosity and fitness appears largely coincidental. The authors caution against using it as a basis for conservation prescription. After earlier recommendations, captive MS stock was replaced with SS fish, and later reintroductions used only SS fish.
- **Broader significance:** Several recent studies suggest that a positive relationship between asymmetry and genetic variation is not universal, and the authors stress publishing non-significant results to avoid oversimplified conclusions.
