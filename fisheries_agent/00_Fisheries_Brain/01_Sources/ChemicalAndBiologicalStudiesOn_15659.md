# Chemical and Biological Studies on Some Saline Lakes of South-East Australia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #athalassic-saline-lakes

## Executive Summary

This 1966 preliminary study by [[I. A. E. Bayly]] and [[W. D. Williams]] describes the chemistry and biology of 17 athalassic (non-marine) saline lakes in south-eastern South Australia and western Victoria. Ionic composition was analysed on one series of samples (March 1964), total dissolved solids (TDS) were tracked at roughly three-monthly intervals over 12 to 16 months, and the fauna was collected mainly in March 1964. The authors conclude that the fauna differs in many respects from comparable habitats outside Australia. Fish and macrofauna records are the central focus, though the paper covers the broader aquatic ecosystem, including invertebrates, microfauna, phytoplankton, and water chemistry.

## Background & Description

- **Study rationale:** Saline lakes are remote and poorly documented. The lakes between [[Adelaide]] and [[Melbourne]] are accessible, span the full salinity range, and include several permanent lakes, which makes the region unusual.
- **Terminology:** The authors adopt "athalassic" (non-marine, never in direct aquatic connection with the sea in Recent times) rather than "inland," and use the 3% salinity boundary between fresh and saline waters. Many athalassic waters are chemically "thalassohaline" (seawater-like ionic composition).
- **Study lakes:** Seventeen localities (plus a peripheral pool, locality 16A) were grouped into three regions:
  - [[Lake Corangamite Series]]: the largest lake, [[Lake Corangamite]] (23,370 ha), with volcanic-origin lakes such as [[Lake Gnotuk]] (a maar, c. 35 m deep).
  - [[Centre Lake Series]]: lakes on Quaternary sediments, all saturated.
  - [[Beachport-Robe Series]]: lakes on calcareous dunes, some slightly below sea level.
- **Climate:** Mean annual rainfall 50-60 cm, mean annual evaporation c. 100 cm, annual temperatures 10-15°C. None of the lakes freeze.
- **Depths:** Lake Gnotuk is the deepest (c. 35 m). Lake Corangamite averaged c. 4.4 m (1956). All other localities probably did not exceed 3 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemistry.** Each region showed a distinctive, homogeneous ionic composition. Sodium was the dominant cation and chloride the dominant anion in every series. The Beachport-Robe lakes were closest to seawater in ionic proportions, with higher calcium (mean 2.7% of cations versus 0.7% in Corangamite). The authors attribute the Corangamite salts mainly to atmospheric supply, and the Beachport-Robe salts to imprisoned seawater plus atmospheric input.

**Salinity dynamics.** TDS fluctuated strongly in most saline lakes, with maxima usually in early autumn (March) and minima in spring. Lake Gnotuk was the exception, and the deeper, large lakes (Corangamite, Gnarpurt) were more stable. Two freshwater lakes near [[Mount Gambier]] varied far less. Long-term data for Lake Corangamite (1875-1965) show secular variation, with the lake saltier than the sea before 1950 and less salty afterwards. This variation appears to correlate inversely with annual rainfall at Camperdown.

**Water balance.** Lake Corangamite's mean annual outflow (evaporation, 121.9 cm) exceeded inflows (catchment rainfall 62.7 cm plus lake-surface rainfall 59.2 cm) in a "normal" year. The authors note that salinity and volume are not simply correlated, which complicates estimates of seasonal salinity regimes.

**Biology.** Most of the fauna is of freshwater descent, but several taxa are of marine or semi-terrestrial origin. The authors define three components: (1) a dominant freshwater-derived group (insects, amphipods, calanoid copepods, one cyclopoid, ostracods, rotifers); (2) a smaller marine/marine-brackish group (mainly harpacticoid copepods, [[Halicyclops]] sp. nov., [[Batillariella estuarina]], possibly [[Aedes australis]]); (3) one or two terrestrial or semi-terrestrial descendants ([[Haloniscus searlei]] and [[Coxiella striata]]).

## Key Findings

**Fish**
- Only one locality (locality 13, a small lake near Lake Eliza, 61.9%) yielded fish: a single genus, *Craterocephalus* (Atherinidae). Species could not be determined from the small sample.
- The fish record at 61.9% exceeds most previous athalassic-water fish records cited (e.g. 23% for *Alburnus tarihi* in Lake Van, c. 42% for *Cyprinodon fasciatus* in Algeria), though lower than c. 140% for *Cyprinodon variegatus* in Texas (Simpson and Gunter 1956).
- Salmo trutta, an exotic, was noted in Lake Bullenmerri (c. 7.8%).

**Macrofauna (selected)**
- **Mollusca:** *Coxiella striata* (live animals found at salinities 21.0 to 112.5%) is widespread in Victoria and south-east South Australia, apparently to the exclusion of congeners. *Batillariella estuarina*, normally marine, was found live at 159.2%, the first record from a locality unconnected with the sea.
- **Anostraca:** *Parartemia zietziana* at 108.9% and 141.9%.
- **Amphipoda:** *Austrochiltonia subtenuis* and *A. australis* were both recorded; *A. subtenuis* was found at salinities up to 29.1% and, in lakes of higher salinity, extends the natural range well above the previous 7.9% maximum. Range in lakes with records of amphipods was 6.5 to 216.3%.
- **Isopoda:** *Haloniscus searlei* was recorded at salinities up to 159% (locality 11), far above the previously known limits. The authors treat *H. salina* as a probable synonym, deferring formal synonymy.
- **Insecta:** *Ischnura aurora* (21.0%), a pyralid larva (Lake Coradgill), *Ephydrella* sp. (53.5%), tipulid larvae (53.5%), Chironomidae (including *Tanytarsus barbitarsus* at 22.3%, new to eastern Australia), two mosquito pupae (up to 125%), Leptoceridae larvae (up to 92.6%), and Coleoptera (*Enochrus andersoni*, *Necterosoma penicillatus* up to 61.9%).
- **Polychaeta:** One Nereidae specimen at 61.9%, probably transported to the lake, given its proximity to the sea (c. 1200 m).

**Microfauna**
- **Calanoid copepods:** *Calamoecia salina* (28-131%) and *C. clitellata* (16-113%) were the most widespread, both often bright red. *Boeckella triarticulata* was recorded at 22.3%, well above the previously recorded tolerance limit (4.3%) for a Tasmanian population.
- **Harpacticoids:** An undescribed *Mesochra* sp. (21-93%), *Onychocamptus* sp., and *Robertsonia* cf. *R. knoxi*, which are predominantly marine groups.
- **Cyclopoids:** Undescribed *Halicyclops* sp. nov. (21-62%), and *Microcyclops arnaudi* (probably halobiontic).
- **Ostracoda:** *Diacypris* spp. (up to 131.4%), with three records above 100%, exceeding the limits listed by [[Löffler]] (1961).
- **Rotifera:** *Brachionus plicatilis* and *Hexarthra fennica* (both under 30%).
- **Nematoda:** Found at 50.3% and 61.9%.

**Phytoplankton**
- *Nodularia spumigena* was abundant in Lake Corangamite in March 1964, and again in April 1965 and May 1966.

**Conclusions and Implications**
- The Australian athalassic fauna includes species with salinity tolerances above 100% that exceed published limits from North America and Africa, and it is largely endemic to Australasia.
- Cosmopolitanism is uncommon. Only two cosmopolitan (rotifer) species occurred, and many taxa are restricted to south-east Australia.
- Abundance does not appear to be related to the position of a population within its salinity tolerance range.
- The authors describe the study as preliminary, given the single biological sampling season and incomplete ionic data.
