# Allozymic Variation in Desert Pupfish from Natural and Artificial Habitats: Genetic Conservation in Fluctuating Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Jason B. Dunham]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University, published in *Biological Conservation*, 1998) compares allozymic (protein-electrophoresis) genetic variation among wild and captive populations of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*). The authors attribute much of the observed variation to founder sources, founder effects, and bottlenecks, and argue that isolation, whether natural or artificial, drives divergence. Historically, periodic flooding allowed gene flow among populations during brief wet periods. Current anthropogenic control of hydrology has largely precluded this exchange. The authors conclude that recovery will require restored natural hydrology, which appears unlikely in the foreseeable future, and recommend a captive management program that mimics alternating gene flow and isolation.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodontidae, *Cyprinodon macularius* Baird & Girard), formerly widespread across the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] in Mexico (Baja California, Sonora) and the American Southwest (Arizona, California).
- **Subspecies:** Two are recognized: *C. m. eremus* (restricted to [[Quitobaquito Spring]], Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona) and *C. m. macularius* (the remainder of the range). A third, unnamed form occurs in the Rio Sonoyta, Sonora. This study addresses only *C. m. macularius*.
- **Status:** Listed as endangered by the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1986). Habitat destruction and exotic species have extirpated and fragmented populations. A [[Recovery Plan]] (Marsh and Sada, 1993) included provisions for maintaining genetic variation.
- **Conservation context:** Translocations are a common recovery tool for North American desert fishes. They may reduce short-term extinction risk but are often ineffective at preserving genetic integrity.
- **Research questions:** (1) Do genetic patterns differ between wild and captive populations? (2) If so, what factors explain the differences: number of founders, time since founding, gene flow, or relative habitat size?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling localities**
- *Captive populations (n = 9):* include [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), the Minckley private pond (WLM), [[Desert Botanical Garden]], [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]], Howard Well, Flowing Wells Junior High School, Simone/McCallum Pond, Living Desert Reserve, and Palm Canyon Pond.
- *Wild populations (n = 4):* all around the [[Salton Sink]] (Salt Creek, Cleveland Street Drain, an unnamed shoreline pool, and San Felipe Creek).

**Lineages.** Captive populations derive from three source lineages: Canal Sanchez Taboada (Dexter, Desert Botanical Garden), Terrace Springs on the Colorado Delta (WLM, Howard Well, Flowing Wells), and Salton Sink stocks (Living Desert from the Salton Sea, Palm Canyon from San Felipe Creek, Simone/McCallum from Salt Creek). Boyce Thompson drew on mixed Mexican sources.

**Habitat history**
- *Colorado Delta:* Historically, perennial springs and streams were periodically connected by flooding. The Terrace Springs founders came from five springheads that were periodically interconnected. A rise of less than 1.0 m inundated four, possibly all five, springs. Upstream agricultural diversions (from the 1900s) and [[Hoover Dam]] (1935) subsequently desiccated the delta. Rewatering in 1982–1983 temporarily expanded pupfish distribution.
- *Salton Sink:* Before 1904, pupfish were restricted to isolated springs and seeps. Flooding in 1904–1907 created the [[Salton Sea]], after which large shoreline populations formed. These declined in the mid-1960s following the introduction of non-native fishes, then rebounded as salinity rose and non-native fishes declined.

**Laboratory methods.** Protein extracts from eye, muscle, and liver were run on 12% starch gels (about 12 h, 25–50 mA) and stained for enzyme activity. Loci were scored with [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] using the BIOSYS-1 program. Analyses included Hardy–Weinberg tests, Fisher exact tests (GENEPOP), and Mantel tests.

## Key Findings

- **Low overall variation:** Only four polymorphic loci (sAh-A, Gpi-A, Gpi-B, Ldh-A) were resolved, consistent with generally low allozymic variation in desert pupfish.
- **Hardy–Weinberg:** Only 2 of 39 chi-square tests deviated significantly: an excess of heterozygotes at sAh-A (Boyce-Thompson) and a deficiency at Gpi-A (Simone/McCallum).
- **Population differences:** Delta-derived populations were fixed for the Gpi-B allele that dominated Salton Sink populations (>90% frequency in six of seven populations, fixed at Living Desert). Dexter and Desert Botanical Garden lacked variation at Gpi-A and Ldh-A. Howard Well and Flowing Wells differed markedly at sAh-A.
- **Within-lineage differentiation:** Significant in three of four lineages (Table 4), including Terrace Springs (sAh-A and Gpi-A, p ≤ 0.001).
- **Heterozygosity:** Ranged from 0.064 (Desert Botanical Gardens and San Felipe Creek) to 0.143 (Living Desert). Heterozygosity did not consistently decline in captive populations relative to sources.
- **Key predictor:** Changes in heterozygosity were strongly associated with founder population size (Mantel test, p = 0.016). They were not associated with time since founding, relative habitat size, or number of founding events.
- **Founder effects:** Dexter (founded with 280 fish in 1983) showed less variation than the smaller, older WLM stock (64 fish in 1976). Wild founders for Dexter came from a heavy-equipment pool near Ciénega de Santa Clara, and the authors attribute low variation there to a bottleneck or a small original source.
- **Salton Sink homogeneity:** Wild Salton Sink populations were genetically homogeneous over a large area, likely reflecting recent range expansion and gene flow. Delta wild populations showed circumstantial evidence of fragmentation.
- **Relation to prior work:** Earlier studies (Turner, 1984; Edds and Echelle, 1989) found fewer captive–wild differences. The authors attribute this partly to limited sampling, a caution they say their results confirm.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Continue searching for and studying wild populations before treating them as homogeneous.
  - Consider local adaptation.
  - Current isolation-based recovery can only lead to systematic loss of genetic diversity.
  - Until natural hydrology is restored, manage captive stocks to mimic alternating isolation and gene flow (e.g., controlled mixing and population fluctuation).
  - Non-native species must be excluded, since the fish otherwise needs little more than water.
- **Prognosis:** Survival is poor. Hydrologic control precludes gene flow, and more than 20 million people depend on Colorado River water, making natural flooding unlikely in the foreseeable future. The authors call for high-priority perpetuation of the species as a charismatic symbol of desert aquatic ecosystems.
