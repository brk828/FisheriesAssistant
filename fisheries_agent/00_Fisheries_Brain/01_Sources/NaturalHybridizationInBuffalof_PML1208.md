# Natural Hybridization in Buffalofishes, Genus Ictiobus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This short research note by Donald W. Johnson and W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) describes ten buffalofish from Council Grove Reservoir, Kansas, that the authors interpret as putative natural hybrids between [[Smallmouth Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) and [[Bigmouth Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*). The fish were compared with reference samples of both parental species using body proportions, gill raker counts and a [[Hybrid Index]]. Hybrids were intermediate for most characters, with one character, the gill raker count on the first pharyngeal arch, showing no individual overlap between the three groups. The note also revisits the long-running debate over whether the [[Black Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus niger*) is itself a hybrid. The authors conclude that the variability of buffalofishes makes hybrid identification difficult, and that multiple characters must be used together.

## Background & Description

The buffalofishes (genus [[Ictiobus]], tribe Ictiobiini of the family [[Catostomidae]]) are notoriously variable across ontogeny and among individuals, which complicates identification. Three species occur in the United States:

- *Ictiobus cyprinellus* (bigmouth buffalofish), described as relatively well defined.
- *Ictiobus bubalus* (smallmouth buffalofish).
- *Ictiobus niger* (black buffalofish), often called "mongrel buffalo" by commercial fishermen in the Mississippi River basin.

Earlier work set the context for the study:

- **Thompson (1935)** suggested that *I. niger* was a hybrid between *I. bubalus* and *I. cyprinellus*.
- **Hubbs et al. (1943)** disagreed with this interpretation but emphasized the similarity between *I. niger* and *I. bubalus* and the difficulty of identifying hybrids between them.
- **Trautman (1957)** reported, without description, the same parental combination from Sandusky Bay, Ohio. Seven fish he studied with Carl L. Hubbs were judged hybrids beyond reasonable doubt on the basis of gill arch morphology, gill raker counts and body depth.
- **Heard (1958)** described hybridization-related problems arising in large artificial impoundments.

Specimens for the present study were collected in autumn 1966 by Charles O. Minckley (Kansas State University) and Lanny Jones and party (Kansas Forestry, Fish, and Game Commission) from [[Council Grove Reservoir]], Morris County, Kansas, in the [[Neosho River Basin]]. Shipment for examination was funded by a Bureau of Commercial Fisheries Grant-in-aid to the Arizona Game and Fish Department (P.L. 88-309). Voucher specimens are deposited in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** The study population came from a Kansas reservoir, one of the large artificial impoundments identified as settings where buffalofish hybridization problems arise.
- **Distribution records:** Black buffalofish have not been recorded from the upper Neosho River basin (Deacon, 1961; Cross, 1967). This distributional evidence weighed against *I. niger* as a parent of the Council Grove hybrids.
- **Possible hybridization elsewhere:** The authors had also recently studied an Arizona reservoir where introgression from *I. niger* or *I. bubalus* may have influenced an *I. cyprinellus* population (unpublished data).
- **Sampling constraints:** All specimens were 150–250 mm standard length, so that ontogenetic variation was minimized and the comparison focused on species-level differences.

## Key Findings

**Sample and design**
- Data came from 50 *I. cyprinellus*, 30 *I. bubalus* and 10 putative hybrids.
- Body measurements followed Hubbs and Lagler (1964), except that head length excluded the opercular membrane.
- All gill rakers on the anterior side of the first branchial arch were counted, including both dorsal and ventral rami. Where rakers were fused ventrally, they were enumerated by separating their bases.
- Hybrid indices followed Hubbs et al. (1948). *I. bubalus* was assigned a value of 0 and *I. cyprinellus* a value of 100.

**Character analysis (ten characters, Table 1)**
- Hybrids were generally intermediate between the parental forms, but the degree of intermediacy varied considerably among individuals (see the [[Hybrid Index]] figure).
- Only two characters showed overlap of 95% confidence limits among all three groups.
- Six characters overlapped between hybrids and *I. bubalus*.
- Two characters overlapped between hybrids and *I. cyprinellus*.
- Plotted ranges revealed "problem individuals," fish for which a given trait was not distinctive.
- The broad overlap among all three populations supports Hubbs et al. (1943), who stated that buffalofish species are characterized by a combination of traits, none of which alone is invariably distinctive.

**Gill rakers and pharyngeal arch (diagnostic character)**
- The number of [[Gill Rakers]] on the inner margin of the first pharyngeal arch was described as an excellent character, with no individual overlap between the three populations.
- Raker length was intermediate in the hybrids. Rakers are long and arches slender in *I. cyprinellus*, and short and arches heavier in *I. bubalus*.
- Arch massiveness in the hybrids was also intermediate.

**Overall appearance and mouth**
- Hybrids resemble *I. bubalus* in general aspect, with deep, narrow bodies and sharply ridged predorsal areas.
- *I. niger* was judged an unlikely parent because its body is robust and shallow, although its raker counts resemble those of *I. bubalus*.
- All ten hybrids have mouths similar in shape and position to those of *I. cyprinellus*, though not as large (Table 1). Lips are smooth and thin, as in the bigmouth buffalofish.

**Conclusions**
- The authors describe the Council Grove fish as putative hybrids of *I. bubalus* and *I. cyprinellus*, a combination previously reported only without description from Sandusky Bay, Ohio.
- Buffalofish identification requires several characters evaluated together, with gill raker counts on the first pharyngeal arch providing the most reliable single discriminator in this sample.
