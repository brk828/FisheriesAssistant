# 3-Trifluoromethyl-4-Nitrophenol (TFM) Control of Tadpoles in Culture Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study, published in the *Progressive Fish-Culturist* (vol. 47, no. 4), evaluates [[3-Trifluoromethyl-4-Nitrophenol]] (TFM) as a selective [[Amphibicide]] for removing tadpoles from fish culture ponds. Tadpoles, mainly [[Bullfrog Tadpole]] larvae, compete with cultured fish for food, contaminate harvests, and add biomass and social stress. No simple, safe chemical control existed before this work. Researchers screened three compounds, selected TFM, and ran static [[96-Hour Lc50 Test]] bioassays and [[On-Site Toxicity Test]] trials. Frog larvae proved more sensitive to TFM than [[Fathead Minnow]] or [[Bluegill]], but the safety margin was narrow, and water chemistry and cost strongly affect practical use.

## Background & Description

Tadpoles are a recurring pest in fish culture. Reported problems include:

- **Biomass:** [[Fathead Minnow]] production ponds in Ohio yielded up to 785 kg/ha (706 lb/acre) of tadpoles, about 78% of the pond's standing crop (Kendall 1984). An Alabama minnow pond produced an inadvertent 2,242 kg/ha (2,106 lb/acre) (Prather et al. 1953).
- **Harvest and competition:** Tadpoles must be sorted and removed from harvests, which is labor-intensive and injures cultured fish. They compete for natural food and consume pelleted and flaked feeds.
- **Growth inhibition:** Tadpoles were linked to reduced fish growth in Illinois and central Ohio ponds.

Current control methods are costly and often ineffective: hand removal of eggs, ducks, killing adult frogs, and mowing pond banks. Earlier chemical attempts also failed. [[Sodium Acetoarsenite]] was ineffective, and [[Formalin]] caused severe oxygen depletion without complete control (Helms 1967).

TFM is a [[Lampricide]] registered only for [[Sea Lamprey]] control. Its use against tadpoles in static systems is outside that label, a point the authors flag explicitly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Target and non-target species:** Frog larvae are generally hardier than fish, but some compounds can be lethal to them at concentrations that spare culture species. The study's aim was to find that selective window.

**Water chemistry:** Efficacy depends on water quality. Hardness, alkalinity, and pH varied among test sites:

| Pond | Hardness (mg/L CaCO₃) | Alkalinity (mg/L CaCO₃) | pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 220 | 110 | 7.8 |
| B | 150 | 70 | 9.2 |
| C | 140 | 80 | ~8.1 |

Across the three ponds, hardness was 140–220 mg/L, alkalinity 70–110 mg/L, and pH 7.7–9.2. Laboratory water was softer (hardness 45 mg/L, alkalinity 32 mg/L, pH 7.4). The authors note that TFM works best in softer, more acidic water, which fits the lab-versus-field differences.

**Environmental fate:** TFM is known to be algistatic, so application during plankton growth may be undesirable. Benthic and food organisms in Arkansas ponds were not affected at 12.0 and 14.0 mg/L (USFWS 1963). Photodegradation can break TFM down before harvest, which favors early application where artificial feeds are used. Frogs with extended breeding seasons complicate timing.

**Toxic mechanism:** Necrotic lesions around the operculum and behavior similar to TFM-treated lampreys suggest a shared response. Lampreys cannot conjugate enough TFM to render it harmless (Lech and Statham 1975). Whether amphibians share this limitation is unknown.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Candidate Screening

- **Compounds screened:** [[Endosulphan]], [[Tannic Acid]], and TFM.
- **Tannic acid:** Not selectively toxic to frog larvae.
- **Endosulphan:** Showed some toxicity but was dropped for low water solubility, environmental persistence, and high toxicity to mammals and waterfowl.
- **TFM:** Selected for 96-hour tests (formulated at 35.7% active ingredient in the lab, 36.1% in the field).

**Laboratory design:** Static 96-hour tests (EPA 1975) used seventeen 75-L glass aquaria with 50 L of soft reconstituted water at 22 °C and a 14 h light / 10 h dark photoperiod. Each vessel held 10 fish or 10 frog larvae. Small [[Bullfrog Tadpole]] larvae (50–60 mm TL) and adult fathead minnows (45–60 mm TL) were collected from central Ohio ponds and acclimated for three weeks. Mortality was recorded at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours. LC50s and 95% confidence intervals were calculated by the [[Litchfield-Wilcoxon Method]].

**On-site design:** Trials were run in three ponds (A, B, C) using suspended containers:

- **Pond A:** 92 L garbage cans. Test 1 used 10 adult fathead minnows and 10 small bullfrog larvae. Test 2 added 10 large (second-year) bullfrog larvae.
- **Pond B:** 214 L polyethylene bags with 15 small [[Green Frog]] larvae, 10 large bullfrog larvae, and 20 adult fathead minnows.
- **Pond C:** Polyethylene bags with 20 small bullfrog larvae and 20 bluegill fingerlings.

TFM concentration was measured colorimetrically (Olsen and Marking 1973).

### Laboratory Results

- **Small bullfrog larvae:** 96-h LC50 of **1.39 mg/L** (95% CI 1.08–1.78).
- **Fathead minnow:** 96-h LC50 of **5.50 mg/L** (95% CI 4.54–6.67).
- The confidence intervals did not overlap. Fathead minnows were about **3.96 times** more resistant to TFM than small bullfrog larvae.
- **Bullfrog tadpoles (96-h LC90):** 2.31 mg/L.
- **Fathead minnows (96-h LC50 in Fig. 2):** 3.40 mg/L.
- **Symptoms:** Fish showed anoxia signs, including gulping at the surface, erratic swimming, loss of balance, and death with splayed opercula. Tadpoles were hyperactive, gulped air, lost buoyancy control, and died at the surface. Nearly all had 3–6 mm necrotic lesions around the operculum.

### On-Site Results

- In all trials, frog larvae were more sensitive to TFM than fathead minnows (ponds A and B) or bluegill fry (pond C).
- In pond A, large (second-year) bullfrog larvae were more resistant than small (first-year) larvae.
- In pond B, green frog larvae were more sensitive than large bullfrog larvae.
- Statistical comparison across ponds was not possible because each site had different water quality.

### Ranking of Sensitivity

The authors ranked the groups from most to least resistant:

**Bluegill and fathead minnow > large bullfrog larvae > small bullfrog larvae > green frog larvae**

### Comparative Fish Toxicity

[[Marking and Olsen]] (1975) tested field-grade TFM at 12 °C on fingerling fish. The 96-h LC50s ranged from **5.00 mg/L** (goldfish) to **16.2 mg/L** (bluegill). Tested species included [[Goldfish]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and bluegill. Those data suggest TFM could serve as a selective amphibicide in other production facilities.

### Safety Margin and Dosage

- Both lab and field data suggest 100% frog-larva mortality could be reached with minimal or no harm to exposed fish.
- The LC50 for minnows and the LC99 for tadpoles from the definitive lab tests indicate only a **narrow margin of safety** for young bullfrog larvae in fathead minnow facilities.
- On-site tests are required to set the safe dose for each pond.

### Economics

- TFM costs about **$16.54/kg A.I.**
- **Pond C (softer water):** 3.0 mg/L was needed, costing about **$56.25 per acre-foot**.
- **Pond A (harder, more alkaline water):** 11.0 mg/L was needed, costing up to **$206.25 per acre-foot**.
- The authors describe this roughly four-fold difference as a reason to perform a [[Cost-Benefit Analysis]] before treatment.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

1. TFM is a promising selective amphibicide for frog larvae, but the lab margin is narrow.
2. Water hardness, alkalinity, and pH strongly affect the effective dose and cost.
3. On-site bag tests should be run before treatment to find the safe dose.
4. Timing matters: avoid application during plankton blooms; apply early where artificial feed dominates, allowing photodegradation before harvest; avoid breeding periods unless egg-laying has ceased.
5. Further work should test TFM on other frog life stages, conduct in-pond studies on environmental fate, and screen other mononitrophenol compounds.
