# Mountain Watersheds and Dynamic Equilibrium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fluvial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This paper, reprinted from the ASCE Watershed Management Symposium (Logan, Utah, 1975) and written by Burchard H. Heede of the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, compares landform development in two pairs of mountain watersheds: the perennial [[Fool Creek]] and [[Deadhorse Creek]] in the Colorado Rocky Mountains, and the ephemeral North and South Forks of [[Thomas Creek]] in the Arizona White Mountains. The central argument is that watersheds can exist in a state of [[Dynamic Equilibrium]], a temporary balance between landform and erosive forces, which is disturbed only when internal factors are triggered by usual or unusual external events. The Colorado streams are judged to be at equilibrium, while the Thomas Creek streams are not. Fish and aquatic fauna are not addressed in the document; the focus is on channel hydraulics, sediment, and landform processes.

## Background & Description

The paper frames watershed behavior through the Davisian erosion cycle and the distinction between geomorphic and geologic age. Heede stresses that landforms shaped during the [[Pleistocene]] persist as macro features (U-shaped and hanging valleys), while micro features erode quickly, since roughly 8,000 years is short in geologic terms.

Two contrasting study areas were selected:

- **Colorado sites (Fraser Experimental Forest):** Subalpine zone above 9,500 ft, headwaters reaching about 11,500 ft. Average annual temperature about 35°F, precipitation 15 to 30 inches (average 24 inches), two-thirds as snow. Streams are perennial, with 80 percent of flow between April and October and mid-June snowmelt peaks of roughly 25 cfs (Fool Creek) and 15 cfs (Deadhorse Creek). Parent material is gneiss and schist; forest is Engelmann spruce, subalpine fir, and lodgepole pine, about 250 to 400 years old.
- **Arizona sites (Thomas Creek, North and South Forks):** Elevation 8,400 to 9,200 ft in mixed conifer forest. Average annual temperature 38°F, precipitation about 30 inches (range 20 to 43 inches), half as snow. Streams are ephemeral, flowing only during intense summer storms and snowmelt, over porous volcanic bedrock with considerable deep seepage.

Stream channels in both regions are controlled by [[Gravel Bars]] and [[Log Steps]] that act as energy dissipators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sediment yields are very small in both systems: about 300 ft³/mi² per year for Fool Creek and 150 ft³/mi² per year for Deadhorse Creek, measured in sediment basins. Soils are shallow (average about 2 ft along Thomas Creek channels) and stony loam.

Hydraulic and structural measurements include:

- **Fool Creek and Deadhorse Creek:** Log and bar steps averaged about 7.5 ft apart. Fool Creek averaged 2.5 logs per 50 ft of channel, with 53 percent of steps being gravel bars and a mean gradient of 2.6 percent. Deadhorse Creek averaged 4.8 logs per 50 ft, with 37 percent of steps being bars and a mean gradient of 3.6 percent. Mean velocities were about 0.37 to 1.75 ft/s (Deadhorse) and 0.52 to 4.85 ft/s (Fool). The ratio of cumulative step height to total fall was 0.98 for Deadhorse and 0.70 for Fool Creek.
- **Thomas Creek, South and North Forks:** Channels cover one-half (North) and three-quarters (South) of valley length. Nickpoints were numerous: 14 on South Fork (1.25 miles) and 18 on North Fork (0.75 mile), mostly in alluvium with scarps 0.5 to 4 ft high. Bars and logs accounted for 97 percent (South) and 93 percent (North) of steps. Bars and logs were spaced 15.8 ft apart (South) and 17.0 ft (North). Cumulative bar and log height offset 67 percent (South) and 65 percent (North) of total fall, rising to 71 and 73 percent with nickpoints included.
- **Flow during snowmelt:** Depths rarely exceeded 0.5 ft; velocities in scour holes were 0.39 ft/s or less, rising to 2.03 ft/s further downstream.

Log condition surveys for Thomas Creek recorded 178 logs on South Fork and 258 on North Fork, classified as above high water (42 and 74), incorporated into the channel (all 140 on North Fork remained in the tally, with South Fork entries partly illegible in the scan), or failed (25 and 44).

## Key Findings

- **Dynamic equilibrium** is a temporary, non-true equilibrium that can be disrupted by storms, earthquakes, or over-steepened banks. Usual events are triggers rather than root causes.
- **Colorado streams (Fool and Deadhorse Creeks)** show the classic indicators of equilibrium: concave longitudinal profiles, small sediment loads, and uniform hydraulic behavior. They are judged to be at dynamic equilibrium, and their landform development is slowed or halted.
- **Thomas Creek (North and South Forks)** show convex profiles and abundant nickpoints, indicating instability. Headcutting is expected to advance into unchannelized headwaters, with the rate depending on the magnitude and frequency of runoff events.
- **Gravel bars and log steps** adjust stream slope. In Colorado, bar formation rose when logs were scarce, and step spacing correlated with gradient and bed material size. This relationship was absent in the unstable Arizona streams.
- **Storm response:** A storm with a return period of several hundred to 1,000 years in October 1972 produced peaks of 13 cfs (North Fork) and 17 cfs (South Fork), compared with 2.7 and 4.5 cfs from snowmelt. A 9-year maximum of 1.3 cfs was recorded earlier.
- **Ephemeral versus perennial flow:** Perennial streams exert more energy over time, so the author suspects ephemeral streams adjust to slope more slowly. Direct comparison is limited because the Thomas Creek equilibrium history is unknown.
- **Management implications:** Evaluating treatment on equilibrium watersheds requires testing equilibrium indicators, not only water yield, sediment, and wildlife. On watersheds not in equilibrium, pre-treatment channel and sediment behavior must be documented so that natural adjustment is not mistaken for treatment effects.
- **Core conclusion:** Landform development is ongoing, not a past geologic event, as shown by volcanoes, earthquakes, subsidence, and erosion.

**Limitations noted by the author:** Only some hydraulic parameters were studied in detail, and the Thomas Creek flows during the study were too small (a few tenths of a cfs) to transport suspended or bedload sediment.
