# A Revised Check List of the Freshwater and Anadromous Fishes of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1958 revision of the official check list of California's freshwater and anadromous fishes, prepared by Leo Shapovalov, William A. Dill, and Almo J. Cordone of the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] Inland Fisheries Branch. It replaces the 1950 edition because nine years of nomenclatural changes, new introductions, and new collections made the original list obsolete. The revised main list contains 110 full species (23 families, 63 genera). The paper also sets out naming rules for [[Common Name Stability]] and [[Subspecies Cline]] policy, a [[Revised Supplementary List]] of exotic and uncertain species, and a record of fishes that the [[Salton Sea]] has come to support.

## Background & Description

The list is the first step toward a planned handbook of California's freshwater and anadromous fishes. Its stated goals are:

- **Accuracy:** to give a current inventory of known species.
- **Stability:** to reduce obsolete and incorrect names in literature, records, and correspondence. The only earlier major list was Evermann and Clark (1931), which had long been obsolete.

The authors note that the taxonomic trend had moved toward condensing and simplifying group names. They were cautious about recognizing genera and subspecies without strong evidence. Following Bailey (1956) and Bailey, Winn, and Smith (1954), they rejected trinomials based on clinal (gradual geographic) variation. They retained binomials wherever clinal variation was the only support for a subspecies. Shapovalov and his colleagues discussed controversial points with Drs. Bailey and Follett. They state, however, that not all differences were reconciled, so the list does not fully agree with every name.

**Common-name rules.** Names were chosen to (1) match common or published usage, (2) agree with authoritative lists (American Fisheries Society 1948, Outdoor Writers Association of America 1958, Roedel 1953), (3) indicate relationship, (4) be descriptive, and (5) be short and distinctive. Each rule is qualified by "other considerations being equal." Genera share a basic name, with prefixes for species, so that the name reflects relationship. For example, all *Siphateles* are called chubs and the *S. bicolor* group are tui chubs. Common names are given to subspecies as well as species, even though the authors agree that only full species deserve them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scope.** The main list includes native species and exotics that have established breeding populations. Native forms were included unless their identity had been disproved in the literature. Forms recorded only outside California were excluded, but the [[Colorado River]] is a boundary stream, so fishes recorded from its Arizona side were included. Hybrids were omitted, though the [[Splake]] (an eastern brook trout × lake trout hybrid) was planted experimentally in the Lakes Basin Recreational Area, Sierra County, in 1955 as part of a trout management study.

**Salton Sea.** Strictly marine species introduced from the Gulf of California into the Salton Sea, an inland body near ocean salinity, were treated separately. Three species on the main list are also established there: *Cyprinodon macularius*, *Gambusia affinis affinis*, and *Gillichthys mirabilis*. Striped mullet (*Mugil cephalus*) had declined to scarcity as irrigation dams cut its access.

**Bait minnow pathway.** The bait minnow trade along the lower Colorado River, with exotic minnows trucked in from many regions, is a likely route for new species. The establishment of *Notropis lutrensis* (see [[Red Shiner]]) there is attributed to escapes from fish farms.

**Management introductions.** The California Department of Fish and Game imports forage and game species after evaluating and screening them to protect existing fisheries. This program is expected to continue to add species.

## Key Findings

**Scale of the revision**
- The revised main list has **110 full species**: **64** native freshwater and anadromous, **14** native marine or euryhaline species that occasionally enter fresh water, and **32** introduced species (marked with an asterisk in the original).
- **11 species and 7 subspecies** were added since 1950.
- Three species and three subspecies were removed as synonyms, and *Salmo clarkii plEURiticus* was moved to the supplementary list.
- The authors expect further revisions every 5 to 10 years.

**Notable additions**
- [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]], introduced in June 1950 into Shasta Lake, was added to the main list because of the large number of fish stocked and the long-term program.
- [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]] were confirmed in Copco Lake (Klamath River, Siskiyou County), after earlier suspicion but no verified specimens.
- [[Red Shiner]] was first collected from the Colorado River in 1953 and is well established there.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] was first recorded in 1950 from a bait tank near the Colorado River; breeding populations are now established in many waters.
- [[Log Perch]] was first documented in March 1958 from artificial lakes in Yuba County, probably introduced with largemouth bass, bluegill, and possibly black bullhead around 1953.
- [[Red-Ear Sunfish]] now breeds in many waters across the state and has been established in the lower Colorado River.
- Several marine or euryhaline species were added, including [[Threespine Stickleback]] subspecies, staghorn sculpins, and the [[Spotted Sleeper]].
- The southern starry flounder (*Platichthys stellatus rugosus*) had been overlooked in 1950 and was added on Dr. Carl L. Hubbs's advice.

**Introduction of threadfin shad**
- A broodstock from Tennessee was flown to California in November 1953 for the [[Threadfin Shad]] program on the Colorado River.
- In November 1954, 520 fish were planted in Lake Havasu, followed by 500 in March 1955.
- By the end of 1955 the population had spread across the Colorado River from Davis Dam to the Mexican border, into irrigation ditches, and into the Salton Sea.

**Salton Sea establishments** (with the year of first introduction)
- [[Sargo]] (*Anisotremus davidsonii*), introduced 1951; first spawned in the sea (juvenile taken October 1956); first verified adult caught September 17, 1958.
- [[Gulf Croaker]] (*Bairdiella icistius*), introduced October 1950; a very large population, firmly established.
- [[Orangemouth Corvina]] (*Cynoscion xanthulus*), introduced October 1950; large and increasing.
- Shortfin corvina (*Cynoscion parvipinnis*), introduced October 1950; small numbers but reproducing, and possibly displaced by *C. xanthulus*.

**Removed from the main list**
- *Salmo gairdneri rosei* (Lake Culver rainbow trout) was shown by Dill and Shapovalov (1954) to be a synonym of *S. g. gilberti*.
- *Catostomus arenarius* was shown by Hubbs and Miller (1951) to be a large-scaled variant of *C. fuscus*.
- *Notemigonus crysoleucas auratus* was dropped because golden shiner shows strong clinal variation.
- *Ictalurus punctatus punctatus* was dropped on clinal grounds, and *Cottus macrops* was synonymized with *C. klamathensis* by Robins and Miller (1957).

**Supplementary list**
- The revised supplementary list covers exotic species that were unsuccessfully introduced or of uncertain occurrence. It has about 40 entries, all in the main list's category of "not established." The authors found no demonstrable evidence that any of them are successfully established in California's fresh waters today.
- Examples include Hawaiian milkfish (*Chanos chanos*), American eel (*Anguilla rostrata*), walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*), redeye bass (*Micropterus coosae*), and northern pike (*Esox lucius*).
- Coleman's (1930) reports of blue catfish and flat bullhead from Clear Lake were judged to be misidentifications of channel catfish.
- The gulf mudsucker (*Gillichthys detrusus*) was deleted entirely after Miller (1952) found no basis for its inclusion.
