# Thin-Skinned Crustal Extension Confirmed

#geology #crustal-extension #seismic-reflection

## Executive Summary

This Science research news item reports geophysical evidence for [[Thin-Skinned Extension]] in the [[Basin and Range Province]] of Utah. Seismic reflection profiling beneath the [[Sevier Desert]] by the [[COCORP]] consortium revealed a low-angle [[Sevier Desert Detachment]] that slices through much of the crust. ## Background & Description

- The Basin and Range province, about 750 km wide, has thin crust and abundant hot springs, which have been attributed to crustal stretching.
- Surface extension is evident on faults bounding the parallel mountain blocks, but how deeper crust accommodates extension had been widely debated.
- Oil-exploration [[Seismic Reflection Profiling]] was applied to the deep crust. Truck-mounted vibrators generated acoustic pulses, and a longer recording time extended imaging to as much as 50 km depth.
- Thin rock sheets had been thrust across the southern Appalachians, but conventional rock mechanics did not allow thin-skinned extension, only compression.

## Key Findings

- **Detachment geometry:** The Sevier Desert detachment dips about 12° and penetrates at least 12 to 15 km beneath the surface, underlying perhaps 10,000 km². The most extensive detachment slices at least halfway through the crust, allowing a 70-km-wide rock sliver to slide over the block below.
- **High-angle faults:** Faults inclined about 60° extend down to the detachment but do not cut it. The classic block-faulting model would have them reach about 15 km depth, so the Basin and Range topography appears to be a relatively superficial expression of limited, geologically recent extension.
- **Timing of slip:** The high-angle faults pierce a 4-million-year-old basalt, so the upper sheet slipped west at least that recently. A younger fault traced into the detachment suggests movement within the past million years.
- **Amount of slip:** Reconnecting strata and faults requires a minimum of 30 km of slip, with a higher estimate of 50 to 60 km.
- **Contribution to extension:** The crust has roughly doubled in area, adding perhaps 300 km to the region's breadth. If the higher slip estimate holds, the detachment could account for about 20 percent of extension in the middle and upper crust.
- **Mechanism unresolved:** Compression created thrust faults in the area more than 65 million years ago. Under the extensional stresses of roughly the past 30 million years, the maximum compressive stress would be vertical, which should lock the fault by increasing frictional resistance. The lubricating process that allows slip remains unidentified.
- **Models:** The observed structure does not match any proposed model of crustal extension exactly. A model by [[Brian Wernicke]] (fault piercing a 30-km crust and 65-km [[Lithosphere]]) resembles the detachment above about 15 km. Splays at the lower end of the detachment may outline lenses of brittle crust around which ductile flow occurred, as proposed by [[Warren Hamilton]]. Reflection data do not clearly show how the deeper crust behaves.
- **Prior evidence:** Oil companies had identified detachments years earlier. [[Robert McDonald]] published shallower profiles in 1976 that drew little attention. The COCORP data are notable because they are public and trace a detachment to the greatest depth yet.
- **Research directions:** Future work must combine surface geology with geophysical data to quantify mid-crustal slip and identify the lower-crust extension mechanism.
