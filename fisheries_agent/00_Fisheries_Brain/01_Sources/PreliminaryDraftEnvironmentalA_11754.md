# Preliminary Draft Environmental Assessment: Fish Barriers on Aravaipa Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This July 1998 internal-review draft by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (lead agency; [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] cooperating) evaluates building two concrete drop-structure fish barriers on lower [[Aravaipa Creek]]. The barriers are required under the 1994 biological opinion on [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water delivery, which found that nonnative fish spread via the CAP would jeopardize threatened native fishes. The barriers would block upstream movement of nonnative fishes from the [[San Pedro River]] into the creek, which holds seven native fish species, the largest and most intact native fish community of any Arizona stream. The assessment compares the proposed action with a no-action alternative. It concludes that the barriers would benefit native fishes substantially, with minor, mostly temporary impacts and some mitigation by land acquisition and a 5-year monitoring program.

## Background & Description

- The [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] (1968) authorized the CAP, completed in 1993 (355-mile system). Concern about nonnative fish invading native habitats led to [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation. The final biological opinion (April 15, 1994) required, as a reasonable and prudent alternative, a pair of fish barriers on Aravaipa Creek.
- **Native fishes (7):** [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] (both federally threatened), [[Roundtail Chub]] (state-listed), [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]] (Cyprinidae), [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonora Sucker]] (Catostomidae).
- **Nonnatives that live in or invade the creek:** green sunfish, yellow bullhead, mosquitofish, largemouth bass, common carp, fathead minnow and [[Red Shiner]]. A single black bullhead was taken in 1990. Channel catfish occur in the lower San Pedro but have never been taken in Aravaipa Creek.
- Critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow was designated in 1994 and revoked March 25, 1998.
- **Design:** each barrier is reinforced concrete, with a 4-ft wall, a roughly 25-ft apron, and upstream (15–20 ft) and downstream (about 12 ft) keys. Each would use about 1,500 cubic yards of concrete. The second barrier would sit about 800 ft upstream of the first. Construction would take about four months, and design life is 100 years.
- **Sites:** A1 and A2, on Indian Trust Allotment 013736 (San Carlos Apache allottees), near the "throat" of Aravaipa Canyon. Eight candidate sites were originally evaluated; the others were rejected for access, critical-habitat, aesthetic or landowner-refusal reasons.
- **Purposes of the barriers:** prevent upstream incursion of exotic fishes, prevent return of nonnatives that are flushed downstream by floods, and provide a control point for monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** the project reach has a gradient of 0.6% (31.7 ft/mile). Monthly mean discharge ranges from 12 cfs (June) to 67 cfs (February) over 64 years of record. The 2-year flood is 3,790 cfs, the 10-year flood 11,300 cfs, and the 100-year flood 28,800 cfs. The largest flow on record was an estimated 70,800 cfs (October 1, 1983).
- **Location:** the project area lies about 10 miles below the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]] boundary and about 5.5 miles upstream of the San Pedro confluence, where perennial flow ends.
- **Vegetation:** four habitat types (Brown 1982): [[Sonoran Riparian Deciduous Woodland]], [[Sonoran Riparian Scrubland]] (seepwillow/burrobrush), Sonoran Interior Strand, and paloverde–cacti mixed scrub. Seepwillow/burrobrush regenerates after floods, and about 85% was scoured in the 1993 flood (13,000 cfs).
- **Aquatic biota:** 133 macroinvertebrate taxa (109 insects) are known, dominated in riffles by Chironomidae and mayflies. Algae are dominated by diatoms and *Cladophora glomerata*. Amphibians and reptiles include the Chiricahua leopard frog (candidate), [[Lowland Leopard Frog]], [[Mexican Garter Snake]], Sonoran mud turtle and nonnative bullfrog.
- **Water quality:** generally good, with slight turbidity.
- **Land and cultural setting:** the allotment is an undeveloped floodplain with unauthorized recreation and cattle trespass. Cultural resources include an Apache burial area at Site B, and Camp Grant is nearby.

## Key Findings

- **Footprint:** permanent loss is under 2 acres of terrestrial habitat (1.51 acres, 93% seepwillow/burrobrush) plus 0.3 acre of open water. Barrier footprints total 1.81 acres of channel. Temporary terrestrial impacts total 9.85 acres, mostly from sedimentation. Aquatic impacts total 2.21 acres (0.3 permanent, 1.91 temporary).
- **Sedimentation:** about 4 ft deep at the barrier, extending about 1,200 ft upstream. The affected area is 8.5 acres and 27,400 cubic yards. Reclamation is considering lowering the lower barrier to 2.5 ft to avoid partial burial of the upper barrier.
- **Flooding:** backwater effects are modest. Aravaipa Road flooding would begin at a 4-year flood instead of a 12-year flood, so the road would be raised to all-weather standards. Wells would see only 1–2 inches more flood depth. A clear-day failure would release an initial surge of about 1,200 cfs, falling over about 20 minutes.
- **Native fish effects:**
  - Spikedace, loach minnow and roundtail chub below the barriers would be genetically isolated from those above, but the lower reach is suboptimal habitat.
  - In 1963–1997 sampling (32 years), loach minnow relative abundance exceeded 4.5–5.0% in the lower reach four times (12.5%) and spikedace 12 times (37.5%). Roundtail chub never exceeded 4.5%.
  - Larval drift and flood losses over the barriers are expected to be negligible to minor.
  - Equipment crossing might kill a few individuals.
- **Nonnative threat:** red shiner first appeared in 1990, disappeared after a flood above 3,000 cfs in 1991, and reappeared in 1997 after a long period of flow stability.
- **Benefits:** substantial benefit to native fishes and likely to leopard frogs and garter snakes through exclusion of nonnatives. No impacts to terrestrial listed species. No Action would leave Reclamation needing to reinitiate Section 7 consultation.
- **Mitigation:** land acquisition at 1:1 for seepwillow/burrobrush and 5:1 for mesquite and mixed riparian habitat. The option is either acquiring 10 acres on the San Pedro River or Aravaipa Creek, or a conservation easement. Staging areas would be revegetated. Gates would restrict unauthorized access. Site B (the preferred contractor area) holds the Apache burial site, which would be fenced, with continued consultation of San Carlos Apache elders, SHPO and BIA.
- **Monitoring:** a 5-year program beginning in 1999 would cover fish responses (including larval drift), vegetation, and geomorphology. It includes contingency plans to relocate fishes from below the barriers. The project would be reassessed, and barrier removal considered, if major unanticipated impacts are found.
- **Social and legal:** the allotment is 160 acres, and about 9.87 acres (6.2%) would be permanently affected. No disproportionate [[Environmental Justice]] impacts are found. The effect on undecreed Winters Doctrine water rights has not been studied. The cumulative-impact discussion cites the statewide displacement of native by nonnative fishes as the key driver.
- **Document status:** Appendix A (biological opinion) is "to be inserted," and the plant list (Appendix C) is "to be added later."
