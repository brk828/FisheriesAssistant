# Developmental Rates of Menidia audens with Notes on Salt Tolerance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae

## Executive Summary

Hubbs, Sharp, and Schneider (1971, *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, vol. 100, no. 4) examined how temperature and salinity affect the embryonic development and adult survival of the [[Mississippi Silverside]] ([[Menidia Audens]]), a species introduced into many warm-water reservoirs as a potential biological control agent. Eggs were incubated across a temperature gradient in water from [[Lake Texoma]], and at graded [[Sea Water Concentration]] levels. Adults were also tested for 24-hour salt tolerance. The authors found that development rate was inversely proportional to temperature, that the species' optimal range is roughly 20–25 °C, and that embryos tolerate salinities up to full sea water, a trait they interpret as a relict of the species' estuarine ancestry.

## Background & Description

*Menidia audens* is a small atherinid silverside native to the large rivers of the Mississippi Valley. The authors note that it may have evolved in the quiet backwaters of coastal streams, but it now forms dense populations in lowland reservoirs. Reservoir construction and enlargement have promoted its spread, and it was deliberately introduced in several places to control insect pests. The authors caution that biological control rarely eliminates a pest and that introduced species can become a problem themselves.

Lake Texoma provided the study stock. Adults were collected from the north shore, mostly at Marietta and Willis Island, and transported in foam coolers. Most tests used lake water pumped from the reservoir and allowed to equilibrate to test temperature. Sea water was assumed to be 33 ppt. Eggs were incubated in culture tubes within a [[Temperature Gradient]] apparatus, and development stages were recorded following [[Oppenheimer Staging]] (1937), with the eyed-egg stage and hatching as key endpoints.

Reproductive biology and population dynamics were drawn partly from the earlier work of Mense (1967) and compared with [[Brook Silverside]] (*Labidesthes sicculus*) and *M. extensa* (Davis and Louder, 1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Competition and replacement.** Shortly after Lake Texoma was built, the brook silverside (*Labidesthes sicculus*) was abundant in shallow water. After *M. audens* appeared in the lake in 1953, *Labidesthes* declined and *Menidia* became abundant in the same habitats. The authors cite Saunders (1959) in suggesting that the two species share similar food and niches, so *M. audens* might be expected to replace *Labidesthes* in warm-water impoundments.

**Habitat use.** Schools of similar-sized fish are found near the surface (typically the upper decimeter, seldom deeper than 2 m) close to shore. In June, 90% of *Menidia* sampled were within 30 m of shore. In July, about half were at stations farther offshore. The authors conclude that sandy bottom and a gradual offshore slope are critical to abundance, and that during severe storms the fish move about 15 m offshore, returning when waves subside.

**Reproduction.** Adults are reproductive from late March or early April through mid-July. By 15 July, over half of the adults appeared non-reproductive. Males were often sparse in open-flat seine hauls but more common among emergent bushes on the shoreline, where eggs were found on algal growth on bush stems.

**Predation.** [[Largemouth Bass]] are major predators. Mense (1967) reported that 78% of identifiable fish in largemouth bass stomachs were *Menidia*, rising to 95% in spring months. The authors argue that predation is a significant population regulator that keeps nearly all individuals from surviving a full year. Females over 150 mm long indicate that some fish live two years.

**Climate and temperature.** Winter water temperatures in Lake Texoma often fall below 10 °C, which the authors say would kill any eggs laid then. The authors consider the species' developmental temperatures to be designed for summer breeding.

## Key Findings

### Developmental temperature effects

- Development time was **inversely proportional** to incubation temperature.
- Gastrulation occurred in under one day at 30 °C and in about four days at about 14 °C.
- Eye pigmentation occurred between two and eleven days after fertilization.
- The range of reasonably successful development is about **17–34 °C**. Several lines of evidence point to an optimum of **20–25 °C**.
- The three highest freshwater survival rates were at **22.0, 23.4, and 24.1 °C**.
- The warmest recorded lake temperature, **33.3 °C**, is near the upper limit for successful development.
- Eggs incubated at 10.2 °C and 36.4 °C still developed pigmented eyes.
- At low temperatures, average starvation death of embryos was about 32 days at 12.6 °C and 42 days at 10.2 °C. Hatching usually occurred about 45 days (12.6 °C) and 53 days (10.2 °C) after fertilization. At these cold temperatures the timing of starvation and hatching converged.
- Temperature had little effect on development above about 30 °C, a moderate effect between 19 and 30 °C, and a strong effect below 19 °C.
- Hatching time was much more temperature-sensitive than developmental stage timing, and the gap between hatching and starvation narrowed at the coldest temperatures.
- Eggs exposed to a short period of lethal cold at early stages often failed to hatch. Eggs exposed at later stages sometimes recovered when returned to optimal temperature.

### Parental thermal history

- Gastrulation became less likely at cold and warm extremes during the study. Adults held at 15 °C produced eggs that gastrulated at 14 °C but not at 32 °C. Adults held at 25 °C produced eggs that gastrulated at 34 °C but not at 18 °C.
- The authors suggest a parental thermal effect on yolk involvement that merits further study. No post-gastrulation differences were detected.

### Salinity tolerance of embryos

- Eggs were incubated at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% sea water.
- At 50% sea water, development was similar to controls through eye pigmentation. Hatching was restricted to 19–33 °C, and all but one young at 20.2 °C died the next day.
- At 75% sea water, hatching was restricted to 22–31.3 °C.
- At 100% sea water, eye pigmentation developed normally, but only one egg hatched (at 20.9 °C), and it died the next day.
- At 22 °C, about 50% of eggs developed dark eyes at all salinities. Hatching occurred in up to 85% sea water and not at 100%. Complete yolk consumption occurred at salinities up to 40% sea water.

### Salinity tolerance of adults

- All control fish survived 24 hours. No fish survived more than 24 hours at salinities above **1.3 times sea water**.
- At full sea water, more than **70%** of fish survived beyond 24 hours.
- At 75%, 50%, and 25% sea water, all fish survived 48 hours.
- All fish at 2× and 3× sea water died.

### Reproductive biology

- A female removed two egg complements within 6 days. The authors describe this as the first demonstration of repeated spawning in *M. audens* females.
- If a female spawns weekly with about 1,000 eggs, she could produce over **15,000 eggs** over three months (April–June).
- The authors reject Mense's conclusion that the species cannot survive the season. They argue that spawning-associated mortality (especially in males) nearly eliminates the adult population before the season ends.
- Males became progressively scarcer as the season advanced, and sex ratios shifted toward females, as Mense also reported.

### Interpretation and applications

- Salt tolerance is a **vestige** retained from an ancestral estuarine habitat. The lack of salinity effects on eye pigmentation is consistent with ontogeny recapitulating phylogeny.
- Because most of the species' range lies in low-salinity water, Lake Texoma (with salinities up to about 3% sea water) is near the species' most saline habitat, and survival at 25% sea water is "a luxury."
- The species' tolerance of warm temperatures and moderate salinities makes it a candidate for [[Biological Control]] of insect pests whose larvae live in warm, moderately saline waters.
- The authors describe their experiments as preliminary and call for further work on parental thermal effects and optimal survival temperatures.
