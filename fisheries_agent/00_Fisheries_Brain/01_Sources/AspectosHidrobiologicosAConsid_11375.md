# Aspectos Hidrobiológicos a Considerar en Todo Intento de Incremento de Especies Acuáticas (Boletín de Piscicultura Rural, Vol. XII, 1963)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 issue of the *Boletín de Piscicultura Rural*, published by the Mexican Secretaría de Industria y Comercio through the [[Dirección General de Pesca]] and the [[Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras]], is a set of technical articles on inland fish culture. The source text contains two complete pieces. The first, by biologist María Luisa Sevilla, is a hydrobiological guide for anyone introducing or increasing aquatic species in Mexico. It argues that no species suits every Mexican ecological setting, and that each water body must be assessed on its own physical, chemical and biological terms before stocking. The second is a commercial fish-farm proposal modelled on a [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) demonstration-farm scheme, with a five-year build-out, capital and operating budgets, and a loan repayment schedule. The text also includes a 1958–1963 table of fish and frog distributions and repopulations by state. The remaining articles listed in the table of contents (a State of Mexico fish plan, a tilapia culture guide, and a water-hyacinth control paper) are not included in the source.

## Background & Description

**Scope and author's caveat.** Sevilla states that the paper does not treat the subject exhaustively and that no universal model applies. Each locality has its own features, and apparent similarities can hide the differences that explain problems. She warns against casual claims, common in Mexican fish-culture work, that a given species can solve the nation's food problem. A good yield in one place does not justify the indiscriminate spread of a species, because this can upset the biotic balance of a [[Biocenosis]].

**Biological requirements.** Successful introduction or enhancement of native or exotic species requires knowledge of their biology and [[Ecology]], including nutrition, growth, reproductive habits and development. Predators such as those that eat small fish, crayfish, snails, insects or buried organisms need adequate forage. Where early maturity leads to overpopulation and stunting, juveniles should be removed. Where reproduction is moderate and commercial harvest is intense, protection during spawning is preferred. Many important reservoir species in Mexico are therefore subject to closed seasons during spawning, and taking juveniles is prohibited.

**Species profiles** (summarised from the source):

- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): carnivorous; they prefer cold, oxygenated, clear water with sand and gravel bottoms. They build bottom nests, so forage species and bottom-disturbing fish must be managed.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), known locally as huro or lobina negra: carnivorous and voracious; it prefers warm lakes and slow streams with muddy bottoms and dense vegetation. The male guards the fry. Minimum spawning temperature is 15.5 °C.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): an Asian species introduced to Mexico in the last century and now widespread. It is omnivorous but feeds mainly on bottom detritus. It grows best in shallow, warm ponds, and the text recommends stocking flooded rice fields, watering holes and dams. A 25–30 cm female can produce over 250,000 eggs, so periodic harvest is needed.
- [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) and [[Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*): best in shallow ponds (about 50 cm) with abundant submerged and floating vegetation. They breed in the rainy season at about 25 °C and produce 10,000–25,000 eggs. They will not accept inert feed and become cannibalistic without moving food.
- [[Tilapia]] (*T. mossambica* and *T. melanopleura*): African fish introduced experimentally. They do best in warm tropical water below 1,500 m, feed most effectively at 24–25 °C, and are mainly herbivorous. They can begin breeding at six months and spawn two to four times a year, so they should be stocked with an active predator such as largemouth bass and fished regularly.
- [[Arapaima]] (*Arapaima gigas*), known as piraruc: an Amazonian giant carnivore tested experimentally at "El Tejar", Veracruz. It prefers 25–31 °C water with humic bottoms and organic suspended matter. It breeds in the flood season in its native range, but has not bred in Mexico.
- Atherinids (charales and pescado blanco): important forage and fishery species in cold, turbid lakes such as Chapala, Pátzcuaro, Presa Requena and Necaxa. They spawn on rocky, wave-exposed shores and are fragile in handling, so the Division of Fish Culture has moved eggs to special incubators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Light.** Light intensity and wavelength control plankton abundance and distribution, and phytoplankton forms the base of the food pyramid. In the absence of light, oxygen depends only on surface exchange, and anaerobic conditions can develop. Light penetration in open sea reaches about 130 m, and faintly to the depth the text prints as "1500" (very likely 150 m). The clearest continental water, Lago Cristal, transmits light to about 30 m, where 1% remains. Most Mexican waters, however, allow light to penetrate only about 1 m. Light is absorbed selectively: ultraviolet and infrared are quickly reflected, while yellow, green and blue penetrate deeper. Coastal water favours yellow-green light and open ocean favours blue-green.

**Thermal structure.** In temperate reservoirs, a thermocline commonly appears around 15–20 m and acts as a barrier for stenothermal fish. Culture ponds are usually shallow and avoid this problem, but large reservoirs can develop it during hot, dry periods. Warm surface layers then promote disease and oxygen depletion. At Lake Catemaco, high mortality of mojarras caused by bacteria occurred in May and eased with the rains.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Oxygen falls with rising temperature, pollution, high stocking density, decomposition of bottom sediments and humic muds. Temperature is the only factor that cannot be controlled. Tilapia reduce feeding when oxygen falls below about 1.5 g O₂/L. The remedies are to limit stocking, remove excess weeds (which also consume oxygen at night), and add gravel or stones to the bottom. The compensation level, where photosynthetic oxygen equals respiratory demand, lies at varying depths, from several metres to about 10 cm in continental waters.

**Water chemistry.** Marine pH is stable at 8.1–8.3 because seawater buffers it. Muddy bottoms show the largest changes. Mangroves can reach pH 8.9 through algal photosynthesis, while bare bottoms become acidic. Freshwater normally ranges from 6.5 to 8.5, with sharper shifts after acid or alkaline pollution. Pollution is a major problem. Industrial, agricultural and domestic discharges can kill fish directly through toxins or caustic substances, or indirectly by depleting oxygen. Even nutritious particles in excess harm filter feeders such as oysters, which close their valves and stop feeding.

**Lentic and lotic environments.** Lakes differ from lagunetas (ponds) in geological age and depth. Lakes are classified by depth, temperature and oxygen. Oligotrophic lakes such as the Nevado de Toluca lagoon are oxygen-rich and suited to trout. Eutrophic lakes lose oxygen at depth through bacterial decomposition and suit bass or huro. Dystrophic (brown-water) lakes are poorly studied in America. Lakes have vegetation zones: emergent (litoral), submerged (central), planktonic and deep. The source lists emergent, marginal, palustre, submerged and floating plant types. Streams are constantly moving. Rivers zone from trout in mountain reaches to huro, lisa, bobo and mojarras in warm, slow lowlands. Seasonal flooding is more abrupt in streams than in lakes.

**Estuaries.** Coastal lagoons and estuaries have tidal and freshwater influence, which creates horizontal, vertical and seasonal salinity gradients. They protect young fish and shrimp, mullet, snook, bobo, catfish and mojarras, and they support oyster production where salinity is suitable. Exposure at low tide limits benthic organisms, which can be partly managed by building dikes or adding shell or gravel to stabilise beds.

**Zonation of Mexico for fish culture.** Four climatic zones are recognised:

1. *Warm tropical* (sea level to 900 m): coastal plain with mojarras, catfish, bobo, lisa, shrimp, oysters; suitable for huro, tilapia (with a predator) and, locally, arapaima and frogs.
2. *Warm temperate* (900–1,500 m a.s.l.): catfish, mojarras, huro in some areas, carp; tilapia and frogs.
3. *Cold temperate* (1,500–1,800 m): charales, pescado blanco, huro, catfish, carp (with limits); atherinids favoured, and weed-eating carp for lakes invaded by water hyacinth (Chapala).
4. *Cold* (above 1,800 m): poor in fish except forage species such as cyprinids, poeciliids, goodeids, charales and crayfish. Trout restocking has improved productivity.

**Pond and reservoir design.** Light penetration above about 3 m is considered insufficient, so fish ponds should be no more than 1.5 m deep (minimum 75 cm). The Directorate prefers restocking large reservoirs and permanent streams. Recommended pond sizes are at least 0.25 to 0.5 hectare.

## Key Findings

- **No universal species or model.** Each water body requires individual assessment of climate, water transparency, temperature, bottom type, current speed, depth, vegetation and associated fauna before choosing a species (Sevilla, 1963).
- **Restocking discipline.** Stocking should match the number of fry or breeders that can sustain production. Overstocking and indiscriminate introductions can disturb the biotic balance and water chemistry.
- **Light and temperature govern the system.** Light drives phytoplankton, oxygen and food production. Temperature sets feeding, growth and breeding thresholds for each species. Examples given include trout at 5–13 °C, tilapia feeding stopping below 15 °C, bass breeding from 15.5 °C and carp growth peaking at 20–25 °C.
- **Oxygen and pollution are the main controllable risks.** Ponds should be stocked within capacity, weeds and bottom sediments managed, and discharges prevented.
- **Productivity needs more than plankton.** High phytoplankton production does not guarantee high fish yield. Grazers and higher trophic levels must also be present, and filamentous algae that cannot be digested harm productivity.
- **Commercial fish-farm proposal (FAO-based, Dr. Yen Lin).** The five-year plan aims for demonstration farms producing 500 kg per week of market fish. Year 1 builds 60 production ponds of 2,000 m² (12 ha) and 15 nursery ponds of 20–1,000 m², which can supply about 120,000 fingerlings a year. Operations start in the sixth month for fingerling supply and harvest begins in the eighth or ninth month of year 2. Years 3 to 5 add a second and third farm, then a fourth demonstration farm with 60–120 production ponds.
- **Yield and economics.** Expected output is 2 t/ha, or 24 t per year from the first farm. Wholesale price is $0.40/kg ($400/t). Initial capital is $20,000 (including $13,000 for ponds using a bulldozer). Annual operating costs are $5,430 and revenue is $10,800 (fish sales plus $1,200 from 60,000 fingerlings sold at $0.02). Gross profit is $5,370.
- **Loan repayment.** A $25,430 loan at 6% annual interest is repaid over 11 years at $3,500 per year. Interest falls from $1,526 in year 2 to $165 in year 11. The schedule is stated in dollars.
- **Distribution records, 1958–1963.** The Directorate distributed trout, huro, mojarras, carp, frogs, charales and tismiche fry. The repopulation summary covers about two dozen states, including Chihuahua, Michoacán, Jalisco, Veracruz and Puebla. Several state totals are partly illegible in the OCR text, so exact national totals cannot be confirmed here.
