# Late Cenozoic Freshwater Fishes of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned chapter, written in the style of a review by Smith, examines the late Cenozoic history of North American freshwater fishes. It contrasts the stable, species-rich [[Eastern Fauna]] with the unstable, extinction-prone [[Western Fauna]], and links both to Pleistocene climate change, drainage history and ancient lakes. The text then addresses evolutionary questions: whether phyletic change is detectable in the fossil record, how adaptive radiations and speciation rates differ among groups, and how [[Introgressive Hybridization]] can produce reversed character states. Much of the page layout is garbled by OCR, and the numerous tables and figures are not recoverable, so this note relies on the readable prose.

## Background & Description

The chapter covers the fish faunas of North America during the late Cenozoic, roughly the Miocene to the Holocene. Its main geographic units are:

- **Eastern drainages:** the [[Mississippi Drainage]] and its former connections to the [[Great Lakes]], plus the Atlantic and Gulf drainages.
- **Western interior:** river basins and several hundred interior drainages separated by mountain ranges, including the [[Snake River Plain]] and the ancient [[Lake Idaho]].

Major taxa discussed include living fossils such as gars ([[Lepisosteidae]]), bowfin ([[Amiidae]]), sturgeons, paddlefishes, and mooneyes. Other groups include minnows, suckers, catfishes, sunfishes, darters, trouts, whitefish, sculpins and pupfish. Two monographs on late Cenozoic groups are cited, for [[Amiidae]] and [[Lepisosteidae]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eastern fauna.** The Mississippi system holds a rich accumulation of the families that occupy North American fresh waters. Most of its major adaptive radiations are centred in the Mississippi basin, in the minnows *Notropis* and *Hybopsis*, the darters *Etheostoma* and *Percina*, the redhorse suckers *Moxostoma*, the catfish subgenus *Amiurus* and *Noturus*, and the sunfish *Lepomis*. The chapter reports that these eight species-rich groups include a majority of the continent's freshwater species. The author attributes the lack of late Cenozoic extinction to the drainage's size, position and orientation. These allowed fishes forced south by Pleistocene glacial cooling to survive and recolonize. Fragmentation or a northward drainage slope would have produced extensive extinction instead.

Pleistocene cooling shifted species ranges southward. Illinoian glacial faunas in southwestern Kansas and adjacent Oklahoma contain species now found in the northern plains, Great Lakes and Appalachians. Cross's work suggests that increased volumes of aquatic habitat can explain some of these distributional changes, not only cooler temperatures. The eastern fauna is largely fluvial. Apart from lake trout, coregonines and blue pike in the Great Lakes (possibly evolved in periglacial lakes) and several sculpins, few lacustrine species occur there. The Laurentian Great Lakes have been inhabited by fishes for only about 14,000 years, which is too brief for species-level evolution.

**Western fauna.** The western fish fauna has an unstable history marked by many extinctions. It contains about one-fourth as many species as the eastern fauna, and local habitats are similarly depauperate. Sunfish (*Archoplites*) and catfish (*Ictalurus*) were formerly widespread but became extinct in the Pleistocene. Mountain-adapted groups, including trouts, whitefish, mountain suckers, dace and sculpins, occupy headwaters and cross barriers frequently. Western interior basins held great lakes of varying permanence in the late Cenozoic. E. D. Cope first recognized their ichthyological significance. Lake Idaho had two large, stable phases of at least one million years each, with rich fish faunas.

## Key Findings

1. **Little late Cenozoic extinction in the Mississippi drainage.** Some lineages changed at species level, but little true extinction can be demonstrated. This contrasts sharply with western faunas.

2. **Pleistocene range shifts.** Glacial cooling pushed species south, and aquatic habitat volume likely explains part of the southern distributional changes.

3. **Lake Idaho was a long-lived, fish-rich lake.** Its two stable phases lasted at least one million years each. Its fauna included specialized catostomids, cyprinids and sculpins.

4. **Living fossils show little phyletic change.** Amiidae and Lepisosteidae have stable lineages over about 40 million years and show no significant phyletic evolution. Lundberg's work found negligible evolution in some ictalurid lines (*Ictalurus punctatus*, *I. furcatus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*) but active change in others. No environmental correlates were observed.

5. **Apparent character reversals on the Snake River Plain.** Living *Acrocheilus alutaceus* and *Mylocheilus caurinus* appear to descend from more specialized Lake Idaho species (*A. latus* and *M. robustus*). Their generalized features are interpreted as reversals. Discovery of generalized forms in contemporaneous fluvial sediments would disprove this, but none had been found. In *Mylocheilus*, the tooth formula went from 2,5-5,2 (Miocene) to 1,4-4,1 (Pliocene) to 1,5-5,1 (Recent).

6. **Introgressive hybridization in *Chasmistes*.** When *Chasmistes* became rare in Utah Lake, Pyramid Lake, Copco Reservoir and Lake O'Woods, it hybridized with local *Catostomus*. In Utah Lake, Copco Reservoir and Lake O'Woods, introgression shifted most remaining individuals toward *Catostomus* in jaw osteology, mouth position, lips, eyes, scale counts and gill rakers. Because *Catostomus* is a generalized sister group, this produced reversed character states. Undetected hybridization of this kind could mislead cladistic analyses.

7. **Body size and radiation.** The largest adaptive radiations involve small-bodied fishes, usually maturing under 8–15 cm. Examples include *Etheostoma* (ca. 90 living species), *Notropis* (ca. 120), *Percina* (30), *Noturus* (25), *Cottus* (24), *Cyprinodon* (27), *Fundulus* (22), *Gambusia* (13) and *Lepomis* (11). The exceptionally diversified large-bodied genera are *Moxostoma* (18 species), *Catostomus* (22) and *Coregonus* (14).

8. **Speciation rates in western minnows and *Lepomis*.** Avise & Ayala compared electrophoretic distances. Mean pairwise distance was 0.63 among ten *Lepomis* species and 0.46 among eight western minnow genera. The chapter estimates about 2.4 speciation events per million years in *Lepomis* (13 species in 5.5 million years since the Miocene) and about one per million years in the minnow genera. The reanalysis argues that rates of change are positively correlated with speciation events, not simply proportional to time.

9. **Species flocks.** Few lacustrine species flocks occur in temperate North America. The main examples are the Great Lakes ciscoes (*Coregonus*), with about seven species that probably colonized the lakes in earliest postglacial time. A last dispersal opportunity from Lake Superior to Lake Nipigon existed about 9,000 years ago, so differentiation probably occurred earlier, perhaps in proglacial lakes.
