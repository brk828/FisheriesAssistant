# A Monitoring Study of Razorback Sucker Reintroduced into the Lower Colorado River in 1987

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #endangered-species #reintroduction-biology #predation-ecology

## Executive Summary

This final report documents a 1987 monitoring study of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) reintroduced into the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Approximately 900,000 larvae produced at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were stocked into selected Colorado River backwaters in late spring 1987, with an additional 200,000 larvae planted at [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] for rearing to juvenile size. The study employed multiple sampling methods (Q-beam spotlights, trammel nets, minnow traps, seines, electro-shocking) to monitor post-stocking survival, movement, dispersion, and predation. Larvae remained in backwaters approximately 2 weeks, attaining lengths of 14 mm TL before disappearing from stocking sites. Juveniles moved at night from release points toward the main channel and virtually disappeared from backwaters within 1 week. Predation was documented on smaller juveniles (less than 130 mm TL) but was less apparent on larger individuals (greater than 190 mm TL). [[Largemouth Bass]] were the primary juvenile consumer, while [[Bluegill]] were the primary larval consumer. Post-stocking monitoring efforts captured few razorback except when locally concentrated immediately after stocking.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] was historically widely distributed throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage from Wyoming to the Colorado River delta. Once abundant enough to support commercial harvest in [[Saguaro Lake]], Arizona as late as 1949, the species has experienced drastic decline throughout its range since the early 1950s. By 1987, razorbacks were sporadically encountered in the lower basin, with adult specimens collected from canals near Blythe, CA and Parker, AZ. Federal listing as endangered was postponed to accommodate an active recovery and reintroduction program initiated in 1974 at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and transferred to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1981.

The study area encompassed 206 km (128 mi) of the Colorado River between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]], including the operational divisions of Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, and Imperial. The region is classified as Sonoran Desertscrub with riparian vegetation including mesquite, willow, cottonwood, and extensive saltcedar. Emergent aquatic vegetation includes giant bulrush, cattail, and common reed, while submergent communities include sago pondweed, water milfoil, and spiny naiad. River discharge below [[Parker Dam]] in 1987 ranged from 611 m³/sec in January to 173 m³/sec in November, with daily fluctuations of 1-2 m affecting backwater habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lower Colorado River has been extensively modified through channelization, diking, rip-rapping, diversion, and damming. Approximately 27 km of the 71 km Parker Division shoreline has been altered by rip-rap and armored jetties. The Palo Verde Division (45 km) has 90% rip-rapped shoreline, while the Cibola Division (31 km) resembles a wide rock-lined canal with nearly 100% armored shorelines. Only the Imperial Division (60 km) retains unaltered shoreline with a network of backwaters, lakes, and marshes, though sedimentation since [[Imperial Dam]] closure in 1938 has closed or isolated many backwaters.

Backwater stocking sites were selected to facilitate acclimation of hatchery fry to lotic conditions, provide escape cover from predators through proximity to shoreline vegetation (cattails, bulrush), and offer warmer, more productive waters rich in phyto- and zooplankton—primary foods for larval razorbacks. Sites included [[Parker Backwater]] (CA and AZ sides), [[Draper Backwater]] (CA and AZ sides), backwater above [[Island Lake]], [[LaPaz Lake]], levee pond north of [[Palo Verde Oxbow Lake]], [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]], and [[Goose Flats Backwater]].

The exotic fish community poses significant predation pressure on reintroduced razorbacks. Pre-stocking gill net surveys documented presence of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Tilapia]] species.

## Key Findings

**Larval Stocking and Movement:**
On March 31, 1987, 1,098,804 one-week-old razorback fry (avg. 9.3 mm TL, range 8.3-10.0 mm) were delivered from Dexter National Fish Hatchery and distributed among multiple stocking sites. Q-beam spotlight surveys documented that larvae remained in close proximity to release points along shoreline cover (brush, bulrush, aquatic vegetation) before gradually disappearing over a 2-week period. One day after release, larvae were most abundant at sites nearest release points. One week post-release showed substantial decrease in numbers, and two weeks after release larvae had virtually disappeared from all sites. Growth was apparent during surveys: larvae collected 1-6 days post-release averaged 9.54 mm TL, those at 7-9 days averaged 11.22 mm TL, and those at 13-16 days averaged 14.01 mm TL.

Movement patterns varied by site. At [[Parker Backwater]] (CA side), larvae dispersed in a clockwise pattern following shoreline. At [[Draper Backwater]] (AZ side), the site was abandoned after dewatering occurred due to decreased river flows (from 391 m³/sec to 286 m³/sec), reducing the site to a shallow muddy area; stocked larvae (N=60,236) were presumed lost. At [[LaPaz Lake]], larvae distributed throughout the small isolated lake over two weeks.

**Larval Predation:**
Stomach content analysis of 428 potential predators collected with trammel nets revealed larval razorback in only 10 stomachs (2.3% occurrence). [[Bluegill]] were the primary larval consumer: 8 of 153 bluegill examined (5.3%) contained larvae, with an average of 4.75 larvae per stomach (range 1-18). Seven of these eight bluegill were captured at Parker Backwaters 1 day after stocking. One [[Threadfin Shad]] (177 mm TL) contained 4 larvae, and one warmouth (171 mm TL) contained 2 larvae. The low detection rate likely reflects rapid digestion; previous studies found razorback larvae reduced to amorphous masses beyond specific recognition within 4 hours of consumption by [[Green Sunfish]].

**Juvenile Rearing and Stocking:**
Of 250,000 larvae planted at [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] on March 31, 1987, 222,963 (89%) were periodically seined and planted between June and November into Colorado River backwaters. Approximately 95% were removed by October 1; anchorworm (*Lernaea* sp.) infestations after that date prompted removal of the remainder. From the [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]], 3,365 one-plus-year-old razorbacks (from 10,000 larvae stocked in 1986) were removed and planted into Colorado River locations in 1987. A fish-kill occurred July 30 at the Golf Course Pond following herbicide ("Roundup") application, killing 427 razorback (avg. 202 mm TL, range 133-349 mm TL); thousands survived in deep vegetated areas.

**Juvenile Movement and Dispersal:**
Post-stocking dispersal studies using trammel nets and minnow traps documented that juveniles moved at night from release points toward the main channel. At [[Parker Backwater]] (AZ side) on May 7-8, following release of 600 juveniles (avg. 156 mm TL) at 1400 hrs, minimal dispersal occurred 2 hours post-release. By 0400 hrs (night), 8 razorback were captured in each of Nets 2 and 3 (progressively farther from release point), indicating nocturnal movement toward main channel. Similar patterns were observed August 18 when 18,120 juveniles (avg. 111 mm TL) were stocked. At [[Parker Backwater]] (CA side) on June 17-18, 390 Golf Course juveniles and 34,224 Niland juveniles were stocked; trammel nets caught 14 of the larger Golf Course stock (3.6% of 390 planted), with all but one captured moving toward the channel. One unmarked specimen (350 mm TL) was captured entering the backwater, possibly representing a survivor from previous years or a wild individual.

At [[Goose Flats Backwater]], following September 18 stocking of 1,444 juveniles (avg. 196 mm TL), overnight trammel netting September 20-21 caught only 4 razorback in Nets 2 and 3, suggesting most had already exited or taken refuge in dense water milfoil beds. Electro-shocking following November 17 stocking of 4,015 juveniles (avg. 126 mm TL) produced 128 razorback 1 day post-release (capture rate 1.6 razorback/min shocking time), with 73% captured below the release site and 73% within 0.80 km of release. One week later, only 13 razorback were captured (0.16/min), and two weeks post-release no razorback were found.

**Juvenile Predation:**
Predation was size-dependent. Examination of predator stomachs collected shortly after stocking larger juveniles (greater than 190 mm TL) from the Golf Course Pond revealed no razorback consumption. However, predation on smaller Niland-reared juveniles (120-133 mm TL) was apparent. At [[Goose Flats Backwater]], 4 of 24 [[Largemouth Bass]] examined (17% occurrence) during November 18-December 1 contained juvenile razorback averaging 109 mm TL. One bass collected November 19 contained 2 razorback. At [[Parker Backwater]] (AZ side), 2 of 2 largemouth bass examined May 8 (100% occurrence) contained razorback averaging 153 mm TL; 1 of 2 bass examined August 18 contained razorback (109 mm TL). At [[Parker Backwater]] (CA side), 1 of 2 bass examined June 18 contained one 60 mm TL razorback, likely from Niland stock.

**Sampling Gear Effectiveness:**
Multiple gear types were employed with varying success. Q-beam spotlights (400,000 candle power) effectively attracted and documented phototactic larvae during the first 2-3 weeks post-swim-up. Seining effort totaled 3,716 m² across sites, capturing only 2 razorback. Minnow traps (small, medium, large sizes) set overnight captured 2 razorback in 96 trap-nights. Oneida trap nets captured 7 razorback in 5 net-nights. Trammel nets (40 m length, 2 m width, 13 mm inner mesh, 15.5 cm outer mesh) set overnight captured 55 razorback in 34 net-nights. Electro-shocking proved most effective for juveniles in main channel and deeper backwater habitats, with 662.4 minutes of shocking time producing 141 razorback observations (plus 4,777 other fish).

**Recommendations:**
The report concludes with five key recommendations: (1) Stock larger fish—predation was undetectable on juveniles exceeding 190 mm TL, giving them better survival prospects. (2) Secure rearing areas—establish isolated earthen pond facilities along the lower Colorado River, treating with ichthyocide to remove predators before larval stocking; coordinate with [[Bureau of Reclamation]] on construction. (3) Mark stocked fish—without marking, distinguishing stocked from wild razorback is impossible, precluding conclusive program evaluation. (4) Continue monitoring efforts—one year is insufficient; extended monitoring (more than 1 year) is needed to survey all available habitat as fish approach reproductive maturity (second or third summer). (5) Use radiotelemetry equipment—radio-tracking may reveal daily and seasonal movement patterns and habitat preferences across life history stages, informing future sampling strategies.

The study documents that both larvae and juveniles disappeared from backwater stocking sites within 1-2 weeks, likely due to a combination of mass exodus triggered by drastic daily water level fluctuations (1-2 m), predation by exotic piscivores, and inability of sampling gear to effectively survey dense aquatic vegetation where razorback may persist. The precarious status of the razorback sucker requires prompt, sustained efforts to prevent extirpation from the lower Colorado River.
