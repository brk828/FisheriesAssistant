# Sacramento River Winter-Run Chinook Salmon and the Endangered Species Act: The Glenn-Colusa Irrigation District Case

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This legal review, written by Gregory S. Weber (McGeorge School of Law), analyzes *United States v. Glenn-Colusa Irrigation District*, 788 F. Supp. 1126 (N.D. Cal., 10 March 1992). The court found that pumping by the [[Glenn-Colusa Irrigation District]] (GCID) "took" threatened [[Sacramento River Winter-Run Chinook Salmon]] in violation of the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) and ordered a halt to pumping during the run's peak downstream migration. The article argues that the ESA is a "double-edged sword": once a species is listed, it provides a prompt and powerful judicial remedy, but the long delay before listing shows the Act's limits in rescuing species already near extinction.

## Background & Description

**Species biology.** The [[Sacramento River Winter-Run Chinook Salmon]] is an [[Anadromy|anadromous]] fish: it hatches in freshwater, matures in saltwater, and returns to spawn in the freshwater of its birth. It is described as a distinct salmon species found only on the Sacramento River. Its return and spawning timing distinguishes it from three other chinook runs on that river. Most fish return as three-year-olds from three year classes. Fry hatch in river gravel from late July or early August, and downstream migration peaks from August through October, overlapping much of the irrigation season.

**Water use and the district.** The [[Glenn-Colusa Irrigation District]] was organized under California law in 1920 as the successor to earlier irrigation companies. It claims rights under state water law and federal contracts to annual deliveries of 800,000 acre-feet. At times it pumps nearly one-third of the river's flow. During the April to October peak season its pumps divert an average of 2,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), with a maximum of 2,900 cfs. The district irrigates 141,000 acres across 1,200 farms.

**Regulatory history.** The district has long disputed fish protection with state agencies:

- 1920: the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CDFG) ordered a fish screen, which a flood washed out the next year.
- 1932: in *People v. Glenn-Colusa Irrigation District*, the California Court of Appeal upheld a public nuisance finding and rejected the district's water-rights defense.
- 1935 and 1938: a new screen was installed, then undermined by a flood.
- 1968: the district and CDFG agreed on a new [[Fish Screen]] design, completed in 1972 with federal and state funds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 1972 screen has failed to work as planned. Three problems affect juvenile winter-run salmon:

- **[[Juvenile Impingement]]:** fish are pinned against the screen when [[Screen Approach Velocity]] (SAV) is too high. SAVs exceed state standards when pumping exceeds 1,100 cfs. Falling water levels worsen this by reducing effective screen area.
- **[[Entrainment]]:** juveniles pass through the screen and pumps and die in irrigation ditches.
- **[[Predation]]:** larger fish feed on trapped juveniles. The diversion channel provides prime predator habitat.

Mortality estimates are imprecise, but some experts estimated that pumps killed between approximately 800,000 and 9 million juvenile winter-run salmon annually before 1972. The post-1972 screen problems were estimated to cause 400,000 to 10 million juvenile deaths annually.

Adult returns collapsed. CDFG studies showed adult returns declining from 84,000 in 1966 to 191 in 1991. The [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) identified GCID pumping as one of several likely contributors, alongside continuing drought.

## Key Findings

**Listing and status**

- NMFS listed the winter-run as threatened under the ESA in November 1990 (55 Fed. Reg. 46515). A petition from the American Fisheries Society, filed 7 November 1985, had preceded the listing by more than five years, during which NMFS declined to list the species.
- On 19 June 1992 NMFS proposed redesignating the run as endangered (57 Fed. Reg. 27416). Because full protection already applied, the author concludes the redesignation adds no new protection.

**ESA framework**

- [[Section 7 Consultation]] requires federal agencies to ensure their actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species or destroy critical habitat.
- [[Section 9 Take Prohibition]] bars any person from taking listed species. "Take" is defined broadly to include harassing, harming, killing, and capturing, or attempting such conduct. NMFS extended this prohibition to the threatened winter-run.
- A [[Incidental Take Permit]] under ESA section 10 allows incidental take if an adequately funded conservation plan minimizes harm and does not appreciably reduce survival and recovery.

**Agency negotiations before the lawsuit**

- The [[Army Corps of Engineers]] issued GCID a 10-year dredging permit in 1976 under the [[Clean Water Act]] and the Rivers and Harbors Act. The Corps extended it to December 1989, conditioned on fish protection studies.
- A November 1989 GCID report named a new screen costing $26 million as the preferred alternative. Fish agencies endorsed it, but funding responsibility remained disputed.
- A December 1989 NMFS biological opinion held that the permit would violate the ESA unless GCID installed an effective screen. The Corps declined to issue the permit.

**Litigation and injunction**

- On 9 August 1991 the United States sued to halt pumping until fish protection measures were in place. The parties negotiated a [[Preliminary Injunction]] capping diversions at 1,100 cfs.
- On 9 January 1992 Judge David Levi issued a memorandum decision permanently enjoining pumping during the peak migration season. The final decision followed on 10 March 1992.
- The litigation lasted exactly five months.

**Six contributions to ESA case law**

1. **Direct vs. indirect take:** the court refused to separate them. The screen was needed only because of pumping, so the pumps caused the take. The court relied on *Palila v. Hawaii Dept. of Land & Natural Resources*.
2. **Water rights:** state water rights provide no defense to a take charge. ESA section 2(c)(2) cooperation language did not override express restrictions. Enforcement affects only how water is used, not the right to it.
3. **Causation:** the court rejected a separate state proximate-cause standard. GCID was a "substantial factor" in the take under California causation law.
4. **State law:** the California Endangered Species Act's definition of take was either identical or weaker, and therefore preempted. A separate California trial court ruling limiting CESA take to hunting or fishing remains pending on appeal.
5. **Timing of enforcement:** enforcement did not have to wait for critical habitat designation or a [[Recovery Plan]].
6. **NEPA:** enforcement is not a "major federal action" requiring [[National Environmental Policy Act]] review. The author notes that the listing decision itself did not trigger NEPA review.

The court also did not "balance the equities" for farmers. The author contrasts this with [[TVA v. Hill]] and later decisions such as [[Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo]] and [[Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell]], which allowed traditional equitable balancing.

**Settlement and outcome**

- About one week after the final decision, GCID and the federal government settled. Interim pumping resumed under a rigid fish protection regime, and GCID agreed to find and help fund a long-term solution. GCID also pledged $4 million toward permanent protection.
- The author predicts the case will reinforce federal salmon protection efforts across California and the Pacific Northwest (including Columbia River basin actions).
- Delta smelt was proposed for listing as threatened on 3 October 1991 (56 Fed. Reg. 50075), which could bring further restrictions on [[Water Rights|California water project operations]].

**Four cautions against overreading the success**

1. The main facts were undisputed, but more remote indirect-take cases may be less clear.
2. GCID's quick settlement was aided by its pending state claims against CDFG, scheduled for trial in winter 1992–1993.
3. The ESA applies only after listing, and the delay before listing likely reflected fear of the Act's restrictions.
4. Heroic rescue efforts at near-extinction are costly and may be less effective than earlier intervention. The author suggests that the $26 million screen could have saved far more salmon if undertaken years earlier.
