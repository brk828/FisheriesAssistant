# Development and Evolution of Skeletal Deletions in a Family of Viviparous Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by Rosen and Kallman, published in the *Quarterly Journal of the Florida Academy of Sciences*, examines why some [[Poeciliidae]] (live-bearing New World cyprinodontiforms) lack part of the skeletal support for their male intromittent organ. Males use a modified anal fin, the [[Gonopodium]], which is braced by [[Gonapophyses]] (modified hemal spines). Some genera have fewer gonapophyses or none at all. The authors removed or transplanted the presumptive [[Gonactinosts]] (internal fin supports) in juvenile fish and showed that these elements induce the overlying gonapophyses to develop in the anterior body region. They propose that this inductive link was lost secondarily in species with a strongly forward-shifted anal fin, and that the deletion of gonapophyses was likely a non-adaptive by-product of selection for a more efficient sperm-transfer mechanism.

## Background & Description

Poeciliids typically bear living young and achieve internal fertilization through the gonopodium. The gonopodium is an elongated anal fin supported by a "gonopodial suspensorium" of appendicular and axial bones (Figs. 1–2). Internal elements, the gonactinosts, articulate with the fin-ray pivot bones and carry the activating muscles. They are anchored to the gonapophyses, which project downward from the vertebral column.

Revisional work on the family (Rosen, unpublished manuscript) revealed that closely related genera differ in the number of gonapophyses present. The loss or reduction of these bones had no clear adaptive explanation. However, species lacking gonapophyses had anal fins displaced unusually far forward relative to the anterior hemal spines (Fig. 3). This led the authors to hypothesize that developing appendicular elements determine the presence, growth, and form of neighboring axial structures.

Normally, the anterior hemal spines undergo [[Histolysis]] during development. In females this process continues, and up to four anterior hemal spines may be lost. In maturing males, bone addition counteracts histolysis, so typically no more than one hemal spine is eliminated. The authors predicted that early removal of the appendicular elements would allow histolysis to proceed to completion, producing males without gonapophyses. Their experiments tested two points: (1) whether an induction arises from appendicular tissue, and (2) whether skeletal elements other than the anterior hemal spines can respond to that induction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study concerns the developmental and evolutionary biology of the family rather than field ecology. The text does not describe habitat, water chemistry, or population ecology of the species.

Taxa examined come from the New World: *Gambusia punctata* (Cuba), *Phallichthys amates* (Middle America), *Xiphophorus maculatus* (Middle America), and *Lebistes reticulatus*, *Limia vittata*, *Phalloceros caudimaculatus*, *Cnesterodon decemmaculatus*, and *Tomeurus gracilis* (South America). The text mentions *Limia caudofasciata* from Jamaica only in a footnote, where it was used for measurements of growth-related fin displacement.

The ecological relevance is evolutionary. Forward displacement of the anal fin is linked to a change in its position relative to the fish's center of gravity and visual field. According to Rosen and Tucker (manuscript), this allows better control during mating maneuvers. The authors describe the forward shift as "one of the primary events in poeciliid evolution" (following Rosen and Gordon, 1953).

## Key Findings

**Gonapophysis number across taxa**
- Most poeciliids have three gonapophyses. Some individuals of *Gambusia*, *Poeciliopsis*, and *Xenodexia* occasionally have four.
- *Lebistes*, *Limia*, and related forms typically have two. *Alfaro* has two, three, or four.
- *Tomeurus* and *Cnesterodon* lack gonapophyses, although other suspensorial elements remain typical.
- The authors argue that the absence of gonapophyses has arisen independently four times in the family. They also state that absence alone is not an adequate phylogenetic criterion; the form and orientation of present gonapophyses are more informative.

**Experimental design**
- Four species were used for manipulation: *Xiphophorus maculatus* (three gonapophyses), *Lebistes reticulatus* (two), *Limia vittata* (two), and *Phalloceros caudimaculatus* (three).
- Total of 140 immature fish were experimented on: 24 *Phalloceros caudimaculatus*, 80 *Xiphophorus maculatus*, 28 *Lebistes reticulatus*, and 8 *Limia vittata*. No more than two experiments were performed on a single fish.
- Post-operative mortality was below 10%.
- Approximately 70% of operated fish eventually differentiated into females, leaving few males per experimental series.
- Operations took less than three minutes with no anesthetic. Fish were kept three days in 0.6% sea salts solution to prevent infection.
- Transplantation of fins followed earlier methods of [[Kallman]] and Gordon (1957, 1958). In *Xiphophorus maculatus*, grafts could be made between individuals of the same [[Inbred Strain]] (isograft). In other species, the fin had to be returned to the same individual (autograft).

**Induction by gonactinosts**
- Partial or complete removal of presumptive gonactinosts from 10-day-old male *Lebistes reticulatus* and *Phalloceros caudimaculatus* caused the overlying gonapophyses to fail to develop normally.
- Induction appears to occur early, before visible structural changes. In *Limia vittata*, normal gonapophyses still formed after gonactinost removal at one month of age.
- Complete removal in *Lebistes* produced gonapophyses no longer than a single vertebra, or absent (Table 1). In *Limia* and *Xiphophorus*, removal at the stated ages still allowed normal gonapophyses (Table 1).
- Partial removal produced gonapophyses that were larger, because they grew outward in relation to the remaining gonactinostal tissue (Fig. 7; Table 2).

**Competence gradient**
- The competence of anterior hemal spines to form gonapophyses declines posteriorly. The first gonapophysis is always the most strongly developed, the second is much less so, and the third is rather poorly developed when present. A fourth (the fifth hemal spine) is extremely rare.
- Anal fin transplants to posterior hemal spines (elements 6–8) produced no gonapophysis, only non-specific growth.
- Ectopic transplants of gonactinosts never produced a gonapophysis-like structure. They did elicit non-specific bone growth in neural spines, pleural ribs, and hemal spines (Table 3; Fig. 8).

**Species-specific differences**
- In *Xiphophorus maculatus*, gonapophyses developed normally after fin removal at 10 days, so they may be determined earlier or develop autonomously. Its gonapophyses are simple and sometimes hard to distinguish from unmodified hemal spines. The authors consider this suspensorium structurally primitive, similar to that of oviparous cyprinodonts.
- Ectopic actinost transplants in females did not produce the definitive osseous growth seen in males. The authors suggest this depends on the male's physiological environment, possibly the rising androgen titers from the maturing testis.

**In situ fin reversal**
- Reversing the anal fin in 10-day-old male *Lebistes reticulatus* (Table 4) changed growth direction. Originally posterior elements became anterior in form, and originally anterior elements became posterior in form.
- The reversed posterior actinosts still showed some differentiation, suggesting they were already partly determined.

**Evocation and individuation**
- The authors adopt the distinction (after Waddington, 1956) between evocation (a non-specific call for response) and individuation (pattern formation in the responding structure).
- Presumptive gonactinosts appear to only evoke bone growth. Gonapophyses that are not induced still show normal pattern features, so they are probably self-individuating.
- In *Xiphophorus*, actinosts evoke growth but are not developmentally integrated with axial elements. This suggests that in more specialized forms, sharing the work of gonapophysis formation with the actinosts allows greater developmental flexibility.

**Proposed mechanisms for gonapophysis reduction**
The authors list five possible routes to fewer gonapophyses:
1. Reduced evocating potency or extent of the morphogenetic field of the actinosts.
2. Changed positions of actinosts and hemal spines, so the hemal spines contact the field only at its edge or not at all.
3. Gradual loss of hemal-spine competence.
4. Altered timing of histolysis or of the inductive stimulus, or a lag in osteogenic response.
5. A combination of these factors.

They conclude that with current data they cannot determine which factor predominates. For *Cnesterodon* compared with *Phalloceros*, the very advanced anal fin insertion of *Cnesterodon* suggests that loss of contact with the actinost-derived inductive stimulus was involved. Philippi's (1909) observation of vestigial gonapophyses in some *Cnesterodon* individuals suggests that the anterior hemal spines retain the ability to form these bones.

**Evolutionary model**
- Forward displacement of the anal fin required that gonapophysis development first come under partial actinost control. Forms such as *Lebistes* and *Phalloceros* retain this control, with gonapophyses bent forward to interdigitate with the transposed actinosts.
- In *Cnesterodon* and *Tomeurus*, extreme forward displacement may have led to secondary loss of the inductive relationship. Compensation comes from extensive connective tissue bridges between the gonactinosts, the vertebrae, and the pelvic girdle, and from modified sexually dimorphic pleural ribs serving a suspensorial role (Fig. 9).
- The authors argue that the transition could not have occurred in one step. Gonapophyses had to be lost gradually, one, then two, then three, so new means of axial support could be established.
- Gonapophysis complexes were highly adaptive as a route to greater specialization. Their subsequent deletion, being incidental to a more efficient sperm-transfer mechanism, may represent a non-adaptive change.

**Developmental requirement for anterior displacement**
- During ontogeny, the anal fin moves forward relative to the anterior hemal spines. The authors attribute this to (1) slower tissue growth in the pelvic and anal region relative to body growth and (2) a constriction of tissue between the pelvic and anal fins. Measurements came from a graded size series of *Limia caudofasciata* from Jamaica. Evolution of an anteriorly inserted anal fin therefore required a special growth pattern and reorganization of ventral tissues.

**Summary of conclusions**
- In more specialized poeciliids, gonactinosts provide an inductive stimulus essential for normal gonapophysis development. This effect is not apparent in more generalized forms.
- Establishment of the axial-appendicular inductive relationship enabled a major shift of the anal fin forward. In the most advanced forms, this shift has secondarily broken the inductive relationship, and gonapophyses fail to form.

**Limitations noted in the text**
- The authors state that the complexity of the inductive relationship prevents definitive attribution of gonapophysis loss to any single mechanism.
- The experiment with transplanting the dorsal fin into the anal position was still in progress and is not reported.
