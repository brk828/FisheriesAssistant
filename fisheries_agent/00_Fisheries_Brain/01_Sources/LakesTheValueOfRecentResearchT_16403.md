# Eutrophication of New Zealand Lakes: Nutrient Budget and Sources in Lake Rotorua

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries Research Division publication by G. R. Fish examines [[Eutrophication]], the artificial enhancement of water fertility, in New Zealand freshwaters. Using a [[Nutrient Budget]] for [[Lake Rotorua]] covering June 1967 to May 1968, the author finds that [[Sewage Effluent]] is an important but not dominant nutrient source. [[Agricultural Runoff]] from pastoral land is probably the more important source of plant-fertilizing salts such as [[Nitrate]] and [[Phosphate]]. A flash flood on a small pasture catchment and a [[Topdressing]] event produced short-lived but very large nutrient discharges. The author concludes that agricultural development is probably the most important cause of eutrophication in local lakes. The data are limited, and the author states that they are too few for general conclusions about New Zealand as a whole.

## Background & Description

Eutrophication increases the plant and animal standing crop of lakes and rivers. The author defines it as an acceleration of natural lake evolution. Published consequences include a decline in [[Salmonidae]]-like fish such as trout or char, an increase in [[Cyprinidae]]-like fish such as perch or carp, a shift from a diverse bottom fauna to one often dominated by [[Chironomid Flies]], and a shift from sparse to heavy plankton or water-weed growth. The main change is an increase in standing crop rather than a change in species. Eutrophication does not pose acute danger to communities, but it degrades clear water, makes it costly to filter, and reduces recreational value.

The paper reports that eutrophication is widespread in Europe and America and proved hard to control. Early pesticide treatments were expensive, ineffective and dangerous. Current control focuses on cleaning nutrient inflows, with success depending on early action. The author also describes a [[Phosphate Sparing Effect]]: the minimum phosphate needed for algal growth varies between lakes, and [[Shapiro et al. 1969]] linked this to dissolved salt concentration, especially magnesium salts.

New Zealand's situation is less documented. Eutrophication is detected mainly in easily accessible, popular waters. Small [[Coastal Dune Lakes]], generally no larger than 40 hectares, are prominent among affected lakes. [[Tomahawk Lagoon]] near Dunedin alternates between planktonic algal blooms and dense water weeds. The [[Waikato River]] is advanced in eutrophication, and [[Lake Taupo]] shows excess weed growth in some bays.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lakes Rotorua and Rotoiti were both described as moderately eutrophic. Lake Rotorua is shallow and well mixed at all seasons. Water samples were collected fortnightly from nine major inflows and the single outflow. Dissolved phosphate, nitrate and ammonia were measured by methods from [[Strickland and Parsons 1968]]. Flow data came from the Hydrological Survey, and rainfall and evaporation came from Rotorua Airport. Net precipitation was calculated from rainfall minus estimated lake evaporation.

The water budget identified rainfall on the lake surface (126.7 × 10⁶ m³) as the largest single water input, though only net precipitation is shown in Figure 1. [[Cold-water Springs]] are probably a much larger source than the surface inflows. Their flow and temperature vary little with season. The [[Hamurana Spring]] is the largest surface inflow. Several streams, including the Awahou, Waiteti and Ngongotaha, are also fed largely by shoreline springs.

The author lists three error sources that could not be quantified: changes in river-bed flow rates in the mobile pumice substrate, lag effects at the outflow where rising lake levels absorb surges, and unmeasured seepage and groundwater flow.

The nutrient budget shows that outflow removed far less than inflow. The author attributes this to the large biomass of aquatic plants absorbing nutrients, to sediment storage with only partial return by decomposition, and to nitrogen fixation and denitrification. Low nitrate and ammonia outflows suggest high denitrification.

## Key Findings

**Nutrient budget (June 1967 to May 1968, Table 1):**

- Total inflow of phosphate-P was 34,494 kg. Outflow was 14,371 kg (41.6% of inflow).
- Total inflow of nitrate-N was 109,338 kg. Outflow was 449 kg (0.4%).
- Total inflow of ammonia-N was 143,715 kg (approximate values). Outflow was 13,200 kg (9.2%).
- [[Hamurana Spring]] supplied the largest phosphate share (7,692 kg; 22.3%), despite low phosphate concentration, because its flow is consistently high.
- [[Awahou Stream]] supplied the most nitrate (30,583 kg; 28.0%). Its catchment is 85% pastoral land.
- [[Sewage Effluent]] carried the most concentrated phosphate and ammonia, but its small flow limited its load. It supplied 4,885 kg of phosphate (14.2%) and 28,500 kg of ammonia (19.9%). No nitrate was detected in the effluent, probably due to its chemically reduced state.
- [[Waiohewa Stream]] supplied 95,500 kg of ammonia (66.5% of total inflow). The source was effluent from geysers in the [[Tikitere Thermal Region]], which are rich in ammonia and other salts, not sewage.
- [[Utuhina Stream]] and [[Puarenga Stream]] carried large phosphate loads (14.5% and 17.0%). Both flow through urban areas.
- Rainfall contributed 2.4% of phosphate, 3.8% of nitrate and 6.7% of ammonia.

**Flash-flood and topdressing events:**

- In the [[Waingaehe Stream]] (catchment 17.8 km², 83% pasture), phosphate rose from a background of 0.09 ppm on 17 February 1969 to 8.9 ppm after air topdressing with superphosphate. It fell back to 0.1 ppm at the next routine sampling. This is roughly a 100-fold increase over background. The source gives the follow-up date as "31 February," which is not a valid date and is probably an error.
- In the [[Puruki Catchment]] (0.016 km² of stable grass-clad slopes), samples taken during a flash flood from 2 to 3 April 1969 showed large discharges. Peak dissolved phosphate was 875 g/h and peak dissolved ammonia was 3,650 g/h on 3 April. Suspended-sediment phosphorus peaked at 610 g/h. Values returned to near background by 10 April.

**Conclusions and interpretation:**

- Sewage is a major, constant source but not the paramount one. Springs and sewage are the main continuous contributors.
- During flooding and topdressing, nutrient discharge exceeded background by two or more orders of magnitude. The author estimates that three days of such flow could equal a whole year of normal flow in nutrient load, although he cautions that too much weight may have been placed on isolated instances.
- The author concludes that eutrophication may largely result from local agricultural practice.
- [[O'Connor 1968]] reported similar concerns about nutrient loss from agricultural land in South Island catchments, suggesting that causes may be similar across New Zealand.
- European and American comparisons show different dominant sources. Industrial effluent is more important in the Rhine and Swedish lakes, while sewage is more important in Lake Zurich, Lake Constance and in the United States.
- The paper acknowledges that eutrophication causes and effects are complex and still poorly understood, and that more biological and chemical monitoring of polluted and unpolluted lakes is needed.
