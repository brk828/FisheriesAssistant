# Arnett Creek Native Fish Re-Establishment Project Phase I

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Heritage Grant #193007, December 1995) from the [[Tonto National Forest]] documents Phase I of a project to prepare [[Arnett Creek]], a [[Queen Creek]] tributary in south-central Arizona, for native fish re-establishment. Phase I had three components: design of a fish barrier to block upstream invasion by non-native fish from Queen Creek, installation of a cattle guard to limit livestock access, and a fish habitat inventory to select reintroduction species. The barrier was designed as a [[Gabion Barrier]] at a remote site (Site 1) selected for stable bedrock and boulder anchoring, a narrow floodprone width, and high gradient. The estimated construction cost was $72,710. The habitat survey found a stream dominated by sand, gravel and small cobble, with limited instream cover. Of five candidate natives, the report recommended [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] as good reintroduction candidates, did not recommend [[Sonora Sucker]] at present, and suggested monitoring for [[Gila Chub]] and [[Gila Topminnow]].

## Background & Description

- **Grant and scope:** Awarded to the Tonto National Forest in August 1993. The Forest Service agreed to design a barrier, install a cattle guard near Telegraph Canyon, and complete a fish habitat inventory of the perennial reach. A watershed-wide map of perennial and intermittent waters rated for [[Fish Toxicant Treatment]] potential was also prepared, though not required by the grant.
- **Location:** Arnett Creek lies south of the Town of Superior at the base of Picketpost Mountain, surrounded by Lower Sonoran vegetation. Riparian cover is a rare co-dominant [[Bonpland Willow]] and [[Goodding Willow]] community. The creek is perennial and intermittent within the project area and ephemeral over most of its length.
- **Target threats:** Green sunfish and mosquitofish, both non-native and aggressive, are known or suspected in Queen Creek. The barrier blocks upstream movement only; downstream movement from Arnett Creek is not restricted.
- **Collaborators:** Staff from the Tonto National Forest, [[Arizona State University]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] contributed to site criteria and design.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Streamflow was measured from August 1991 at a site about 2 miles upstream of the barrier. The watershed above the barrier covers approximately 19,000 acres at a mean elevation of 1004 m. Mean annual rainfall is 14 to 22 inches. Runoff is flashy because of tilted, faulted bedrock and sparse upland ground cover. Design flood magnitudes (cfs) were: 2-yr 356; 5-yr 1,644; 10-yr 2,886; 25-yr 5,194; 50-yr 9,207; 100-yr 13,882. Discharge on 11 March 1994 was 0.58 cfs. Summer 1995 flow was too low to measure and was judged below 0.58 cfs.

**Riparian vegetation.** Myers (1994) described the riparian forest as healthy and young, with a low tree basal area and high stem density. Dominant trees were Bonpland willow, Goodding willow, ash, cottonwood and sycamore. Deer grass plays a key role in bank stability.

**Livestock management.** Before the [[Arnett Creek Riparian Pasture]] was built, up to 314 livestock plus 174 yearlings had access for 180 days per year. After construction, access is typically 3 to 15 days, mostly in winter, with fewer than 30 days per year and generally about 20 animals.

**Cultural resources.** None are present in the construction area. Because the site lies within an active channel and materials will be flown in by helicopter, no impacts are expected.

## Key Findings

### Barrier site selection
- Four sites were evaluated against eight criteria: floodprone width, anchoring substrate, gradient, adjacent terrace stability, habitat protected, access, environmental effects of access, and funding.
- **Site 1 (selected):** Floodprone width about 30 feet. Bedrock on the north bank and a large boulder on the south bank provide stable anchors. Gradient is the highest of all sites. The terrace is at least 7 feet above the channel and shielded by two large boulders. A flood-era fence on the terrace remains in excellent condition, which indicates the terrace has not been reached by flood waters, including the 1993 event. Access is by helicopter only. Preliminary cost was $32,428 against $50,000 available, later supplemented by $23,781 from the Bureau of Reclamation. The trade-off accepted was that some perennial habitat downstream would not be protected from reinvasion; structural soundness was judged more important.
- **Site 2 (rejected):** Floodprone width about 95 feet, making the barrier too large and costly. The floodplain is confined with no terrace.
- **Site 3 and Site 4 (rejected):** Floodprone width about 100 feet, requiring a barrier about 120 feet long with 20 feet keyed into the terrace. Gradient was extremely low and the adjacent terrace was low and unstable, so the channel would likely re-divert and the barrier could not withstand floods. Site 3 protected the least habitat. Site 4 protected the most but needed a temporary road on private and Forest Service land, with vegetation clearance and sand/gravel placement in the creek.

### Barrier design
- Design standard: withstand the 25-year flood (5,194 cfs), with a 3 to 5 foot vertical drop on the downstream face, edges higher than the centre, a permeable structure to avoid permanent impoundment, and a design life of 50 to 100 years.
- A downstream apron is required to prevent undercutting and to limit pool formation that would let fish rest or jump the barrier.
- Concrete was rejected because it would create an impoundment, require a road, and cost more. A two-barrier "Control Reach" design was rejected for lack of funds.
- Specifications include a 630 gabion wall with a horizontal rock-bolt and cable anchoring system (50,000 lb design load per anchor) and vertical #8 rebar grouted into bedrock. Construction is limited to low-flow periods. The barrier is about 31 feet wide at the stream and roughly 13 feet high and 26 feet wide at the wall.
- The Forest Service requested a 1 January 1996 contract action with a 30-day contract time and a 1 June 1996 start. The engineer's estimate was $72,710 (Mobilization $6,610; gabions 110 cubic yards at $600 per cubic yard). It is kept confidential until public bidding ends.

### Cattle guard
- Installed in summer 1994 on FS Road 2412 when the Riparian Pasture was built. Livestock use fell sharply after the pasture was created.

### Fish habitat inventory
- **Survey extent:** 21,732 feet of stream surveyed from Queen Creek to the upstream edge of the Riparian Pasture. Of this, 10,338 feet were dry, mostly in the lower reach.
- **Habitat composition (watered length):** low-grade riffles 40.7%, runs 39.7%, pools 19.6%.
- **Pool and width medians:** residual pool depth 0.8 ft overall. Watered pool depth was 0.83 ft in the pasture reach and 0.49 ft below it. Watered width was 7 ft overall, 9 ft in the pasture reach and about 4.5 ft below it.
- **Backwaters and side channels:** about 3% of the channel was backwater pools (median depth 0.8 ft) and about 8% was braided side channels (median width 4.5 ft).
- **Bank stability and embeddedness:** average bank stability rating 3 (50–79% cover), supported by riparian vegetation and a thick mat of an unidentified aquatic plant. Average embeddedness was 3.5 in the main channel and 4 in side channels.
- **Instream cover:** only 3.5% of banks were undercut. Small woody debris provided no cover in 83% of habitat units, and large woody debris was virtually absent. Terrestrial vegetation gave no cover in 62% of units. Substrate gave no cover in 52% of units, and most cover from substrate came from boulders (70%). Algae provided the most aquatic cover, but 53% of units had none.
- **Substrate:** sand, gravel and small cobble dominated, with some large cobble and silt/clay. Three pebble counts were taken (two in the pasture, one below).
- **Riparian community:** six permanent photopoints were established in the Riparian Pasture, with more planned in an exclosure.

### Candidate native fish
Five species were evaluated. The only known native fish record in the drainage is Gila chub. Recent surveys found only green sunfish and mosquitofish, which the authors said indicates an adequate food base.

- **Longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*):** Recommended. It tolerates flashy floods and is among the fastest to repopulate, as shown in Pinto Creek in 1993. It is usually found below 4900 ft and the reach lies below 2600 ft. Runs and shallow pools make up nearly 60% of habitat.
- **Desert sucker (*Catostomus clarki*):** Recommended as a good candidate, though habitat is suboptimal. Riffles and shallow pools (about 60% of habitat) are adequate but would improve with deeper pools. Known range is 300 to 6560 ft.
- **Sonora sucker (*Catostomus insignis*):** Not recommended at present because adults need deep, rocky pools with large cover (1 ft or deeper) that the stream lacks.
- **Gila chub (*Gila intermedia*):** Doubtful for now. It needs deep pools, undercut banks and vegetated margins. The authors recommend monitoring for 5 to 10 years.
- **Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*):** Not preferred at present. It is not highly resistant to flood displacement, the stream lacks cover, and it is not found below 5000 ft. Re-evaluation is recommended.

### Reintroduction prerequisites
- An Environmental Assessment by the Forest Service (underway).
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department 12-step reintroduction process (to begin with the Region 5 Fisheries Program Manager).
- Stocking source, rates and transport are for the Department to set. A monitoring plan covering habitat, fish populations and barrier integrity is planned for a later phase.
