# Federal Responsibilities for Trout Management in the Rocky Mountain Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Rick Stowell of the [[Forest Service]] (Missoula, Montana) defines the current responsibilities of the [[Forest Service]] (FS) and the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) for trout management in the [[Rocky Mountain Region]]. The author argues that the core federal role is to provide aquatic habitat capable of supporting native and desirable non-native fish populations. That task is complicated by overlapping policies, the growing influence of [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) listings, and the [[Exotic Species]] policy of [[Executive Order 11987]]. The paper concludes that [[Conservation Agreements]] (CAs) with State agencies and interested parties are the recommended path forward, and that the agencies are ready to enter them.

## Background & Description

**Scope of federal lands.** The FS and BLM administer over 450 million acres of public land for multiple uses. These lands contain more than 320 thousand miles of fishable rivers and over 4.6 million acres of surface water, and a significant share of this habitat lies in the Rocky Mountain region.

**Recreational access.** The paper states that, apart from Alaska, no other geographic area offers greater public access to fisheries than federal lands in the Rocky Mountains. Premier trout waters named include the [[Snake River]], the [[Yellowstone River]], the Green River, the South Platte River, and the [[Madison River]].

**Governing policy.** Federal agencies work under a growing and sometimes conflicting body of policies, regulations, priorities, and strategies. Congressional legislation provides the justification and foundation for agency policy, which is set out in agency manuals. Both manuals address native, exotic, and threatened or endangered (T/E) species, as well as introduction, transplantation, and augmentation.

**Key policy directives quoted in the paper:**
- **FS wildlife and fish policy** calls for recovering threatened or endangered species, maintaining at least viable populations of native and desired non-native species, and providing diverse aesthetic, consumptive, and scientific uses.
- **BLM general policy** aims to ensure optimum populations and natural abundance and diversity of wildlife and fish resources.
- **BLM aquatic habitat objectives** include restoring the natural integrity of aquatic ecosystems, maintaining or restoring natural aquatic biota diversity, managing habitat for anadromous and resident species of economic, social, or scientific value, and expanding recreational fisheries through partnerships.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threatened and endangered species.** The author notes that T/E issues are driving fish management. Traditionally, managing fish has been the responsibility of individual States, but T/E species have become, at least in part, a federal responsibility. [[Section 7 Consultation]] of the ESA requires federal agencies to ensure that their actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species or destroy or adversely modify their critical habitats. Potential listings include [[Bull Trout]] and perhaps some [[Inland Cutthroat Trout]] populations. The paper also mentions the recent listing of a rainbow trout population. Each new listing adds to the difficulty of balancing traditional recreational fishing with newer approaches such as [[Ecosystem Management]] and biodiversity protection.

**Exotic species.** [[Executive Order 11987]], signed by President Jimmy Carter on May 24, 1977, directs executive agencies to restrict the introduction and importation of exotic species into natural ecosystems on lands they own, lease, or administer. Exotic species are defined as species not naturally occurring in any ecosystem of the United States. The order also restricts using federal funds or programs to export native species for introduction abroad. It may be waived if the Secretary of Agriculture or the Secretary of the Interior finds that the action will not harm natural ecosystems. The author says this policy has the potential to extensively influence sport fisheries on federal lands.

**Cooperative frameworks.** Both agency manuals stress that permitted activities, such as T/E recovery programs, must be coordinated with State agencies and interested parties. The paper cites the [[Sikes Act]] of 1960 as providing for cooperative efforts in sport fisheries management.

**Stocking, transplantation, and augmentation.** Both agencies have issued manual policy on introducing fish and wildlife species, distinguishing wild from planted trout.
- **BLM (1992 policy):** Native species are to be used unless the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) process shows that (1) suitable native species are unavailable, (2) biological diversity will not be diminished, (3) exotic or naturalized species can be confined, (4) ecological site inventory shows the site cannot support reestablishment of a historically present species, and (5) management objectives cannot be met with native species.
- **FS policy:** Introductions or stocking may restore resources after environmental change, provide recreation where natural reproduction cannot meet demand, or introduce new species under established rules. Native or desirable non-native species are favored over new exotics.

**Historical impacts.** The author states that introducing many exotic salmonid species into the Rocky Mountains has severely limited both their range and the ability to reestablish valued species in their historic range. The result is isolated salmonid populations at high risk of extinction from stochastic events and genetic problems. The paper argues that the central issue is no longer "wild vs. hatchery" but "exotic vs. indigenous." The author states that the law is clear: the FS and BLM must provide for native species recovery.

## Key Findings

- **Scale:** The FS and BLM manage over 450 million acres, including more than 320,000 miles of fishable rivers and over 4.6 million acres of surface water. The Rocky Mountain region holds a large share of this habitat.
- **Core mandate:** Federal agencies must provide aquatic habitat that supports native and desirable non-native fish, a goal the author describes as difficult to meet.
- **Driver of change:** ESA listings, especially for [[Bull Trout]] and possibly [[Inland Cutthroat Trout]], are reshaping fisheries management and increasing federal involvement.
- **Exotic species restriction:** [[Executive Order 11987]] (1977) limits exotic species introductions on federal lands, with a waiver option, and is expected to strongly influence sport fisheries.
- **Native-first policy:** The BLM's 1992 policy requires native species unless five specific conditions are met, and FS policy favors native or desirable non-native species.
- **Conflict:** The paper describes a tension between hatchery-supported fisheries that meet public demand, self-sustaining non-native fisheries that are popular, and indigenous species near extinction that the public also wants protected.
- **Recommended solution:** Coordinated management through joint agreements with State fish and wildlife agencies and local interested parties, with [[Conservation Agreements]] as the main mechanism. Several draft CAs already exist in the Rocky Mountain area. The author states that the FS and BLM are ready to enter these agreements now.
