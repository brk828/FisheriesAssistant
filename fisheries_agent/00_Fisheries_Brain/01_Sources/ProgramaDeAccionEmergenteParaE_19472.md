# Emergency Action Program for Valle de Cuatrociénegas

#conservation #wetland-management #endemic-species #desert-springs #ichthyology #marine-biology

## Executive Summary

This document presents the final report of the Emergency Action Program (Programa de Acción Emergente) for the [[Valle de Cuatrociénegas]] Flora and Fauna Protection Area in [[Coahuila]], [[México]], completed in December 1998. The program was a collaborative effort between the North American Wetlands Conservation Council, the Government of the State of Coahuila, and Protección de la Fauna Mexicana, A.C. (PROFAUNA). The valley, located at 26°45'00"-27°00'00"N and 101°48'49"-102°17'53"W, encompasses approximately 150,000 hectares at elevations from 700 to 2,200 m.a.s.l. The region is characterized by an arid climate with mean annual precipitation of 200 mm and features an extraordinary concentration of natural springs (manantiales) and pools (pozas) in a desert environment. The program documented severe degradation from traditional resource use, particularly livestock grazing and water extraction, threatening endemic species and requiring immediate management intervention.

## Background & Description

The [[Valle de Cuatrociénegas]] is an arid valley in central [[Coahuila]], [[México]], surrounded by mountains reaching 2,200 m elevation. The climate is arid with a rainy season from May to October. The city of Cuatrociénegas (population 11,000) is located at the northwest extreme, with ejidal settlements around the periphery. The central valley remains largely unpopulated.

The valley's hydrology, though poorly understood, is remarkable for the presence of numerous springs in a desert environment. Most springs emerge near mountain foothills, with natural channels forming pools (pozas) and lagoons of variable diameter and depth throughout the valley. The valley floor supports halophytic grassland vegetation used primarily for grazing horses, with evident overgrazing and trampling damage particularly severe around pool margins.

The aquatic ecosystem supports 19 reported fish species, including 10 endemics and 3 exotic species. Endemic fish include [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] and [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]], with the former requiring very specific shallow (6-8 cm), high-temperature habitats for reproduction. Other fauna include native shrimp ([[Palaemonetes suttkus]]), native turtles (box turtle, softshell turtle), and exotic species including red-eared slider turtles, crayfish ([[Orchonectes]] sp.), and jewel cichlid ([[Hemichromis]] sp.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The valley's springs and pools represent critical habitat for endemic and migratory species in an otherwise arid landscape. Traditional land use has severely impacted these aquatic systems through:

1. **Livestock impacts**: Free-ranging horses and burros access all pools, causing trampling of riparian vegetation, water contamination, and habitat degradation. Horse tracks and signs were the most commonly observed animal evidence.

2. **Water extraction**: Artificial canals drain pools for agricultural irrigation, lowering water levels below natural thresholds. Many pools have artificial channels, and water extraction often occurs at the pool floor level rather than from overflow.

3. **Exotic species**: Introduction of non-native fish (jewel cichlid, largemouth bass [[Micropterus salmoides]], bluegill [[Lepomis megalotis]], catfish [[Ictalurus lupus]]), crayfish, and red-eared slider turtles threatens native species through predation and competition.

4. **Tourism pressure**: Popular pools like [[Pozas Azules]] experience degradation from visitor access.

The riparian zones around pools typically consist of halophytic grassland, heavily trampled and grazed in most locations. Aquatic vegetation includes native water lily ([[Nymphaea ampla]]) and invasive exotic water hyacinth ([[Eichornia crassipes]]) in the southeast valley.

## Key Findings

### Database Development

The program created a comprehensive database of wetland pools within the valley:

- **793 quadrants** surveyed using INEGI geological maps with Universal Transverse Mercator grid
- **165 pools** documented with GPS coordinates, physical-chemical parameters, and biological observations
- Pool density categories: zero (no pools), low (1-3 pools), medium (4-8 pools), high (9-14 pools)
- **Discrepancies** noted between mapped pools and field observations; some mapped pools not found, others not previously mapped

### Pool Characteristics

Physical parameters showed high variability:
- **Size**: Most pools in categories 2-3 (3.5-25 m length)
- **Temperature**: 18.7-36.0°C
- **Dissolved oxygen**: 0.2-11.8 mg/L (1-163% saturation)
- **Conductivity**: 782-7,520 µS
- **Total dissolved solids**: 292-3,770 mg/L
- **pH**: 5.76-8.68

### Biological Observations

**Fish**: [[Gambusia]] sp. observed most frequently (126 pools), followed by [[Astyanax mexicanus]] (72 pools) and [[Cichlasoma minckleyi]] (71 pools). Endemic [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] and [[C. bifasciatus]] found in specific locations. Exotic crayfish ([[Orchonectes]] sp.) discovered in eastern valley pools where not previously reported.

**Other fauna**: Horses and burros most common (ubiquitous access), followed by coyotes, raccoons, native shrimp ([[Palaemonetes suttkus]]), box turtles, softshell turtles, and exotic red-eared sliders.

**Vegetation**: 49 pools lacked apparent aquatic vegetation. Where present, grasses most common (often colonizing due to water level decline), followed by native water lily. Exotic water hyacinth found in southeast valley.

### Conservation Actions Implemented

**Exclusions (Fencing)**: Six sites fenced to exclude livestock while permitting wildlife access:
- **Poza Tío Cándido** (Rancho Orozco): 3.39 hectares protecting main pool and four springs
- **Pozas Azules** (Rancho Santa Marta): Three exclosures (3,000 m², 2,250 m², 706.5 m²) protecting tourist-impacted pools
- **Complejo Churince** (Ejido 6 de Enero): Two exclosures (3.32 hectares, 2,091 m²) protecting [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] reproduction sites and hybrid zone between C. atrorus and C. bifasciatus

Fences constructed with white cedar posts and three wire strands, allowing wildlife passage while excluding cattle and horses. Local residents hired for construction, creating economic benefit and demonstration sites.

**Water Level Control**: Two sites identified for engineering works to regulate water flow and restore pool levels:
- **Poza de la Becerra**: Popular swimming area with depleted water level due to extraction; existing gate at canal floor level rather than allowing overflow
- **Poza Santa Tecla**: Drained pool lacking flow control gate

Engineering designs prepared with budget estimates for simple, low-cost structures (gavion-type weirs with secondary maintenance channels) to raise water levels while maintaining irrigation water availability.

### Management Recommendations

The Emergency Action Program, delivered to the Dirección de Ecología del Gobierno del Estado de Coahuila in August 1997 (with corrections January 1998), provides 3-4 year guidance for short and medium-term actions and identifies needs for governmental and non-governmental support. The program emphasizes:

1. Restoration of degraded habitats
2. Protection of endemic species
3. Improvement of conditions for migratory waterfowl
4. Engagement of landowners and service providers in management
5. Expansion of exclusion and water control programs based on demonstration sites

The database (provided on diskette in Excel format) enables future Geographic Information System development for ongoing monitoring and adaptive management.
