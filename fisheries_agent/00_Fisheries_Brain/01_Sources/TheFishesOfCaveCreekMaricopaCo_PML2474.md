# The Fishes of Cave Creek, Maricopa County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the fish fauna of [[Cave Creek]], a normally intermittent desert stream in Maricopa County, Arizona, sampled at nine stations in 1969. The fauna comprised three native species and no introduced species: [[Agosia chrysogaster]] (longfin dace), [[Gila intermedia]] (Gila chub) and [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace). The longfin dace was widespread wherever water persisted, while the other two species were found only in the spring-influenced section at [[Seven Springs Wash]]. The authors also examined how pool size relates to the length and weight of longfin dace.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Cave Creek is a stream in south-eastern Yavapai County that flows about 3.5 miles before being joined by the spring-fed Seven Springs Wash, which supplies most of the flow in dry periods. The semi-permanent stream then enters a canyon about four miles long, bordered by New River Mesa and Skull Mesa. Below the canyon, the creek continues toward Phoenix and ends at the Arizona Canal.
- **Setting:** The channel lies at about 4,400 feet elevation. The climate is hot in summer and cool in winter, with a reported temperature range of 5 to 35°C. Mean annual precipitation at Seven Springs is approximately 20 inches and decreases downstream.
- **Vegetation:** Riparian vegetation includes sycamore (*Platanus wrightii*), cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*), willow (*Salix* spp.) and smaller plants such as watercress and saltcedar. Surrounding country is typical Upper to Lower Sonoran vegetation.
- **Geology:** The creek flows through volcanic agglomerate. Stations in the central study section are bordered by diorite and mineralized schist.
- **Authors and affiliation:** G. G. Stout, Edward Grant Bloom and James K. Glass, Arizona State University, Tempe. The work was part of a partial fulfillment of requirements for a degree in fishery biology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Collections were made at nine stations. Stations I and II lay in the lower Seven Springs section; Station II was near a campground. Stations I and II were the only stations with year-round flowing water sufficient to prevent severe stagnation. Stations III and IV lay between the campground and the canyon, Stations V to VII were inside the canyon, and Stations VIII and IX lay between the canyon mouth and Chalk Canyon. No data were collected from the lower creek, where the stream was essentially dry below Station IX. Station I had been partly modified by concrete. Air temperatures during sampling ranged from 18 to 31°C. Fish were collected with a quarter-inch seine and a fine-mesh dip net.

**Habitat associations.** Earlier work on [[Aravaipa Creek]] showed that longfin dace prefer shallow, sandy areas of moderate current, with young fish in shallow eddies and larger adults in algal mats and debris. The authors observed the same habitat use in Cave Creek. Young longfin dace move downstream into normally intermittent reaches when possible and can persist for a time under drying conditions.

**Spring influence.** The Gila chub and speckled dace were confined to the Seven Springs section, where constant spring inflow kept water temperature less variable. The authors suggest that the temperature stability of this section, rather than the wider stream, supports these species.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna:** Three native species were recorded, with no introduced species. The longfin dace was the most abundant fish in the Gila River drainage of Arizona and southwestern New Mexico.
- **Dominance:** Longfin dace made up 81 percent of the fish fauna at Station I, and were the dominant species at most other stations.
- **Distribution:** Longfin dace occurred throughout the stream wherever sufficient water remained. The Gila chub and speckled dace were found only at Seven Springs.
- **Gila chub:** The species is restricted to the [[Gila River Basin]]. Its presence at Seven Springs was reported with evidence of reproductive activity.
- **Breeding:** Longfin dace appear to have an extended breeding season beginning in early April.
- **Size and habitat:** Different-sized longfin dace occupied different sections of the stream. Larger fish preferred deeper pools, and deeper pools held heavier fish per unit length class than shallow pools.
- **Statistics:** Pool size was correlated with fish length and with weight per unit length. Analyses used the [[Spearman Rank Correlation]], with P values below 0.01 in both cases, indicating at least 99 percent reliability.
- **Depth and size:** The authors could find no published material on a relationship between stream depth and intraspecific size, although such a relationship was shown for longfin dace in Cave Creek.
- **Length distributions:** Length-frequency histograms were prepared for each of the nine stations. Sample sizes ranged from N = 14 to N = 299 individuals per station.
- **Limitations:** The collecting dates for the canyon and lower stations are partly illegible in the source. Sampling was limited to a single year, and the discussion of barriers and reproductive stages is incomplete in the available text.
