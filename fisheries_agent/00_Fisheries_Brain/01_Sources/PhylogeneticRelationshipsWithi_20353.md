# Phylogenetic Relationships Within the North American Hydrobiid Snail Genus Tryonia: Taxonomic and Biogeographic Implications

#malacology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether the freshwater snail genus [[Tryonia]] is a natural (monophyletic) group and reconstructs its evolutionary relationships to infer the regional biogeographic history of western North America. The authors sequenced a 603 bp fragment of the mitochondrial [[Cytochrome C Oxidase Subunit I]] (COI) gene for all described congeners they could collect, plus related "[[Littoridina Group]]" genera and Australasian outgroups, and analysed the data with [[Maximum Parsimony]] and [[Maximum Likelihood]] methods. The main result is that [[Tryonia]], as currently defined, is **polyphyletic**: its species fall into several distinct clades. The authors interpret the distribution of living species as the product of vicariance linked to Cenozoic tectonics (Colorado Plateau uplift, Mogollon Rim formation, Death Valley pull-apart opening, and strike-slip faulting), while acknowledging that these interpretations are largely speculative.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The family [[Hydrobiidae]] is the most diverse group of freshwater snails in North America, with about 35 genera and 285 described species. These small, benthic, obligately aquatic snails disperse slowly, which ties their distributions closely to drainage history. The family is regionally Paleogene or older, making it a potentially powerful tool for testing responses to [[Vicariance]]. Few phylogenetic hypotheses had been built for hydrobiids, which the authors identify as a major gap.

**Genus Tryonia.** The genus contains at least 22 Recent species, most of them locally endemic, and sympatry among congeners is rare. Distribution by region:

- **Great Basin:** 8 unique congeners
- **Pecos River-Rio Grande:** 6 species
- **Gila River:** 1 species
- **Colorado River:** 1 species
- **Widespread:** *T. protea* is uniquely parthenogenetic and occurs in the Great Basin and Colorado River drainage
- **Pacific coast:** *T. imitator*
- **Gulf of California coast:** *T. quitobaquitae*
- **Peninsular Florida:** two broadly disjunct species, including *T. aequicostata* and *T. brevissima*
- **Northern Mexico:** a poorly known fauna; the sole described representative of one species, *T. hertleini*, went extinct several decades ago after its type-locality springs dried

**Taxonomic history.** Tryonia was loosely defined on the basis of a few shell and genitalic features that are not demonstrably synapomorphic, and many species have never been described in detail. The genus was placed in the informal "Littoridina group" of the subfamily [[Cochliopinae]], in which males have glandular papillae on the penis. [[Mexipyrgus]] was conjectured to be its closest relative. The subgenus *Paupertryonia* (Taylor 1987) was erected for five Rio Grande species lacking penial basal papillae.

**Fossil record.** The oldest confirmed record is a minimal Miocene age, based on *T. imitator*-like fossils from the Mint Canyon Formation (southwestern California). Higher-spired shells resembling Tryonia from Paleocene-Eocene lake beds (Flagstaff Formation) may indicate older origins, but their affinities are uncertain. Taylor (1987) suggested extant species are generally of Miocene age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** *Tryonia* typically lives in inland thermal springs. Some species tolerate salt: *T. salina* lives in Cottonball Marsh, Death Valley, where salinities reach several times that of seawater (LaBounty and Deacon 1972). Floridian species occur in both lakes and springs, and *T. imitator* lives in coastal strand habitats across a broad salinity range (Kellogg 1985).

**Distributional parallels.** *Tryonia* distribution and endemism closely parallel those of the [[Cyprinodon]] pupfishes, one of the better-studied groups of western fishes (Miller 1981). This parallel motivates comparative biogeography across taxa.

**Thermal springs.** The authors argue that the spatial clustering of living fauna reflects the uneven distribution of thermal springs, which depend on magmatic heat sources and on faulting that creates vertical permeability (Waring 1965), rather than sampling bias alone.

**Biogeographic setting.** The region has a complex Cenozoic history, including the post-Laramide, Eocene-Oligocene erosional surface, the [[Colorado Plateau]] uplift, formation of the [[Mogollon Rim]], Rio Grande rift graben development, and right-lateral strike-slip faulting along the [[Walker Lane Belt]] and [[Eastern California Shear Zone]]. This history frames the vicariance hypotheses below.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Data

- **Sampling:** All described *Tryonia* species were targeted except *T. brunei*, which could not be relocated at its single known locality in west Texas. Three undescribed species were included (two from the Great Basin, one from the Alabama coastal plain). Related genera from the "Littoridina group" and "Heleobia group" were included, along with Australasian outgroups (*Ascorhis*, *Phrantela*). Specimens were live-collected and preserved in ethanol or liquid nitrogen; vouchers are at the USNM.
- **Molecular work:** A 710 bp COI segment was amplified with primers COIL 1490 and COIH 2198 (Folmer et al. 1994).
- **Analysis:** Heuristic parsimony searches were run in PAUP* 4.0 (Swofford 1998), with 500 bootstrap iterations.

### Sequence Variation

- Aligned matrix: 603 bp (the text also cites 601 bp in one place), corresponding to positions 1566-2168 of the *Drosophila yakuba* mtDNA.
- Variable sites: 247; phylogenetically informative sites: 212.
- Average base frequencies: 25% A, 40% T, 17% C, 18% G; no base-composition bias among taxa.
- Transitions saturate in outgroup comparisons, but not among ingroup taxa.
- First and second codon positions are informative among distant taxa; third positions are most informative among close relatives.
- Intra-specific divergence in *Tryonia*: 0-0.2%.
- *T. variegata* samples from the Amargosa River drainage differed by 5%, suggesting the species needs revision.
- Inter-specific ingroup divergence: 1.3-14.8%.
- Ingroup versus outgroup divergence: 16.1-22.5%.

### Phylogenetic Results

- Parsimony yielded eight trees of 863 steps (CI = 0.45). The preferred tree had the consistently lowest likelihood score across models.
- Australasian outgroups consistently fell basally, supporting the monophyly of the Cochliopinae.
- Four well-supported clades were recovered:
  - **Clade I:** four species currently in *Tryonia* (two from the Pecos River-Rio Grande, two from the southeastern US)
  - **Clade II:** other "Littoridina group" members (*Littoridinops* and an undescribed *Pyrgophorus*)
  - **Clade III:** two "Heleobia group" members
  - **Clade IV:** the type species *T. clathrata*, 13 other congeners, and their sister genus *Mexipyrgus*
- An undescribed Great Basin species resembling *Tryonia* was the most basal ingroup taxon.
- **Monophyly test:** *Tryonia* is polyphyletic, as are its subgenus *Paupertryonia* and the penial-papilla-based "Littoridina group" classification. The authors attribute this mainly to incomplete study, particularly of female genitalia, rather than conflict between morphology and molecules.

### Morphological Support

- Clade IV's "true" *Tryonia* and *Mexipyrgus* share a short sperm tube, whereas other "Littoridina group" members have an elongate, distally fused sperm tube.
- Two species (*Tryonia* n. sp. 2 and *T. robusta*) have a different penial gland type that is not homologous with the papillae of other snails, which may explain their divergent placements.

### Biogeographic Conclusions

- **Ancestral range:** The authors assume a broadly distributed ancestral *Tryonia* linked to the post-Laramide, Eocene-Oligocene erosion surface, and an eastward ancestral range along the northern Gulf of Mexico coast, with later vicariance from marine transgressions or deltaic changes (Winker 1982; Salvador 1991).
- **Two major sub-clades:** One includes Colorado River and upper Amargosa species associated with the Colorado Plateau margins. The other includes lower Amargosa, Great Basin, California coastal, lower Colorado and Pecos species. The authors link this split to mid-Tertiary uplift of the Colorado Plateau and formation of the Mogollon Rim, which bisected Arizona (Oligocene uplift minimum).
- **Pecos River:** Subsequent vicariance of *T. circumstriata* and *T. cheatumi* may reflect Miocene-Pliocene Rio Grande rift graben development or Pecos Valley solution collapse.
- **Amargosa River as a composite basin:** The upper and lower Amargosa segments form non-overlapping endemism areas with different sister relationships, consistent with an early Amargosa-Colorado "paleoriver" that was later diverted into Death Valley. Death Valley opened by pull-apart faulting only about 2-6 Ma (Serpa and Pavlis 1996).
- **Southern origin:** The Death Valley sub-clade is most closely related to Rio Grande snails. The authors propose a southern derivation, paralleling the proposed origin of Death Valley pupfishes from northern Mexico (Echelle and Dowling 1992; Parker and Kornfield 1995). Transport on displaced crustal blocks (schollen) along the San Andreas system is suggested, with displacements of over 300 km and a dispersal window of 6-20 Ma.
- **Coastal and estuarine habitat:** A freshwater origin is implied for *Tryonia*, because the sister group and basal ingroup taxa occupy inland springs. *T. imitator*'s highly derived position suggests it secondarily invaded estuarine habitats.
- **Mexico:** Phylogenetic data for northern Mexican fauna are lacking and are needed to test the origin of the Death Valley *Tryonia*.

### Caveats and Future Work

- The authors describe their biogeographic interpretation as "largely speculative," because multiple geological events could explain the same vicariance and the fossil record is sparse.
- The taxonomy requires revision: *T. variegata* appears to comprise divergent lineages, and many species lack detailed anatomical study.
- Further tests should include the female genitalia, additional northern Mexican taxa, and comparisons with other groups such as [[Death Valley Pupfish]]. The authors hope the work will prompt similar studies, responding to Minckley et al. (1986).
