# Arizona Comparative Risk Project Ecosystem Analysis: Streams and Rivers

#freshwater-ecology #conservation #endangered-species #habitat-degradation #anthropogenic-impacts #risk-assessment #native-fish-fauna #stream-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive risk assessment examines anthropogenic stressors affecting Arizona's stream and river ecosystems. The document, authored by W.L. Minckley and Dennis M. Kubly from Arizona State University and Arizona Game & Fish Department, evaluates physical, chemical, and biological alterations to flowing-water systems. Arizona contains approximately 240,300 stream-km, of which only ~8,000 km are perennially flowing. The native fish fauna has suffered catastrophic decline: 24 of 31 native taxa are imperiled, six have been extirpated from the state, one is extinct, 17 (55%) are federally listed as threatened or endangered, and 12 (39%) are candidates for federal listing. The analysis emphasizes that streams cannot be separated from their watersheds and that most Arizona streams have suffered major degradation during the last century from multiple human activities including mining, grazing, agriculture, damming, water diversion, channelization, urbanization, and species introductions.

## Background & Description

Early limnological investigations focused on classification and description of local phenomena such as [[Water Temperature]], [[Flow Regime]], [[Water Chemistry]], and substrate. As understanding evolved through integration of hydrology, soil science, and related disciplines, it became evident that streams function as ecological units inseparable from their watersheds. Streams ultimately receive and transport materials deposited or appearing in their catchments, with only a small percentage decomposed in the terrestrial environment. Therefore, any noxious or deleterious material, action, process, or organism—whether input at watershed limits or directly into channels—may profoundly affect stream ecosystems.

Arizona's high natural variability in climate and topography produces comparable variation in stream ecology. The state contains approximately 240,300 stream-km total, with ~173,000 km on non-tribal lands. Only about 8,000 km support perennial flow; the remainder are ephemeral (flowing only after precipitation) or intermittent (with short permanent reaches), either naturally or due to human intervention. Some reaches remain permanent due to wastewater disposal. The analysis uses 8,000 km of perennial watercourses as the environmental baseline, though this excludes many aquatic communities in temporary waters, hyporheic biota beneath dry beds, springs, and ciénegas—habitats notorious for unique, relict, and endemic organisms.

The native fish fauna originally comprised 31 taxa. Historical documentation of decline includes work by Dill (1944), Miller (1946 et seq.), Minckley (1965 et seq.), and others. Changes are chronicled in technical and popular publications through narrative and photographic comparisons spanning the last century. Among aquatic organisms, Arizona fishes have been most studied and are most useful for interpreting ecosystem-level impacts of stream alteration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Physical and Chemical Alterations

**Natural Sources**: Toxic materials may originate from ore deposits, metals in soils, or contaminants in springwaters. Examples include iron and sulfate in springs on [[Chase Creek]] near Clifton, saline hot springs entering the [[San Francisco River]] at Clifton, Pah Tempe Spring on the [[Virgin River]], and saline springs on the [[Black River]] and [[Salt River]]. During low discharge from human depletions or natural drought, such inflows damage aquatic organisms.

**Accidental Spills and Toxic Releases**: Mining operations have consistently produced toxic releases. Historical records document decimation of fishes in Chase Creek and downstream Gila River from Clifton-Morenci operations. The [[San Pedro River]] suffered repeated severe pollution from mines at Cananea, México. [[Pinto Creek]] exemplifies chronic toxic releases from mine-tailings and tailings ponds. Fish kills from toxic releases are common near Phoenix. A 1974 pesticide spill in the Mainline Canal killed fishes from upstream of Yuma to the USA-Mexico boundary.

**Air Pollution**: Viewed as a potentially chronic problem from mining, metal extraction, industry, coal-fired generating stations, and automobiles. Five statewide monitoring stations reported no violations of sulfur dioxide or nitrogen dioxide emissions during October 1986 to September 1991. Well-buffered waters and alkaline soils suggest lack of acid deposition problems, though emissions from Mexican copper smelters contribute to acid precipitation (pH 4.7-5.0) near [[San Bernadino National Wildlife Refuge]]. Acid precipitation is suspected in extirpation of [[Tarahumara Frog]] from [[Santa Cruz River]] tributaries.

**Grazing**: Ecosystem-level impacts transmitted through progressive physical and biological watershed degradation—trampling and vegetation removal lead to heavier runoff, increased erosion and sediment input, arroyo cutting, and reduced groundwater infiltration and storage. Nutrient and organic-matter loading from livestock wastes affects water quality. Concentrated livestock directly modify banks and bottoms, degrading water quality, with effects exacerbated during drought. Few published scientific studies quantify relationships in Arizona despite considerable evidence from other western states.

**Agriculture**: Largest direct impacts occur from flow depletion and water-quality degradation. Flow depletion results from dams, impoundments, diversions, and floodplain pumping lowering water tables. Water-quality deterioration results from increased erosion, loss of riparian vegetation, and runoff or aerial transport of fertilizers and pesticides. Floodplain development involves subsurface drainage systems, removal of natural riparian vegetation, and direct bank alterations. AZDEQ (1992) identified agriculture as the "predominant source of contamination in streams."

**Transportation Corridors**: Highways and roads affect streams on short-term and long-term scales. Short-term, high-magnitude impacts during construction include modified drainage patterns, soil disruption, accelerated erosion, and altered streambeds and channel geometry. [[Interstate Highway 15]] through the Virgin River gorge exemplifies construction directly above rivers. Chronic effects persist near bridge crossings, bank hardening, and wherever modified drainage increases erosion and sediment transport. Culverts act as barriers to upstream organism movement, increasing population fragmentation. Sand and gravel extraction from channels and floodplains destabilizes channels and increases turbidity.

**Energy Production**: Major sources include nuclear plants, fossil-fuel-fired generating plants, and hydroelectric dams. Dams have the most direct and pernicious effects, blocking streams and increasing population fragmentation while precluding migration. They alter downstream hydrological patterns, thermal regimes, sediment and nutrient inputs/outputs, and biotic productivity, diversity, and taxonomic composition. Hydroelectric power demands drive water release schedules, resulting in large daily discharge fluctuations and major modifications in seasonal hydrologic patterns. Requirements for sufficient head dictate delivery from impoundment depths, producing perennially cold releases nearly devoid of seasonal temperature changes—native Arizona fishes other than trouts cannot reproduce in these cold waters. Released waters are clear and erosive, removing sediments without replacement and modifying channel geometry and substrate composition. When dams are chained in sequence, as in major Arizona streams, effects are overwhelming with little hope for restoration without determined effort.

**Mining**: Arizona contains numerous mineral deposits. Surface mining scars landscapes during overburden stripping, vegetation removal, road construction, and stream dredging or channelization. Mining pollutes streams through suspended sediments, toxic heavy metals, and acids. Effects vary with extraction method, metal toxicity, extraction efficiency, and development/operation of wastepiles, haul roads, tailings ponds, stockpiles, and processing plants. Both short- and long-term impacts result; pollution from abandoned mines may persist for decades. Recent legislation requires rehabilitation and reclamation performance bonds, though rehabilitation rarely equals restoration and abandoned mines often escape coverage.

**Timber Management**: Harvesting and silvicultural treatments share features with grazing and mining regarding watershed alteration and with transportation corridors regarding road construction impacts. Chemicals (pesticides, fertilizers, fire retardants) applied to protect, rehabilitate, or enhance timber resources may affect biotas directly through toxicity or indirectly through bioaccumulation. Pesticides may affect non-target organisms important in stream trophic economy. Timber operations open access to remote areas for vehicular, recreational, and other uses. Fire suppression leading to accumulated debris and subsequent wildfires results in substantial damage when ash and dissolved/particulate materials enter with post-fire runoff. The Dude Fire in 1990 caused substantial aquatic life losses along the [[Mogollon Rim]]; wildfire in [[Gila Wilderness Area]], New Mexico, in 1993 eliminated endangered [[Gila Trout]] populations.

**Interbasin Water Transfers**: Four known transfers exist: between [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] (Little Colorado River basin) and [[East Verde River]] (Verde River basin); between [[Showlow Lake]] (Little Colorado basin) and [[Carrizo Creek]] (Salt River basin); between [[Black River]] (Point of Pines, Salt River basin) and [[Eagle Creek]] (Gila River basin); and between [[Lake Havasu]] on the Colorado River mainstem and central/southern Arizona via the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The first three have been circumstantially implicated in faunal transfers. The CAP Canal created considerable environmental controversy, including a USFWS Jeopardy Biological Opinion based on projected impacts of non-indigenous fishes on threatened and endangered indigenous fishes ([[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Sonoran Topminnow]]). Other factors include potential parasite transfers, water depletion effects in contributing streams, enhancement in receiving systems, and future organism introductions using interbasin waterways for dispersal.

**Channelization**: Alignment of stream channels into straight or artificial configurations, often with bank hardening. Results in removal or loss of riparian vegetation, loss of instream cover, altered riffle-pool ratios and substrate composition, increased current velocities and water temperatures, divorce of stream from floodplain, curtailed meander and oxbow formation, and incision/rejuvenation increasing downstream sediment loads. Environmental consequences are severe within modified reaches and at distances upstream and downstream. Estimated to affect ~40% of existing perennial stream kilometers in Arizona. Effects documented for lower [[Colorado River]], [[Salt River]], and Santa Cruz River. Magnitude of negative short-term and long-term impacts is high; stream communities have low to moderate resistance or resilience. Restoration through dechannelization has received little attention in Arizona.

**Water Diversion**: Used by Native Americans for irrigation long before European occupation. Modern canal networks supplying agriculture, industry, and municipalities are far more extensive. Beheading streams by headwater impoundments reduces overall water input, traps nutrients and sediments, reduces groundwater infiltration at lower elevations, and reduces downslope discharge variance. Diversions reduce downstream volumes, influencing all discharge-dependent stream functions. CAP-magnitude diversions raise questions about water depletion downflow from intakes, discharge enhancement at delivery points, watershed interconnection, biological contaminant transport, and future non-indigenous organism introductions using interbasin waterways.

**Groundwater Pumping**: Until recently, Arizona water law maintained absolute distinction between surface and groundwaters. New recognition that these are interconnected has yet to realize full effects. In basins with perennial streamflow, floodplain pumping promotes channel-to-groundwater flow, reduces shallow aquifer flow into channels, and can ultimately obliterate springs and all but flood flows. Impacts of water-table depletion are profound and often fatal to aquatic ecosystems. Resistance and resilience are low; restoration possibilities are remote unless recharge can be rapid, extensive, and reliable.

**Impoundments and Dams**: Perennial reservoirs are sites of multiple recreational activities including fishing, boating, and water skiing. Meeting recreational user desires promotes non-native fish introductions, marina construction with associated waste disposal and petroleum product storage/sale. Most impoundments were created before environmental legislation; native biota needs are seldom factored into operations.

**Recreation**: Water-based recreation is tightly associated with reservoirs and dam/impoundment development. Recreational fishing lakes were constructed on streams along the Mogollon Rim, White Mountains, and southeastern Arizona in the 1960s-1970s. Off-highway vehicles operating in riparian zones and stream channels, and hiking in channels, have been implicated in habitat disruptions associated with reduced substrate stability, increased turbidity, and direct effects on fish foods, spawning sites, and populations; impacts may influence downstream reaches.

**Urbanization**: Directly or indirectly involves large proportions of other stressor categories—power demands, potable and industrial water, recreational development. Streams flowing through or near urban zones are typically impounded, diverted, dewatered, channelized, and polluted, with indigenous biotas extirpated. Systems are altered downstream through desiccation from impoundment, diversion, or groundwater pumping, or may be perennial due to wastewater discharge. Some perennial reaches have been re-created by wastewater but are typically inhabited by non-indigenous organisms and often contaminated by toxic materials or domestic wastes posing human health hazards. Hazardous conditions recorded in [[Gila River]] downstream from Phoenix Metropolitan area and in Santa Cruz River below Nogales and Tucson.

### Biological Alterations

**Grazing**: Biological effects mostly at watershed scale, resulting in nutrient and organic matter loading from livestock wastes. Excessive vegetation removal alters biological composition of watershed cover, changing nutrient inputs to streams qualitatively and quantitatively. Streamside trampling and browsing reduce important shading and trophic inputs from riparian zones.

**Hunting and Fishing**: Direct hunting impacts mostly related to off-road vehicle use. Concentrated fishing with specialized methods can alter fish populations; historically, large numbers were removed using traps, spears, nets, and explosives for food and fertilizer. However, no indigenous fish population in Arizona was demonstrably destroyed by fishing, nor does today's recreational fishing have apparent direct impact on indigenous populations. Indirect influences of stocking non-indigenous fishes and organisms for recreation are significant. Measures have been taken to suppress or eliminate native fish fauna to ensure successful colonization of introduced sportfishes. Purposeful poisoning of "trash" fish populations to prepare streams and lakes for desirable sport fishes (mainly trouts) resulted in substantial losses to indigenous populations—some temporary with recolonization, others permanent. Ichthyocides are now used with variable results to renovate contaminated streams for reintroducing [[Apache Trout]] and other indigenous species.

**Illegal Collecting**: No documented instance where illegal or legal collecting caused extirpation of a stream-inhabiting Arizona species.

**Species Introductions**: Massive human disturbance and non-indigenous species introduction/establishment were essentially coincidental in the American West, making separation of impacts difficult. The lowermost [[Colorado River]] downstream from [[Parker Dam]] exemplifies the problem—only two native fishes remain (marine species entering from the Sea of Cortez); otherwise this reach has the dubious distinction of being the only major North American watercourse populated by an entirely non-native fish fauna.

Non-indigenous species appearances are attributable to intentional introductions, unintentional introductions, or unknown sources. Organisms were stocked for food ([[Common Carp]], buffalofishes), sportfishing (basses, catfishes, perches, pikes, trouts, [[Bullfrog]]s), as forage or bait for sportfishes (scuds, [[Opossum Shrimp]], crayfishes, shad, shiners and other minnows, [[Tiger Salamander]]s), and for biological control of aquatic weeds and insect pests (tilapia, [[Grass Carp]], [[Mosquitofish]]). They appeared as unintentional contaminants with other organisms (e.g., [[Silver Carp]]), when discarded from home aquaria ([[Sailfin Molly]], [[Guppy]], [[Walking Catfish]], exotic aquatic plants, snails), as unused bait and aquaculture escapees (some shiners and minnows, African mouth-brooders), or from unknown sources ([[Asiatic Clam]] clogging irrigation canals and conduits; Asian snail feeding on fish eggs and sessile organisms). Some organisms like [[Zebra Mussel]], spreading rapidly in eastern and central United States, can be transported in any moist container. Fishes and organisms stocked in adjacent states have ready access through interstate waters; from there they can disperse statewide through artificial interbasin connections like CAP.

How indigenous fishes are replaced by non-indigenous species remains debated. One school supports habitat disruption enhancing suitability for non-indigenous species while reducing it for indigenous kinds. The other advocates direct predation, competition, or other negative interactions. Ultimate extirpation causes likely result from factor combinations. Second to habitat loss and disruption (or perhaps first now that most dam sites are used and water resource development may be stabilizing), introduced organisms are deemed by some as the most important current danger to Arizona's indigenous aquatic biota. Unlike physical/chemical ecosystem changes, which can often be corrected or ameliorated, naturalized non-indigenous species are difficult to control and essentially impossible to eradicate. Typically, resistance, resilience, and restorability of ecosystems affected by biological pollutants are minimal except on local scales at substantial expense.

## Key Findings

### Native Fish Fauna Status

- **24 of 31 taxa** (77%) comprising the original native fish fauna are considered imperiled and disappearing
- **Six taxa** have been extirpated from the state (some reintroduced)
- **One species** is extinct
- **17 taxa** (55%) are federally listed as threatened or endangered
- **12 taxa** (39%) are candidates for federal listing
- **23 taxa** (74%) included by AZGFD as threatened, endangered, or candidates for listing

### Stream Extent and Baseline

- Arizona contains **~240,300 stream-km** total
- **~173,000 km** are on non-tribal lands
- Only **~8,000 km** support perennial flow (used as environmental baseline)
- Remainder are ephemeral or intermittent, naturally or due to human intervention
- Excluding intermittent and ephemeral streams excludes myriad aquatic communities from coverage
- Including formerly permanent reaches dewatered by damming, diversions, and groundwater pumping, or inundated by reservoirs, would dramatically increase impact estimates

**Channelization**: Estimated to affect **~40% of existing perennial stream kilometers**. Magnitude of negative short-term and long-term impacts is high; stream communities have low to moderate resistance or resilience.
