# Decline of the Lake Victoria Cichlid Species Flock

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a December 1986 action report from the [[Leiden University]] Research-Group Ecological Morphology, with appended press cuttings and correspondence. It documents the collapse of the [[Haplochromine Cichlids]] species flock of [[Lake Victoria]] after the introduction of the [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) in 1960. The authors state that roughly 300 closely related cichlid species, once the dominant fish fauna, face near-extinction. They propose four action programmes (wild-life conservation, research on remaining stocks, multi-purpose specimen collections, and a review publication) and request international support, because the work exceeds the group's financial and personnel capacity. The report also summarises international reaction, including a 1985 resolution from the [[Fifth Congress of European Ichthyologists]] and support from nearly 200 scientists.

## Background & Description

**The predator.** The Nile Perch is a large carnivorous fish. Sources in the document give maximum sizes of about 1.8 m and 120 to 150 kg, with other reports citing 100 kg or more and individuals reaching 200 kg or more. It is not native to Lake Victoria; it was stocked in ponds near the lake in the 1950s, and a 1960 introduction into the northern lake followed. Early stocking was contested; scientists and local observers warned against it before the release.

**The prey.** Lake Victoria, roughly twice the size of the Netherlands and about 750,000 years old, holds about 300 haplochromine species of the family Cichlidae, most described only recently. Each species has a restricted range and ecological specialisation, including diet (algae, snails, plankton, detritus, fish) and habitat (rocky shores, sand, papyrus). The authors describe the flock as an exceptional model for evolutionary study and as an "incipient phase of vertebrate evolution."

**The human context.** Haplochromines formed small-scale fisheries that supported poorer, largely women-processed trade (sun-drying). Nile Perch is too oily to sun-dry, so it must be smoked, which raises firewood demand and deforestation. Its size requires stronger, costlier nets, which shifted fishing toward wealthier operators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Documented decline.**
- In Kenyan waters, pre-Nile Perch trawls caught "several hundred thousand" haplochromines per hour; only two were caught with similar effort in 1984, with the catch mainly Nile Perch.
- Haplochromines had disappeared from the Kenyan Kavirondo Gulf by about 1979, and by the report's writing were absent from the northern lake.
- In the Mwanza Gulf (southern lake), Nile Perch rose exponentially from early 1984. A 1985 HEST trawl survey in deeper water caught almost no fish other than Nile Perch.
- Press reports cite a two-thirds fall in fishers' catches.

**Trophic and health effects.** Some haplochromines are snail-eaters. The report notes that certain snails carry the parasite behind [[Bilharzia]] (schistosomiasis), and that losing these predators may increase snail numbers and disease risk. The report also warns of cascading effects on algae, plankton, insect larvae, and prawns, and of cannibalism by Nile Perch as prey runs out. Press articles speak of possible eutrophication and oxygen depletion as the lake loses organisms that process algae and detritus.

**Scale of the problem.** The report estimates the flock was at risk of most species disappearing "within a few years," and that without faster research many species would vanish before being described.

**Contested views.** A Tanzanian fisheries scientist, Francis Chale, argues in a later article that available catch data show no clear link between Nile Perch and declines in tilapia or other species, and that fishing pressure (synthetic gill nets, smaller mesh, trawlers, beach seines) better explains tilapia losses. Other researchers reported bacterial infection (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) and low oxygen as causes of fish die-offs. These views were not reconciled in the document.

## Key Findings

**1. Species loss and urgency.** The authors judge that most of the approximately 300 haplochromine species will be lost, and that without extensive research the true diversity will not be documented. Their stated priorities are immediate action and research funding.

**2. Four action programmes proposed:**
1. **Wild-life conservation:** culture haplochromines in ponds around the lake (also for snail control and ecological experiments) and breed them in aquaria, with help from hobby aquarists and scientific institutes. Damming bays was considered too costly.
2. **Research on the remaining stock:** treat the introduction as a "large-scale natural experiment," studying changes in surviving species' ecology and morphology.
3. **Multi-purpose specimen collections:** preserve specimens and collection data from normal fishery catches, since only a fraction of species can be kept alive.
4. **Review publication:** publish a book on the biology and taxonomy of the flock, since much recent data remains unpublished.

**3. Management measures discussed in press and meetings.** A late-January 1986 meeting in Leiden (with the EC, FAO, Tanzanian and Kenyan fisheries directors) agreed to:
- Use selective [[Gill Nets]] with 15 to 25 cm mesh to catch large Nile Perch while letting haplochromines escape.
- Supply strong nets to local fishers, with Dutch funding (reported as 2.4 to 2.5 million guilders, or "several million" in other reports).
- Upgrade fisheries institutes and train local staff.

**4. International support.** The 1985 [[Fifth Congress of European Ichthyologists]] (Stockholm, 12 to 16 August 1985) adopted a resolution, and nearly 200 scientists from many countries endorsed it. The resolution's five sub-resolutions recommended:
1. Placing threatened species in gene banks ([[Cryopreservation]] and aquaria, though cryopreservation was judged not yet feasible).
2. Intensive selective fishing of Nile Perch, plus research on biological control.
3. Reintroducing held species once Nile Perch stocks fall.
4. Greatly increased research funding and personnel.
5. Comprehensive [[Museum Collections]] as permanent records.

Supporters broadly opposed Nile Perch introduction, but considered reintroduction of captive-bred cichlids unrealistic.

**5. Socioeconomic impacts.** Impacts include loss of an ornamental-fish export trade, a shift to wealthier operators, deforestation from smoking, and food insecurity for Tanzania, Kenya and Uganda. The report estimates about 50,000 fishers around the lake.

**6. Wider lessons and policy.** Introductions were flagged as a global risk: [[Lake Malawi]] and [[Lake Kivu]] were named as possible targets, and Australia reportedly dropped plans to stock Nile Perch for sport fishing. The [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]] wrote to governments warning that new introductions "will almost certainly mean more regrets than benefits." Its press release also backed transferring other threatened species to aquaria. The [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] was asked to focus on rational exploitation of indigenous stocks, efficient distribution of catches, and reduction of post-harvest losses. The FAO publicly denied that it had sanctioned the introduction. Press coverage also included commercial proposals to import Nile Perch fillets to Europe, which conservationists criticised.

**7. Remaining gaps.** Authors acknowledged that nobody knows the current biomass of haplochromines, whether stocks are overfished, or how environmental variation has interacted with the predator. Key biological information on Nile Perch (breeding, feeding) was limited.
