# Native Fishes at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge and Arizona State University Research Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Paul C. Marsh (Centre for Environmental Studies and Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]]) reports the stocking of endangered [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] into artificial ponds at two sites in Arizona: the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (grow-out) and the [[ASU Research Park]] (co-occurrence with non-native fishes). Both native species grew exceptionally well at both sites. [[Colorado Squawfish]] was stocked but was not recaptured at ASU, probably because of gear selectivity. The study concludes that stock tanks are an effective grow-out tool and that the ASU ponds also raised public awareness of endangered fishes. An English and Spanish bilingual version is included.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), all endemic to the Colorado River system of southwestern North America and all listed as endangered or threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and several western states.
- **Status:** Bonytail was described as possibly the most critically endangered fish in the United States. Its recovery plan calls for placing all wild individuals collected into refugia. Razorback Sucker adults remain abundant in [[Lake Mohave]] but show no recruitment there. Colorado Squawfish has been extirpated from the lower Colorado basin for decades.
- **Threats:** Habitat alteration and destruction, and interactions with non-native fishes, including predation by non-native [[Ictalurid Catfishes]].
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies found that stocked razorback suckers disperse downstream, mostly at night. Predation on juveniles was a significant mortality source. Marsh and Brooks recommended captive-rearing razorbacks to about two years of age before planting.
- **Hatchery context:** Production at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico was limited by grow-out space, prompting the search for additional rearing ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge (NWR)**
- About 40,500 ha of grassland and rocky desert mountains in southern Arizona, within the Altar Valley (Santa Cruz River system, Gila River drainage).
- Aquatic habitats are mainly artificial stock tanks ("tanks") that depend on rainfall. Arivaca Creek is the only perennial stream.
- Tanks range in size from small to several hectares. Most are shallow, mud-bottomed, and inundated for part of the year; some hold water year-round.
- Preliminary surveys in 1985–1986 found non-native fishes (including Red Shiner and tilapia) in some tanks, but no native fish. Bullfrogs were also noted.

**Arizona State University Research Park (Tempe)**
- Three constructed lakes (North, Middle, and South) filled with Salt River Project canal water, interconnected by pipes.
- Lakes initially held few fishes, but by 1986 tilapia had reproduced to an estimated 1,000 or more individuals. Red Shiner, Green Sunfish, Bluegill, and mosquitofish were also present, mostly invaders from SRP canals.
- In 1987, a die-off of tilapia occurred during a cold period, and later in the year hundreds of dead tilapia were found.

## Key Findings

**Stocking**
- Native fishes were stocked as juveniles in October–November 1986. At ASU, 1,000 razorback suckers (80–200 mm TL), 877 bonytail (76 mm), and about 26 mm Colorado Squawfish juveniles (5 October 1986) were released.
- At Buenos Aires, bonytail and razorback suckers were planted into King's, Big Bertha, Road Camp, Rock, and Tequila tanks, and Mormon Lake received a large fry stocking (about 40,000, ca. 10 mm).

**Growth and survival at Buenos Aires NWR**
- Razorback suckers taken in early 1987 averaged 166 mm TL and 65 g; later samples (August–September) reached 270–311 mm and 200–400 g (mean 378 g).
- Bonytail sampled in early 1987 averaged 130 mm TL and 21 g; later fish reached 280–300 mm range by autumn 1987.
- Largest fish reached 370 mm (bonytail) and 426 mm (razorback sucker) by autumn 1987.
- Fish were actively feeding at dusk; bonytail showed a rosy-red coloration at the base of the anal and paired fins, reminiscent of breeding coloration in adults.

**Growth and co-occurrence at ASU Research Park**
- Razorback suckers in April 1987 averaged 220 g; in September 1987 they measured 305–426 mm TL (mean 363 mm, n = 60) and averaged 478 g (n = 12).
- Bonytail in September 1987 averaged 281 mm TL (n = 9, range 330–370 mm per the reported range) and 155 g (n = 6).
- Razorback suckers made up 102% of the trammel-net catch in April (CPUE 7.2–9.2 fish per 100 m) and about 80% in September (CPUE 79.2 fish).
- Fish showed no external parasites and no evidence of poor health. Bonytail survival could not be reliably interpreted due to sampling bias.
- A total of 19 species was recorded by April 1987; only four (Bonytail, Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, and Redbelly Tilapia) were intentionally stocked.
- In September 1987 trammel nets collected 11 species, including Yellow Bass, White Crappie-like Black Crappie, and Largemouth Bass (not previously known from that system since 1967).

**Colorado Squawfish**
- No squawfish were captured at ASU. Fish were likely too large to enter seines and too small to be entangled in trammel nets. Electroshocking may be needed.

**Conclusions**
- Stock tanks at Buenos Aires NWR are suitable for growing native fish to substantial size before release into open waters.
- Non-native fish density did not appear to affect growth of natives at ASU, though the exotics were largely absent at the time of initial stocking, giving natives a head start.
- Exotic fishes may still reduce recruitment through larval predation, a question for future controlled study.
- Fish reached sizes in less than a year that should greatly improve survival after reintroduction into historical habitat.
- The ASU ponds enhanced public awareness through media coverage, public telephone inquiries, and interpretive signs.
