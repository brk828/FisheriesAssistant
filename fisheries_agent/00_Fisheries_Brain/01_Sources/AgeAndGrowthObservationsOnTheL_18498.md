# Age and Growth Observations on the Largemouth Bass, Micropterus salmoides (Lacepede) in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #reservoir-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis by C. O. Minckley (University of Nevada, Las Vegas, August 1972) presents a comprehensive age and growth study of [[Largemouth Bass]] populations in [[Lake Mead]], Nevada-Arizona. The research analyzed 876 fish collected from three locations (Las Vegas Bay, Temple Bar, and Boulder Beach) between 1959 and 1971, using scale analysis to determine age structure, growth rates, condition factors, and length-weight relationships. A major focus was comparing growth patterns before and after the 1954 introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] (Dorosoma petenense) as a forage base. The study was partially supported by the Nevada Department of Fish and Game through a grant to Dr. J. E. Deacon investigating effluent effects on largemouth bass in Las Vegas Bay.

## Background & Description

[[Largemouth Bass]] were introduced to [[Lake Mead]] between 1935 and 1942 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and became the dominant sport fish in the reservoir. The species provides the majority of angler catch and faces increasing exploitation pressure. Prior to 1954, largemouth bass fed primarily on [[Black Crappie]]; after December 1954, [[Threadfin Shad]] became available as forage. The study employed standard ichthyological aging techniques using scales collected from the left side of fish above the lateral line and below the dorsal fin insertion. Scale impressions were made on cellulose acetate slides and examined using an Eberbach scale reader at 32X magnification. Fish were collected primarily by electrofishing at night using a portable generator mounted on a 20-foot boat, supplemented by creel census samples from the Nevada Department of Fish and Game.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mead]] is a large Colorado River reservoir exhibiting considerable environmental variability. Mean monthly surface water temperatures at the intake tower ranged from approximately 7.2°C to 29.0°C across the study period, with temperatures frequently remaining above the critical 15.6°C threshold for largemouth bass growth for 8-10 months per year. Water levels fluctuated dramatically, ranging from lows near 1084 feet (1956) to highs exceeding 1205 feet (1958) at Hoover Dam. The 1952 water year was particularly notable, with levels rising from March through July to exceptionally high levels, coinciding with optimal growth for young-of-the-year bass. Largemouth bass in Las Vegas Bay shifted diet seasonally, feeding primarily on chironomids during winter and threadfin shad during spring, summer, and fall—their period of maximum growth. Parasitism incidence in Lake Mead fish was reported as low. The reservoir supports multiple game fish species and experiences variable environmental conditions that influence fish growth through complex interactions of temperature, water level, and food availability.

## Key Findings

**Annulus Formation and Timing:**
- Initial annulus formation occurred between January and May, varying by year and location
- Primary annulus formation occurred in February 1959 (Temple Bar) and March 1963 (Boulder Beach)
- By May 1971, most Las Vegas Bay bass had not yet formed annuli, suggesting later formation that year
- All age group 0 fish formed an annulus during their first year of life
- Annulus formation appears related to interactions between temperature, food availability (particularly the shift from chironomids to threadfin shad), and possibly spawning stress

**Body Length-Scale Relationship:**
- The relationship was linear across all locations with high correlation coefficients: Temple Bar (r=0.83), Boulder Beach (r=0.83), Las Vegas Bay (r=0.91)
- Regression lines differed significantly between locations (p<0.05, df=7)
- The Lee-Fraser method was appropriate for back-calculating length at annulus formation

**Age Structure and Growth Rates:**
- Three year classes were represented in all samples; the oldest fish reported was 8 years old
- Mean calculated total lengths at first annulus: Temple Bar 247 mm, Boulder Beach 223 mm, Las Vegas Bay 243 mm (overall mean 238 mm)
- Age group I mean lengths: Temple Bar 315 mm, Boulder Beach 284 mm, Las Vegas Bay 338 mm (overall mean 312 mm)
- Age group II mean lengths: Temple Bar 348 mm, Boulder Beach 374 mm, Las Vegas Bay 372 mm (overall mean 364 mm)
- First-year growth increments averaged 238 mm, second-year 67 mm, third-year 58 mm

**Impact of Threadfin Shad Introduction (1954):**
- **Dramatic increase in first-year growth:** Age group 0 fish averaged 105 mm longer after shad introduction (240 mm post-1954 vs. 135 mm pre-1954)
- **Reduced growth in older fish:** Age group I growth increments declined from 126 mm (pre-1954) to 73 mm (post-1954); Age group II from 86 mm to 43 mm
- By age group II, total lengths were similar regardless of shad presence, suggesting shad primarily benefit young-of-the-year bass
- Possible ecological isolation between bass and shad as both species age may explain reduced growth in older fish

**Environmental Influences on Growth:**
- **Water temperature:** Years with mean temperatures above 15°C in March generally showed better growth pre-shad introduction; post-shad, temperature relationship was less clear
- **Water level fluctuations:** The exceptional growth year of 1952 (pre-shad) coincided with rising water levels from March to July, reaching record highs; recently inundated shoreline vegetation likely increased forage availability
- **1956 and 1969** showed best post-shad growth for age group 0; 1956 had extremely low but rising water levels through summer
- High April temperatures correlated with good first-year growth (1952 and 1949 had highest April means)

**Length-Weight Relationships:**
- Formulas varied by location:
  - Temple Bar (1959): log W = -4.5970 + 2.9957 log(L), n=2.9957 (near isometric)
  - Boulder Beach (1963): log W = -3.0068 + 2.2477 log(L), n=2.2477 (allometric, length>weight)
  - Las Vegas Bay (1971): log W = -3.9398 + 2.6208 log(L), n=2.6208 (allometric, length>weight)
- The n value typically increased with age (except Boulder Beach 1963)
- Age group 0 n values ranged 1.3200-3.6190; Age group I: 2.6870-3.2762; Age group II: 1.1808-3.7743
- Monthly variation evident, with declines in March-April attributed to spawning weight loss
- Temple Bar fish showed fastest weight increase; calculated weight at 300 mm was 666 gms (Temple Bar) vs. 364 gms (Boulder Beach) and 357 gms (Las Vegas Bay)

**Condition Factors:**
- Relative condition factors (C) were consistent across locations: Temple Bar 1.006, Boulder Beach 1.019, Las Vegas Bay 1.018
- No significant differences detected between age groups, sexes, or years
- Values fall within normal range for largemouth bass (literature range 0.92-1.40)
- Boulder Beach had highest mean condition factor despite lower water levels in 1963
- Lack of seasonal variation may reflect inadequate sampling of gravid females throughout the year

**Comparative Growth Performance:**
- First-year Lake Mead bass (post-shad) were larger than comparable fish from other U.S. regions
- By age group II, Lake Mead bass were similar in size to other populations despite exceptional first-year growth
- Growth comparable to: Sutherland Reservoir (CA), Lake Havasu (CA), Norris Reservoir (TN), Bull Shoals (AR)
- Sexual maturity reached by second year of life

**Population and Management Implications:**
- Average age of bass entering angling catch: 2-3 years old
- Fish up to 5 years fairly common in earlier studies; older fish (>2 annuli) puzzlingly absent in 1959 and 1963 creel samples
- No significant growth differences between sexes in Las Vegas Bay collections
- Growth differences between locations (Temple Bar, Boulder Beach, Las Vegas Bay) were not statistically significant within age classes for 1956-1971 period
- Differences between age groups were highly significant at all locations

The study concludes that threadfin shad introduction fundamentally altered largemouth bass growth dynamics in Lake Mead, providing exceptional forage for young fish but potentially creating ecological separation as both predator and prey age. Environmental factors—particularly water temperature, reservoir level fluctuations, and food availability—interact in complex ways to influence annual growth variation. The research provides essential baseline data for understanding population dynamics and managing this important sport fishery under increasing exploitation pressure.
