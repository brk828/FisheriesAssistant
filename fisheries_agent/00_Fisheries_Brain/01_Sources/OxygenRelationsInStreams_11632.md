# Oxygen Relationships in Streams: 1957 Seminar Proceedings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the published proceedings of the first national seminar on dissolved oxygen (DO) relationships in streams. The seminar was sponsored by the Water Supply and Water Pollution Program of the [[Public Health Service]] Sanitary Engineering Center, held October 30 to November 1, 1957, in Cincinnati, and issued in March 1958. It brings together state regulators, university engineers, consultants, and biologists to review how stream oxygen resources are measured, modeled, and managed for waste assimilation.

Fish are addressed directly in one central paper, "Dissolved Oxygen Requirements for Fishes" by [[Clarence M. Tarzwell]], and in discussions of salmon, trout, and warm-water species. The other papers focus on the engineering of the [[Oxygen Sag Curve]], reaeration, sludge deposits, estuarine oxygen balance, and regulatory practice. The main conclusion is that DO criteria must be based on fish habitat requirements and expressed as minimum allowable values. Engineers, however, must still balance those criteria against the stream's natural capacity to assimilate waste.

## Background & Description

The proceedings contain 4 sessions, ten formal papers with discussions, and a closing panel. The volume is dedicated to [[Harold W. Streeter]], whose 1925 work with Phelps established the mathematical basis for the oxygen sag. The seminar was intended as a forum for exchanging views, summarizing knowledge, and identifying unsolved problems. Discussants included engineers from the [[New York State Water Pollution Control Board]], the Oregon State Board of Health, and the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] in Stevenage, England.

Main contributions:

- **A. F. Dappert (New York):** use of stream data in classifying waters and approving treatment plans, including the Skaneateles Creek basin and Niagara River examples.
- **C. M. Tarzwell (Robert A. Taft Center):** oxygen requirements of fishes, based largely on [[Lytle Creek]] field work and laboratory studies.
- **H. W. Streeter and M. LeBosquet:** the oxygen sag equation and a simplified method using the reciprocal of flow.
- **D. J. O'Connor:** theoretical and empirical estimation of the [[Reaeration Coefficient]].
- **C. J. Velz:** sludge deposits and their effect on the DO profile, with field examples from the Kalamazoo, Miami, and James Rivers.
- **A. L. H. Gameson and M. J. Barrett:** oxidation, reaeration, and mixing in the [[Thames Estuary]].
- Additional papers covered mixing and diffusion (H. A. Thomas Jr.), impoundment effects (M. A. Churchill), sampling methods (P. D. Haney and J. Schmidt), waste treatment design (G. J. Schroepfer), and algae (T. F. Wisniewski).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The seminar links oxygen chemistry directly to fish survival and habitat use. Tarzwell notes that temperature is the dominant control on oxygen solubility in fresh water and that oxygen consumption rises with temperature while solubility falls. He reports that the annual temperature range of temperate streams may reach 28°C, so oxygen demand at peak temperatures may be several times that at minimum temperatures, while the water holds only about half as much oxygen.

Other ecological points raised:

- Fish oxygen needs depend on activity. Fish require two to four times more oxygen when active than when quiescent.
- Fry and young fish have higher metabolic rates than adults, and eggs in gravel need more oxygen than adults because water moves slowly through the gravel.
- Sludge deposits produce major DO sags. Velz attributes most fish kills to scour of accumulated deposits during freshets, not to low-flow accumulation. [[Tarzwell]] and [[Ingols]] agree that scour periods are critical.
- Algae raise DO through photosynthesis and can drive large diurnal swings. Tarzwell reports diurnal fluctuations of as much as 18 ppm in southwest Ohio streams.
- Pulp mill effluents in Oregon caused slime growths and odor problems while DO remained high. Bottom biological communities were altered even though oxygen was adequate.
- Upwelled ocean water on the Pacific northwest coast may carry DO of about 5 ppm, and sometimes less than 2 or 3 ppm, for days, independent of pollution.

## Key Findings

### Tarzwell: Dissolved Oxygen Criteria for Fishes

- Criteria should be based on knowledge of habitat requirements, and expressed as the lowest allowable ppm rather than averages or percent saturation.
- Survival alone is insufficient. Levels must sustain growth, reproduction, and the food supply.
- Recommended levels for warm-water fish communities:
  - Well-rounded warm-water population: not below 5 ppm for more than 8 hours of any 24-hour period, and not below 3 ppm at any time.
  - Coarse-fish population: not below 5 ppm for more than 8 hours of any 24-hour period, and not below 2 ppm at any time.
- Salmonid recommendations: DO should not be less than 6 ppm for good production. Successful egg and fry development requires at least 6 ppm, and full activity for brook trout requires 7.6 ppm at 15°C and full air saturation at 20°C and above.
- Lethal and sublethal data cited include:
  - Smallmouth bass died in 5 to 9 hours at 0.7 to 1.17 ppm (52 to 72°F, Burdick).
  - Brook trout could endure short exposure to 1.2 ppm; asphyxiation occurred at 2.5 ppm.
  - Largemouth bass oxygen consumption was 282 percent of the 15°C level at 25°C (Wiebe and Fuller).
- Avoidance tests at Oregon State showed up to 95 percent avoidance of low oxygen by some salmon, so blocks to migration are significant.

### Regulatory and Engineering Practice

- **New York:** Class D waters require removal of floating and settleable solids and at least 3 ppm DO. Dappert reports that the department applies the minimum consecutive 7-day low flow with a once-in-10-years return frequency. The department reviews about 1,000 plans per year.
- **Oregon:** the minimum DO standard is 5.0 ppm in all cases, with a possible increase to 7.0 to 8.0 ppm for fish spawning. Lower sections with no fish propagation may tolerate 3.0 ppm.
- **Cincinnati Pool example (LeBosquet):** with a design deficit of 4.38 ppm, Q of 14,000 cfs, and projected 1970 loads, the required treatment is 65 percent. The Ohio River Valley Water Sanitation Commission adopted a 65 percent BOD reduction, with a floor of 35 percent when conditions permit.
- **Skaneateles example:** a village with about 2,300 people was approved for primary treatment, with forewarning that more treatment may be needed.

### Streeter and Oxygen Sag Modeling

- The oxygen sag is the net result of deoxygenation (proportional to unoxidized substance) and reaeration (proportional to oxygen deficiency).
- Typical deoxygenation rate: k1 approximately 0.1 at 20°C, with a temperature coefficient of 1.047 to 1.065.
- Surface-active agents, oils, and detergents can reduce reaeration. Vegetable oil and gasoline, mineral oil, mixed fatty acids, and soap reduced rates by up to 60 percent. Sewage mixtures at 4 to 24 percent reduced rates by 35 to 55 percent.
- **Simplified method (LeBosquet and Tsivoglou):** when no initial deficit exists, the critical deficit in pounds is roughly constant for a given load, so a plot of deficit against 1/Q is linear. Correlation coefficients of 0.90 and above were reported.
- Example figures (Connecticut River): projected DO falls from 4.9 ppm in 1929 to 3.26 ppm in 1970, with population rising from 750,000 to 1,540,000.

### O'Connor: Reaeration Coefficient

- Equation-based estimates were compared with observed values for the Clarion, Tennessee, Ohio, and other rivers. Calculated values agreed closely with observed ones. For example, the Clarion River observed 4.8 against calculated 5.4 and 5.8.
- Depth variability matters. Wabash River depths had a coefficient of variation of 0.38 across 147 samples. Clarion depths had a coefficient of variation of 0.13 across 17 samples.
- Surface-active substances create a barrier to oxygen diffusion. Waste oxygen-saturation differences of about 0.5 ppm can significantly affect the calculated coefficient.
- A laboratory oscillating-grid method and a magnetic-stirrer oxygen-uptake method were suggested for field determinations.

### Velz: Sludge Deposits

- Channel cross-sections every 500 feet, combined with time-of-passage curves, identify where velocities fall below 0.6 ft/s (deposition) or reach 1.0 to 1.5 ft/s (scour).
- At equilibrium, accumulated BOD in a deposit reaches about 14.5 times the daily addition. Streeter's rate constant k' of 0.03 at 25°C corresponds to about 7 percent per day.
- Kalamazoo River: an unexpected DO sag at mile 72 was traced to former dredging that left the channel filled with sludge.
- Miami River: DO profiles differed sharply between 8 days and prolonged accumulation at similar runoff.
- Upper James River: a short accumulation period (about 10 days) and a prolonged one (over 50 days) produced very different DO profiles, with computed results matching observed values.

### Gameson and Barrett: Thames Estuary

- The Thames Estuary survey ran from 1949 to 1954 and covered a reach of about 60 miles. Middle-reach waters became anaerobic under normal summer flow and temperature.
- The exchange coefficient f is preferred over the absorption coefficient K because it accounts for depth.
- Weir reaeration can be as great as that between weirs a mile or two apart. A 6.5-foot weir with an upstream deficiency of 8 ppm adds about 4 ppm.

### Other Notable Points

- Dappert noted the Niagara River flows about 230,000 cfs, and that slugs of pollution traveled as far as Kingston, Ontario, after a 1933 gastroenteritis outbreak.
- Pacific northwest salmon and steelhead fisheries were estimated by Perry at $17,500,000 annually for the Columbia River, at wholesale value.
- Perry advocated 7 ppm as a general minimum for salmon and trout spawning, while Pearson questioned whether the data supported that value for all waters. Hatcheries can achieve about 98 percent hatch, versus 25 to 50 percent in natural redds.
- Sacramento River concern: a proposed pulp mill near a major salmon spawning area.
- The seminar agreed that DO requirements should be realistic and that a portion of the stream's oxygen resource must remain available for waste assimilation.
