# The Freshwater Fishes of New Brunswick: A Checklist with Distributional Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #checklist

## Executive Summary

This 1959 checklist by W. B. Scott and E. J. Crossman, published as [[Royal Ontario Museum]] Contributions No. 51, is the first comprehensive treatment of New Brunswick's freshwater fish fauna since [[Philip Cox]]'s 1896 catalogue. It combines the authors' May–June 1958 collections, mainly from the [[Saint John River]] and [[St. Croix River]] systems, with research-collection records from the [[Royal Ontario Museum]] and the National Museum of Canada. The annotated list records 46 species in 18 families, including anadromous and catadromous fishes. The authors also review the literature from 1672 onward, correct many nomenclatural and distributional errors, and argue that the province's fish fauna remains poorly known and needs systematic survey.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** Early workers included [[Moses H. Perley]] (1852, the first comprehensive systematic list), [[A. Leith Adams]] (1873), and Cox (1893–1924). Cox is credited with founding freshwater ichthyology in the province. Most 20th-century papers focused on introductions, fishery biology, and game species such as [[Atlantic Salmon]] and [[Brook Trout]].
- **Taxonomic corrections:** The authors identify several erroneous records. The 1874 papers attributed to Charles Lanman were word-for-word copies of Perley's work without credit. Records of [[Chain Pickerel]] were misattributed to Maine localities, and the gizzard shad record was probably a mistaken extension of range.
- **Methods:** Collections used 20-foot seines, hand seines, dip nets, gill nets, and angling. Stations (numbered 1–33 in Table 2) were sampled in 1958, and older collections (ROM 1–47, NMC 1–16) were catalogued by locality. The nomenclature follows Scott (1958).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Anadromous and catadromous species:** [[Sea Lamprey]], [[Atlantic Sturgeon]], alewife, American shad, [[American Eel]], [[Striped Bass]], and smelt use freshwater and estuaries. The American eel does not occur in the Saint John River above Grand Falls. Sea lamprey is not known in the Saint John above Grand Falls.
- **Marine species in freshwater:** Euryhaline forms such as [[Fourspine Stickleback]], [[Threespine Stickleback]], Atlantic tomcod, and mummichog enter rivers. Two fourspine sticklebacks were taken at Station 16 on the Saint John River, about 100 miles from the sea. Tomcod have been recorded at least 90 miles from the sea.
- **Lakes and cool waters:** [[Lake Trout]] occurs in headwaters of the Saint John, St. Croix, and Restigouche rivers, and in some isolated southwestern lakes, but not in the Miramichi system. [[Arctic Char]] is known from only two lakes, Walton Lake and Upsalquitch Lake.
- **Distribution limits:** [[Round Whitefish]] is restricted to parts of the Saint John and Restigouche watersheds. Several species are known only from southwestern New Brunswick. The authors describe the province's fauna as spatially patchy, with the north largely unsampled.

## Key Findings

**1. Species inventory**
- 46 species in 18 families, including five introduced species in three families: [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Chain Pickerel]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]].
- Two species were newly added to the New Brunswick list. The [[Fathead Minnow]] was a first provincial record, with two specimens (NMC cat. no. 19,705) from a flood plain near Edmundston, Madawaska County.
- Ten species previously reported were excluded as unverified or erroneous, including Atlantic herring, blueback herring, gizzard shad, creek chubsucker, longnose dace, white bass, green sunfish, sauger, yellow walleye, and mottled sculpin. The mottled sculpin specimens examined were found to be [[Slimy Sculpin]].

**2. Notable records**
- A shortnose sturgeon was captured on 29 May 1957 in the Saint John River estuary, the first valid record for the province.
- [[Brook Trout]] is probably the most widely distributed freshwater species in the province.
- [[Blacknose Dace]] is the most widespread and abundant freshwater fish, recorded from every major watershed.
- The yellowbelly sunfish has only two valid provincial records (Canaan River, 1948; Kennebecasis River, 1949), though resident populations were reported.
- Smelt are landlocked in Chamcook and Utopia lakes.

**3. Introductions**
- Chinook were planted in the Saint John River in 1881 and 1882 without establishment.
- Brown trout were introduced in 1921, mainly in the Loch Lomond system.
- Rainbow trout were introduced in 1900 to MacFadden's Lake and established in Crooked Creek.
- Smallmouth bass reached the St. Croix system from plantings in Maine beginning about 1870.

**4. Data gaps and conservation recommendations**
- Twenty-one species are insufficiently represented in collections, eight of which are anadromous. These include [[Sea Lamprey]], [[Atlantic Sturgeon]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Round Whitefish]], [[Lake Whitefish]], [[American Eel]], and [[Striped Bass]].
- Counties needing survey include Restigouche, Gloucester, Kent, Westmorland, Albert, Saint John, and the northeastern parts of Victoria, Carleton, and York.
- Locality records should be supported by preserved specimens. The authors recommend deposition in the [[Royal Ontario Museum]], which holds the largest New Brunswick collection.
- Fish collections are useful for zoogeography, systematics, fisheries science, and understanding postglacial changes.
