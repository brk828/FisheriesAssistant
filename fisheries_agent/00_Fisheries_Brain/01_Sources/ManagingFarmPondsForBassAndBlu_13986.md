# Farm Fishponds for Bass and Bluegills

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 USDA Farmers' Bulletin (No. 2094, by Verne E. Davison of the [[Soil Conservation Service]], superseding Bulletin 1983) is a practical guide to building, stocking, fertilizing and fishing farm and ranch ponds. Its central message is that a high-yielding pond depends on a sound site, a safe dam, erosion control on the watershed, a [[Bass-Bluegill Stocking Combination]], steady [[Pond Fertilization]], and active control of [[Waterweeds]]. The bulletin states that most fishpond disappointments result from management mistakes, not from poor water productivity.

## Background & Description

The bulletin presents fishponds as profitable, multi-use features that conserve soil and water. Ponds can supply food, livestock water, fire protection, swimming and boating, and fishing-permit income. The author stresses that all ponds produce some fish, but well-built and well-managed ponds produce far more.

The text is organized into sections on:
- Site selection and dam construction
- Erosion and runoff control
- Stocking
- Fertilization and weed control
- Fishing practices
- A closing table of common pond troubles and treatments

Dam and site guidance includes these points:
- **Watershed size:** The site should have a relatively small watershed that delivers little silt, but enough water to refill the pond within a year. If the inflow fills the pond in less than a month, fertilizer cannot be used economically.
- **Depth:** At least 4 feet in the South (6 feet or more is better), 8 to 10 feet in the Central States, and deeper in the North to protect fish from winter freezing.
- **Dam:** The top width should be at least 10 feet to deter [[Muskrats]] and to provide a roadway.
- **Core trench:** Cut to watertight soil along the full dam length and backfilled with dam soil, so gravel, sand or organic layers cannot allow seepage beneath the dam.
- **Spillway:** Wide spillways are needed, because flows deeper than 6 inches let usable-size fish escape. Screens are discouraged because they clog and endanger the dam.
- **Shallow edges:** Edges should be deepened to a minimum of 18 to 24 inches, with 3 feet preferred, to limit weeds and protect fish from bass predation.
- **Clearing:** Trees, brush and stumps should be removed from the pond area, with a 20- to 30-foot buffer left cleared around the edge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bulletin describes the pond as a food web driven by fertility. Fertilizer feeds microscopic algae, which support insect larvae and other invertebrates. These support bluegills, which in turn feed bass. The text gives a food-chain estimate of 4 to 5 pounds of insect larvae per pound of bluegill, and 4 to 5 pounds of little bluegills per pound of bass.

Several environmental factors are highlighted:
- **Watershed erosion:** Silt fills the pond, muddy water reduces fish production, and floodwater can flush fertilized water and usable fish out through the spillway. A vegetative cover, diversion ditches and contour farm roads are recommended.
- **Livestock:** Fencing is advised when animals muddy the pond, because it is needed for successful fertilization. Grazing can help control edge weeds.
- **Temperature:** A surface temperature of 85°F or higher indicates a warm-water pond suited to bass and bluegills. Trout do well in waters up to 75°F in spring-fed, cold-water ponds.
- **Oxygen:** Decomposing weeds during low-oxygen periods can kill fish. Superphosphate can help restore oxygen through microscopic plant growth.
- **Pesticides:** Insecticides such as DDT, toxaphene, aldrin and chlordane from nearby crops can cause fish die-offs.
- **Regional limits:** The bulletin notes that fertilization is poorly developed for cold or highly alkaline Western waters, so stocking rates there remain uncertain.

## Key Findings

**Site and construction**
- A good site needs a suitable watershed, adequate depth, impermeable soil, and a sound dam site. Local [[Soil Conservation Service]] districts assist with site selection.
- The bulletin recommends eliminating shallow water and clearing trees, brush and stumps before flooding.
- A drainpipe is required by law in many states. An overflow pipe or trickle tube controls the normal water level.

**Stocking**
- Warm-water ponds larger than 1/4 acre should be stocked with a combination of [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Experiments at the Alabama Agricultural Research Station found this combination gave the best fishing and the most pounds of fish per acre with the least trouble.
- Recommended rates per surface acre:
  - Well-fertilized ponds: 1,000 to 1,500 bluegills and 100 to 150 bass.
  - Ponds of average fertility: 500 bluegills and 50 bass.
  - Ponds below average fertility: lower rates than the average-fertility figure.
- Target ratio: about 4 pounds of bluegills to 1 pound of bass.
- Expected size at fishing start (12 to 18 months after stocking): bluegills 3 to 6 ounces; bass 12 ounces to 1 pound.
- Stocking dates: bluegills from August to January, bass the following March through June. Bluegills stocked between January and July seldom produce good fishing.
- Native fish must be eliminated before stocking. Overstocked ponds can be cleared with 3 pounds of 5-percent [[Rotenone]] per acre-foot, subject to state permits.
- Other species (crappie, catfish, carp, buffalo) gave varying results. Crappie and catfish work well in large [[Irrigation Reservoirs]] that can be drawn down and refilled, while carp, catfish and buffalo can be grown to market size in drained and restocked ponds.

**Fertilization**
- Fertilization serves three goals: increasing fish production, enabling uniform stocking rates, and preventing submerged waterweeds.
- Mineral fertilizer is preferred. A satisfactory analysis is 8-8-2 (nitrogen-phosphorus-potash). Organic fertilizers such as manure, blood meal and leaves encourage pond scum.
- Typical amounts: average ponds need 800 to 1,200 pounds per acre per year, and the least fertile need 1,200 to 1,600 pounds. Amounts vary with rainfall.
- Water clarity is monitored with a white disk on a marked stick. The pond is fertile enough when the disk disappears about 12 inches below the surface. If it is visible at 18 inches or more, fertilization is needed.
- Each application should be at least 100 pounds per surface acre; the upper limit is illegible in the scan.
- Application may be done from a boat, by hand from the bank, or through a submerged platform (one platform per 8 to 10 acres) that lets wind and waves distribute the fertilizer.
- Fertilize as soon as the pond begins to fill, during the algae growing season from early spring into fall.

**Production and yields**
- A well-fertilized pond supports 400 pounds or more of bluegills and bass per acre, yielding 150 to 200 pounds annually by fishing.
- An average pond of natural fertility supports about 150 pounds and yields only 15 to 35 pounds per acre annually.

**Waterweeds**
- The bulletin lists submerged weeds (naias, elodea, potamogetons), floating plants (water hyacinth, duckweed, water lettuce), marsh and edge plants (cattails, rushes, parrotfeather, pennywort), and filamentous algae (pond scum).
- Prevention through deep edges, high fertility, unobstructed wind action and organic matter control is described as the cheapest and most effective approach.
- Control methods include winter fertilization (200 pounds per acre of 8-8-2, repeated every 2 weeks, taking 4 to 5 months), [[Sodium Arsenite]] (hazardous, requiring expert advice), 2,4-D in diesel oil for edge spraying, cutting or pulling cattails, and copper sulfate at 2 to 4 pounds per acre-foot for filamentous algae.

**Fishing and size control**
- A pond's fish weight reaches its limit by the second year after stocking. Removed weight is replaced by growth, so harvest equals production.
- Usable sizes are 2-ounce bluegills and 4-ounce bass (11-ounce bluegills are preferred). Intermediate-size bluegills (2 inches to about 1 1/4 ounces) are of little use to bass or anglers.
- Good fishing needs 70 percent or more of total poundage in usable fish. When intermediate bluegills exceed 25 percent of poundage, fishing is usually poor.
- If fewer than 1 pound of bass exists per 6 to 7 pounds of bluegills, the bulletin recommends seining out intermediate bluegills with a 50- or 60-foot, half-inch mesh seine and restocking bass.
- Ponds should not be fished until bass have reproduced successfully, confirmed by catching young bass in a minnow seine.
- Bait and tackle: bass respond to minnows, crawfish, spring lizards, flies, plugs and spoons. Bluegills respond to worms and crickets on the bottom and artificial flies near the surface. Hook sizes are 8 to 12 for bluegills and 6/0 to 2 for bass.

**Troubles and treatments (summary)**
- Wild fish: kill all fish with rotenone before stocking.
- Submerged weeds: destroy before improving fish populations.
- Bass failure in year one: restock with 100 to 150 fingerlings.
- Too many small bass: remove all caught, and stimulate bluegill spawning with more fishing and fertilizer.
- Die-off: if more than half the poundage is lost, kill the remaining fish and restock.
