# Arizona Trout Recovery Plan (1979)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 recovery plan, prepared by the Arizona Trout Recovery Team for the threatened [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache* Miller, 1972), sets out the species' taxonomy, life history, distribution, land ownership, conservation history, limiting factors, and a set of recovery actions with a four-year funding estimate. The stated ultimate objective is delisting once 30 self-sustaining discrete populations of pure Arizona trout are established within the historic range. The plan is mainly a coordination document: on-ground implementation rests with land managers. Its estimated total budget is $352,600 for FY 1980–1983. Agency comments, included in the appendices, raised concerns about undefined objectives, cost basis, grazing and buffer-zone standards, and the genetic purity of stocked fish.

## Background & Description

- **Classification:** Order Salmoniformes, family [[Salmonidae]], genus *Salmo*, species *S. apache*.
- **Diagnostic traits:** Deep, compressed body; large dorsal fin; pronounced, roundish, fairly uniformly spaced spots; yellow-olive ground colour with purple and pink tints on fresh fish; no red or pink lateral band; cream or yellow fin tips; yellow cutthroat mark.
- **Meristics:** 58–61 vertebrae; 21–41 pyloric caeca; 133–172 lateral-line scales; 32–40 scales above the lateral line; 56 diploid chromosomes (106 arms).
- **Hybridization indicators:** mean vertebrae above 60, mean lateral scales below 150, fewer than 34 scales above the lateral line, mean pyloric caeca above 32, and erratic spotting or colouration.
- **Taxonomic history:** Native trout were known from the White Mountains from 1873, but were not described as a distinct species until 1972. Earlier authors variously placed them in *S. pleuriticus*, *S. mykiss pleuriticus* and [[Gila Trout]] (*S. gilae*).
- **Genetic purity:** Comprehensive data on genetic purity are generally lacking. The main threat is hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*), and isolated races may also exist.
- **Size:** Present stream populations seldom exceed 25 cm total length. The world angling record is 56 cm and 1.64 kg, from Bear Canyon Lake (1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Habitat information is limited and largely inferred from other salmonids. Surviving populations occupy headwater areas above natural barriers. Streams are harsh in winter, with anchor ice and ice bridges, and are subject to extremes of flow and diurnal temperature. They generally have low pool-riffle ratios, and widths greatly exceed depths.

**Feeding.** In [[Big Bonita Creek]], fish 6–9 cm fed mainly on Ephemeroptera, while fish of 15 cm and above took more Trichoptera. Terrestrial insects were eaten by all size classes. Diet shifted seasonally.

**Reproduction.** Spawning occurs from March to mid-June, varying with elevation. Redd construction starts as water temperatures reach 80 (as printed in the source; likely a units error for °F). Fecundity records vary widely: 72–238 eggs in Big Bonita Creek fish (13.1–19.1 cm), 646–1083 eggs in Christmas Tree Lake fish (29.84–34.92 cm), and average egg counts of 72 (1964) and 4215 (1969) per female for the Ord Creek brood stock. Smallest mature females were 13.0 cm and males 14.5 cm, corresponding to a spawning age of about three years. Egg-to-fry emergence took 60 days in Big Bonita Creek.

**Abundance.** The largest concentrations are in the headwaters of the White and Black rivers on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. Larger streams may hold several thousand fish. Post-winter numbers in small tributaries may be under 100, with autumn numbers usually four to five times higher. Intermittent tributaries serve as spawning and nursery areas but rarely hold year-round residents.

**Competition and predation.** Competition with introduced salmonids is the main cause of decline. Introduced fish outcompete Arizona trout for food and space and prey on them. Hybridization with rainbow and cutthroat trout further contaminates the gene pool. Rainbow trout are identified as the major limiting factor. Brown and brook trout have a lesser competitive effect.

**Historic and present distribution.** The species historically occupied the headwaters of the Little Colorado, Salt and San Francisco rivers. Its original range was estimated at about 965 km (600 mi). Genetically pure populations now occupy about 48 km (30 mi) or less of small streams.

**Land ownership.** All known native and introduced populations are on [[National Forests]] or the Fort Apache Indian Reservation.

## Key Findings

**Conservation history**
- The [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]] first protected the species in the late 1940s and early 1950s, and closed sport fishing on Mount Baldy streams on 24 March 1955.
- Pure Ord Creek stock was collected in 1962 and propagated at the Sterling Springs Hatchery near Flagstaff. Stocking ran from 1965 to 1974.
- The species was recommended for the Secretary of the Interior's list in 1964 and listed as endangered by the IUCN in 1969.
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) brought it under federal protection. It was downlisted to threatened in 1975.

**Known populations**
- Natural pure populations occur in six streams on the reservation and national forests, including Boggy, Crooked, South Fork Diamond, East Fork White River, Centerfire and Soldier creeks.
- A further eight streams are listed as likely pure, including Firebox, Little Diamond, Big Bonita Cienega, Little Bonita, Flash, Paddy, and Stinky creeks.
- Three hybridized populations were identified: Deep Creek, North Fork Diamond Creek and Paradise Creek.
- Table 1 lists about 30 waters where the species has been introduced, including streams and lakes on the Coronado, Kaibab and Apache-Sitgreaves national forests. Lake populations are not expected to reproduce.

**Recovery objective and actions**
- The ultimate objective is to establish and maintain 30 self-sustaining discrete populations of pure Arizona trout. Delisting follows once this is achieved.
- **1.1** Survey known pure waters at least every three years and maintain barriers.
- **1.2** Survey candidate waters, build barriers where needed, remove competing salmonids, and introduce fish from contiguous pure streams where possible.
- **1.3** Maintain a hatchery brood stock as a refugium, a source for reintroduction, and a sport fishery supply. Prevent domestication, inbreeding and hybridization.
- **1.4** Review the literature, study life history, and develop field methods for identifying pure fish.
- **2.0** Survey non-historic populations, and stop expansion beyond the Pinaleno Mountains and North Kaibab Plateau until historic waters are re-established.
- **3.0** Protect habitat: manage grazing, logging, buffer strips (100 ft on level ground, plus 4 ft per 1% slope increase), mining, chemicals, instream flow, fire, recreation and roads.
- **4.0** Improve enforcement of Federal, State and Tribal laws.
- **5.0** Run a public information and education campaign.

**Funding**
- Estimated total of $352,600 for FY 1980–1983: $94,400 (1980), $111,700 (1981), $72,400 (1982), $73,100 (1983).
- The largest share is Arizona Game and Fish Department, Fish and Wildlife Service and Tribal work on surveys, enhancement and hatchery activity. Enforcement (4.0) is the largest single line item at $15,000 per year from the Tribe.

**Agency and public comments**
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department praised the plan but questioned the budget basis and when delisting would occur. The Department of the Interior's Bureau of Indian Affairs asked for Tribal Council approval before implementation.
- The Forest Service wanted clearer, measurable objectives and questioned the plan's prescriptive buffer-zone and fire-retardant language.
- [[Robert R. Miller]] of the University of Michigan warned that stocking hybrid fish would defeat the plan's purpose, and noted the Christmas Tree Lake stock was questioned as pure.
- The Arizona State University reviewer recommended formal documentation of all stockings of Arizona trout and rainbow trout within its range.
- The Plan's authors responded that land managers would implement site-specific measures and that the species would be recommended for delisting when 30 self-sustaining populations exist.
