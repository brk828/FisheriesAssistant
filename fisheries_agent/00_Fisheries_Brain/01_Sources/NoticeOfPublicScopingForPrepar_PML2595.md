# Proposed Fish Barrier in Redfield Canyon: Public Scoping Comments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes two public scoping letters submitted in November 2010 on the proposed construction of a [[Fish Barrier]] in [[Redfield Canyon]], Graham County, Arizona. The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] is preparing an [[Environmental Assessment]] (EA) for the project. The first letter, from fish biologist [[Sally E. Stefferud]], strongly supports the barrier as a means of protecting native fishes from [[Nonnative Fish Invasion]]. The second, from the law firm Brown & Brown on behalf of the owners of the [[Bayless & Berkalew Ranch]], asks the EA to analyze downstream [[Water Rights]], [[Groundwater Wells]], [[Turbidity]] and water quality impacts, and effects on [[Livestock Grazing]].

## Background & Description

**Project.** The proposed structure is a fish barrier intended to block upstream movement of nonnative fishes into Redfield Canyon. The letters refer to construction in the work area using a helicopter and sling load bucket, and to possible chemical treatment to remove unwanted plants and aquatic life before construction. No design dimensions are given in the text.

**Stefferud letter (November 8, 2010).** Sally E. Stefferud, of Phoenix, Arizona, writes that she has more than 25 years of experience in conserving native fishes of the American desert southwest. She has taken part in installing barriers against nonnative fish and in controlling or removing established nonnative fish.

**Brown & Brown letter (November 4, 2010).** The firm, based in St. Johns, Arizona, represents Andy and Stefanie Smallhouse, who own and operate the historic Bayless & Berkalew Ranch on the San Pedro River at the Redfield Canyon confluence. The ranch holds private land and leases state land under Arizona State Land Department Grazing Lease No. 05-83. The firm asks to be added to the project notice list and to review the draft EA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish community.** Redfield Canyon supports five native fish species. It is also designated high-priority habitat for the repatriation of four additional native fishes.
- **Conservation status.** All four repatriated species and one of the extant species are federally listed as endangered or threatened.
- **Intermittent flow.** Downstream reaches have intermittent flow. Stefferud argues that periodic dryness is only a temporary barrier to upstream movement of nonnative fish, citing many desert southwest examples where nonnatives moved through dry reaches and harmed native fish.
- **Invasion source.** Nonnative fishes already occur downstream in the [[San Pedro River]]. Stefferud also warns of novel nonnative species that could invade in the future.
- **Groundwater connection.** The Brown & Brown letter states that a possible direct link exists between Redfield Canyon and the quantity and quality of groundwater in wells downstream of the barrier.

## Key Findings

1. **Strong conservation rationale.** Stefferud states that the barrier is needed to remove the risk of adverse impacts to extant native fishes from nonnative invasion. It would also protect the time and resources already invested in [[Native Fish Repatriation]].

2. **Intermittent reaches are not a sufficient barrier.** Existing information, as summarized by Stefferud, indicates that dry downstream reaches delay but do not prevent nonnative fish movement upstream.

3. **Water rights analysis requested.** The client asks the EA to assess legal and practical effects on downstream water rights, including groundwater wells and surface water rights on deeded and public lands. The firm says the scoping maps were scaled down too much to show subsurface areas that could be affected.

4. **Subsurface impact mapping.** The EA should present the subsurface areas more clearly and explain how water flows will change because of the barrier.

5. **Turbidity during construction.** Sediment, clay and silt may be stirred up during construction and cloud water used downstream. The letter asks the EA to analyze this risk.

6. **Chemical treatment and water quality.** The letter asks whether chemicals will be used to remove plants and aquatic life before construction, and asks the EA to address resulting water quality effects.

7. **Long-term water quality.** The EA should evaluate the completed project's effects on turbidity and water quality.

8. **Grazing and livestock access.** The client asks to take part in studies of how construction and operation affect seasonal pasture grazing and livestock movement and access to water.

9. **Stakeholder participation.** Both writers ask to be kept informed and to review the EA when it is released.
