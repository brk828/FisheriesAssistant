# Population Analysis of the Tropical Freshwater Shrimp *Atya scabra* (Leach)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atyidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 population study by Rezneat M. Darnell (Tulane University) examines a population of the poorly studied tropical freshwater shrimp [[Atya Scabra]] collected on 27 April 1950 from a boulder riffle of the Rio Sabinas, a headwater tributary of the [[Rio Tamesi]] drainage in southern Tamaulipas, Mexico. The study covers sex ratio, size structure, proportional growth, diet, reproduction, and mortality, and concludes that the species breeds through most of the year, that males dominate the riffle population, and that males grow larger than females. The author presents the work as a brief analysis justified by limited specimens and the scarcity of published ecological data on the species.

## Background & Description

*Atya scabra* is a tropical atyid shrimp with a wide, disjunct distribution recorded from Mexico, Central America, Panama, South America, the Antilles, West Africa, Australia, and New Caledonia (Bouvier 1925; Oliveira 1945). Prior ecological work was sparse: [[Ortmann Distribution Study|Ortmann (1894)]] addressed distribution, [[Allee and Torvik 1927]] noted pools in the Panama Canal Zone, and [[Villalobos 1943]] provided a detailed morphological study of a population in the Rio Necaxa, Vera Cruz, which serves as the main comparison in this paper.

Specimen identification was confirmed by Dr. Fenner A. Chace, Jr., of the [[U.S. National Museum]]. Field work was funded by the [[Tozer Foundation]] (University of Minnesota) and the [[Tulane University Council on Research]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study site:** Storms' ranch, 7 km northeast of Gomez Farias, about 160 km inland from the [[Gulf of Mexico]], at 100 m above sea level.
- **Habitat:** A turbulent riffle 0–1 m deep with a rock and boulder bottom. Endogenous vegetation was filamentous algae ([[Lyngbya]] and [[Oscillatoria]]), under 5% of total vegetation. Exogenous river-borne material (branches, twigs, leaves, seedpods) made up the rest, totaling 45.1 g per square meter of riffle bottom.
- **Associated fauna:** An abundant and varied gastropod and insect community was present; the author stated that it would be reported separately.
- **Seasonality:** Six discontinuous months of field work (winter, spring, early summer) yielded no shrimp outside the riffle during daylight. At night, shrimp were occasionally found on rocky bottoms in adjacent shallow backwaters near weed beds.
- **Hydrology:** The author states that seasonal delimitation in eastern Mexican headwaters is driven mainly by turbidity, volume, and flow rate rather than temperature, a point to be developed in a later paper.

## Key Findings

**Population density and sex ratio**
- 46 specimens were hand-collected between 11:00 and 12:00 am; estimated density was about 10 shrimp per m² of riffle bottom.
- 28 males (61%) and 18 females (39%) were recorded. The difference was not statistically significant (chi-square, P = .301), but it resembles the strong male bias reported by Villalobos (1943) in Vera Cruz (100 males vs. 27 females, P < .001; a second collection of about 400 showed a greater difference).
- Five small to moderate-sized females seined from shallow backwaters 20–50 feet upstream suggested that females, especially juveniles, may occupy marginal habitats, which would make the overall sex ratio more balanced.

**Size and sexual dimorphism**
- Males: mean length 62.65 mm (SD 11.29). Females (riffle plus backwater): mean 48.30 mm (SD 5.30).
- The mean male length exceeded the upper limit of riffle females. The difference was highly significant (P < .001) by T-test. Backwater females averaged about 5 mm shorter than riffle females, so habitat does not explain the size difference.
- Vera Cruz specimens were larger: average male 68.5 mm, average female 50.0 mm, maximum male 94.5 mm.

**Proportional growth**
- No direct growth-rate or population-expansion data were collected.
- Cephalothorax length regressed on total length: males Y = 1.48 + 0.37X; females Y = 2.57 + 0.31X. Females have relatively shorter cephalothoraxes and longer abdomens at larger sizes, which the author links to egg production and the ovigerous habit.

**Diet**
- Stomach contents of 25 specimens were examined. One was empty; the other 24 contained mostly unidentifiable detritus (100% of stomachs, 91.3% of total food volume) and plant remains (100%, 6.9% volume).
- About 46% of stomachs contained arthropod remains (under 1% volume), mainly cast exoskeleton fragments and some possibly live-eaten insect larvae or naiads.
- Algae came from upstream sources (*Navicula* spp., *Pinnularia* sp.), weed beds (*Spirogyra*, desmids), and the riffle (*Oscillatoria*). Common items included *Navicula* spp. (100%) and *Cosmarium* sp. (83.3%). A single protozoan, *Euglypha* sp., was found.
- The shrimp fed mainly on detritus filtering to the lower riffle bed. Abundant immature insects formed no major part of the diet.

**Reproduction**
- Of 23 females taken in late April, 4 (17%) were ovigerous. Three of these had undeveloped eggs and were probably recently spawned; the fourth had well-developed eggs with pigmented eyes.
- Mean mature egg length was 0.84 mm in the ovigerous females.
- The smallest non-ovigerous female with mature-sized eggs measured 39.0 mm, tentatively taken as the minimum size at sexual maturity.
- Egg counts ranged from 746 to 8,000 among ovigerous females (one count was a partial estimate of 2,500 for the largest). The author estimated about 60% mortality in the 50.5 mm female with eye-pigmented eggs (1,111 counted vs. about 3,000 predicted). This estimate rests on only three specimens.
- Gonad data support Villalobos' view that breeding occurs through most of the year, but seasonal peaks could not be determined.

**Mortality and predation**
- One juvenile shrimp was eaten by a 69 mm [[Cichlasoma steindachneri]], the only fish seen feeding in the riffle.
- Potential predators include [[Macrobrachium carcinus]], [[Gobiomorus dormitor]], and [[Ictalurus australis]]. These threaten shrimp in marginal habitats, especially during nighttime backwater foraging.

**Zoogeographic significance and conclusions**
- The Rio Tamesi headwater population is the northernmost recorded locality for the species.
- *A. scabra* is one of the few tropical species in this fauna that uses the boulder riffle as its primary habitat.
- The continuous breeding pattern contrasts with the seasonal breeding of temperate species. The author concludes that further seasonal studies are needed to clarify breeding intensity, minimum size at maturity, and the environmental drivers involved.
