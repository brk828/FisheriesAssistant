# Small Impoundment Fisheries Management Studies in West Virginia (Project F-3-R-3)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Job Completion Report from the [[West Virginia Conservation Commission]] Fish Division, Project F-3-R-3, documents a series of investigations into the stocking, management, and biology of small farm ponds. Work covered study-pond selection, creation of experimental ponds, physical and chemical monitoring, creel census, population sampling, aquatic-plant and crayfish control trials, and fish trapping. The report is largely descriptive; its authors state that one year of data does not support firm conclusions about stocking desirability, and they recommend continued multi-year monitoring.

## Background & Description

The project aimed to determine the desirability and success of recommended management procedures for small impoundments in West Virginia. Eight jobs (A through H, plus a lectures item) structured the work:

- **Job A – Study pond selection:** About 20 to 25 stocked, managed ponds were chosen with help from [[Soil Conservation Service]] technicians, using owner cooperation, stocking time and method, and pond location as criteria. Table I lists the ponds; four were later discontinued (a bluegill/green sunfish overpopulation, a complete fish kill in early 1954, conversion to commercial catfish stocking, and an owner who could no longer keep records).
- **Job B – New or reclaimed experimental ponds:** Management agreements were made with ten private pond owners, and three experimental ponds were established at the [[Palestine State Fish Hatchery]], with two more used for holding and rearing fish. Three new state ponds were built at the Medium Security Prison Farm near Huttonsville, Randolph County. The Ridge State Fish Hatchery was excluded because of its uncertain status.
- **Job C – Physical and chemical data:** Surface area was measured by plane-table survey, and water was analysed for dissolved oxygen, total acidity and alkalinity, free carbon dioxide, and pH.
- **Job D – Creel census:** Voluntary angler census forms and measuring boards were distributed, with a census shelter at the Palestine reservoir.
- **Job E – Population sampling:** Minnow seine and full drain-and-inventory methods were used.
- **Job F – Corrective procedures:** Trials of cutting, herbicides, copper sulphate, sodium arsenite, and crayfish control agents.
- **Job H – Pond fish trapping:** Comparison of Carlander-type traps and modifications.
- **Field demonstration lectures:** Two lectures were given to the public.

Species mentioned include [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] as the principal stocked pair, [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Yellowbelly Sunfish]] as candidates for pond culture, [[Chain Pickerel]] as a planned stocking species, and unwanted [[Green Sunfish]], [[Gizzard Shad]], and [[Common Carp]] that entered ponds through water supply. Rainbow trout were stocked in two ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stratification and depth:** Most study ponds were thermally and chemically stratified. One pond with a maximum depth of 9 feet showed a 20°F surface-to-bottom temperature difference, and many ponds with normal surface dissolved oxygen had no measurable oxygen at depths as shallow as 8 feet. The authors suggest that, where winter kill is rare, ponds deeper than 6 feet are not necessary, except where inflow is significant during warm months.

**Water chemistry:** Water samples were taken at the surface (6 inches) and 1 foot above maximum depth. Dissolved oxygen followed the sodium azide modification of the Winkler method, pH was read with a Coleman Compax electrometer, and free CO₂ was derived from pH and alkalinity using Moore's nomographs. The Palestine ponds treated for algae had pH values of 8.5 to 9.6, which affected treatment outcomes.

**Plant and crayfish problems:** Emergent and submersed plants, filamentous algae, and [[Burrowing Crayfish]] (*Cambarus diogenes*) around Palestine Hatchery ponds were the main nuisance taxa in the vegetation and crayfish trials.

**Balance concept:** Population balance was assessed with [[Swingle Ratios]] (F/C, Y/C, and ΔY ratios) for the bass-bluegill combination.

## Key Findings

**Study ponds and experimental ponds**
- Table I lists ponds across many West Virginia counties, with stocking dates and sizes; ponds from June 1954 onward were located near Palestine Hatchery.
- Ten new or reclaimed private ponds came under management agreement. Two previously stocked, out-of-balance ponds were drained or poisoned and restocked.
- The report recommends that future study ponds be new or reclaimed ponds stocked by project staff, and that about 10 new or reclaimed ponds be established each year until about 50 are in use.

**Stocking outcomes**
- Of 11 study ponds stocked at a 10:1 bluegill-to-bass ratio, 10 showed both bluegill and bass reproduction and were assumed balanced.
- Stocking bluegill fingerlings first, with bass three or more months later, gave little chance of achieving balance. Early-June bass fingerlings followed by early-fall bluegill fingerlings produced fishable ponds the second summer, with bass spawning the next spring.
- Two ponds (Haymond, Ritchie County; Haymaker, Calhoun County) were judged out of balance and dominated by small bluegill.

**Palestine reservoir (2.2 acres)**
- Opened to public fishing in March 1954. In the first 10 days, anglers reported 78 adult bluegill (8 to 9 inches) and 35 bass (10 to 17 inches).
- At the September 1954 drain, the population weighed large bass 9.5 lbs, small bass 15.2 lbs, large bluegill 1.5 lbs, intermediate bluegill 19.3 lbs, small bluegill 17.9 lbs, and 7.2 lbs of extraneous species, mainly gizzard shad, and was out of balance. It was restocked in October 1954 at a total weight of 35.1 lbs (as reported).

**Population inventories**
- Pond #5 (1.5 acres, 45 adult bluegill, 150 bass fingerlings) was not in balance when drained.
- Pond #9 (2.0 acres, 10:1 stocking) was also unbalanced and held 28 lbs of green sunfish, 350 lbs of gizzard shad, and 29 lbs of carp that entered through the water supply.

**Creel census**
- Table III harvest records were collected, with most private ponds yielding under 100 angling hours per acre per year. Low harvest is attributed to under-utilization.
- Weights were estimated from total length–median weight relationships in Carlander's *Handbook of Freshwater Fishery Biology*.
- Scale samples returned by anglers were too few for age and growth analysis.

**Fish trapping (Job H)**
- The double-end trap caught only one small bluegill and one small bass over three days, while an unaltered Carlander-type trap took 301 small and intermediate bluegill.
- Over 7.5 days, the unaltered Carlander trap took 1,046 small and intermediate bluegill. Adding 15-ft wings raised catch substantially (738 vs. 75 over 4.5 days, per the report), and a 15-ft lead performed no better.
- Brush cover reduced catch by about 50% relative to the unaltered trap.
- Daytime checks caught 4 to 5 times as many bluegill as nighttime periods, so the report recommends evening checks for once-daily removal.
- A total of 3,771 small and intermediate bluegill were removed, about 20% of the population estimated by the inventory.

**Physical and chemical monitoring**
- Chemical data were collected only on study ponds, not on experimental ponds established in 1953 and later.
- The report notes that correlating physical and chemical data with fish populations will require several years of repeated surveys.

**Aquatic plant control (Job F)**
- **Cattails (*Typha latifolia*):** Complete control was obtained by six cuttings at three-week intervals. A 0.5% 2,4-D ester in kerosene gave 100% control on one plot. Early-season spraying during active growth was needed.
- **Soft stem bulrush (*Scirpus validus*):** Approximately 80% control with eight cuttings at two-week intervals. A single 0.5% 2,4-D application gave about 99% control.
- **White waterlily (*Nymphaea odorata*):** Cutting, 1.0% and 2.0% 2,4-D, and Methoxone Chlorax Liquid all failed to give lasting control.
- **Dwarf spike rush (*Eleocharis obtusa*):** Approximately 90% control with 0.5% or 1.0% 2,4-D. Chlorax Spray Powder at 3.5 lbs per 100 sq ft gave regrowth on 25% of its plot.
- **Copper sulphate (1 ppm):** Results were variable and depended on pH and algae type. Pond #12 (pH 9.6) got little control; Pond #5 was cleared in one week, with transparency falling from over 6 feet to 18 inches. The bucket-and-dipper method was faster than knapsack spraying.
- **Sodium arsenite (2.5 to 3.0 ppm):** Complete algae kill, with partial control of pond weed in Pond #12. In Ponds #7 and #8 (pH 8.5 to 8.9) no living plants remained a month after treatment, with no harm to fingerling bass.

**Crayfish control (Job F, VII)**
- DDT bait gave an estimated 6% control after correction for a 28% natural decline in the control plots. The authors suspect heavy rain washed it out.
- Chloride of lime solution gave an apparent 93% control (after a 41% correction for control plots), and the report describes it as practical for average pond owners.
- Sodium hydroxide pellets showed no apparent control and need further testing.

**Recommendations and outlook**
- Continue creel census; place census stations with mailboxes and signs at each pond access point.
- Monthly chemical analysis of selected ponds near headquarters, with annual sampling of all study and experimental ponds.
- Pursue yellowbelly sunfish, redear sunfish, and chain pickerel for future experimental stocking combinations with largemouth bass.
- Continue field demonstration lectures for pond owners.
