# Management and Maintenance of Genetic Variability in Captive Endangered Species

#mammalogy #conservation-genetics #cervidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two linked pieces. The opening fragment reports perinatal mortality in zoo ungulates, with a focus on the [[Dorcas Gazelle]] herd at the [[Smithsonian National Zoological Park]]. The main article, by Chesser, Smith and Lehr Brisbin of the [[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]], is a population-genetics analysis of how to maintain genetic variability in captive endangered species. It argues that [[Captive Breeding]] programmes must balance [[Genetic Drift]], selection, [[Inbreeding Depression]] and [[Outbreeding]] simultaneously. The authors use a hypothetical [[White-Tailed Deer]] case to give quantitative guidance on subpopulation size, exchange rates and the minimum number of animals per subunit.

## Background & Description

**Perinatal mortality (opening fragment).** Inbred calves may be more susceptible to [[Selenium-Vitamin E Deficiency]]. Some cases may have been missed in diagnosis, or may simply reflect small numbers of inbred calves. The authors argue that careful, long-term animal records are necessary to detect [[Perinatal Mortality]] trends. Without them, a considerably higher mortality in inbred animals can go undetected. The [[Dorcas Gazelle]] herd at the National Zoo is regarded as a successful breeding group. Ninety-three young have been born over an 11-year period, and the herd is expected to become more productive as new animals are added. Related herds in the United States were also referenced.

**Genetic management (main article).** Many species now depend on captive breeding populations for survival. Breeding populations must be large enough to avoid inbreeding and drift. Captivity can also change selective forces. The primary objective of captive breeding is continued reproduction of the species. Conserving natural levels of genetic variability is also desirable, particularly if animals are later re-established under natural or seminatural conditions. The authors note that mutation is the only route to restoring lost variability, but it is too slow to matter on ecological timescales.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Genetic variability is expressed as alternative alleles (polymorphisms) within a population. It can be carried by heterozygous individuals, or by isolated subpopulations that are each homozygous for different alleles. The authors describe two management options:

1. Maintain a high frequency of heterozygous individuals.
2. Maintain isolated subunits, each fixed for different alleles.

Management for homozygosity across many loci leads to inbreeding depression. Management for high individual heterozygosity in a small, randomly breeding population fails because of drift. A combination of the two is therefore recommended.

Population subdivision, such as animals held in several zoos, helps maintain polymorphisms. Subdivided populations also reflect how many [[Endangered Species]] are already managed. Large aggregations at a single site are a poor strategy, because they increase the risk of catastrophe and disease.

## Key Findings

**Loss of heterozygosity and population size**
- The loss of heterozygosity is inversely related to effective population size (N_e) and accelerates with inbreeding (Crow & Kimura model, equation 1).
- Even a zoo population of about 100 cannot maintain heterozygosity without additional factors such as selection.
- With N_e below 10, drift overrides selection, causing loss of heterozygosity and likely extinction through inbreeding. Subdividing such small populations does not help, because the outbreeding needed to avoid inbreeding effectively creates a randomly breeding population.
- Equalising family sizes increases N_e but reduces the selective advantage of heterozygotes. Such measures alone are insufficient for most managed populations.

**Selection for heterozygotes**
- Maintaining polymorphism within subdivided populations depends on three factors: the exchange rate between subpopulations, the relative size of each subpopulation, and the relative fitness of homozygotes versus heterozygotes.
- Higher exchange rates between institutions require stronger selection for heterozygotes to keep polymorphisms.
- Heterosis resulting from outbreeding is temporary. It cannot be sustained by further outbreeding, so exchange should be pulsed, timed to periods of high inbreeding.
- Current zoo practice may already produce some heterosis unintentionally through limited exchange among small units.
- Selection of 0.05 or less for heterozygotes results in a loss of heterozygosity (Fig. 3, with N_e = 30).

**Worked example: White-tailed deer**
- The authors assume a hypothetical extinct-in-the-wild [[White-Tailed Deer]] population held as 100 breeding animals with a 1:1 sex ratio.
- They propose dividing the 100 animals equally among ten zoos.
- Using a selection estimate of 0.75, derived from starch-gel electrophoresis of a South Carolina population, the maximum exchange that maintains polymorphism is 20% of animals in each subdivision. For a value of 0.70, the allowable exchange is 30% (Table 1).
- Each subpopulation should contain at least ten individuals, since fewer cannot maintain polymorphism at low selective advantage.
- The population should be rapidly expanded, ideally to 500 or more, and the number of generations held at a low population size should be minimised.

**Practical recommendations**
- Breeding and exchange should be pulsed, with exchanges timed to signs of inbreeding depression.
- Breeders for exchange should be chosen at random, with roughly equal contributions from each subpopulation over longer intervals.
- Using one sex for exchange through artificial insemination is cheaper than moving animals, but it removes the effects of intrapopulation competition on selection.
- Electrophoretic techniques, as in [[Manlove et al. Electrophoretic Methods]], can estimate the realised inbreeding coefficient and help identify exchange timing.
- Subpopulations should be kept isolated, with limited movement of individuals to preserve existing polymorphisms. Such a strategy is economically more favourable than complex artificial selection schemes.
- The authors note that similar proposals were already used in practice, and that periodic breakup of inbred lines is an accepted approach.
