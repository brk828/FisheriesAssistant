# Distributional Atlas of Upper Mississippi River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Mississippi-fish-fauna

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes publication (No. 73, May 1971) by Philip W. Smith, Alvin C. Lopinot, and William L. Pflieger documents the present distribution of fishes along the [[Upper Mississippi River]]. It was produced as a joint contribution of the [[Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee]] (UMRCC) Fish Technical Section and the Illinois Natural History Survey. The atlas is meant to serve as a baseline for detecting future changes in river environmental quality, given concern over pollution and habitat alteration. The annotated list covers 134 species, of which about 30 are considered accidental or stragglers. Most records were gathered since 1962 through [[Minnow Seine]], [[Electrofishing]], and [[Otter Trawl]] collections.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The river is defined as extending from river mile 0 at the mouth of the [[Ohio River]] to river mile 854 at St. Anthony Falls. Most of it is a series of [[Navigation Pools]] separated by numbered locks and dams, where each pool takes the number of its downstream lock and dam. There is no pool 23. Below dam 27 the river is unimpounded, and the reach below dam 26 is called B-26.

**History.** Knowledge of the fauna before 1943 rested on work by Meek (1892), Forbes & Richardson (1908), and Greene (1935), together with museum and federal and state records. The UMRCC began field work in 1944, and its two-year survey of the river between Caruthersville, Missouri, and Dubuque, Iowa, produced Barnickol & Starrett (1951) on commercial and sport fishes. In 1962 a cooperative small-fishes survey was proposed. It was carried out in 1963, when biologists from several agencies made over 100 minnow-seine collections and a few otter-trawl collections. In 1966, collections were made at the mouths of 63 tributaries using seines and electrofishers; these were summarized in Nord (1967). Collections are now available for virtually the entire upper river (see [[Collecting Stations]]).

**Data sources and methods.** A distribution map was made for each species known from three or more sites. Solid symbols mark specimens personally examined. Hollow symbols mark the most recent published records, and questionable or very old records appear only in the annotated list. Specimens are mainly held by the Illinois Natural History Survey, with others at the University of Kansas Museum of Natural History and the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology. Names follow Bailey (1970), and families are arranged after Greenwood et al. (1966).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Indicator value.** Many of the non-game species are small and hard to identify, but the assemblage and its numerical relationships can indicate ecological conditions in the river.
- **Tributary contributions.** Of the 134 species, 30 are considered stragglers. Eight species probably come from marginal sloughs and lakes. The [[Missouri River]] is the most important tributary contributor, introducing silt-tolerant species such as [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus albus*), [[Plains Minnow]], [[Flathead Chub]], and [[Sicklefin Chub]]. The [[Ohio River]] dilutes the Mississippi's silty water, and near its mouth abundances increase for species including [[Skipjack Herring]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Silverband Shiner]], [[Mimic Shiner]], and [[Mississippi Silverside]]. Medium-sized rivers contribute little or nothing to the fauna.
- **Physical structure.** The reach between [[Lake Pepin]] and pool 14 and the reach below the Missouri differ sharply in species composition. For example, [[Silvery Minnow]] is absent from the intermediate section.
- **Causes of change.** The authors attribute observed declines mainly to drainage of marginal lakes and sloughs, flood-control dams, habitat modification for navigation, and excessive siltation. They note that current threats include pesticide residues, mercury, industrial and domestic wastes, oil spills, toxicant spills, thermal pollution, a proposed 12-foot navigation channel, and loss of marginal refuges.

## Key Findings

**Overall fauna**
- The river holds a rich fish fauna and supports good populations of most native species. With the exception of the [[Muskellunge]], no indigenous species are known to have been extirpated in historic times.
- Non-game fishes make up well over half of the species known from the Mississippi but had received far less study than commercial and sport species.

**Notable species and records**
- **[[Lake Sturgeon]]** (*Acipenser fulvescens*) is rare. Two specimens taken near Quincy in 1966 totaled 56 pounds, and a 32-pound fish was caught near Elsberry.
- **[[Pallid Sturgeon]]** is extremely rare. Records include a fish from the mouth of the Missouri (1944) and a small specimen at river mile 75 (spring 1970). A Keokuk record is rejected as too old.
- **[[Alligator Gar]]** (*Lepisosteus spatula*) is rare. Photographs document a 130-pound fish from Cairo and a 110-pound fish from Chester, both taken in 1965.
- **[[Paddlefish]]** (*Polyodon spathula*) is common from pool 9 downstream but rare in the upper pools.
- **[[Gizzard Shad]]** (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) is abundant throughout the river. The [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*) is the most abundant fish, and the [[River Shiner]] (*Notropis blennius*) is second.
- **[[Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*) is abundant and important throughout the river.
- **[[Grass Carp]]** (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) was caught at Chester in January 1971, a 34-inch, 21-pound male. The species was recently introduced in southern states and is reportedly being caught at other sites.
- **[[White Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus catus*) is recorded for the first time from the Mississippi. Adults were taken near New Boston and Wolf Lake, Illinois, in summer 1970, and are regarded as escapees from stocking.
- **Alabama Shad** (*Alosa alabamae*) is represented by one small specimen from a 1962 seine haul at river mile 152.
- **[[Burbot]]** (*Lota lota*) is rare. A specimen was taken in a hoopnet near New Boston in late 1969.

**Lampreys and other taxa**
- Chestnut lamprey (*Ichthyomyzon castaneus*) is sporadic above pool 20 and may be extinct there. Silver lamprey (*Ichthyomyzon unicuspis*) is uncommon below the Missouri and probably still occurs above pool 10.

**Historic and problematic records (rejected or unverified)**
- Twelve species from Bailey's preliminary list have not been taken in the Mississippi, including spotted gar, brown trout, muskellunge, redfin shiner, blacknose dace, and longnose dace.
- Recent "spotted gar" records were reidentified as shortnose gar.
- The cisco (*Coregonus artedii*) reported from pool 8 does not occur naturally in the river.
- The cypress minnow records were reidentified as juveniles of *Hybognathus nuchalis*.
- Six small species common in tributaries, including ribbon shiner and slender madtom, may eventually be found as stragglers.

**Conservation message**
- The authors conclude that the constant surveillance of the Mississippi River is increasingly necessary, because of current pollutant and habitat threats, and because the 1971 baseline is meant to detect future change.
