# Twin Lakes, Colorado: 1980 Report of Findings on Currents, Lake Trout, Mysis, Sedimentation, and Vegetation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report, prepared by the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] at Colorado State University, documents baseline limnological and fishery conditions in the two connected [[Twin Lakes]] before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] begins full operation. The study covers five components: lake currents (surface, bottom and under-ice), [[Lake Trout]] spawning in shallows, a photographic method for estimating [[Mysis Shrimp]] density, sediment trapping and suspended-sediment budgets, and the organic energy contribution of vegetation that will be flooded by the raised lake level. The work is framed as a baseline for predicting powerplant impacts on the aquatic environment at future [[Pumped-Storage Development]] sites.

## Background & Description

Twin Lakes are a pair of connected montane drainage lakes of glacial origin on Lake Creek, at 2802 m elevation (39°05' N, 106°20' W). The upper lake covers about 263.4 ha (depth about 20 m) and the lower lake about 736.5 ha (depth about 27 m). A controlled outlet built early in the twentieth century and a dredged connecting channel mean both lakes fluctuate essentially as one. The lakes are dimictic, with surface temperatures reaching up to 18 °C in late July to mid-August and ice cover usually from December to May. Chemically they are soft, dilute calcium bicarbonate waters, usually neutral to slightly basic.

The phytoplankton is dominated by diatoms and other golden-brown algae. The zooplankton is a mysis shrimp–copepod–rotifer association. Lake trout and mysis shrimp were both introduced. The fishery includes a self-reproducing lake trout population and a put-and-take [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery.

The Mt. Elbert powerplant occupies the northwest corner of the lower lake and houses two 100-MW pump-generators. The first unit began operating in September 1981 and the second was scheduled for 1983. Closing a new dam below the lower lake will raise the maximum water surface to about 2805 m, inundating the isthmus and creating one large reservoir. Baseline limnology and fishery studies have run since 1971.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report is built around several linked hypotheses about how the powerplant and larger lake will alter the system:

- **Currents and biota:** Increased fetch may raise current velocities. [[Mysis Shrimp]] are sensitive to turbulence, and larval fish depend on sheltered shorelines. Stronger flows could also resuspend bottom sediments.
- **Winter effects:** Under-ice currents could erode or unevenly freeze the ice sheet, affecting safety for ice fishing and snowmobiling, or keep sediments suspended, raising turbidity and changing the lake's energy budget.
- **Entrainment:** Lake currents carry mysids toward the powerplant even in winter. A passively drifting shrimp at 32 cm/min could cover the 1.7 km from Interlaken to the powerplant in under 4 days.
- **Spawning habitat:** Water-level fluctuations of 0.7 to 1.1 m per day could expose lake trout eggs laid in shallow water.
- **Nutrients and oxygen:** Inundated vegetation adds organic matter that may raise biological oxygen demand and reduce [[Hypolimnetic Oxygen]], which is harmful to cold-water lake trout.

The lake's [[Convection Currents]] are thermally driven, with a seasonal reversal near 4 °C, the temperature of maximum water density.

## Key Findings

**Chapter 1: Lake Currents**

- Surface currents are strongly wind-driven. Surface velocities ranged from 0.4 to 17 percent of windspeed within a single day. Lake-average values ranged from 0.9 percent (November, upper lake) to 4 percent (September, upper lake).
- Maximum observed surface current was 15.2 cm/s in the upper lake and 14.5 cm/s in the lower lake. Maximum bottom currents were 9.8 cm/s (upper) and 8.9 cm/s (lower).
- On calm days, an intrinsic clockwise circulation emerges, likely from a combination of thermal and wind forces. It is overridden once winds increase.
- Upper-lake bottom sediment is warmer in shallow water (2 °C/m temperature gradient at 1.8 m) than at 13.6 m depth (0.6 °C/m), so more heat enters the shallows.
- Under-ice velocity increased with depth in the first 2.6 m, but little additional increase occurred beyond about 2 m. Current direction was unaffected by the ice underside.
- Under-ice circulation in the upper lake was mostly anticyclonic in winter, with a single large convection-cell gyre. The lower lake showed more variable, pulsed flows and multiple small gyres.
- Strong bottom currents (above 6 cm/s) were recorded at Hartman Point, Mackinaw Point, Rocky Point and the south shore. These are suspected [[Lake Trout Spawning Habitat]] areas.
- Near the powerplant tailrace, bottom currents rarely exceeded 3 cm/s because the sheltered northwest corner limits wind-driven flow.

**Chapter 2: Lake Trout Spawning in Shallows**

- About 250 m of shoreline were vacuumed to 1.5 m depth in 1979 and 700 m in 1980, giving over 950 m searched in total.
- Method efficiency test: 37 of 100 scattered eggs (37 percent) were recovered.
- No trout eggs of any kind were found. The authors concluded lake trout in Twin Lakes are unlikely to spawn in water shallower than 1.5 m.

**Chapter 3: Mysis Population Estimates**

- A photographic sled trawl was developed to replace the older net trawl. Photos covered a known bottom area and reduced depletion and net-avoidance bias.
- Digital flowmeter distance accuracy was ±4 percent (95% CI). Float-passage accuracy was ±14 percent, rising to 16 percent in windy conditions. The authors recommended discontinuing float passage.
- Polyethylene towrope often lifted the trawl off bottom. Steel cable kept trawls on bottom 100 percent of the time at speeds below 0.9 m/s.
- The net captured only 42 percent of photographed shrimp, so netted counts should be multiplied by at least 2.4.
- Density near the powerplant (15–20 m depth) was 157 shrimp/m² (17 Oct 1980), 199 (17 Nov 1980), 89 (4 Jun 1981), 112 (10 Jun 1981), 232 (20 Jun 1981) and 142 (10 Jul 1981).
- Analysis of 195 photographs (1981) fitted a negative binomial distribution (χ² = 5.36 < 9.49), indicating clumped distribution. Clumps are likely under 10 m in diameter.
- The authors suggested October as the best month for whole-lake annual sampling.

**Chapter 4: Sedimentation Rates**

- Sediment traps gave a weighted upper-lake sedimentation rate of 2.2 mm/a. Suspended-sediment sampling gave 0.091 mm/a, about one-seventh of the 0.63 mm/a core-based estimate by Bergersen (1976), which had a range of 0.42–0.83 mm/a.
- Trapped sediment was 10 to 150 times the amount expected from suspended-sediment sampling, suggesting resuspension of bottom material.
- A road built on the south shore of the lower lake in spring 1980 caused an order-of-magnitude increase in sedimentation (96 percent inorganic). Silt filled rock crevices used for spawning, and the report judged that spawning habitat was adversely affected.
- Winter is recommended for monitoring powerplant effects, when background sedimentation is low (about 0.3 mm/a) and current velocities are lowest.
- Benthic material was 92 percent inorganic and 8 percent organic. One gram of dry residue equalled 3.86 mL of wet sediment.

**Chapter 5: Organic Contribution of Inundated Vegetation**

- Twenty random line-intercept stations were sampled between 2799 and 2805 m elevation.
- Willows (37.9% cover dominance) and old-field grasses (44.2%) were the dominant types. The total inundated vegetation was estimated at about 4,000 metric tons, with total energy of 11.2 × 10⁹ kcal (64.9 × 10⁹ kJ).
- Annual lake primary productivity was estimated at 2.3295 × 10⁹ g C/a for both lakes combined. Terrestrial vegetation input was estimated at 4.2 to 5.1 times annual plankton production, depending on the carbon-to-energy conversion factor used.
- Recommended management: keep water at 2800 m for the first several winters. Raise water halfway to maximum the first spring, draw down in summer to expose killed vegetation, then fill to full pool the second spring while advising a summer-through-winter drawdown.
