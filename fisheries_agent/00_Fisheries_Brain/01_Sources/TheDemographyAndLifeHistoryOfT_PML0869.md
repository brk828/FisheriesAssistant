# Demography and Life History of the Colorado Squawfish: Foundations for Population Viability Analysis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 draft by Michael Gilpin (Department of Biology, University of California, San Diego) develops a demographic foundation for a [[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA) of the [[Colorado Squawfish]], an endangered large cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Because life history data are scarce, the author builds an age-based [[Leslie Matrix]] model using a 1400-record capture-recapture database (the [[CRFP Database]], 1978–1989), length distributions from 1981–1989, and growth data. The central estimate is an adult annual survival rate of about 0.81, a stable population with a generation time of roughly 5 years, and a conclusion that the species' life history reflects evolutionary compromises made before human disturbance, so current demographic rates are out of equilibrium with present conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Life history:** Juveniles migrate long distances downstream and remain there for many years before returning upstream to spawn. Adults continue growing throughout life and become more fecund with size.
- **Age versus size:** Size and age are closely correlated through the reproductive period (to about age 7), so the author adopts an age-based framework because it is more mathematically tractable.
- **Demographic framework:** The analysis uses a one-year time step with observation in early May, before fry are present, so that the first matrix row reflects production of one-year-old juveniles by adults.
- **Reference case:** Methods are cited from work on the [[Grizzly Bear]] and [[Desert Tortoise]], where life-history matrices underpin PVA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical regimes:** The species evolved as a predator in a wild river system over nearly all of its evolutionary history. Harvest pressure increased from roughly a few hundred years ago until the last twenty years of the document's framing. Construction of major dams began in the 1930s, altering stream ecology. Exotic fishes added competition and predation on juveniles. The species was listed as endangered under federal law in 1974, reducing harvest.
- **Evolutionary speculation:** Juveniles face the highest mortality until they reach roughly 400 mm, after which adult natural mortality is estimated at under 1% per year. Reproductive value is hypothesized to increase with age, unlike typical mammals, because size increases fecundity and cold-blooded animals do not senesce.
- **Harvest effects:** Human harvest raises adult mortality, which should favor earlier and greater reproductive effort. Because generation time is long, evolutionary adjustment is slow, and current demography is considered out of equilibrium.
- **Study area:** The adult population is assumed stable in the Green and Yampa rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Data source:** The 1400-record CRFP database (1978–1989) is the primary dataset, supplemented by provisional judgments. A separate, larger database (about 50% larger) was noted as less consistent under constant standards.
- **Length distribution:** Capture length distributions for 1981–1989 are relatively constant. The year 1983 shows more subadults, which lowers the median; excluding it, the median size is stable.
- **Juvenile growth:** High juvenile growth of roughly 50 mm per year is accepted, though juveniles are treated as a "black box" with a one-year delay before appearing as adults.
- **Adult growth:** Estimates vary (Tyus, Hawkins 1991 at 30–50 mm/year, Nessler 1992 near 50 mm/year). The CRFP recapture data indicate 10–15 mm per year, with high individual variation and slightly higher absolute growth in larger fish.
- **Adult survival:** The slope of the log-transformed survivorship curve is −0.201 (explaining 93% of variance), giving an antilog of 0.81, so annual adult survival is about 0.81. Sensitivity analysis gives 0.87 at 10 mm/year growth and 0.76 at 20 mm/year growth. The constant slope indicates no senescence and no size-dependent survival.
- **Leslie Matrix:** Survival is 0.81 for adult age classes. Fecundity parameters must satisfy a net reproductive rate of 1.00; example solutions include j = 0.1 with m ≈ 1.25×10⁻³ and j = 0.5 with m ≈ 7.97×10⁻⁸ (among the five combinations given).
- **Steady-state age distribution:** Adult ages follow a geometric distribution with death rate d = 0.19. The mean adult age (generation time) is 1/d plus the age offset, approximately 5 years. Without human harvest, the generation time would be longer, about 17 years under a lower death rate example.
- **Conclusions:** Demographic rates reflect pre-human evolutionary compromises. The analysis provides a self-consistent base for subsequent PVA work, including exploration of river geometry threats to the species' future.
