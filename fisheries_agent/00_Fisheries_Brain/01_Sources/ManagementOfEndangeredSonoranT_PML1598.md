# Management of Endangered Sonoran Topminnow at Bylas Springs, Arizona: Description, Critique, and Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reviews efforts from 1982 to 1990 to recover and secure the three remaining natural populations of the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] at [[Bylas Springs]], Arizona. Flooding of the [[Gila River]] in 1977-78 allowed the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] to invade, and topminnow populations declined. Despite [[Antimycin-A]] poisoning, exclusion barriers, and livestock fencing, all three populations were lost or reduced to near extirpation by 1990. The authors critique past management and recommend a more intensive recovery program based on reassessment of earlier efforts and new approaches to eradicating and excluding mosquitofish.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Sonoran (Gila) topminnow is a poeciliid fish endemic to the Gila River basin of Arizona and New Mexico, and to the Concepción, Sonora, and Yaqui basins of Sonora, Mexico. It was listed as endangered in 1967 because of predation by introduced mosquitofish and habitat degradation.
- **Current status:** Natural US populations are restricted to fewer than 10 sites, all in Arizona. Most earlier recovery work emphasized reintroductions within the native range. This paper addresses only attempts to maintain the natural populations.
- **Study system:** Three natural populations occupied small springs adjacent to the Gila River on [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] lands near Bylas. Two were discovered in 1968 and the third in 1981. No other fishes were present. The springs are labelled S-I (with west and east sources), S-II ([[Middle Spring]]), and S-III ([[Salt Creek]]), from west to east.
- **Spring characteristics:**
  - Base flows range from a few liters to a few tens of liters per minute. S-III has the greatest flow.
  - S-I and S-II rise through alluvial fill along a stony escarpment and have essentially no surface watershed.
  - S-III rises in the channel of an otherwise ephemeral watershed of more than 50 km².
  - All springs are intermittent in their lower reaches and were originally isolated from the Gila River, either by desiccating or percolating into the terrace (S-I and S-II) or by falling over an alluvial escarpment more than 2.5 m high (S-III).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Invasion:** Unusually high winter flooding in 1977-78 allowed mosquitofish to invade S-I. Mosquitofish and [[Red Shiner]] (recorded only once) also colonized S-III.
- **Declines:** Topminnow populations declined sharply after invasion. In S-I, topminnows persisted in the upper run reaches (62-72% of fishes present) but became rare or absent downstream, where mosquitofish were numerous. S-II was never invaded and topminnows remained common until 1988.
- **Habitat changes from management:**
  - Barrier construction and fencing altered flow regimes and created small ponds on the low-gradient runs. These ponds were quickly exploited by mosquitofish.
  - Sedimentation and [[Cattail]] (*Typha angustifolia*) expansion followed livestock exclusion. Increased evapotranspiration and accumulated debris dried S-II in 1990.
  - Sediment trapped behind barriers in S-III became extensive enough to bypass the structures.
- **Other vegetation:** A large [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) threatening damage to a barrier was removed at S-I.

## Key Findings

**Management timeline**

- **1982, S-I poisoning:** S-I was treated with [[Antimycin-A]] in March 1982. No live fish were found three weeks later. More than 150 topminnows and many invertebrates, salvaged before treatment, were reintroduced. By July 1982 both were re-established, but mosquitofish were found downstream, indicating the poisoning had not removed them from the lower system.
- **1983-84, barriers and fencing:** [[Barrier Weirs]] were built on the runs of all three springs. Each was a low (0.7-0.8 m) concrete V-notch weir with earthen wings extending 3.5-6.6 m laterally and upstream. [[Livestock Exclusion Fencing]] surrounded the western S-I source, the S-II source, and each barrier.
- **1984-1987, S-I repeat cycles:** S-I was poisoned again in April and June 1984 and restocked in July with more than 200 topminnows. Mosquitofish bypassed the barrier during spate-augmented flows in late summer and reinvaded. Their share of fishes rose from a minority in late 1985 to 69% in September 1986 and to a dominant share by July 1987.
- **1989-1990, S-I status:** In January 1989, nine topminnows and six mosquitofish were collected at the fenced west source. Only mosquitofish were taken downstream. In April 1990, three topminnows and one mosquitofish were collected at the fenced source. At the unfenced source, topminnows were part of a sample of 189 fishes, but the scanned share is illegible. No topminnows were found above or below the barrier, where mosquitofish were abundant.
- **1989-1990, structural changes:** The S-I channel was realigned to flow over its barrier. Low (<0.5 m) [[Gabion Revetments]] of wire-constrained and loose rock were extended 19-24 m on either side to replace the earthen berms. In 1990 the barrier notches were reshaped from V to approximately rectangular, roughly doubling their area to 1500 cm² to accommodate larger flows. The upstream base of the S-I barrier was sealed with [[Bentonite Sealing]] to stop seepage.

**Population outcomes**

- **S-I:** Repeated mosquitofish invasions following each poisoning and barrier bypass have kept the population unstable.
- **S-II:** Never invaded by mosquitofish and remained secure from them, but topminnows disappeared in 1990 from surface water depletion following cattail invasion.
- **S-III:** Topminnows were extirpated by 1984. A 1983 flood incised the lower run and destroyed the former waterfall barrier. After the spring was poisoned in April 1984, about 300 topminnows were stocked, but the restocked population dwindled to a few individuals in the rock-walled pools. By 1990 mosquitofish had reinvaded, and no fish were found in July 1990 despite exhaustive searching.

**Critique**

- Of the original stocks remaining in the Gila River system in 1987, the authors judged that two of the three Bylas Springs populations were likely already gone, with the third controlled only by the barrier.
- Barriers and fencing did not prevent invasion. Floods overtopped barriers, and in some cases the barrier design itself altered habitat in ways that favored mosquitofish.
- Cumulative costs of repeated, unsuccessful operations were high, and the authors argue that effort should be focused on fewer, more effective actions.

**Recommendations**

- A more intensive, coordinated recovery program is needed quickly, with greater commitment to eradication and exclusion of mosquitofish.
- Barriers should be engineered to withstand flood flows, and fencing and livestock management should be reconsidered.
- Measures should prevent accidental or deliberate movement of mosquitofish into topminnow habitat.
- Monitoring and reassessment should guide decisions. The authors also note that previous [[Recovery Plan]] actions, such as reintroductions of topminnows to unrelated sites, were unsuccessful and should not be assumed to secure the species.
