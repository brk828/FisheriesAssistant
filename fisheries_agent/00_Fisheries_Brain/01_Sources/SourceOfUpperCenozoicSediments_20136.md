# Source of Upper Cenozoic Sediments in the Colorado Delta Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #foraminifera

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1965 paper from the *Journal of Sedimentary Petrology* (vol. 35, no. 4, pp. 911–916) by [[Richard Merriam]] and [[Orville L. Bandy]] of the University of Southern California. It asks whether the coarse and fine Cenozoic sediments filling the Colorado delta basin share a single source, a question the authors describe as never previously settled. Using sand mineralogy, grain-size parameters, and reworked [[Upper Cretaceous Foraminifera]], they conclude that the upper Cenozoic fine-grained deposits were carried into the basin by the [[Colorado River]] from the [[Colorado Plateau]], and that this river has supplied most of the sediment filling the basin since at least the Pliocene or latest Miocene.

The scanned pages also include an unrelated second fragment (from a Texas Petroleum Research report) on mercury-injection methods for estimating rock pore-size distribution and permeability. It is summarized separately below.

Fish and fish habitat are not discussed. The biological content is limited to microfossils.

## Background & Description

**Study setting.** The [[Colorado Delta Region]] is a northwest-trending trough that appears to be the sediment-filled continuation of the [[Gulf of California]]. Geophysical and geological studies show a pre-Tertiary crystalline basement overlain by up to 20,000 feet of Cenozoic sediment. The sediment comes in two broad types:

1. **Coarse debris** (agglomerate, conglomerate, breccia, coarse arkosic sandstone), derived from surrounding mountains and a few high crystalline masses within the basin.
2. **Fine sediment** (clay, silt, fine sand), the basin-ward time equivalent of the coarse debris.

The study area lies between the [[Salton Sea]] and the Gulf of California. It is flat, with exposures limited to canal cuts and channels. The [[Peninsular Range]] forms the western margin, with crest elevations of 4,000 feet or more. It is built of Cretaceous batholithic rocks (gabbro and granite, mainly tonalite) and quartz-mica schists. Lower, discontinuous eastern mountains are metamorphic and granitic, with subordinate Tertiary volcanic rocks.

**Formations mapped in the Imperial Valley.** Coarse clastic units include:

- [[Anza Formation]]: about 1,100 feet of reddish granitic conglomerate with arkose wedges.
- [[Split Mountain Formation]]: up to 2,700 feet of moderately cemented granitic and dioritic breccia, probably Miocene.
- [[Mecca Formation]]: a correlative of the Split Mountain Formation.
- [[Canebrake Conglomerate]]: the most widespread coarse unit, 0–9,000 feet thick.
- [[Ocotillo Conglomerate]]: a younger coarse unit, up to 2,400 feet thick.

Fine-grained units include:

- [[Imperial Formation]]: buff to gray sandstone, claystone and siltstone, about 3,500 feet observed. Fossils indicate late Miocene age for some sections and perhaps Pliocene for others.
- [[Palm Spring Formation]]: mainly fine sand, silt and clay, with few tuff beds. Vertebrate fossils indicate Pleistocene age and a terrestrial setting, with thin oyster beds marking brief marine conditions.
- [[Borrego Formation]]: lacustrine light gray claystone with fine sandstone, 6,000 feet thick at the type locality (Borrego Badlands).
- [[Brawley Formation]]: lacustrine-equivalent sediments west of the south end of the Salton Sea, about 2,000 feet exposed.

**Regional structure.** The basin is controlled by tectonic activity, mainly faulting of the [[San Andreas Fault System]]. The principal northwest-striking faults are the Banning, Mission Creek, San Jacinto and Elsinore faults. Movement on the first two is right-lateral and probably began in early Tertiary time. Strata as young as Pleistocene stand vertically locally, and folding and faulting remain active, as shown by marked seismicity and creep measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sedimentary record reflects a depositional system that moved between marine and fresh-water conditions. Several controls shaped it:

- **Shifting distributaries** and **structural events** repeatedly changed the depositional setting.
- **Marine incursions** are recorded by oyster beds and marine fossils within otherwise terrestrial or lacustrine units.
- **Lacustrine fauna** in the Borrego beds marks lake conditions.
- **Recent delta sediment** is chiefly silt and fine sand, with clay an important component downstream.

The Colorado River drains the [[Colorado Plateau]], including the [[Mancos Shale]], which is the likely source of the reworked Cretaceous microfossils. Sediment from the Colorado River system is the main source of the basin fill.

## Key Findings

### 1. Sediment provenance by mineralogy and grain size

The authors compared sands of known origin with sands of unknown origin.

- **Known-origin samples** were taken from the Recent Colorado delta, the Colorado delta in Lake Mead, and streams draining the Peninsular Range.
- **Unknown-origin samples** came from thick, uniform, poorly bedded fine sands of the Palm Spring, Imperial, Borrego and Brawley formations. The authors estimate these sands make up about 30 percent of the total fine sediments.
- Sands were studied rather than clay-silt because the clay-silt was too fine for petrographic analysis.

**Table 1: Characteristics of sands** (values as printed, with ranges):

| Sample | Median diameter (µm) | Sorting coefficient | Carbonate (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palm Spring | av. 84 (12–160) | av. 1.72 (1.6–2.0) | av. 7.4 (3–14) |
| Colorado delta (Recent) | av. 115 (15–210) | av. 1.68 (1.6–1.9) | av. 9.3 (5–13) |
| Lake Mead | 150 | 1.3 | 10 |

The Palm Spring and Imperial sands match the Recent delta and Lake Mead sands in grain size and sorting. The authors argue this indicates a common mode of origin.

**Mineralogy (Table 2).** All sands except the Peninsular Range samples have similar mineral suites.

- Quartz is about 40–64 percent of the grains. Rock fragments are mainly volcanic groundmass fragments, typically around 16–31 percent.
- Calcite and dolomite appear as detrital grains at about 5–10 percent. Carbonate rocks are rare in the mountains immediately around the delta, although they are abundant in the wider Colorado River drainage.
- Heavy minerals such as hornblende, magnetite and biotite are common to abundant in several samples. Augite, garnet, epidote and sphene are rare to common.
- Peninsular Range washes (samples 17 and 18) contain much more plagioclase (about 35–39 percent) and lack carbonate. The authors say their plagioclase is intermediate to calcic, typical of the Peninsular Range.
- The Peninsular Range heavy minerals (biotite, hornblende, augite) are rare in the delta and Palm Spring sediments, so that range is not the main source.
- Volcanic rocks crop out around the sediment basin, but the volcanic areas are small. If they were the source, nearby volcanic-rich sediment should be present, and it is not.
- A geothermal well drilled south of the Salton Sea yields sediments mineralogically similar to the delta sediment (Muffler and White, 1964).

### 2. Reworked Upper Cretaceous foraminifera

- Detrital [[Upper Cretaceous Foraminifera]] occur in all samples of the Palm Spring Formation, the modern Colorado River delta, the upper Pleistocene Borrego and Brawley lacustrine sediments, the Lake Mead delta, and the Imperial Formation (mostly Pliocene).
- Specimens are generally 100–200 µm. They are filled with calcite and show abrasion; more single or double chambers occur than complete tests.
- **Table 3** lists seven reworked taxa: *Globotruncana globigerinoides* (?), *Globigerinelloides aspera* (?), *Heterohelix globulosa*, *H. reussi*, *H. striata*, *Neobulimina canadensis*, and *Pseudouvigerina cretacea*.
- The reworked taxa are often associated with indigenous *Elphidium gunteri* populations in marine and brackish deposits.
- The Cretaceous taxa are interpreted as currently transported by the Colorado River, probably derived from the [[Mancos Shale]] of the Colorado Plateau. Foraminifera from the Peninsular Range province (Bandy, 1951) are better preserved and lack calcite fill, which separates the two sources.

### 3. Conclusions

- The **Colorado River began depositing sediment in the Gulf of California at least as early as the Pliocene or latest Miocene.**
- Deposition has been continuous since then, alternating between marine and fresh-water conditions. Shifts reflect structural events and changes in distributaries.
- **The bulk of the sediment filling the basin from the Salton Sea to the Gulf of California was imported by the Colorado River.**
- The fine-grained deposits are contemporaneous with, but do not grade into, the coarse clastic masses, so the two are not the same source.

### 5. Second fragment: mercury-injection rock analysis (Texas Petroleum Research)

This unrelated fragment reviews methods for estimating porosity and permeability of reservoir rock from [[Mercury Injection Capillary Pressure]] data.

- Henderson used low-pressure mercury injection to estimate sand permeability and floodability. He found that the pressure needed for permeability prediction was excessive and that accuracy was limited.
- Gournay and Reichertz used mercury injection up to 1,500 psi and developed equations to estimate permeability from pore-size distribution. They concluded that, with these equations, permeabilities could be predicted from injection data.
- Felsenthal and Conley described a pore-size distribution apparatus that reduced analysis time to under two hours per sample, using a digital computer and small samples.
