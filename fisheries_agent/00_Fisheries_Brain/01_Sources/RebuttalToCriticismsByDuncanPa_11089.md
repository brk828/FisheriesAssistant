# Rebuttal to Criticisms on USFWS Study Area Selection in Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #habitat-ecology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #methodology

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal rebuttal by USFWS biologists Owen T. Gorman and Stuart C. Leon (dated May 15, 1992) responding to criticisms by Duncan Patten, Senior Scientific Advisor for the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES), regarding the justification of study area selection in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) for [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) research. The authors defend their microhabitat-based approach to stream fish ecology, argue that their study area selection follows long-accepted conventions in the field, and contend that demands for additional justification are unwarranted and have impeded research progress for over 11 months. The document provides detailed methodological justification for the microhabitat approach versus macrohabitat methods, defends the use of percent similarity indices for habitat analysis, and outlines the rationale for selecting five study areas spanning 25% of the lower 21 km of the LCR.

## Background & Description

The USFWS Arizona Fishery Resources Office was contracted to conduct habitat research on [[Humpback Chub]] in the [[Little Colorado River]] as part of GCES Phase II and conservation measures 5 and 7. The research team identified five study areas in the LCR: confluence (0.5-1.0 km), lower LCR/Powell camp (2.5-3.5 km), upper LCR/Salt camp (10.5-11.5 km), Atomizer Falls (14.0-15.0 km), and Blue Springs (20.0-21.0 km). Each study area spans 1000 m and includes at least two pool-riffle-run complexes, following standard protocols for 4th-5th order streams.

The USFWS approach employs microhabitat methodology measuring three primary parameters: depth, current velocity, and substrate. This method, originally developed by Gorman in 1974 and published in *Ecology* (1978), has become a standard approach in stream fish ecology. The team conducted habitat mapping along transects spaced at 100 m intervals for the lower 21 km of the LCR. The "Roubidoux" or habitat pole method for measuring current velocity was validated on April 28, 1992, with 24 measurement points showing mean estimated velocity of 0.621 m/sec (SD 0.251) versus current meter measurements of 0.606 m/sec (SD 0.236), correctly categorizing current in 23 of 24 cases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Little Colorado River]] is a 5th order tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The lower 21 km exhibits longitudinal habitat variation with three distinctly unusual reaches: the confluence area, the Triple Drop to Atomizer Falls reach (characterized by travertine features), and the Blue Springs reach. The middle sections (km 2-13) are relatively homogeneous, though the upstream portion (km 8-14) contains more conspicuous travertine reefs and dams than the lower reach (km 0-8).

The document emphasizes fundamental principles of stream fish ecology: fish assemblage structure changes longitudinally with stream habitat (documented for over 30 years in literature), and fishes track microhabitats rather than macrohabitats. In larger streams (>4th order) like the LCR, macrohabitats form a mosaic rather than strict longitudinal arrangement, making macrohabitat delineation subjective and less predictive of fish presence. The horizontal component in large streams means any given stretch contains multiple macrohabitat types, and these macrohabitats are less homogeneous than in smaller streams.

Microhabitats represent the smallest homogeneous habitat units used by organisms. Mesohabitats are suites of related microhabitats (e.g., edge habitats of pools, deep water portions). Macrohabitats are collections of microhabitats in qualitatively recognizable types (pools, riffles, runs). The authors argue that microhabitat approaches yield transferable results because fish track specific microhabitat characteristics across different macrohabitat types.

## Key Findings

**Study Area Selection Rationale:**
- Five study areas identified based on standard stream fish ecology conventions: three unusual reaches (confluence, Atomizer Falls, Blue Springs) plus two sites in more homogeneous sections (Powell camp, Salt camp)
- Study areas collectively represent 25% of the lower 21 km, considered extensive coverage
- Each 1000 m study area includes minimum two travertine reef/dam-pool/run complexes
- Powell and Salt camp locations selected for proximity to research camps to support intensive day/night sampling

**Habitat Analysis Results:**
- Preliminary habitat analysis using percent similarity (PS) indices showed Powell and Salt study areas are representative of their respective river sections
- Analysis identified three distinctly different reaches: confluence, Atomizer Falls, and Blue Springs
- Sampling at 100 m transect intervals adequate to assess 1 km reaches but not 500 m reaches
- The LCR between km 2 and 13 exhibits homogeneous habitat quality with no single "most representative" site

**Methodological Defense:**
- Microhabitat approach using depth, current, and substrate is widely accepted in stream fish ecology for nearly 20 years
- Schoener's percent similarity index judged most accurate overall for categorical data with non-normal distributions
- USFWS data meets reliability requirements outlined by Ricklefs and Lau (1980) and Linton et al. (1981)
- Simple univariate analyses appropriate for straightforward habitat patterns; multivariate analyses would be superfluous
- First-order analyses required for interpreting second-order analyses and provide absolute habitat measurements

**Research Impediments:**
- GCES-ACT delayed USFWS research implementation for more than 11 months
- Team spent one year collecting habitat data without permission to collect fish data
- Level of justification demanded exceeds that required by NSF or peer-reviewed journals
- No other GCES contractor required to justify methodologies at expense of research effort

**Historical Context:**
- USFWS intended study area approach from project inception
- Study area locations and sampling design outlined in May 28, 1991 study plan
- Study plan shared with Arizona State University researchers prior to July 1991 field work
- Approach represents 35 years combined experience of lead biologists in stream fish ecology

The authors conclude that further analysis to demonstrate "representativeness" is unnecessary, that their methodology follows established scientific conventions, and that continued delays prevent fulfillment of contractual responsibilities for endangered species research.
