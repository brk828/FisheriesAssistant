# Heredity of Pigmented Tumours in Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xiphophorus

## Executive Summary

This 1950 article by [[Myron Gordon]], reprinted from *Endeavour*, argues that fish are valuable models for cancer research, especially for pigment-cell tumours ([[Melanoma]]s). Using crosses between [[Platyfish]] (*Platypoecilus maculatus*) and [[Swordtail]] (*Xiphophorus hellerii*), Gordon shows that a specific pigment-cell gene, the macromelanophore gene Sp, initiates melanomas in hybrids. He also reports a rare tumour type, the [[Erythromelanoma]], which appears only when the red-pattern gene Rt is physically linked to Sp on the same sex chromosome. The author concludes that the spatial arrangement of genes, not only their identity, determines tumour outcomes.

## Background & Description

Gordon opens by noting that tumours in sharks, fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals resemble human tumours in structure and microscopic detail. He states that the melanoblast is the common cell type in melanomas across the six vertebrate classes, and that melanoblasts from fish, mouse and human melanomas look almost identical in tissue culture.

Fish are useful because pigment patterns are genetically variable. Pigment cells (chromatophores) are named by their pigment:

- **[[Melanophore]]s** contain black pigment.
- **[[Erythrophore]]s** contain red granules.
- **[[Xanthophore]]s** contain yellow pigment.
- **Guanophores** contain guanine crystals.
- **[[Xantho-Erythrophore]]s** combine yellow and red pigments. Their yellow component is lutein and zeaxanthin, and their red component is erythropterin.

In the platyfish, two melanophore types create its patterns. Micromelanophores (gene St) cover the body and give an olive-grey colour. Macromelanophores (gene Sp) are visible spots. Gordon cites embryological work on the [[Neural Crest]] as the origin of pigment cells in salamanders, birds and mice.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Tumour occurrence in wild fish is rare:

- **Melanotic tumours** occur in wild representatives of eleven orders of elasmobranchs and true fishes.
- **Wild swordtails and platyfish:** no melanomas were found in more than 20,000 carefully examined wild-caught specimens.
- **Sea of Japan survey:** in a twelve-year survey of 100,000 fish, only two coloured tumours were found, a guanophoroma in a greenling and an allophoroma in a gurnard.
- **Other records:** one xanthoma was reported in Europe, in a shark. Four erythrophoromas were found in wild fish: two in European trout, one in a tunny and one in an American winter flounder.

Aquarium-reared fish showed more cases. Three erythrophoromas occurred in the dorsal fin region of golden, red-finned platyfish-swordtail hybrids, and one occurred in a black-spotted red hybrid. Gordon counts only eight red and red-black tumours across all fishes, and notes that his own single brood produced eight such cases.

## Key Findings

### 1. The macromelanophore gene Sp initiates melanomas
- The "crucial experiment" used a pure platyfish with macromelanophores (Sp) but no micromelanophores, and a swordtail with no melanophores. A pink-eyed albino swordtail was found to carry a hidden dominant micromelanophore factor, so the researchers bred a special recessive albino stock.
- In the F2 cross (albino swordtail × macromelanophore platyfish), **20** hybrids were deeply black with hyperplastic macromelanophore growth, and many developed melanomas. **29** were yellow, black-eyed and normal.
- Conclusion: macromelanophores alone can initiate melanomas in hybrids.

### 2. Erythromelanomas depend on the RtSp gene linkage
- The exceptional male came from a routine sex-determination study. It carried Rt (ruby-throat, a sex-linked xantho-erythrophore gene) and Sp (spotted sides) on the same Y chromosome.
- In pedigree 173, a brood of **94** platyfish contained one exceptional RtSp male. Gordon attributes it to a chance **crossing-over** on the Y chromosome, which he puts at about **1 per cent** in this species.
- In pedigree 206, RtSp hybrids (with albino modifier genes A and B from the swordtail) developed erythromelanomas of the head. Eight of them developed tumours at two years or older.
- In the second-generation brood (mating 5), **102** offspring were produced, **53** were RtSp, and **11** had erythromelanomas of the head. About 39 were expected if the RtSp and A/B combination alone caused tumours, so other unidentified factors were also involved.
- Mating 4 with a golden platyfish (RtSp st) produced **26** offspring, **11** RtSp and **15** recessive rtsp. None was abnormal. Over **300** later fish from domesticated RtSp lines were also normal, so the tumour phenotype needs the hybrid background.

### 3. Linkage with Sb produces melanomas but not erythromelanomas
- In pedigree 207 (mating 6), **12** hybrids carried RtSb with A and B. All were highly melanotic, and **7** developed typical melanomas of the mid-ventral areas at one to two years.
- Gordon states that RtSb with A and B produces melanomas but cannot produce erythromelanomas.

### 4. Back-cross evidence
- In mating 7, a back-cross produced 24 offspring: **3** RtSp albinos (red-eyed), of which **2** had amelanotic melanomas and **1** had no tumour; **7** RtSp melanotic, of which **3** had melanomas at six months; plus **7** rtsp albino and **7** rtsp golden normal fish.
- The RtSp albinos were pigmented at birth. Gordon explains that Sp, interacting with A and B, overrides the albino gene ii.

### 5. Gene position matters
- If Rt and Sp were on separate sex chromosomes, RtSp-bearing platyfish would be expected to produce mainly melanomas, not erythromelanomas. Erythromelanomas would appear only occasionally, through crossing-over.
- Gordon concludes that knowing parental gene identities is insufficient. Their spatial arrangement on chromosomes must also be known to predict results.

### 6. Histology
- Sections of erythromelanomas (Figures 28–31) show macromelanophores and erythrophores, a connective-tissue framework, blood-vessel invasion, and destruction of muscle, cartilage and bone of the eye.
