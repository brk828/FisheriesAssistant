# The Systematics of Gasterosteus aculeatus in Central and Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County technical paper (Contributions in Science No. 243, June 1973, by Stephen T. Ross) examines the taxonomy of the threespine stickleback, [[Gasterosteus aculeatus]], in central and southern California. The study compares three principally allopatric forms: the anadromous [[Gasterosteus aculeatus aculeatus]] and the freshwater [[Gasterosteus aculeatus microcephalus]] and [[Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni]]. Morphological, breeding and chromosome data are presented, with emphasis on the two freshwater forms. The author concludes that subspecific nomenclature should be retained, while noting that evidence for postmating reproductive isolation is limited.

## Background & Description

The threespine stickleback shows complex variation across its range. Earlier work on Pacific Coast populations by Hagen, McPhail, Miller and Hubbs, Narver, and Hagen and Gilbertson framed a debate: Hagen and McPhail argued for specific recognition of *G. trachurus* and *G. leiurus*, whereas Miller and Hubbs supported retaining the three subspecies proposed by [[Jordan and Hubbs Nomenclature]] (1925). The present study follows the Jordan and Hubbs nomenclature for central and southern California populations.

Key taxonomic history includes:
- *G. a. williamsoni* was described by Girard (1854). Its type locality was re-determined by Miller (1960) as Soledad Canyon of the Santa Clara River, Los Angeles County. Synonyms include *santaeannae* (Regan, 1909) and *cataphractus*.
- *G. a. microcephalus* was described by Girard (1854) from Four Creek (Kaweah River), San Joaquin Valley. Its range is freshwater Pacific Coast drainages from the Bering Strait to Arroyo del Rosario, Baja California, with a hiatus between the Santa Clara River and San Juan Creek.

Methods included meristic counts (lateral plates, gill rakers, vertebrae), morphometrics measured with dial calipers, a composite [[Character Index]] scored from 0 to 10, reciprocal laboratory crosses incubated at 5 to 30°C, and chromosome karyotyping using [[Chromosome Preparation Methods]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation:** Predators such as [[Cottus asper]] and *Cottus aleuticus* are abundant in coastal streams from San Luis Obispo County northward, yet the author found no clear predation reduction in headwater populations. South of the Ventura River, streams are largely without piscivorous fishes, apart from exotic salmonids, centrarchids and [[Gambusia affinis]]. The author suggests reduced predation may have shaped the *williamsoni* form, though this needs further study.
- **Hydrology and isolation:** Nacimiento and San Antonio river populations are far from the ocean, and the Salinas River often dries in summer, which likely reduces gene flow.
- **Introductions:** Sticklebacks were not previously found in the endorheic Mohave River. *G. a. williamsoni* and intergrades were introduced there, and *G. a. microcephalus* was introduced into Alamo Creek in 1940.
- **Decline:** *G. a. williamsoni* is probably extirpated across much of its range, with the Miller (1969) reference giving an extirpation window of 1942 to 1961. Miller (1961) linked this decline tentatively to the introduction of Gambusia affinis.

## Key Findings

- **Character index separation:** The index separated *williamsoni* and *microcephalus* combined samples with 96% joint nonoverlap. The author considers this above the arbitrary 90% threshold for subspecies recognition (Mayr, 1969).
- **Plate counts and spines:** Lateral-plate numbers separated 18 populations into three groups corresponding to the three subspecies. Dorsal spine lengths showed 91% joint nonoverlap between the long- and short-spined groups, and pelvic spine length showed 92%.
- **Gill rakers:** Gill raker numbers differed significantly among the 18 samples (p<0.005), but overlap was too high for taxonomic grouping.
- **Vertebrae:** Counts from 12 populations (range 29 to 33) showed no clear geographic trend.
- **Reciprocal crosses:** Control crosses averaged 90% fertilization (sd = 0.15), versus 69% for test crosses (sd = 0.31; p<0.01). Embryo and larval mortality did not differ significantly between control and test crosses (t = .65 and .78). Crosses with *G. a. microcephalus* females fertilized at higher rates than those with *williamsoni* females.
- **Aculeatus cross:** A *williamsoni* female crossed with an *aculeatus* male from Scott Creek showed 88.3% fertilization in the control and 50% in the test cross. Embryo and larval viability were not reduced.
- **Temperature:** Embryo mortality was high at and below 10°C and above 25°C, with least mortality at 15 to 20°C.
- **Hybrid dominance:** *G. a. microcephalus* is dominant over *G. a. williamsoni* in both meristic and morphometric traits. F1 hybrids therefore cannot be reliably separated from *microcephalus* by plate number, and the character index is only slightly more reliable.
- **Karyotype:** All three forms have a diploid chromosome number of 42, with karyotypes matching published *G. aculeatus* data (Chen and Reisman, 1970).
- **Arroyo Trabuco population:** This population (San Juan Creek drainage) is intermediate. Its character index suggests affinity with *williamsoni*, but plate number, gill rakers and pubic-plate length are intermediate, so it is provisionally treated as a *williamsoni* x *microcephalus* intergrade.
- **Alamo Creek and Santa Clara River:** Their character index values fall in a third group, distinct from the partially plated populations by over 96% joint nonoverlap (CD = 1.97).
- **Conclusion:** Postmating isolation is limited to a 21% differential in fertilization. Premating isolation was not investigated. Because the forms are taxonomically distinct, the author favors retaining subspecific recognition, using the trinomial in the sense of Mayr (1963) without implying an evolutionary unit.
