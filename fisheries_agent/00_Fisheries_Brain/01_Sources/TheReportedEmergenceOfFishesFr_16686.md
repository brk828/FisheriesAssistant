# The Reported Emergence of Fishes from California's Subterranean Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gasterosteidae #subterranean-ecology #artesian-wells

## Executive Summary

This note documents seven historical records of fish emergence from deep wells and artesian sources in [[California]], spanning 1870-1953. The author examines whether these fish truly originated from subterranean waters or were inadvertently introduced during drilling operations. Geological evidence suggests limited possibility for true subterranean fish populations in California due to the scarcity of limestone solution channels. Laboratory experiments demonstrate that [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) can survive in heavy mud slurries for several hours, and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) tolerate 200 pounds pressure for 48 seconds, supporting the hypothesis that fish could survive drilling conditions.

## Background & Description

California's geological stratigraphy consists primarily of formations lacking extensive solution channels (limestone), which would theoretically be required for organisms to emerge alive from underground sources. The documented emergences involve three primary species: [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus* and subspecies), [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*), and unidentified "trout."

Early records include: an 1870 San Jose artesian well producing fish and sawdust; an 1874 Point Hueneme well at 143 feet depth producing thousands of two-inch "trout" with water temperature 64°F; and an 1884 San Bernardino well at 191 feet producing a 14-inch stickleback (*G. aculeatus williamsoni*).

Recent emergences (1946-1953) occurred at wells ranging from 465 to 1,300 feet depth, with drilling operations using 500 to 36,000 gallons of water. The [[Cholame Ranch]] emergence of approximately 50 sticklebacks from a 600-foot well with 1,200 gallon per minute artesian flow was witnessed by multiple observers who reported seeing fish swimming within the well shaft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Drilling operations pump considerable water into well shafts as lubricant, circulated under pressure heads up to 50 pounds. This water may originate from surface sources including main ranch wells, roadside pools, and adjacent wells. The nearest permanent water bodies to emergence sites ranged from 2 to 3 miles distance.

Laboratory experiments by the author demonstrated [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus a. aculeatus*) survival for several hours in heavy mud slurry conditions. Rowley (1955) found eight-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] suffered no ill effects from 200 pounds pressure applied for 48 seconds, indicating fish physiological tolerance for drilling conditions.

Geological formations at emergence sites included Eocene clay, sand and shale (Ione formation) and Quaternary alluvium with conglomerate and heavy adobe. These formations lack the solution channel characteristics that would support true subterranean fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Historical Records**: Seven California fish emergence records documented between 1870-1953, concentrated in central and southern California coastal and interior valleys.

**Recent Emergences (Table 1)**:
- James Clack Ranch (1946): 1 unidentified fish (14 inches, silvery) from 465-foot well
- Delbert Dowd Ranch (1950): 5 [[Sacramento Squawfish]] from 543-foot well near [[Lincoln]], specimens preserved at California Academy of Sciences #20,504
- [[Cholame Ranch]] (1952): approximately 50 [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*G. aculeatus microcephalus*) from 600-foot well, 1,200 gallon per minute artesian flow, specimens at California Academy of Sciences #20,693
- Daryl Arnold Ranch (1953): 5 sticklebacks from 1,300-foot well near [[Oxnard]], 1,150 gallons per minute flow

**Cholame Ranch Evidence**: Multiple witnesses (Howard Jack, Mrs. Palmateer Gibson) observed sticklebacks swimming within the well shaft. The 1,200 gallon per minute artesian velocity would have prevented fish from swimming up into the well from surface sources, contradicting Noble (1952). However, inadvertent introduction with 36,000 gallons of drilling water from roadside pools remains possible.

**Geological Assessment**: California's geological makeup contains very little limestone formation necessary for solution channels that could support subterranean fish populations. Core samples and stratigraphy indicate small possibility for organisms emerging alive from underground sources.

**Physiological Tolerance**: Fish demonstrate survival capability under drilling conditions, supporting contamination hypothesis over true subterranean origin.

**Conclusion**: Most reported emergences likely result from fish entering wells with drilling water rather than representing true subterranean populations, though the [[Cholame Ranch]] case presents compelling observational evidence requiring further investigation.
