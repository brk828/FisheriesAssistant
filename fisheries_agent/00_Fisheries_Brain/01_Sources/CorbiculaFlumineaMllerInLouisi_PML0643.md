# Range Expansion of the Asian Clam and Notes on Southeastern Land Snails

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #corbicula

## Executive Summary

This short 1963 note, published in *The Nautilus*, documents three mollusk topics: the rapid North American spread of the introduced [[Corbicula Fluminea]] (Asian clam), a taxonomic correction regarding the distribution of [[Succinea Ovalis]] in the southeastern United States, and the defensive shell-twisting behavior of [[Mesodon Thyroidus]]. The largest theme is the clam's dispersal, which covered much of the country in less than 25 years after its first U.S. record in 1938. The author presents this as evidence of the hazards of accidental or deliberate biological introductions.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula fluminea* is described as a small, slow-moving, bottom-dwelling bivalve. It was first observed in the United States in 1938. The note cites a 1961 Tennessee report on the introduced clam and reviews its life history, ecology, and general habits in works cited by the author. No morphological measurements are given.

*Succinea ovalis* is a land snail whose documented range is discussed through museum lots and published records. *Mesodon thyroidus* is a land snail observed in the field in Alabama, where its behavior was recorded during handling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The clam's spread was linked to river systems across the country and to sampling by state and federal pollution-control and fisheries biologists. The author notes that the clam's rapid colonization illustrates how introductions, whether accidental or deliberate, can have wide-reaching consequences.

For the snails, the note stresses that earlier distribution records were often unreliable. Several claimed southeastern records were traced to misidentifications or mixed labels, so the verified range rests on specimen checks.

## Key Findings

**1. Rapid continental dispersal of *Corbicula fluminea***
- First U.S. observation in 1938. Spread from the Columbia River system in Washington into the Willamette, Sacramento, and San Joaquin rivers, the Colorado River drainage, and the Gila River in Arizona.
- Entered the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) system, first recorded at Paducah, Kentucky, on the Ohio River. It then spread into the Tennessee, Cumberland, and Green rivers, where it was observed in abundance along all of them.
- Gulf Coast records include the Calcasieu River at mile 66 in Calcasieu Parish, Louisiana (October 1961), Assumption Parish, Louisiana, and Bayou Sorrel (May 1962).

**2. Alabama distribution of *Corbicula fluminea***
- Abundant in the Mobile River north of Bucla.
- Found at Chastang Bluff, east of Chastang.
- Not found in the Tombigbee River at Fishing Camp, east of Mt. Vernon.

**3. Correction to *Succinea ovalis* records**
- Walker's 1928 report of *S. ovalis* from several northern Alabama localities was considered probably based on misidentifications, despite extensive collecting by the author without finding the species there.
- The only *S. ovalis* lot in the University of Michigan collection from Alabama came from Mobile, but a Carnegie Museum lot labeled *S. ovalis* was identified as *S. texana* Hubricht, and the Mobile specimens had mixed labels.
- Pilsbry's North Carolina record (Macon? Co.) was also judged erroneous.
- The verified range runs from Maryland to Kentucky, extends into the mountains as far as the Great Smoky Mountains, ranges farther west along the Mississippi River, is not known from central Missouri, and occurs in the Missouri River valley and in Kansas, Nebraska, and South Dakota.

**4. Shell-twisting defense behavior in *Mesodon thyroidus***
- When a snail was pulled by the shell from a surface, an occasional *M. thyroidus* reacted by twisting its body from side to side until the shell was free, with the foot still attached.
- The shell rotated upon the body during this process.
- Similar twisting was observed in animals burdened by other snails.
- The author suggests the behavior may be a response to unfavorable conditions and a possible defense reaction, and that it may help the snail extricate itself from litter.
