# Fisheries Survey of the Lower Virgin River, Arizona to Lake Mead, Nevada (1993–1997)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report, prepared by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] in March 1999, documents five years of seine-based fish surveys (July 1993 to December 1997) in the lower [[Virgin River]]. The study was started as a cooperative effort with the [[Nevada Division Of Wildlife]] to determine whether the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) persisted in the lower river, whether it reproduced locally, and which habitats allowed it to survive low flows. In 1996 and 1997 the study area was extended upstream to [[Beaver Dam Wash Reach]] and downstream to [[Mesquite Reach]].

Across 28 sampling trips, the authors concluded that a self-sustaining woundfin population persisted below Riverside, Nevada, despite very low summer flows and abundant [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). Physical habitat, rather than flow or red shiner density, appeared to determine where woundfin lived.

## Background & Description

The lower Virgin River has been heavily altered since irrigation withdrawals began in the late 1800s, and the lowest 35 miles have been inundated by [[Lake Mead]] since the mid-1930s. Woundfin and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*) are both listed as endangered. Woundfin historically inhabited main-channel habitats and were likely abundant. Red shiner, introduced in the 1950s or 1960s, was considered a probable factor in woundfin declines.

Monitoring by the Woundfin Recovery Team in the 1970s and 1980s showed woundfin concentrated near Beaver Dam Wash and the [[Bunkerville Diversion]], with few fish at the Riverside and Halfway Wash stations. Following a dramatic drop in the mid-1980s, the Recovery Team concluded that woundfin could not sustain a population alongside abundant red shiner, so monitoring at the Riverside stations was often skipped in the 1980s and early 1990s.

**Study objectives (1993–1995):** determine whether woundfin persisted in the lower river; whether they reproduced locally or arrived by downstream drift; and which habitats supported them through low flows.

**Study objectives (1996–1997):** assess the importance of different reaches, test the contribution of downstream drift to the Riverside population, and identify physical habitat factors that maintain woundfin.

**Methods:** A stratified random design sampled run, riffle, pool, backwater and eddy habitats in 0.25-mile sections, primarily with a 4 ft x 15 ft seine (0.125 in. mesh). Habitat, depth and substrate were recorded for each sample. Catch rates were expressed per 10,000 ft² seined and log-transformed. A significance level of 0.1 was used because of high natural variation and small sample sizes. Electrofishing was attempted once and abandoned as ineffective in turbid water. Trips were not made when flows exceeded 300 cfs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime:** 1993 and 1995 were high-flow years, with a 1995 flood reaching 14,300 cfs. In 1994 and 1996, summer flows at Riverside were less than 5 cfs, and the river was dry above the Riverside Bridge in July and August 1994 and 1996. Irrigation diversions below the [[Littlefield Gage]] reduced downstream flows.

**Temperature:** Stream temperatures reached about 35°C during late summer low-flow periods, with a maximum of 36°C in August 1996 and a 15.5°C daily swing in July 1994.

**Clarity:** The river is normally turbid. During the low-flow summers of 1994 and 1996, water below Riverside was exceptionally clear because it consisted mainly of channel seepage.

**Substrate and geology:** Beaver Dam Wash reach has the highest gradient (0.5%), the most cobble and gravel, and cliffs that fix the channel. Mesquite reach (gradient 0.3%) is about 80% sand. Riverside reach (gradient 0.2%) is mostly sand with somewhat more riffles.

**Habitat comparison, December 1997:**

**Critical Thermal Maximum (CTM):** [[Woundfin]] 39.5°C; [[Red Shiner]] about 39°C; flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker and speckled dace about 37°C; Virgin River chub about 36.5°C (Deacon et al. 1987).

## Key Findings

**Woundfin persistence and reproduction**
- Woundfin were absent from the first trip (July 1993) but present on every subsequent trip. The greatest numbers were captured in low-flow summer 1994, peaking at 160 per trip.
- Young-of-the-year (YOY) appeared in 1994 and 1995. The authors inferred that woundfin matured at the end of their first year, spawned twice, and disappeared by about 24 months of age.
- At Riverside, 20 small YOY were found, compared with three at Mesquite and none at Beaver Dam Wash. The authors concluded that reproduction was self-sustaining in the Riverside reach and probably at Mesquite.
- The Riverside population was estimated at roughly 200 to 400 individuals, depending on season and reproductive success.
- Stocking by the Nevada Division of Wildlife (about 23,500 woundfin and 1,100 Virgin River chub from October 1993 to April 1997) was accounted for. Of nearly 1,000 wild woundfin, roughly 20 untagged stocked fish may have been included.

**Habitat use during low, clear flows**
- Normal woundfin habitat is sand runs. During low-flow summers, woundfin concentrated in small pools and runs immediately below riffles. Up to 38 fish were taken in a single haul, compared with about two per haul at other times.
- Pools made up 43% of woundfin captures against 18% of samples in July and August 1994.

**Distribution and downstream movement**
- Woundfin were consistently found from RM 49.25 to RM 41.00, peaking near RM 45–46 in 1993–1994. Distribution shifted upstream after the 1995 and 1997 floods.
- The Beaver Dam Wash reach held only seven woundfin over two years, all in 1996.
- The authors judged downstream drift an unlikely primary source, citing the concentrated YOY pattern and the lack of fish in the Virgin Gorge. Some limited drift is possible.

**Causes of decline and community patterns**
- Catch rates of all native species dropped after August 1994. The authors attributed this to low flow, high temperatures, and predation by fish-eating birds, which may have affected concentrated fish.
- Beaver Dam Wash supported much higher densities of flannelmouth sucker, desert sucker, speckled dace and Virgin River chub. The authors linked this to stable flows, cobble substrate, depth and cooler summer temperatures.
- The authors suggest that substrate change from sand to cobble at Beaver Dam Wash in the mid-1980s may have contributed to the woundfin decline there, rather than red shiner alone.

**Other species**
- Red shiner were the most abundant fish, with catch rates often above 1,000 per 10,000 ft² compared with 10 or fewer for natives. Densities were similar among reaches, so red shiner did not explain differences in native fish distribution.
- Seven Virgin River chub were collected at Riverside in 1993–1995. Chub were most common at Beaver Dam Wash, where pools and runs are deeper.
- Ten Virgin spinedace were taken, all in July 1995, probably washed down during the spring flood.
- Western mosquitofish density rose sharply in low-flow 1994, when the species occupied run and riffle margins. Common carp were widespread, and most were YOY.
- Sporadic nonnative species included channel catfish, black bullhead, largemouth bass and green sunfish.

**Management conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors concluded that physical habitat, not flow or red shiner density, was the main factor for woundfin. A mixture of sand runs and riffles appeared important, which differs from the traditional view that sand runs alone are key.
- Flow augmentation to the Riverside reach may help by reducing late-summer temperatures.
- Recommended future work: continue seasonal sampling at Riverside and Mesquite; mark wild woundfin with reach-specific marks for mark-recapture estimates; intensify habitat measurement; and sample for YOY in May or June using finer mesh (0.0625 in.) in low-velocity habitats.
