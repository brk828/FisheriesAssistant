# Bylas Springs and the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow: Long-Term Relationships to Changes in Climate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 annual report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Arizona Fishery Resources Office describes the first full year of a long-term monitoring program on the [[Bylas Springs]] complex, three small springs in east-central Arizona that hold one of the few remaining natural stocks of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]], an endangered fish. The study was funded under the climate change component of the [[Fishery Resources Monitoring Program]] and asks how persistent, localized climatic change affects topminnow habitat. Monitoring covered water temperature (hourly thermographs), water quality, discharge, topminnow abundance and spatial distribution, and local climate records. The authors conclude that discharge, ambient temperature and rainfall likely jointly control the quantity and quality of habitat available to the fish.

## Background & Description

The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a poeciliid fish endemic to the Gila River basin of Arizona and New Mexico and to the Concepción and Sonora basins of Sonora, Mexico. It was formerly widespread but was listed as endangered in 1967. Declines were attributed to predation by the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and to habitat degradation. Populations in the USA are now restricted to shallow, marginal habitats of a few isolated springs.

Two of the 10 known natural stocks remaining in the Gila River basin occupy the Bylas Springs system, which lies adjacent to the Gila River floodplain in the upland [[Sonoran Desert]] at about 853 m elevation, with average annual precipitation of about 25 to 28 cm. Two endemic hydrobiid snails, *Tryonia gilae* and *Apachecoccus arizonae*, also occur in the system. Base flows range from a few to a few tens of liters per minute, and lower reaches become intermittent by desiccating or percolating into the terrace.

Springs are named by convention: S1 (western-most, with two spring heads), S2 (middle), and S3 (eastern-most). Management emphasizes control of introduced mosquitofish, and surface water depletion is the primary threat throughout the basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** S3 has relatively high, consistent discharge (tens of liters per minute) and stable temperatures. S2 has reduced discharge and consistently higher temperatures. S1 shows the greatest temperature variation.
- **Climate:** Air temperatures peaked in June and July 1993, and rainfall was most prevalent in January and August. Climate data came from the Arizona Meteorological Network station at Safford, about 48 km southeast of Bylas Springs.
- **Biotic interactions:** Mosquitofish made up the majority of catches at all locations except the East spring head of S1. Predation may limit survival of young topminnow.
- **Habitat:** Low discharge in S2 made habitats more homogeneous. Topminnow persisted only at the margins of shallow pools and in deeply incised spring heads.
- **Hypothesis:** Sustained reductions in discharge, such as those under increased regional aridity, may raise base flow temperatures and reduce shallow pool habitat.

## Key Findings

**Objectives:** The study set six objectives: hourly water temperature and daily air extremes; monthly water quality and discharge; seasonal topminnow abundance and distribution; vegetation mapping within drainages; effects of major precipitation events; and compilation of historical data. Discharge-frequency and flow duration curves were planned for each spring.

**Temperature (hourly thermographs, Nov 1992 to Oct 1993):**
- S1 (near barrier): monthly mean temperatures ranged from about 17.3 °C (Nov 1992) to 21.4 °C (Apr 1993), with the widest monthly ranges (e.g., minimum 12.3 °C, maximum 22.4 °C in Nov 1992).
- S2: monthly means were consistently highest, roughly 29.4 to 30.9 °C, with monthly minimums as low as 18.5 °C and maximums up to 32.3 °C.
- S3 (near head): monthly means rose from 23.2 °C (Nov 1992) to 25.3 °C (Jul 1993), with the smallest within-month variation (standard deviations often 0.2 to 0.4 °C).

**Topminnow abundance:**
- Fish were collected in two of three springs. They were absent from S2.
- The East spring head pool of S1 held the highest concentrations, with 14 fish in June 1993, 47 in July 1993, and an average of 32 per sampling date.
- No topminnow were captured at the S1 barrier.
- At S3, fish were taken at the barrier on two of seven dates and at the road crossing (65 m upstream) on one of five dates.
- Sampling was done on nine occasions using block seines, dip nets and backpack electrofishing, with each reach sampled until two consecutive passes yielded no fish.

**Water quality (monthly, 1993):**
- S1 West head: pH 7.0 to 8.5; dissolved oxygen 2.0 to 3.4 ppm; conductivity 3,840 to 4,290 µS (reported as micromhos).
- S1 East head: pH 8.0 to 10.3; dissolved oxygen 4.0 to 5.8 ppm; conductivity 1,290 to 1,710 µS.
- S2 head: pH 6.7 to 8.4; dissolved oxygen 2.1 to 4.0 ppm; conductivity 3,620 to 3,810 µS.
- S3 head: pH 6.3 to 8.0; dissolved oxygen 2.1 to 6.9 ppm; conductivity 3,650 to 5,940 µS.

**Habitat physical assessment:** Depth, velocity and substrate were measured on three occasions in 1993 along transects. Substrates were scored from silt (low heterogeneity) to vegetation/detritus (high heterogeneity). S2 was sampled every 5 m along its full reach, and the other springs in two 100 m segments.

**Conclusions and plans:**
- Discharge, ambient temperature and rainfall may jointly regulate habitat quantity and quality for topminnow.
- Differences in discharge and temperature among the three springs offer a natural basis for predicting habitat resiliency under different climate scenarios.
- Data from several consecutive years are needed before flow models can be built.
- Historical data are being compiled by the San Carlos Fishery Resources Office and entered into dBase IV.
