# Mechanisms Structuring Stream Fish Assemblages: Lessons From Introduced Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Stephen T Ross]] (University of Southern Mississippi) asks whether the introduction of [[Exotic Fishes]] and transplanted native species can reveal the processes that structure [[Stream Fish Assemblages]]. The author treats unplanned introductions as natural experimental perturbations. Of 31 studies covering 26 systems, the majority (77%) documented declines in native fishes after an introduction. Biotic interactions were implicated in 62% of systems, and the proposed mechanisms were split evenly between [[Competition]] and [[Predation]]. The paper concludes that pre-disturbance data are largely lacking, so the processes that originally structured these assemblages cannot be determined.

## Background & Description

Introduced fishes have become a problem of global extent. In North America, at least 42 exotic species have established within about 300 years, and introduction rates rose sharply after the 1960s. Most exotic fishes have had undesirable effects on native assemblages. Native species are also frequently transplanted between watersheds where they do not naturally occur.

The paper frames these introductions as a potential research opportunity. Rather than simply documenting damage, the author asks what native responses reveal about the relative importance of biotic versus abiotic structuring processes. The work builds on earlier reviews by [[Simberloff 1981]], [[Courtenay and Hensley 1980]], and [[Moyle 1986]], which disagreed on whether introductions typically cause competition or have little effect.

The conceptual framework follows Schoener's (1987) axis 3, physical versus biological control. The author argues that benign streams are generally controlled by biotic interactions, while physically harsh streams are controlled more by abiotic factors. Introduced species should therefore establish more readily in non-equilibrial, abiotically controlled systems, where resource competition is minimal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diversity and invasion success.** Southeastern US streams hold more native species than northern or western streams. The author tested whether less saturated, lower-diversity faunas are more open to invasion. Five low-diversity western states (California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico) had 36 documented introductions, compared with 9 in five high-diversity states (Alabama, Kentucky, Tennessee, Illinois, Mississippi). Establishment success was somewhat higher in the low-diversity states (reported as 44%) than in the high-diversity states (33%). The author notes that sampling effort could bias these comparisons.

**Establishment rates.** Table 1 gives establishment rates of 38–77% for known introductions across regions, including Florida, California, Nevada, Pennsylvania, Europe, and New Zealand. These figures may overestimate success because many introductions go unrecorded. Higher success in California and Nevada is linked to human habitat alteration, especially reservoir construction and irrigation canals.

**Pathways of impact.** Taylor et al. (1984) classified exotic effects into habitat alteration, disease or parasite introduction, hybridization, trophic alteration, and spatial alteration. This paper focuses on trophic effects (competition and predation), because these reveal functional control mechanisms.

**Causes of failure.** The author lists three reasons an introduction might be eliminated: physiological intolerance of the new environment (probably the most common), biological interaction with resident fishes (little information available), and an initial population too small for reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Literature base.**
- 31 papers covering 26 systems met the inclusion criteria.
- 69% of systems were in North America.
- 60% of systems were affected by a single introduced species, and over half of those species were salmonids.
- Studies lacking pre-impact data were excluded.

**Population responses.**
- Roughly 77% of cases showed reduction or elimination of native fishes, agreeing with [[Herbold and Moyle 1986]] and differing sharply from Simberloff's (1981) finding that 79% of introductions had no effect.
- Documented examples include declines of native [[Brook Trout]] after introductions of [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], declines of western and southwestern fishes after [[Mosquitofish]] introductions, and depletion of galaxiid faunas in New Zealand and Australia after salmonid introductions.

**Growth and reproduction.**
- Data on growth responses are nearly nonexistent.
- Only one study (Meffe 1985) examined this in streams. Under laboratory, but not field, conditions, native *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* raised with *Gambusia affinis* showed lower somatic weight and reduced reproductive output.

**Resource use and niche shifts.**
- Only 10 systems were studied for shifts in resource use.
- Five studies documented or suggested micro- or macrohabitat shifts.
- No studies examined trophic dimensions.
- Fausch (1988) noted that many studies of competition in salmonids had inadequate experimental design.

**Inferences on community control.**
- Biotic interactions (competition or predation) were implicated in 16 of 26 systems (62%).
- Only five studies tested mechanisms experimentally, and three of these addressed populations rather than assemblages.
- Proposed mechanisms were divided equally between competition and predation. Predation was better documented (3 studies) than competition (2 studies).
- Deacon (1979) linked biotic interactions to problems faced by 54% of threatened western US fishes, but to only 9% of threatened eastern fishes.
- Moyle (1986) attributed the lack of disruption in eastern faunas to their stochastic, assumed non-equilibrial nature. More recent work (e.g., [[Ross et al. 1987]], [[Freeman et al. 1988]], [[Matthews et al. 1988]]) suggests some southeastern assemblages show equilibrial characteristics.

**Conclusions.**
- Introduction data provide circumstantial evidence that many post-disturbance assemblages lie nearer the biotic control end of the gradient.
- Two major difficulties limit this conclusion. First, few studies looked for subtle changes in assemblage structure or niche shape. Second, predisturbance data are generally lacking, so the processes that originally structured these communities cannot be determined.
- The author calls for planned descriptive studies and manipulations of native fishes in the absence of introduced species, and argues that because introductions are likely to continue, they should be used more deliberately to learn how natural communities function.
