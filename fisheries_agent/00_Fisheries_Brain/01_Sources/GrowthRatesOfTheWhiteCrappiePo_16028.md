## Executive Summary
This study, conducted by the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources, analyzed age and growth data for 925 [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) sampled from [[Kentucky Lake]] in 1952. The research aimed to define population factors supporting a successful crappie fishery in the reservoir, following earlier netting studies that focused primarily on rough fish. The findings reveal that Kentucky Lake crappie exhibit growth rates significantly higher than comparable populations in Indiana and Ohio, reaching legal/catchable sizes rapidly. The study concludes that due to high growth rates and early sexual maturity, minimal size restrictions are necessary for sustainable management of this species.

## Background & Description
- **Length-Weight Relationship:** The relationship is defined by the standard formula $W = cL^n$, calculated specifically for this population as:
  $$Log W = -3.7664 + 3.4566 Log L$$
- **Growth Trajectory (Calculated Lengths):**
  - End of Year 1: 4.63 inches
  - End of Year 2: 7.87 inches
  - End of Year 3: 10.44 inches
  - End of Year 4: 11.86 inches
  - End of Year 5: 12.76 inches
- **Growth Increments:** The largest growth increment occurred between Year 2 and Year 3 (5.27 inches), following an initial slower increment in Year 1 relative to other studied populations (e.g., [[Foots Pond]], Indiana).
- **Age Structure:** The oldest fish sampled were in their sixth year of life (Age Group V). No fish younger than Age Group 0 (young of year) were effectively sampled due to gear selectivity.
- **Sexual Dimorphism (Size at Maturity):**
  - Males matured earlier and at smaller sizes; the smallest mature male was 7.5 inches.
  - The smallest mature female recorded was 9.3 inches.
  - 97.19% of crappie over 9 inches were sexually mature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Context:** [[Kentucky Lake]] is a massive 260,000-acre impoundment on the Tennessee River, characterized by shallow average depth (23 feet), sand/muck substrates, and gradually sloping shallow shelf areas which likely provide productive nursery/feeding habitat.
- **Population Context:** Initially considered a minor component of the fish community (comprising only 0.19% of the total weight of commercial rough-fish net catches), the [[White Crappie]] population supported a consistently high-success recreational fishery over the preceding three years of the study.
- **Sex Ratio:** A skewed sex ratio was observed in the sampled population, with 636 females to 190 males (a ratio of 335 females per 100 males), suggesting potential differential vulnerability to gear or natural mortality between sexes.
- **Reproductive Strategy:** The species exhibits a highly prolific reproductive strategy, which the author notes provides "adequate protection under almost any circumstances" against overfishing.

## Key Findings
1. **Superior Growth Rates:** Kentucky Lake crappie displayed growth rates substantially exceeding other regional studies. For example, the Kentucky Lake population reached 10.44 inches by year 3, compared to only 7.8 inches reported by [[Johnson (1945)]] for Greenwood Lake, Indiana, and required only 5 years to reach 12.76 inches, compared to Ohio populations needing 5 years to reach just 12.1 inches ([[Caldwell]]).
2. **Gear Selectivity Bias:** The methodology confirmed that netting and rotenone sampling techniques were biased toward larger, older individuals, leading to an underrepresentation of Age Group 0 and I fish in the initial population estimates, despite the reservoir hosting a viable spawning population.
3. **Annulus Formation Timing:** Consistent with findings by [[Hansen (1951)]] regarding Illinois populations, the majority of scales collected in April had not yet formed the current year's annulus, implying annulus formation likely occurs in late spring (May), similar to Lake Decatur, Illinois populations.
4. **Management Implications:** Due to the exceptionally high growth rate and the fact that nearly all individuals over 9 inches were mature, the study concluded that a specific size limit was unnecessary for the white crappie population. The existing statewide creel limit of 30 (combined White and Black Crappie) was deemed sufficient primarily to regulate potential commercial exploitation rather than to protect spawning stock.
5. **Rough Fish Impact:** The study determined that competition or "crowding" from rough fish species did not present a significant ecological problem for the game fish populations in this specific impoundment.
