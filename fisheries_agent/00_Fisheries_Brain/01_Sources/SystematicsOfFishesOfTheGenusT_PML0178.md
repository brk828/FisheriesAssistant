# Systematics of Fishes of the Genus Tilapia (Perciformes: Cichlidae) in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University master's thesis (Paul James Barrett, 1983) reviews the introduction and taxonomy of African cichlids of the genus [[Tilapia]] in the American Southwest. Using meristic, morphometric and pigmentation data from collections made between 1964 and 1982, the author concludes that [[Tilapia Mossambica]] and [[Tilapia Hornorum]] have largely hybridized into a single reproducing population, that [[Tilapia Aurea]] is now the dominant cichlid in Arizona, and that T. aurea is hybridizing with the T. mossambica complex in a drainage canal near San Luis, Arizona. The conclusions are explicitly described as tentative.

## Background & Description

- African cichlids were introduced to the United States in the 1940s and 1950s for food production and biological control of plants and insects.
- Taxonomic stability was not achieved before introduction. [[Tilapia Aurea]] was synonymized with [[Tilapia Nilotica]] until Trewavas (1965) resurrected it, and T. hornorum was described as a full species after both it and T. mossambica had been introduced.
- Hybrids of T. mossambica and T. hornorum (the "Malacca" hybrid) were produced experimentally and were favored for pond culture because of predominantly male offspring. Not all hybrids were infertile, which compounded early confusion.
- Generic-level taxonomy remains contested: Trewavas (1973) elevated the subgenus *Sarotherodon* to generic rank (opposed by Thys van den Audenaerde 1978 and Robins et al. 1980), and Trewavas later proposed *Oreochromis* for the mossambica, niloticus and aurea group.
- Identification of the species relies on fin-ray and gill-raker counts, body proportions and breeding-male coloration. Breeding males of T. hornorum and T. mossambica are both black-bodied, but differ in fin margins and gill-raker counts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Much of the lower Colorado River basin is subtropical, but it still supports these fishes, and the author notes that problems with African cichlids in the region (Minckley 1973) motivated the study.
- Fish were stocked from hatcheries with little pattern beyond a tendency to select low-elevation habitats. Thermal ponds, irrigation canals, and urban lakes (used for aquatic vegetation control) were common sites.
- The T. mossambica complex formerly occupied most of the Gila River from Tempe to its mouth and the lower Colorado River from Imperial Dam to the Gulf of California. Since about 1970 it has been displaced, and its populations are becoming spotty.
- The San Luis drainage canal population is subject to irrigation-driven water-level fluctuations, high turbidity from agriculture, and year-round cultivation, all conditions the author considers conducive to hybridization. Turbidity may also disrupt visually cued mate choice in cichlids.
- Minckley (1979) did not find T. aurea from San Luis in 1975, indicating the population arrived later.

## Key Findings

- **Introduction history:** Tilapia mossambica was imported to Arizona from Hawaii in 1961 and experimented with at the AGFD Papago Park hatchery. By 1964 it was established near the Salton Sea, and it was recorded in Bard Valley, California, in 1968. Officially stocked in southern California in 1971. T. hornorum entered Arizona in 1962.
- **Hybridization in the T. mossambica complex:** Specimens (n = 203 analyzed) could not be assigned with certainty to T. hornorum or T. mossambica. Meristic means lay between the two species, and the author concludes the complex represents hybridization and merger into one reproducing population. Hybrid males produce nearly 100% male offspring and reciprocal crosses yield a 3:1 male-female ratio (Hickling 1960). The hybrids are partly fertile and can backcross.
- **Dominance of T. aurea:** T. aurea has spread rapidly from its introduction and is now dominant in southwestern Arizona. Its distribution ranges from east of Phoenix into Mexico, and upstream along the Colorado River at least to Imperial Reservoir. Its dorsal spine counts (mean 15.6 in lower basin fish) trend toward T. nilotica.
- **Possible T. nilotica presence:** Two specimens from Alamo Reservoir (ASU 10180 and UA 80-15) have darkly fringed dorsal fins and banded caudal fins suggesting T. nilotica, but their dorsal spine counts (15) fall well below the T. nilotica range. The authors conclude these most likely belong to T. aurea, with possible genetic contamination.
- **San Luis population:** PCA and DFA classified 99.3% of reference specimens correctly (jackknife). Of the San Luis fish, 83 were classified as T. mossambica and 9 as T. aurea by DFA. Morphometric classification placed 26.8% of the San Luis fish with T. aurea, the highest share of any analysis. Pigmentation of fins did not differ significantly between San Luis and T. mossambica, but banding did. The author interprets the population as introgression from T. aurea × T. mossambica hybrids, with mixed morphology (mossambica-like meristics and jaws, aurea-like body shape and coloration).
- **Tilapia zillii:** Established in Arizona; specimens have 11 to 16 gill rakers, 14 to 16 dorsal spines and 10 to 13 dorsal rays. A "Tilapia marking" is retained on the dorsal fin even in adults.
- **Coptodon rendalli (tentative):** Specimens from near Blythe, California, previously identified as T. zillii, were tentatively re-identified as T. rendalli based on dorsal fin and caudal fin coloration, a lighter lower lip, and meristic counts (gill rakers 12-13, dorsal spines 14-16, dorsal rays 12-13). PCA separated them from T. zillii.
- **Commercial stocks:** Nilotica-type fish were being reared in fish farms in Scottsdale, Arizona, making further introductions possible.
- **Conclusion:** The author describes the conclusions as tentative and more a statement of the current evolutionary status of these introduced exotics than a definitive systematic revision. Further research should include careful color descriptions and photographs, since color diversity fades after preservation.
