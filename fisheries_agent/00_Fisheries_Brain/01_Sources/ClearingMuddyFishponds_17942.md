# Clearing Muddy Fishponds

#aquaculture #pond-management #water-quality

## Executive Summary

This Alabama Polytechnic Institute Extension Service leaflet (4H-35) provides practical guidance for clearing muddy fishponds using gypsum (calcium sulfate) treatment. Authored by Earl Franklin Kennamer, Specialist in Fish and Wildlife Marketing, the document addresses the negative impacts of turbidity on fish production and presents both temporary chemical treatment and permanent erosion control measures. The leaflet is designed as a 4-H Club educational resource with demonstration protocols.

## Background & Description

Muddy ponds produce fewer pounds of fish than clear ponds due to two primary mechanisms: (1) turbid water blocks sunlight necessary for producing fish food (presumably phytoplankton and aquatic vegetation), and (2) heavy mud deposits flowing into ponds cover and kill fish roe (eggs). 

Pond turbidity varies in duration and cause. Some ponds clear naturally within days after rainfall, while newly constructed ponds may remain muddy for weeks due to clay particles from dam construction, scraped pond bottoms, and disturbed nearby areas. Some ponds never clear naturally due to persistent clay material suspended in the water column.

The document identifies regular plasterer's gypsum ([[Calcium Sulfate]]) as an effective and inexpensive flocculating agent that settles clay particles. Treatment effectiveness lasts one year or longer under stable conditions, but is ineffective if heavy rainfall washes the gypsum out of the pond system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasizes watershed management as critical to long-term water quality. Muddy water originating from external sources requires permanent erosion control measures rather than repeated chemical treatment. The recommended integrated approach includes:

- Planting cover crops on hillsides to prevent soil erosion
- Installing diversion ditches to redirect excess water flow
- Riprapping raw banks and dams with logs or rocks to prevent wave-action erosion
- Establishing [[Centipede Grass]] or [[Bermuda Grass]] on dams and bare banks
- Fencing ponds and providing alternative livestock watering through below-dam troughs supplied by pipes

These measures address the root causes of turbidity while protecting aquatic habitat from continuous sediment loading and physical disturbance.

## Key Findings

**Gypsum Treatment Protocol:**
- Application rate: 15 pounds of gypsum per 1,000 cubic feet of water for chronically muddy ponds
- Supplemental treatment: Add up to 25 additional pounds if pond does not clear within 2 weeks
- Application method: Shovel gypsum from a boat, spreading evenly over pond surface, then immediately stir water with an outboard motorboat to distribute the flocculating agent
- Duration of effectiveness: One year or longer under stable hydrological conditions

**Demonstration Protocols:**

*Fruit Jar Demonstration:*
1. Fill two clean quart jars with water from a constantly muddy pond or pothole
2. Add 2 heaping tablespoonfuls of gypsum to one jar, shake well, and label "Treated"
3. Label the second jar "Untreated" without adding gypsum
4. After several hours, treated water becomes clear while untreated water remains muddy

*Pond Demonstration:*
- 4-H Club agents should document pond treatment with color photographs before treatment and after clearing
- Before-and-after images can be converted to slides for educational presentations to clubs and farm groups
- Additional photographic documentation should show permanent erosion control measures

**4-H Project Requirements:**
- Demonstrate fruit jar test with muddy water
- Visit one pond with excessively muddy water and outline detailed corrective measures
