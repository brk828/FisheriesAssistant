# Benthic Environment and Sediment Studies of Twin Lakes, Colorado

#limnology #benthos #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-76-12, October 1976) compiles three studies of [[Twin Lakes]], a pair of connected glacial lakes in Lake County, Colorado. The work was a baseline before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operating, so that future short-term and long-term effects could be measured against it. The three papers cover (1) the profundal benthic fauna, (2) the age and composition of the bottom sediments, and (3) bacterial conditions in water and mud.

The benthic fauna is sparse overall. [[Chironomus]] midge larvae dominate, and [[Fingernail Clams]] occur mainly in the lower lake. The upper lake is depleted after severe winters because anaerobic bottom water releases [[Heavy Metals]]. Sediment cores show rising copper in recent upper-lake sediments, which the authors link to mining that began about 100 to 125 years ago. Coliform bacteria are very low, but iron and sulfur bacteria are abundant in the mud, which creates a corrosion risk for the planned powerplant.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The report is one part of an ecological investigation of the impact of pumped-storage operation on Twin Lakes. Its stated purpose was to document conditions before operation, because little was previously known about the benthic environment. Work was coordinated by [[James F. LaBounty]] of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The three papers are by LaBounty and [[James J. Sartoris]], by [[E. P. Bergersen]] (Colorado State University), and by [[Wayne O. Deason]].

**Lake setting.** Twin Lakes lies in the Southern Rocky Mountain Physiographic Province at about 39°05' N, 106°20' W, on Lake Creek, a tributary of the Arkansas River. Lake Creek basin drains 238 km² and includes Mt. Elbert (4399 m), the highest point in Colorado. The upper and lower lakes have maximum surface areas of 263 ha and 737 ha, and maximum depths of 28 m and 27 m. The lakes are classed as relatively oligotrophic, second-class dimictic lakes.

**Fauna in brief.** The benthic macrofauna sampled comprised:
- Mollusca (Sphaeriidae): *Pisidium casertanum* and *P. pauperculum sterki*.
- Diptera (Chironomidae): *Chironomus* sp., *Phaenopsectra* sp., and *Dicrotendipes modestus*.

Oligochaetes were noted occasionally but were not identified. The introduced [[Mysis Relicta]] is abundant and is a major factor in the [[Lake Trout]] fishery. Native fish historically included two trout and a minnow, but the only native species now present is the [[Common Sucker]]. Benthic fauna are the main food of the common sucker, and midges are eaten heavily by trout during emergence.

**Methods.** Samples were taken with [[Petersen Dredge]] and [[Ekman Dredge]] samplers. Sieving used a No. 30 sieve (590 µm), and samples were preserved in 10% formalin, counted, and weighed wet and dry (dried at 40°C for at least 24 h). Sampling ran from June 1974 through April 1975 at least monthly, then twice monthly at selected stations from June through November 1975. Sediment cores were sliced at 5 mm intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting.**
- Bottom sediments are covered by [[Glacial Flour]]. Divers reported that fin movement 1 to 2 m above the bottom raises turbidity within 3 to 4 m of the bottom, and flour was found at depths of 4 to 23 m.
- Water level fluctuations increased from an estimated 0.6 m to 7.8 m after the dam was built (1907), and a diversion tunnel was added in 1935. These changes reduced a marsh at the western end of the upper lake and added organic debris to its bottom.
- Water is soft. Calcium is the main cation, and bicarbonate followed by sulfate are the main anions.
- Winter ice lasts from early or mid-December to late April or May, with ice 630 to 760 mm thick. Dissolved oxygen fell to 0.0 mg/L on April 17, 1975 at the bottom of the upper lake, and to 2.4 mg/L at the bottom of the lower lake on April 18, 1975.
- Spring turnover in 1975 showed about 0.09 mg/L copper and 0.06 mg/L zinc in the upper lake.

**Anaerobic cycle and metals.** Long, snow-covered winters exclude light, and low inflow from frozen Lake Creek cannot recharge the hypolimnion. Organic debris from the former marsh and untreated sewage raise the biochemical oxygen demand. Oxygen is then exhausted, reducing conditions release heavy metals from the sediment, and the lower hypolimnion of the upper lake becomes toxic to aquatic life at times.

**Human and historical influence.** Zooplankton has changed since the early 1900s. [[Juday]] (1906) recorded 6 rotifer, 5 copepod and 10 cladoceran species. Based on over 500 collections, only rotifers and 2 copepod species now remain, and the authors attribute the loss of cladocerans to the mysis shrimp.

**Sediment context.** The bottom sediments are unvarved. Lake Creek carries a considerable sediment load, and the upper lake appears to act as a nutrient and material trap.

## Key Findings

### 1. Benthic fauna distribution
- Benthic macrofauna is comparatively sparse. *Chironomus* is by far the most abundant group, and clams occur almost exclusively in the lower lake.
- Chironomid abundance increases with depth. Clam abundance peaks at a shallower depth, and no clear depth trend was found for clams.
- The lower lake has higher abundance at its center, and abundance drops toward the shores and where sediment is coarser.
- Clams have never been found at or near upper-lake station 4.

### 2. Seasonal dynamics (lower lake)
- Midge larvae abundance fell from a high of about 3800/m² (February and April) to about 1500/m² in November 1975. This is a loss of 2300/m² (58%), or about 11/m² per day.
- Most of the spring loss came from emergence of adult midges, which peaked in mid-June after ice-off on about May 17. Predation by common suckers also causes continuing losses.
- Chironomid larvae make up about 47% of common sucker stomach contents.
- Biomass lost during summer 1975 was 150 kg/ha (54%), or 1.25 kg/ha per day.
- Individual larvae grew at a mean of 0.38 mg/day, or 0.8% per day. In Lake Itasca, Minnesota, growth was 0.2% per day at 6 m and 1.0% per day at 11.5 to 12.5 m. The authors conclude the midges of Twin Lakes are univoltine.

### 3. Upper lake die-off
- Upper-lake larvae reached about 200/m² in June 1974 after spring turnover. After the severe 1974–75 winter, larvae were scarce and some samplings had none.
- The authors conclude that a die-off of benthic invertebrates occurs in the upper lake after severe winters, caused by metal release from anaerobic sediment.
- Clam numbers in the lower lake ranged from 20 to 600/m², with significantly more collected in 1975. The authors consider the lower-lake clam population to be thriving.

### 4. Comparative benchmarks
- Under the Northcote and Larkin classification, the lower lake is "abundant." The upper lake has the most sparse benthic fauna of any lake reported in the compared literature.
- Using the Hayes/Rawson framework, the lower lake is ranked near the mean for abundance among compared lakes, while the upper lake is oligotrophic.

### 5. Sediment accumulation and age
- Sedimentation was estimated from [[Cesium-137 Dating]]. The lower-lake peak lies at 5 to 10 mm depth, giving an estimated rate of 0.63 mm/yr (range 0.42 to 0.83 mm/yr).
- Since accelerated placer mining began (1859), an estimated 49 to 96 mm of sediment has accumulated.
- The sedimentation rate is higher than in small Colorado lakes studied by Pennak, which is consistent with Lake Creek's sediment load.

### 6. Sediment composition and metals
- The upper lake had significantly higher total carbon than the lower lake, which the authors read as evidence of nutrient trapping.
- Copper rose steadily from about 100 mm depth to the surface in the upper lake and only slightly in the lower lake. Zinc and silver showed a weaker trend, and lead was higher in the lower lake.
- The copper rise is attributed to mining in the watershed, starting about 100 to 125 years ago.

### 7. Diatom profiles
- The araphidineae/centric (A/C) ratio was used as a trophic indicator. Oligotrophic lakes have ratios of 0 to 1.0, mesotrophic 1.0 to 2.0, and eutrophic above 2.
- In midlake upper-lake cores, the ratio rises sharply at 100 to 150 mm depth, which suggests a shift toward eutrophic conditions. Lower-lake values were generally below 1.
- The author cautions that this may not reflect true eutrophication, because primary productivity is lower in the upper lake. Heavy metals under anaerobic conditions may have affected the diatoms.

### 8. Bacteriology
- Coliform densities are very low at all sites over a 3-month period. Mean total coliforms were 3.1 at site 5 and 6.4 at site 6 (upper lake), and all counts were below EPA recommended limits for public freshwater supplies (2000 fecal and 10,000 total coliforms per 100 mL).
- Highest counts occurred in September at site 6, attributed to warmer weather and more visitors.
- Mud coliform counts were under 100 per gram at all stations.

### 9. Corrosion risk for the powerplant
- Bottom mud contains about 1% iron by dry weight, and the orange flocculent surface material contains about 9%.
- Iron bacteria were high at sites 2 and 3. All mud samples contained sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, and sulfate-reducing *Desulfovibrio* are a concern in anaerobic, buried, or stagnant pipe sections.
- The author concludes that the high-velocity, highly oxygenated forebay and afterbay are unlikely to be affected, but buried pipe in wet soil and stagnant pipe seams may undergo bacterial corrosion.

### 10. Recommendations and purpose
- The data serve as a pre-operation baseline. The studies are to be repeated shortly after pumped-storage operation begins and again years later to detect short- and long-term effects.
- The benthic results set a benchmark for judging whether the powerplant alters sediment suspension, the mysis-dependent food web, or the lower-lake midge and clam populations.
