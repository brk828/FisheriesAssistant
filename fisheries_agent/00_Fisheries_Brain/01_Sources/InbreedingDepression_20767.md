# Inbreeding Depression and Adaptive Genetic Variation in Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 proposal to the [[National Science Foundation]] (NSF Conservation Research program, NSF 90-66) from [[Arizona State University]]. It was submitted by [[Philip W. Hedrick]] (Principal Investigator, Department of Zoology) and [[Wendell Minckley]] (Co-Principal Investigator). The project studies the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), a small live-bearing fish known from only nine remnant sites in Arizona.

The proposal requests **$334,514** over **36 months** (June 1, 1994 to May 31, 1997). It has three goals:

1. Measure **inbreeding depression** over two generations of sib mating.
2. Measure **heterosis** and **outbreeding depression** from crosses among four metapopulations.
3. Document variation in the **[[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC)**, the region thought to be most important for adaptive disease resistance.

The work is framed as [[Conservation Genetics]] for an endangered species that can be bred in captivity.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The Gila topminnow is a live-bearing member of the [[Poeciliidae]]. Males are less than 25 mm long and females average about 30 to 45 mm. It is one of two Sonoran topminnow subspecies in Arizona. The species was once among the most abundant fishes in the Gila River watershed. It was federally listed as endangered in 1967 and listed as threatened by the State of Arizona in 1988. Its decline has been attributed to loss of shallow-water habitat and to the introduction of [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish). It is still present in Sonora, Mexico, though exotic fishes and development are also affecting it there.

**Prior support.** The investigators' previous NSF award (BSR-8923007; June 1989 to May 1992; $140,000), "Conservation Genetics and Inbreeding Depression," used [[Drosophila Melanogaster]] as a model. Key results reported in the proposal include:

- Male-mating success was affected by inbreeding much more than viability or female fecundity.
- A faster rate of inbreeding produced a greater decrease in fitness components.
- Theory suggests that inbreeding depression caused by lethal genes can be purged in a few generations, while depression caused by many small-effect detrimental alleles is not easily purged.
- In the Askania Nova [[Przewalski's Horse]] pedigree, only 6.823 distinct genes out of 24 possible survived at the average locus.

**Conceptual framework.** The proposal presents three ideas:

- *Inbreeding depression* is the loss of fitness from increased homozygosity. In [[Drosophila Melanogaster]] studies, about half of the viability effect was attributed to recessive lethal alleles. Ralls et al. (1988) estimated lethal equivalents in captive mammals from 0.01 (Sumatran tiger) to 9.17 (brown lemur).
- *[[Heterosis]]* can occur when crosses between populations fixed for different recessive alleles mask those alleles in hybrids.
- *[[Outbreeding Depression]]* can occur after longer isolation, when hybrids or F2 progeny lose fitness through recombination of locally adapted gene complexes.

**Fish-specific background.** The proposal reviews inbreeding studies in fishes (mostly trout and commercially important species). It notes effects on survival, growth, courtship and deformities. Some effects appeared only after several generations, as in the convict cichlid, or were not significant after one generation, as in guppies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current distribution.** As of 1993, remnant populations exist at **nine locations** in springs and stream headwaters. These form **four metapopulations**, defined after the 1993 draft recovery plan as all populations in one sub-basin with some probability of gene flow:

| Metapopulation | Sites / setting | Notes from the proposal |
|---|---|---|
| (A) [[Bylas Springs]] | Near the mainstem Gila River | About 110 miles overland from the other sites; probably recent, linked to a 1914 change in the Gila River course |
| (B) [[Cienega Creek]] | Creek headwaters and springs | Thought to be the largest population; lower creek dry for decades |
| (C) San Rafael Valley | Includes [[Sharp Spring]] | Thermally fluctuating arroyo with deep pools |
| (D) [[Sonoita Creek]] | Includes [[Cottonwood Spring]] and [[Monkey Spring]] | Monkey Spring is a constant-temperature spring (about 27 °C), separated from Sonoita Creek by a 10 m waterfall |

All sites except Cienega Creek, Cottonwood Spring and Monkey Spring are contaminated with mosquitofish. Historically, populations probably contracted during droughts and were recolonized in wet years, with possible gene flow.

**Genetic differentiation.** Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) surveyed **25 allozyme loci**. Only Sharp Spring was polymorphic, with two alleles at two loci (Pgd and Est-4). Bylas Springs and Monkey Spring were fixed for one allele set, and Cienega Creek was fixed for the alternative. The proposal reports that the species is fixed for allozyme variation at all populations except Sharp Spring.

**Life history and population differences.** Quattro and Vrijenhoek (1989) found that laboratory-reared Monkey Spring fish grew more slowly, had lower fecundity and were more asymmetrical than Sharp Spring fish. The proposal's own data on newly captured females are summarized in Table 1 of the source.

| Population | N | Length (mm, SD) | Brood size (SD) | Egg number (Quattro & Vrijenhoek) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cottonwood Spring | 10 | 35.4 (2.3) | 4.0 (text) | N/A |
| Monkey Spring | 9 | 37.2 (3.8) | 7.0 (1.7) | 5.8 |
| Sharp Spring | 11 | 36.6 (2.1) | 14.6 (2.8) | 13.1 |

Brood size at Monkey Spring was significantly smaller than at Sharp Spring. The proposal notes that clutch differences could be environmental, since Monkey Spring canal fish have more food than headspring fish.

**Asymmetry.** Mean bilateral asymmetry (right-left count difference) from Table 2 of the source:

| Trait | Sharp Spring (N=35) | Monkey Spring (N=25) | Monkey Spring 1964 museum (N=26) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lateral line scales | 0.14 | 0.56 | 0.42 |
| Pectoral fin rays | 0.03 | 0.28 | 0.077 |
| Pelvic fin rays | 0.26 | 0.84 | 0.038 |

The proposal notes that the museum sample was symmetrical, except for one fish.

**Husbandry and controlled conditions.** Experiments are planned at **26 °C**, a **14-hour photoperiod**, and **dissolved solids of 400 to 500 ppm**, which approximate desert water. The authors note that Arizona water is naturally high in dissolved solids, which makes this easier to match there.

**Conservation context.** The authors argue that understanding survival factors in the Gila topminnow will inform the management of other western native fishes, many of which face near-term extinction (Minckley and Deacon, 1991). They also state that habitat maintenance and protection remain essential, and that genetic factors are one complementary consideration.

## Key Findings

**Planned research design**

- **Inbreeding (Objective 1):** Sib-mate fish for two generations (Generation 0 inbreeding coefficient 0.125 or 0.25 in the first generation, then 0.3125 or 0.5 in the second). Measure survival to maturity (12 weeks), growth (length at 0, 6 and 12 weeks), brood size, male courtship (thrusting frequency and coloration) and bilateral asymmetry.
- **Crosses and heterosis (Objective 2):** Cross individuals among the four metapopulations. Evaluate F1 heterosis and F2 outbreeding depression on the same traits.
- **MHC variation (Objective 3):** Amplify and sequence class II exon 2 (a region involved in peptide presentation). Use primers supplied by Jan Klein, then develop restriction fragment assays and survey variation within and between the four metapopulations.

**Experimental scale**

- Collect **20 fish** from each of the four sites (permits allow 20 per locality).
- Each replicate contains **14 different matings**: sib matings, random-mating controls and interpopulation crosses.
- **Five replicates** at one time gives **70 matings and aquaria** in total, close to the facility limit.
- Two temporal replicates are planned: the first from June 1994, and the second beginning September 1, 1995, finishing about October 1996.
- The first replicate timetable is about **14 months**.

**Expected outcomes and significance**

- The proposal expects to show whether the four populations carry detrimental alleles that heterosis would hide, or whether they are different enough to show outbreeding depression.
- The MHC survey aims to show whether an allozyme-monomorphic species nonetheless carries adaptive variation.
- The authors call the work "a landmark study in conservation biology," because controlled breeding tests are feasible in an endangered species.

**Preliminary findings cited**

- Monkey Spring and Sharp Spring fish differ in clutch size, growth and asymmetry (Quattro and Vrijenhoek, 1989).
- Savolainen and Hedrick (1993) and Houle (1989) found no positive correlation between allozyme heterozygosity and fitness in large studies, questioning the expectation from Vrijenhoek et al. (1985).
- A Monkey Spring population held outdoors at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery for 12 years persisted through natural temperature fluctuations, which suggests it may tolerate varied environments.
