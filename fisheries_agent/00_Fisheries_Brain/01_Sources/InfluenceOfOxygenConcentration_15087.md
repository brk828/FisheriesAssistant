## Executive Summary
This study by Swift (1963) investigates whether dissolved oxygen concentration limits the growth of juvenile [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), testing a hypothesis proposed by Fry (1957) and Swift (1961) that the respiratory system's inability to meet oxygen demand at higher temperatures (above 12°C) causes the seasonal decline in growth rate. Ten hatchery-reared brown trout (<1 year old) were held in each of three constant-environment aquaria at 16°C, across a gradient of dissolved oxygen concentrations (100%, 50%, and 20% air saturation). After 8 weeks, specific growth rates (length and weight) were compared. No statistically significant differences in growth were found among the three oxygen treatments, indicating that oxygen availability within this range does **not** limit growth in brown trout at 16°C. This result parallels findings in [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) and [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) yolk-sac alevins, where growth was similarly unaffected until oxygen levels dropped below ~50% air saturation. The authors conclude that the temperature-related decline in salmonid growth rate is **not** caused by a restriction in oxygen-carrying/respiratory capacity.

## Background & Description
- **Life stage:** Juvenile brown trout, hatchery-reared, less than 1 year old.
- **Identification method:** Individually marked via fin (tin) clips for repeated measurement.
- **Measured traits:** Body length and body weight, recorded at the start and end of the 8-week experimental period.
- **Growth metric:** Specific growth rate expressed as percentage increase in length and weight per week.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Thermal ecology:** Brown trout growth is closely tied to seasonal water temperature changes, with maximum growth reported near [[Thermal Optimum in Salmonids|12°C]] (Swift, 1961).
- **Respiratory ecology:** The study addresses whether respiratory/oxygen-delivery limitations underlie the decline in growth at temperatures above the thermal optimum, a phenomenon previously documented in [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) by Job (1955), where active respiration becomes oxygen-limited above 19°C in larger individuals.
- **Comparative context:**
  - [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*): Herrmann et al. (1962) found no growth effect from oxygen variation above 5 mg/l at 20°C.
  - [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) yolk-sac alevins: Gray (1952) found growth effects only below 50% air saturation (tested at 65%, 56%, 46% saturation).
- **Habitat conditions replicated:** Constant-environment aquaria at 16°C, simulating a mid-range thermal condition within the trout's tolerance, with controlled dissolved oxygen via air/oxygen gas mixture stirring.
- **Feeding regime:** Fish fed to satiation daily with minced liver set in gelatine (per Swift, 1960 method), controlling for food availability as a confounding growth variable.

## Key Findings
- **Experimental design:** Three groups of 10 juvenile brown trout held at 16°C under three dissolved oxygen concentrations:
  - 100% air saturation (~10 mg O₂/l)
  - 50% air saturation (~5 mg O₂/l)
  - 20% air saturation (~2 mg O₂/l)
- **Growth rate results (mean specific growth rate ± SE, % per week):**

| Oxygen Concentration | 100% Saturation (10 mg/l) | 50% Saturation (5 mg/l) | 20% Saturation (2 mg/l) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of fish | 9 | 10 | 7 |
| Growth in Length (%/wk) | 2.2 (±0.4) | 2.4 (±0.2) | 2.3 (±0.5) |
| Growth in Weight (%/wk) | 7.5 (±1.4) | 9.5 (±0.9) | 8.8 (±1.4) |

- **Statistical outcome:** No significant difference in growth (length or weight) was detected among the three oxygen treatment groups.
- **Central conclusion:** Oxygen concentrations between 20% and 100% air saturation have **little to no effect** on salmonid growth, consistent with comparative data from coho salmon and Atlantic salmon alevins.
- **Implication for the "respiratory limitation" hypothesis:** Since growth was unaffected by substantial reductions in dissolved oxygen at a fixed temperature (16°C), the results argue **against** the hypothesis (Fry, 1957; Swift, 1961) that inadequate respiratory/oxygen-delivery capacity is the proximate cause of reduced brown trout growth at higher water temperatures (>12°C). An alternative physiological mechanism — potentially linked to [[Thyroid Activity and Seasonal Growth in Trout|thyroid gland activity]] (Swift, 1955) — is implied as more likely responsible for the seasonal/thermal growth-rate cycle.
