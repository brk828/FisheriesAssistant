# San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the cooperative plan for the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (Implementation Program). Its purpose is to protect and recover the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] while water development proceeds in compliance with federal and state law. The program is meant to run for 15 years. It was built on a 1991 [[Memorandum of Understanding]] and adopted through a 1992 [[Cooperative Agreement]]. The document also includes a 1995 [[Long Range Implementation Plan]] (LRP) and a comparison with the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]].

The core strategy is a seven-year research effort on the San Juan River, followed by recovery actions based on the results. These actions include flow management from [[Navajo Dam]], habitat protection, water quality improvement, control of non-native fishes, and augmentation of wild populations. The suggested annual budget is $1,980,000, with a stated annual shortfall of $160,000.

## Background & Description

**Basin and river.** The San Juan River sub-basin drains about 38,000 square miles of southwestern Colorado, northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, and southeastern Utah. The river is a major tributary of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It has few perennial tributaries, and the Animas River contributes the greatest flow. Mean annual discharge from 1914 to 1986 was 2,574 cfs near Bluff, Utah. Recorded flows ranged from 69,986 cfs to 0 cfs.

**Native fishes.** Surveys documented at least eight and possibly nine native species. These include Colorado River cutthroat trout, roundtail chub, Colorado squawfish, speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, razorback sucker, and mottled sculpin, with bonytail chub possibly present. Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, and bonytail chub are federally listed as endangered. Humpback chub may also have historically occurred in the river. The 1995 LRP states that only four of the eight verified native species remain comparatively common.

**Non-native fishes.** At least 40 non-native species have been introduced to the Upper Colorado River Basin since the late 1800s. Of the 23 reported in the San Juan sub-basin, 21 have been documented in the river since 1987. Common carp and channel catfish are the only common, widely distributed non-natives in warmwater reaches. Red shiner, fathead minnow, and mosquitofish are the most common in low-velocity habitats.

**Regulatory history.** The Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were listed in 1967. Bonytail was listed in 1980 and razorback sucker in 1991. The program was spurred by section 7 consultation on the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] (ALP), whose 1991 Biological Opinion found that cumulative conditions jeopardized the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime alteration.** Before [[Navajo Dam]] was completed in 1962, the river had large spring peaks, typically from March to early July, and low base flows. After completion, spring peaks were reduced, base flows increased, and late winter flows rose. Hypolimnetic releases lowered mean water temperature and reduced temperature fluctuations.

**Water depletion and diversion.** Many diversions between Navajo Dam and Lake Powell create a net depletion of up to 657,200 acre-feet annually. Net depletion at Bluff is 632,200 acre-feet after return flows from Dolores River imports. Six diversion structures on the New Mexico mainstem are potential barriers to fish movement, especially at low flows. The Hogback diversion became impassable to fish after reconstruction in September 1987.

**Water quality.** The basin is naturally seleniferous. Mainstem selenium rises downstream from Archuleta, New Mexico (<1 µg/l) to Bluff, Utah (4 µg/l), largely through irrigation return flows and shale contributions. Sampling in 1990 and 1991 found high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, including naphthalene, benzo(a)pyrene, and phenanthrene, in fish. Oil and gas development, urban runoff, sewage, and industrial wastes are also cited as sources.

**Habitat needs.** Young-of-year Colorado squawfish are most often found in low-velocity backwaters and side channels with silt and sand substrates. Adult razorback suckers use quiet eddies, pools, and mid-channel runs, and spawn in spring over gravel and cobble bars. Spawning and nursery habitats for neither species have been identified.

**Trust responsibilities.** Four tribes hold reserved water rights in the basin, most not yet quantified. These include the Navajo Nation, Southern Ute, Ute Mountain Ute, and Jicarilla Apache. Related projects include the [[Navajo Indian Irrigation Project]] (NIIP) and the Colorado Ute settlement tied to ALP.

## Key Findings

**Species status**

- A small reproducing population of Colorado squawfish persists. Since 1987, 21 adults have been captured between Bluff and Hogback, and young-of-year fish have been collected downstream of Shiprock and near Lake Powell.
- Only one razorback sucker was collected in the river from 1987 onward. Several others were captured in the Lake Powell inflow area, many in spawning condition, but no recruitment has been documented.
- Bonytail has not been captured in the San Juan since systematic study began in 1987.
- Taxa are described as "endangered" in the 1992 document and "extremely rare" for razorback sucker in the 1995 LRP.

**Program goals and structure**

- Goal 1: conserve Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker consistent with ESA recovery goals.
- Goal 2: proceed with water development in compliance with federal and state law, compacts, court decrees, and tribal trust responsibilities.
- Governance includes a Coordination Committee, a Biology Committee, and a Navajo Dam Operating Committee. Most decisions require a two-thirds majority vote.
- The Service (Region 2) assumes data management responsibilities. The Program Coordinator is responsible for overall coordination.

**Research and recovery elements**

- Section 3 sets out research and recovery elements for distribution, genetics, habitat, water quality, non-native species, and monitoring.
- Recovery actions may include habitat modification, control of non-natives, augmentation through hatchery propagation, cryogenic gamete preservation, and water quality improvement.

**Flow commitments and legal protection**

- Reclamation agreed to operate Navajo Dam to mimic a natural hydrograph for the life of ALP, if research shows this benefits recovery.
- Habitat flows will be protected under state and tribal law and will not be obtained through condemnation.
- Navajo Dam releases fall between April 1 and November 30. Operating decisions must first satisfy senior water rights.

**Section 7 compliance and "sufficient progress"**

- The ALP reasonable and prudent alternative offsets 57,100 acre-feet of depletion.
- Minor depletions in the seven-year research period may total no more than 3,000 acre-feet annually.
- The most important progress elements are reservoir operation to mimic the natural hydrograph, identification of flow needs, legal protection of releases, evidence of population or habitat improvement, and water quality improvement.
- The 1995 LRP sets milestones for management decisions, including the identification of limiting factors and the development of augmentation plans.

**Non-native control**

- The program states that stocking of warmwater non-native species should cease. Only salmonid stocking is to be allowed in cold-water reaches away from endangered species habitat.
- Chemical control is described as largely infeasible. The 1987 high flows reduced channel catfish reproduction, suggesting flow manipulation as a control tool.

**Budget and funding**

- The suggested annual budget is $1,980,000, which includes $20,000 per participant for administration and $200,000 for the Fish and Wildlife Service.
- Suggested funding sources total $1,820,000, leaving a $160,000 annual shortfall.
- Capital costs are estimated at $5 million, to be drawn from the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]] or other sources.
- Depletion charges in the Upper Colorado program were set at $12.71 per acre-foot for FY 1995. The San Juan program does not use this charge.

**Comparison with the Upper Colorado program**

- The San Juan program covers water quantity, water quality, and physical habitat. The Upper Colorado program focuses mainly on depletion impacts.
- The San Juan program uses a native fish community approach. The Upper Colorado program is limited to the endangered fishes.
- The Service has stated that both programs can serve as reasonable and prudent alternatives for critical habitat adverse modification, provided they show documented on-the-ground progress.
