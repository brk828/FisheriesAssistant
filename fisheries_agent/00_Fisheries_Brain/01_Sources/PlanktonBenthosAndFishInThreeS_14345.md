# Plankton, Benthos and Fish in Three Strip-Mine Lakes With Varying pH Values

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 paper by Niles F. Stockinger and Horace A. Hays, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science* (Vol. 63, No. 1), reports the first study of plankton, benthos and fish in acid strip-mine water in southeastern Kansas. Three [[Strip-Mine Lakes]] with different pH levels were sampled roughly monthly from March to December 1957. Acidity strongly shaped the biota. The most acid lake (pH about 3.2–3.6) had no fish, a reduced plankton community, and the largest benthic volume of the three. The two less acidic lakes supported stunted fish populations. The issue also contains a short botanical paper by Daniel L. Marsh on [[Phlox Oklahomensis]] and the [[Phlox Bifida]] complex. That paper is terrestrial and outside the aquatic scope of this note.

## Background & Description

Local [[Strip-Mine Lakes]] in southeastern Kansas were popularly called "alkali" pits. Earlier work by Schoonover and Coates (1949) showed they were acidic, with pH ranging from about 5–7 (slightly acid) to 2–4 (highly acid). Highly acid lakes were typically clear, sparsely vegetated, and fishless. Earlier productivity studies in acid mine water existed (Lackey, 1939; Crawford, 1942; Heaton, 1951; Lewis and Peters, 1954), but the authors state that little was known about the plankton, benthos and fish of these Kansas lakes.

The three study lakes were numbered by increasing pH:

- **Lake No. 1 (highly acid):** Oval, about 2 acres, maximum depth 12 ft. It had one inlet arm that filled during heavy rain.
- **Lake No. 2 (transitional, slightly acid):** About 1.2 acres, maximum depth 10 ft. Its small watershed had no true inlets.
- **Lake No. 3 (neutral to slightly basic, control):** A "rather typical" strip-mine lake of about 6 acres, maximum depth 18 ft.

**Methods:** Sampling ran from March through December 1957, with physico-chemical readings taken between 6:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. Temperature was measured with a reversing thermometer at the surface and bottom. Turbidity was read with a 10 cm [[Secchi Disc]]. pH was measured with a [[Hellige Field Comparator]]. Plankton was collected with a 3-liter [[Kemmerer Sampler]] at three depths. Benthos was collected with a 6 × 6 inch [[Ekman Dredge]]. Fish were collected by [[Rotenone Poisoning]] and hook and line. Measured fish were checked for age from scales, following Lagler (1950).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and physics.** Lake No. 1 stayed strongly acid, with pH 3.2–3.6 and a surface-to-bottom variance of no more than 0.2 units. Rainfall had little effect on pH. Its temperature varied by no more than 1 °C between surface and bottom, so it stayed homeothermic. Lake No. 2 ranged from pH 6.2 (July) to a higher value in April, which the OCR garbles as "7A." Its average was slightly acid. Lake No. 2 showed one partial, temporary thermal stratification in July (32 °C surface, 21 °C bottom). Lake No. 3 ranged from pH 7.0 to 7.8, with partial stratification in July and August (July: 32 °C surface, 21 °C bottom; August: 27 °C surface, 23 °C bottom).

**Turbidity.** Visibility in Lake No. 1 was 85 to 156 inches. Lake No. 2 ranged from 36 inches (May) to 146 inches (October), and its turbidity tracked spring rainfall. Lake No. 3 ranged from 25 to 80 inches. The authors concluded that turbidity was generally linked to acidity, with a progressive increase from Lake No. 1 to Lake No. 3. Turbidity influenced thermal stratification, with the more turbid Lake No. 3 showing the stronger and longer-lasting stratification.

**Plankton.** Rotifers and microcrustaceans made up more than 90% of the plankton in all three lakes. Lake No. 1 had a restricted community dominated by Scapholeberis among cladocerans, with a single March pulse of 6,240 organisms per liter. Its rotifers were mostly [[Keratella]], which the authors suggest is an indicator of acid water. Lakes No. 2 and No. 3 had larger, more diverse plankton. Lake No. 3 contained protozoans (Frontonia, Arcella, Euglena, Difflugia) and was dominated by rotifers Kellicottia and Polyarthra.

**Benthos.** Insect larvae were the dominant benthic group in all three lakes. Lake No. 1 unexpectedly had the largest benthic volume and number per square foot. This was driven mainly by [[Chironomidae]] larvae (genus Tendipes), which peaked in May, June and July, and by Odonata naiads. Lake No. 2 was the poorest benthic producer. Lake No. 3 had the most diverse fauna, along with Amphipoda (Hyalella), a gastropod (Physa, peaking at 35 and 52 per square foot in April and August), and several Coleoptera genera.

**Fish and food-web relationships.** The authors identified Chironomid larvae as perhaps the most important group in the fish food chain. They proposed two explanations for Lake No. 1's high benthic volume. First, the dominant groups may be pioneer, acid-tolerant taxa (Crawford, 1942). Stickney (1922) found that the dragonfly *Libellula pulchella* tolerated pH as low as 1. Second, the absence of fish may allow more benthic biomass to accumulate, following Hayne and Ball (1956). Fish were absent from Lake No. 1, which the authors attribute to acidity. They note that pH 4.0 or below is generally lethal to most freshwater fish (Ellis, 1948).

## Key Findings

1. **Fish were absent from the most acid lake.** Green sunfish and common carp were reportedly present locally, but none were found in Lake No. 1 during poisoning operations, even though the lake had been stocked earlier. The authors conclude that high acidity (mean pH about 3.5) was the limiting factor.

2. **Two fish-bearing lakes showed stunting.** Lake No. 2 yielded six species: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]] and [[Black Bullhead]]. Age-and-growth data for 21 bluegill showed average standard lengths of 62, 71, 80 and 99 mm at ages 2 to 5. Stunting was present in all age groups. All bass in this lake belonged to one age group, so no comparison was possible.

3. **Lake No. 3 showed a mixed growth pattern.** Four species were regularly collected: largemouth bass, green sunfish, warmouth and bluegill. [[Channel Catfish]] and black bullhead were not collected but are known to occur. Bluegill (n = 17) averaged 101 mm, 118 mm and 150 mm at ages 2, 3 and 4. Largemouth bass (n = 20) averaged 151 mm, 170 mm and 180 mm at ages 2, 3 and 4. Bass grew comparably to other strip-mine lakes but more slowly than bass in natural lakes (Carlander, 1950, comparison in Table 2). Growth was comparable to artificial waters in the first two years, but stunting became pronounced after year three.

4. **Stunting was attributed to food shortage and an unbalanced fish population.** The authors judged stunting more severe in Lake No. 2 than in Lake No. 3.

5. **Summary of the study:** This was described as the first study of plankton, benthos and fish in acid strip-mine water in southeastern Kansas. Water chemistry changed little over the roughly 10-month study, and spring rain did not materially affect pH.

**Non-aquatic content in the same issue:** Daniel L. Marsh's paper, "Relationship of *Phlox oklahomensis* to *Phlox bifida* Complex," concerns a terrestrial plant. It notes that Phlox oklahomensis was first described by Wherry (1944) from Woodward County, Oklahoma, and was later reported from Cowley and Butler counties in Kansas (Horr and McGregor, 1949, 1951). Those Kansas records are about 120 miles from the type locality. The paper also proposes that the first-named subspecies of *P. bifida* be designated by the simple epithet *bifida*, not the "alpha" form, following the International Code of Botanical Nomenclature.
