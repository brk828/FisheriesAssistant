## Executive Summary

This 1989 paper by Camm C. Swift describes fossil freshwater fish remains recovered from Pit 91 at [[Rancho La Brea]], the type locality of the [[Rancholabrean Age]] mammalian faunas. Seventy fish bones, dated to between 25,000 ± 1,000 and 33,000 ± 1,750 yr BP, represent three extant species: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Arroyo Chub]] (*Gila orcutti*), and [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*). This constitutes the first fossil record of *Gila orcutti*. Morphological analysis of stickleback pelvic and dorsal spines indicates a lightly armored form consistent with the modern, endangered [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni*). The fish assemblage, combined with taphonomic and geological evidence, supports the interpretation of a local, permanent freshwater stream environment near the deposit rather than fish transported long distances from mountainous headwaters. Notably, as with other ectothermic taxa at Rancho La Brea, no extinct fish species were identified, in contrast to the significant megafaunal extinctions documented at the site.

## Background & Description

### Salmo gairdneri (Rainbow Trout) — 15 bones
- Elements: 1 ceratohyal (4.2 mm), 1 atlas vertebra (1.8 mm), 5 abdominal vertebrae (1.3–1.4 mm), 9 caudal vertebrae (0.9–2.0 mm).
- Vertebral surfaces are flat or nearly so, with excavations appearing as "holes punched in a flat surface" — distinguishing them from *Salmo clarki* (cutthroat) and *Salmo aguabonita* (golden trout), which have longitudinal ridges between excavations.
- Fossil ceratohyal regression estimate: fish of ~61 mm SL (ceratohyal length = 0.057·SL + 0.708, N=12, r=0.990).
- Largest vertebrae correspond to fish of ~150 mm SL.
- Because vertebrae are disarticulated, the true number of individuals is unknown.

### Gila orcutti (Arroyo Chub) — 4 bones
- Elements: 1 fragmentary left dentary (3.6 mm), 1 anterior (first) vertebra (0.6 mm), 2 fifth/sixth abdominal vertebrae (1.3 mm).
- Three of the four bones (excluding one fragmentary vertebra) may represent a single individual of ~62 mm SL (dentary length = -1.672 + 0.0639·SL, N=25, r=0.943).
- Diagnostic features distinguishing *G. orcutti* from *[[Gila bicolor]]* (tui chub), *[[Rhinichthys osculus]]* (speckled dace), and *[[Hesperoleucas symmetricus]]* (California roach):
  - Shorter anteriormost vertebra than in the other three taxa.
  - Prominent circular pit on ventral edge of anterior vertebra, absent/incipient in others.
  - In *Rhinichthys*, anterior vertebrae are wider transversely than deep (not circular as in others).
  - Anterior ramus of dentary broader and flatter laterally than in *G. bicolor*, and markedly more so than in *Hesperoleucas* and *Rhinichthys*.

### Gasterosteus aculeatus (Threespine Stickleback) — 51 bones
- Elements: 14 right pelvic spines, 25 left pelvic spines (up to 4.8 mm), 6 first/second dorsal spines (2.2–3.6 mm high), 4 abdominal vertebrae, 1 left pelvic girdle, 1 left sphenotic.
- Represents at least 23 individuals (based on left pelvic spine count).
- Armor reduction analysis: fossil pelvic spines show few, poorly developed ventral serrations, matching modern *[[Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni]]* from the Los Angeles Basin, and fewer than *G. a. williamsoni* from the upper Santa Clara River or *G. a. microcephalus*.
- Fossil dorsal spines entirely lack serrations — a condition otherwise unique to Los Angeles Basin *G. a. williamsoni* (only occasional males show up to 3 basal serrations).
- Serration counts increase progressively southward-to-northward across subspecies: LA Basin *williamsoni* (least armored) → upper Santa Clara *williamsoni* → *microcephalus* → *G. a. aculeatus* (most armored) — reflecting a documented latitudinal armor-reduction cline (Miller & Hubbs 1969; Bell 1976).
- Estimated fossil fish size: 35–40 mm SL, using standard length = 1.63·(spine length) + 32.2 (N=15, r=0.16 — low r indicates high variability).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- All three taxa require **permanent, small riparian stream conditions**, with the following minimum shared requirements (per Moyle 1976):
  1. Water temperature ≤ ~22°C
  2. Dissolved oxygen > 8 ppm (via deep pools, groundwater inflow, and/or shading)
  3. Available cover from predators
- The three species form an ecologically graded thermal/oxygen tolerance series:
  - *Salmo gairdneri* — coldest, most oxygenated water required.
  - *Gasterosteus aculeatus* — intermediate requirements (Feldmuth & Baskin 1976).
  - *Gila orcutti* — warmest, least oxygenated water tolerated (Castleberry & Cech 1986).
- Abundance of trout and stickleback fossils suggests conditions on the cooler end of this spectrum, attributable to either a colder Pleistocene climate or local spring-fed inputs.
- Absence of the more montane taxa *[[Catostomus santaanae]]* and *[[Rhinichthys osculus]]* — both present today in upstream San Gabriel Mountain tributaries of the [[Los Angeles River]] — argues against long-distance fluvial transport of the fossils; this supports a local, in-situ depositional origin.
- Fossil bones show little to no abrasion despite disarticulation, consistent with local deposition rather than distant transport (10–30 km from mountainous source areas).
- Historically, seven fish species inhabited southern California inland freshwaters: *Lampetra tridentata*, *Lampetra cf. pacifica*, *Salmo gairdneri*, *Gila orcutti*, *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Catostomus santaanae*, and *Gasterosteus aculeatus* — all occurring in the pre-1851 [[Los Angeles River]] channel, which flowed ~3–4 km south of La Brea.
- Lampreys, lacking bone, would not be expected to fossilize and were absent from the record.

## Key Findings

- **First fossil record** of *Gila orcutti* (arroyo chub) reported in this study.
- *Salmo gairdneri* and especially *Gasterosteus aculeatus* have extensive prior fossil records in the western United States; this adds a new Late Pleistocene locality.
- The stickleback fossils indicate that a **relatively unarmored form** (morphologically matching modern endangered *[[Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni]]*) existed in the Los Angeles Basin at least 30,000 years ago — indicating long-term morphological/ecological stability of this locally adapted, reduced-armor phenotype.
- No extinct freshwater fish species were identified at Rancho La Brea, paralleling patterns seen in other ectothermic groups (amphibians, reptiles, insects) at the site — in stark contrast to the extensive megafaunal (large bird and mammal) extinctions documented from the same deposits.
- Despite this apparent long-term stability, two related historical changes occurred in the last ~45–100 years: the disappearance of the Pacific brook lamprey (*Lampetra cf. pacifica*) from the region, and the formal listing of the unarmored threespine stickleback as an endangered species.
- Fossils originated from three distinct microhabitats within Pit 91: (1) stream drift in the southeast corner (containing all chub bones and most trout bones), (2) a lens-shaped deposit in the northeast corner with evidence of subaerial exposure (source of remaining trout bones), and (3) the central-western margin of the "central bone mass." Stickleback remains were distributed across all three areas (13, 31, and 7 bones respectively).
- Radiocarbon dating brackets the fish-bearing deposits between ~25,000–33,000 yr BP, a span of up to 10,750 years (including error), implying both wetter-cool and drier-warm intervals may be represented.
- Taphonomic evidence (channel deposits up to 8 m wide, later invaded by asphalt) combined with the small body sizes of the fossils supports deposition from **small local tributaries** rather than a major mainstem drainage.
- Findings collectively support a paleoenvironmental reconstruction of the La Brea area as having **wetter winters and cooler, drier summers** than today, under a Mediterranean climate buffered by marine influence, consistent with contemporaneous plant fossil assemblages (closed-cone pine forest, relict coast redwood, inland dry foothills, marsh/pond taxa).
- Cross-reference: related Pleistocene stickleback fossils have since been found in the Mojave drainage at [[Lake Manix]] (Roeder 1985), representing a genetically distinct evolutionary lineage from the La Brea/Los Angeles Basin populations.
