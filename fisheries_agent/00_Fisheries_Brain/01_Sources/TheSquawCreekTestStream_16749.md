# The Squaw Creek Test Stream: Creel Census and Trout Returns, 1937–1940

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonid

## Executive Summary

This progress report by Alan C. Ranger (U.S. Forest Service) and Frederick K. Cramer (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) presents four seasons (1937–1940) of [[Creel Census]] data from [[Squaw Creek]], a [[Pit River]] tributary in the [[Shasta National Forest]]. The authors describe the stream as a test site for trout management. They find that wild, naturally spawned [[Rainbow Trout]] supplied more than 97 per cent of the catch, that planted trout returned very little to anglers (about 1 in 108 marked rainbow), and that fishing success declined, most sharply along the road-accessible reach. The authors call the results provisional and recommend continued study.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Squaw Creek lies about 40 miles northeast of Redding. Its 35-mile course descends from 4,000 ft to about 1,000 ft in a sequence of pools and riffles. The creek is considered a typical northern California mountain trout stream. A single road gives access to the creek, so the creel census could stay below anglers and record nearly all catches. The creek is also the location of a planned [[Shasta Reservoir]] inflow after [[Shasta Dam]] is completed.

**Purpose.** The test stream was set up to supply facts for trout management. The questions addressed were: what happens to small planted trout, what natural reproduction yields, and which methods give the highest trout yield.

**Funding and direction.** The U.S. Forest Service financed the first three years. In 1940 the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] also contributed. Technical direction came from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The California Division of Fish and Game supplied trout and helped with marking and planting.

**Fish fauna.** Eight genera and nine species have been recorded in the creek. Two are introduced: brown trout and eastern brook trout. The other seven are native: rainbow trout, Sacramento pike, hardhead, northern roach, dace, perch, and bullhead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** No water temperature above 76 °F was recorded, and the authors consider this within the tolerance range for trout. Forenoon temperatures at Big Tree camp ranged from 60 to 70 °F between June 15 and September 1, the warmest period. The warmest water occurs in the lower reaches, where angling intensity and catch per hour are lowest.

**Habitat.** Good spawning beds occur at the lower end of most pools. The authors judge that predation, food supply, and physical characteristics do not distinguish Squaw Creek from other northern California mountain streams.

**Fishing pressure.** Fishing intensity rose sharply over the study. Part of this increase came from construction workers on [[Shasta Dam]], who made up 54.8 per cent of the 1940 anglers. Peak use occurred on opening day, Memorial Day, the Fourth of July, and the fall deer-season opening.

**Stocking practices.** Planting was carried out using hatchery stock from the [[Mt. Shasta Hatchery]] and a [[Rearing Pond]] on the creek. The rearing pond was meant to acclimate fish before release. Since 1938, planted trout have been marked by fin clipping, with a different mark for each lot.

## Key Findings

**1. Catch totals and effort**

- The total recorded catch for 1937–1940 was 15,428 trout.
- Catches by season (summary figures): 2,504 trout by 140 anglers (1937); 3,258 by 378 (1938); 5,204 by 600 (1939); 4,462 by 688 (1940).
- Average catch per angler-day fell from 17.8 (1937) to 8.6 (1938 and 1939) and 6.4 (1940).
- Average catch per hour was 2.44 (1937), 1.38 (1938), 1.59 (1939), and 1.57 (1940).
- In 1940 anglers increased by 14.6 per cent over 1939 but caught only 85.74 per cent as many trout.

**2. Wild versus planted fish**

- Wild, naturally spawned rainbow trout made up 97.89 per cent of the rainbow catch and 97.71 per cent of the total trout catch in 1939–1940.
- [[Brown Trout]]: 32 caught (0.2 per cent of the total). About 70 per cent were under two years old. Brown trout were last planted in 1936.
- [[Eastern Brook Trout]]: 12 caught (0.08 per cent of the total), all in 1940, four years after the last planting. All were about 6-inch fish.

**3. Survival of marked rainbow trout**

- Marked rainbow planted: 6,200 (1938), 15,800 (1939), and 5,040 (1940). Recovered: 204 marked fish, all from the 1938 and 1939 plantings.
- The 1938–1939 plantings (22,000 fish) returned 204 fish, or about 1 in 108 (0.93 per cent). Of the 1940 catch, 90.1 per cent came from the 1939 plant.
- Rearing-pond survival was 23.9 per cent in 1938 (5,000 stocked, 1,196 recovered) and 14.5 per cent in 1939 (4,950 stocked, 717 recovered).
- The authors cite a Michigan study by Shetter (1939) in which legal-size returns never exceeded 1.6 per cent of fingerlings planted.
- Fin regeneration may have caused some marked fish to be missed. The authors estimate this error at no more than 10 per cent.

**4. Spatial patterns**

- No marked trout were caught more than one mile above the uppermost planting point. A few were caught one mile below the lowest point.
- The 1940 headwater plant (5,040 fish) was expected to show downstream movement of up to 18 miles. No fish from that plant were recovered in the report period.
- Along the road-accessible reach (75 per cent of fishing hours), catch per hour fell by 54 per cent between 1937 and 1940. That reach yielded only 51 per cent of the 1940 trout.
- Tributaries and remote reaches produced 49 per cent of the 1940 catch, caught by 20 per cent of anglers. Their catch per hour was 2.6 to 2.9 times that of the road reach (2.94 vs. 1.13 trout per hour in 1940).

**5. Size structure and exploitation**

- Size composition shows no clear evidence of depletion across 1937–1940. The predominant length class was 6 to 7.9 inches in every season.
- Some 90 per cent of fish under eight inches were in their second year of life, which means they had not yet spawned. The authors conclude there is a heavy drain on the spawning population.
- The catch is heavy enough that "very few mature spawners are left in the stream."

**6. Management conclusions and recommendations**

- Artificial stocking has not worked under present conditions. Management should focus on aiding natural propagation.
- Planting of brown and eastern brook trout should be discontinued.
- Options for protecting breeding stock include a size limit or season adjustment to protect younger fish. A bag-limit reduction was judged ineffective because of heavy angling intensity.
- Continued data collection is needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. The authors also plan to study [[Scale Age Analysis]] and downstream migration of the 1940 marked plant.
