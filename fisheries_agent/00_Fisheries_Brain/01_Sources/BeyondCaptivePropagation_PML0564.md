# Beyond Captive Propagation: Zoo and Aquarium Contributions to Southwestern Aquatic Species Recovery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-conservation

## Executive Summary

This article describes how zoos and aquariums contribute to the recovery of native species beyond captive propagation and visitor education. Using the [[Phoenix Zoo]] as its main example, it covers work on a terrestrial snail, native fishes, and southwestern frogs. Activities include habitat renovation, population surveys, basic research, control of non-native species, interpretive design, and maintenance of [[Genetic Refugia]]. The article is a feature piece rather than a research paper, and its evidence is mostly descriptive program reports with a few reported counts and survival figures.

## Background & Description

Zoos and aquariums are described as partners in recovery of native species such as the [[Black-Footed Ferret]] (*Mustela nigripes*), [[California Condor]] (*Gymnogyps californianus*), and [[Mexican Wolf]] (*Canis lupus baileyi*). Their roles include propagating animals for reintroduction, interpreting species' plight for millions of visitors, and participating in habitat work. Zoo and aquarium staff across North America are said to take part in population surveys, basic research, non-native species control, and interpretive materials design.

The Phoenix Zoo is presented as an active example of these efforts across three taxonomic groups:

- **[[Kanab Ambersnail]]** (*Oxyloma haydeni kanabensis*): an endangered terrestrial mollusk known from only three populations in the American Southwest.
- **Native fishes**: including [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), and [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*).
- **Southwestern frogs**: four native species, including the [[Tarahumara Frog]] (*Rana tarahumarae*), [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*), [[Northern Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*), and [[Ramsey Canyon Leopard Frog]] (*Rana subaquavocalis*).

The article's author, formerly the Ectotherm Curator at the Phoenix Zoo, is now the Amphibians and Reptiles Program Manager at the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Kanab ambersnail.** The three populations are two in privately owned, drought-stressed wetlands in southern Utah and one at a large spring along the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Threats include groundwater pumping, commercial development, and livestock grazing on private land. Large water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] could threaten the Grand Canyon population. The [[Kanab Ambersnail Working Group]] is an informal partnership of federal and state agencies, university researchers, and non-governmental organizations, including the Phoenix Zoo.

**Native fish.** The article attributes the extirpation or extinction of many southwestern native fishes to introduced sport fish and to river diversion and impoundment. The zoo's grounds contain artificial lakes filled decades ago with water diverted from a river that once supported native fish.

**Southwestern frogs.** The article says many environmental threats facing native fishes also affect amphibians. The Tarahumara frog has been extirpated from the state, and the other listed species are experiencing severe declines. Partners include the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Forest Service, Department of the Army, Bureau of Land Management, The Nature Conservancy, and private landowners.

## Key Findings

**1. Kanab ambersnail refugium and recovery support**
- Zoo and Arizona Game and Fish Department staff designed and built two outdoor refugia with a dripping spring, host plants collected from the canyon, and appropriate sandstone substrate. The refugia serve as a backup in case a catastrophe eliminates the Grand Canyon population. The article states there are no current plans for reintroduction from captive animals.
- Zoo staff have taken part in habitat and population surveys and in translocating snails to new Grand Canyon sites.
- A public exhibit with interpretive information was scheduled to open in fall 2000.

**2. Native fish refugium and head-starting**
- Working with partners including [[Arizona State University]], the [[American Zoo And Aquarium Association]] Freshwater Fish Taxon Advisory Group, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the zoo converted its main lake (approximately 15 acre-feet) into a refugium. The lake was chosen for its central location and size, and FWS fishery biologists judged it suitable long-term habitat.
- Objectives were to create a genetic or broodstock refugium and to head-start juvenile fish in a semi-natural environment.
- Before release, exotic sport fish were removed to other urban lakes. The lake was drained and refilled with water filtered through a passive gravel bed to limit sport fish reinvasion.
- In summer 1996, approximately 200 bonytail chub and 5,000 razorback suckers reared at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] were released into the lake.
- By fall 1998, some fish had reached a predator-safe, sexually mature size. Over 100 razorback suckers were returned to their historic range in the Colorado River. A further head-start release was planned for the following year.
- Interpretive panels with life-size fish sculptures explain the plight of native fishes.

**3. Other fish and mosquito control**
- The zoo also maintains three ponds for desert pupfish and Gila topminnow, established as refugium or research populations.
- These fish provide an unexpected benefit: natural mosquito control for the zoo.
- A small group of adult and juvenile pupfish was planned for transfer to [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] to establish another refugium population.

**4. Frog head-starting and rearing**
- Due to high predation and mortality of wild eggs and larvae, small portions of egg masses are removed from the wild and reared at the [[Montane Anuran Conservation Center]] (MACC), a facility of repurposed refrigerated ocean cargo trailers fitted with air conditioning, full-spectrum lighting, and plastic pools with separate filtration and aeration. Volunteers known as "The Tadpole Taskforce" care for the animals.
- Frogs are toe-clipped for future identification and returned to the original egg collection site.
- Embryo-to-frog survival at the facility is reported as over 90 percent.
- As of spring 1999, staff had produced and released over 3,000 animals to supplement declining wild populations. At least two head-started animals had reproduced at one release site.
- Staff have also contributed findings on captive husbandry, dietary needs, larval growth rates, rearing densities, and life support system design.

**5. Public education and outreach**
- The zoo's frog program uses outdoor educational displays, environmental fairs, print and video media, community presentations, a visitor-center information display, and an interactive box of interpretive material lent to schools.
- The article identifies public awareness of threats facing amphibians, locally and globally, as the program's most important contribution to frog conservation.

**Conclusion.** The article argues that zoos and aquariums contribute to wildlife conservation well beyond captive propagation, through habitat work, monitoring, research, invasive species control, refugium management, and public education.
