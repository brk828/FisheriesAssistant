# Early Life History of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #catostomidae #razorback-sucker #larval-fish #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study investigated the early life history and ecology of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, to determine causes of recruitment failure in the only substantial remaining population of this endemic Colorado River species. Adult suckers spawned successfully from January through April, producing abundant larvae that reached average lengths of 10.6 mm but disappeared by mid-April. Larvae reared in predator-free environments (backwater habitat and cages) achieved lengths exceeding 20-34 mm, demonstrating that Lake Mohave water quality does not preclude growth. Small larvae (<12 mm) fed primarily on zooplankton ([[Bosmina]], [[Daphnia]], copepods, rotifers), while larger individuals consumed more diverse prey including benthic invertebrates. [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) predation on sucker larvae was documented, though impacts remain unquantified. The study suggests larval fate involves either predation or downstream transport through Davis Dam, explaining the complete absence of recruitment to the adult population since the 1950s.

## Background & Description

[[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of southwestern North America. Once abundant from Wyoming to Sonora, Mexico, the species has declined dramatically throughout its native range. The only substantial remaining population inhabits [[Lake Mohave]], a Colorado River reservoir in Arizona-Nevada. This population consists entirely of large, old individuals (>400 mm TL, >20 years age) with no evidence of successful recruitment since the 1950s, despite successful annual spawning and production of viable embryos and free-swimming larvae.

Adult razorback suckers in Lake Mohave concentrate in three primary spawning areas: [[Arizona Bay]], [[Hammerhead Cove]], and [[Six Mile Area]]. Spawning occurs over coarse gravel or rubble substrates in shallow water (<2 m depth, typically <5 m maximum). The species exhibits no diel pattern of reproductive activity, with spawning occurring throughout 24-hour periods when surface conditions permit. Wave action exceeding approximately 25 cm amplitude causes cessation of spawning activity in shallow nearshore areas.

Larvae first appear in early February, approximately 3-4 weeks after initial spawning observations. They occupy shallow (<3 m) littoral zones with substrates varying from cobble-gravel to fine sand, often associated with partially submerged riparian vegetation (primarily *Tamarisk*). Larvae are attracted to bright light at night, facilitating collection by dip net. The largest larvae collected from open Lake Mohave waters measured only 12.2 mm TL, with average size remaining constant at 10.6 mm throughout the February-April period, indicating continuous spawning and cohort production with no detectable growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mohave]] is a mainstream Colorado River reservoir created by Davis Dam. The lake exhibits thermal characteristics typical of desert reservoirs, with isothermal conditions in winter when razorback sucker spawning occurs. Water temperatures during the 1985 spawning season ranged from 11.5°C at spawning initiation in early January to higher temperatures through April.

The zooplankton community in Lake Mohave during the larval sucker period was dominated by cladocerans ([[Bosmina]] and [[Daphnia]]), with adult copepods comprising most of the remainder. Total zooplankton densities ranged from <100 to >7,500 individuals/m³, with high spatial and temporal variability. [[Rotifera]], *Chydorus*, *Sida*, and Ostracoda were rare in open lake waters.

An experimental backwater adjacent to [[Arizona Bay]] provided critical insights into larval ecology. This naturally formed habitat (0.85-2.1 ha surface area, 1.7-3.7 m maximum depth) was separated from Lake Mohave by a wave-formed gravel berm and fed by lake water exchanging through the porous substrate. Water temperatures paralleled the lake but averaged slightly cooler (-1.1°C) in winter and warmer (+1.8°C) in spring. Substrates consisted of coarse gravel around margins and organic silts in deeper areas, with dense stands of [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum spicatum*) and [[Curly Leaf Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton crispus*) in shallow regions.

The introduced fish community in Lake Mohave includes [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*). These non-native species are abundant in spawning areas and nearshore habitats occupied by larval razorback suckers.

## Key Findings

**Spawning Chronology and Distribution**: Adult razorback suckers spawned from early January (first observed 8 January at 11.5°C water temperature) until late April 1985. Spawning in [[Arizona Bay]] began one week earlier than in [[Hammerhead Cove]] (15 January). More than 1000 individuals were observed on optimal viewing days in Arizona Bay, while Hammerhead Cove supported only 20-30 adults. Spawning density per unit area was similar between sites, with total numbers reflecting available spawning habitat. Spawning showed no diel pattern but was strongly influenced by wave action, ceasing when waves exceeded approximately 25 cm height.

**Larval Distribution and Abundance**: Razorback sucker larvae were first captured 9 February 1985 in Hammerhead Cove, three weeks after initial spawning at that site and one month after spawning began in Arizona Bay. Larvae became abundant in Hammerhead Cove within days but were rarely collected in Arizona Bay despite extensive reproductive activity there. Larvae occupied shallow (<3 m) littoral zones and were sporadically found in Cottonwood Cove. Extensive larval trawl sampling (22.0 net-hours, 2-15 m depth) in open lake areas yielded only a single larva, indicating larvae were either unavailable in open water, present at very low densities, or located at depths >15 m or near bottom. Larvae disappeared from nearshore habitats by mid-April.

**Larval Growth and Size**: Average total length of larvae collected from Lake Mohave remained constant at 10.6 mm from early February through early April, with no evidence of increasing size through the period. The largest larva captured from open lake waters measured 12.2 mm TL. Since swim-up is achieved near 8.5 mm and larval growth rates are known to be rapid at prevailing temperatures, maximum age of the largest specimens was estimated at only a few weeks, suggesting high mortality or emigration of older/larger individuals.

**Growth in Protected Habitats**: Larvae reared in predator-free environments achieved dramatically larger sizes than those in the lake at large:
- **Arizona Bay Backwater**: Larvae first captured 7 March (two months after adult stocking) initially showed mean TL similar to lake fish but attained lengths >16 mm by 28 April, with the largest specimen reaching 20.3 mm TL
- **Cage Experiments**: Despite 80% mortality in first 2-3 weeks (attributed to vandalism, escape, and wave action), surviving larvae showed mean length increases up to 3.1 mm (nearly 30% of initial size). Ten relatively large (18 mm TL) larvae placed in a cage survived >3 weeks and attained lengths up to 34 mm TL, the largest observed outside hatchery situations

These results demonstrate that survival and growth in Lake Mohave water are not precluded by physical or chemical constraints, contradicting earlier hypotheses. Razorback suckers have the potential to recruit to larger size classes than yet collected in nature.

**Habitat Use Ontogeny**: Habitat use changed with larval size. Small (<12 mm) larvae occupied shallow margins of the backwater, attracted to bright light from nearshore areas. Larger (>15 mm) larvae attracted to light appeared to come only from deeper, offshore habitat toward the middle of the backwater. These observations, consistently repeated, provide evidence that razorback larvae assume a benthic habit in deeper water as they increase in size beyond approximately 12 mm TL.

**Food Habits and Feeding Ecology**: 
- **Small larvae (<12 mm)**: Fed primarily on zooplankton; cladocerans ([[Bosmina]], [[Daphnia]], *Macrothrix*), copepods, and rotifers were dominant items. Most stomachs were empty (76% in Lake Mohave, 28% in backwater), likely because larvae had not yet developed fully functional mouths. Many individuals with empty guts had remnant or recently absorbed yolk sacs.
- **Larger larvae (>12 mm)**: Had significantly fewer empty stomachs and more diverse diets including larger foods such as chironomid larvae, ostracods, and trichopterans. Benthic invertebrates, due to their larger sizes, may contribute more to larval nutrition than indicated by occurrence and numbers data.
- **Prey Selection**: Larvae selected [[Daphnia]] averaging 0.66-0.86 mm in length, approximately one-half the average size (1.42-1.60 mm) available in the plankton. Among predominant foods, sucker larvae took individual items significantly smaller than in the plankton population at large.
- **Foraging Habitats**: Use of benthic invertebrates plus *Macrothrix* (a vegetation-associated cladoceran not collected in plankton samples) indicates larval suckers utilize varied food resources and forage among essentially all available habitats (limnetic, benthic, and macrophytic).

**Zooplankton Resources**: Lake Mohave zooplankton was dominated by cladocerans ([[Bosmina]] or [[Daphnia]]), with adult copepods comprising most of the remainder. Total densities ranged from <100 to >7,500 individuals/m³, varying greatly in space and time. The Arizona Bay backwater showed different composition with [[Rotifera]] predominant, followed by [[Daphnia]], mature and immature copepods, and [[Bosmina]]. Total densities in the backwater were generally lower (maximum ~1000/m³). Plankton sizes were similar between lake and backwater, except copepods (0.12-0.24 mm longer in backwater) and [[Daphnia]] (0.15-0.18 mm longer in backwater).

**Predation Documentation**: 
- **Lake Mohave**: Experimental gill nets (12-hour sets, February-March) in Hammerhead Cove yielded 81 potential predator fish. Only [[Green Sunfish]] contained razorback sucker larvae (2.7% occurrence, mean 2.0 larvae per predator). However, digestion experiments revealed larvae become unidentifiable after 6-8 hours, suggesting predation rates may be significantly underestimated in standard sampling.
- **Digestion Rate Experiments**: Green sunfish consumed 10 razorback larvae in <0.25 hours. At 2 hours post-ingestion, larvae appeared as whole individuals but with eroded fins and distorted body shape. At 4 hours, larvae were digested into amorphous masses beyond specific identification but still enumerable. After 6 hours, only 12 of 20 eye lenses remained detectable. After 8 hours, larvae were completely undetectable.
- **Arizona Bay Backwater**: After the berm was breached on 4 April 1985 and introduced fishes (including green sunfish, bluegill, threadfin shad, channel catfish, largemouth bass, common carp) invaded, 37% of green sunfish examined contained razorback larvae (mean 3.9 larvae per fish). Five direct observations of green sunfish attacking and eating sucker larvae were recorded. Abundant sucker larvae in the backwater disappeared within 4 weeks after fish invasion. When the backwater was reclaimed in October 1986, only introduced species were present; no razorback suckers (adults or young) were taken.

**Proposed Fate Hypothesis**: The study proposes that benthic larval razorback suckers move offshore into deeper water adjacent to spawning areas, based on three lines of evidence: (1) larvae consume benthic organisms, (2) larvae were not captured in surface waters away from shorelines despite extensive trawling, and (3) larger larvae apparently inhabited deeper portions of the Arizona Bay backwater. Two potential fates await larvae in limnetic habitat:

1. **Predation**: Particularly by [[Threadfin Shad]], the most abundant fish species in Lake Mohave, which occupies primarily pelagic habitats during winter, feeds on planktonic and benthic organisms, and can move throughout the isothermal lake without temperature constraints during the spawning season.

2. **Downstream Transport**: Deep currents resulting from flow-through discharge. Using conservative estimates (cross-sectional area 19,500 m², discharge 500 m³/s), current velocity is estimated at approximately 2.6 cm/s. Expanding parameters to include cross-sectional areas of 5,900-40,000 m² and discharges of 100-1000 m³/s yields current velocities of 1.0-25 cm/s. If deep currents are constrained by canyon walls or weak thermal stratification, velocities may be even higher. Larvae continuing downstream may be depleted by predation, with survivors ultimately discharged through Davis Dam, potentially explaining scarce individuals collected in downstream habitats.

**Hammerhead Cove Accumulation**: Down-reservoir currents may combine with prevailing winter winds and local hydraulic conditions to temporarily entrain larvae in the Hammerhead Cove area, located where Lake Mohave widens into Cottonwood Basin and just upstream from substantial deepening. This would explain the abundance of larvae in Hammerhead Cove despite minimal spawning activity there after late January.

**Management Implications**: The complete absence of recruitment to the adult razorback sucker population in Lake Mohave since the 1950s, despite successful annual spawning and larval production, represents a critical conservation concern. The last juveniles collected in the area were 6,100 specimens (12-42 mm) seined near Cottonwood Landing in 1950, just before Lake Mohave filled. The current adult population consists entirely of individuals >400 mm TL and >20 years of age, with frequency distributions showing no evidence of younger cohorts. Without intervention, this population faces extinction as the aging adults die without replacement.

**Recommended Future Research**:
1. Determine possible dispersal of razorback sucker out of Lake Mohave by intensive sampling of mainstream Colorado River immediately below Davis Dam during peak larval abundance
2. Determine depth, direction, and magnitude of down-reservoir currents and their potential role in dispersing larvae from spawning areas
3. Continue predation studies through refined digestion-rate experiments with additional predator species and close-order sampling during larval abundance periods
4. Monitor razorback sucker in Arizona Bay backwater during subsequent years with emphasis on larval growth and feeding
5. Examine larval survival and growth through refined cage experiments
6. Examine preserved larvae for morphological characteristics at yolk-to-feeding transition, especially mouth and anus dimensions relative to particle ingestion and passage capabilities
