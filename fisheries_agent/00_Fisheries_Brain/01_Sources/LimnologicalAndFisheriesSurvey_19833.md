# Limnological and Fisheries Survey of the Upper Skunk River, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A 1970 survey of a 50-km reach of the [[Skunk River]] near Ames, Iowa, combined water-quality monitoring with fish sampling. Treated sewage from Story City and Ames measurably altered several chemical parameters, and nutrient levels were high enough to promote algal growth. Fish diversity was highest upstream of Ames, where bedrock lies close to the surface and creates a heterogeneous streambed of pools and riffles. Downstream, a low-diversity assemblage dominated by [[Carp]] occupied homogeneous shifting sand. The authors concluded that geological substrate influenced fish distribution more strongly than the observed water-quality differences. The study also served as a pre-impoundment baseline for a proposed flood-control [[Reservoir]] 2.4 km north of Ames.

## Background & Description

The Skunk River drains 6,968 km² as a tributary of the [[Mississippi River]], flowing through a long, narrow basin from northcentral to southeastern Iowa. The upper reach passes through three zones:

- **Postglacial valley (above Ames):** a meandering channel of rock, sand, and mud with riffles and pools.
- **Preglacial channel (north of Ames, widening below Ames):** a shifting sand substrate, reflecting underlying bedrock profiles.
- **Channelized reach (about 5 km below Ames to Colfax):** dredged between 1893 and 1923 to speed drainage, and now re-establishing a meandering course.

Two U.S. Geological Survey gauges bracketed the study reach. The northern gauge (507.15 km² drainage) had an average discharge of 3.79 m³/s, with a range of 244.23 m³/s to no flow. The southern gauge (895.16 km² drainage, below the [[Squaw Creek]] confluence) averaged 6.79 m³/s, with a range of 262.06 m³/s to no flow.

Municipal inputs included secondarily treated sewage from Story City (population 2,104) and Ames (population 39,505), discharged directly to the river, and a septic-tank system at Cambridge (population 661) draining to a tributary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Sampling sites were grouped into an upstream section (Story City to Ames) and a downstream section (Ames to Cambridge). Downstream of Ames, mean orthophosphate rose from 0.52 to 1.50 mg/L, and nitrate rose from 3.0 to 8.1 mg/L. Ammonia, nitrite, chloride, chlorophyll a, COD, and specific conductance changed less. Effluent concentrations were high: the Ames effluent averaged 22.56 mg/L PO₄ and 8.72 mg/L NH₃-N, and the Story City effluent averaged 21.76 mg/L PO₄ and 10.34 mg/L NH₃-N.

**Flow and biota.** Chlorophyll a showed a significant inverse relationship with stream flow, and algal concentrations were higher at stations above Ames. Orthophosphate and specific conductance were the only variables not altered uniformly by discharge.

**Substrate and fish distribution.** The fish assemblage shifted sharply between Stations 3 and 4, where the streambed changes from boulders and gravel to shifting sand as bedrock drops beneath the channel. The authors judged the sand substrate to be the limiting factor for diversity, with channelization having no detectable additional effect.

**Historical change.** Meek (1892) described the river as bordered by marsh and bayous. Subsequent tile drainage and channel straightening are considered the likely causes of the loss of eight fish species from the reach since 1892.

## Key Findings

- **Fish collection:** 8,292 fish were collected, representing 35 species, 6 families, and 3 orders. Cyprinidae made up 72% of the catch, and carp alone made up 52%.
- **Sport fish:** Only seven sport-fish species were recorded. Forty-one [[Channel Catfish]] (28 above Ames), 19 [[Bluegill]] (14 above Ames), and 51 [[Smallmouth Bass]] (37 young-of-the-year, all above Ames) were collected. One [[Northern Pike]] and one [[Largemouth Bass]] were taken. Twenty-seven of 29 [[White Crappie]] were above Ames.
- **Dominant taxa:** Common shiner dominated seine catches above and below Ames (37.1% and 34.1%). Carp dominated electrofishing (32.4% above, 88.5% below Ames) and hoopnet catches below Ames (82.3%).
- **Diversity index (Wilhm and Dorris):** Values were higher above Ames (Station 2 = 4.08, the highest) than below (Station 5 = 1.19, the lowest). Carp made up 80–84% of fish at Stations 4B, 5, and 4A. Station 2 had 30 taxa; Station 5 had 21 taxa, with carp at 84%.
- **Substrate control:** The authors attributed the diversity contrast mainly to substrate. The Ames outfall could not be separated from the substrate change, since the plant sits near the geological transition.
- **Flow and nutrients:** Nutrient concentrations declined during high-chlorophyll periods, and orthophosphate below Ames was negatively related to discharge, consistent with effluent dilution.
- **Nutrient status:** Nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations exceeded levels considered likely to promote algal growth (Sawyer, 1947). The authors judged algal growth probably controlled by physical factors rather than nutrients.
- **Recovery zone:** Water quality below the effluents resembled a recovery zone (Bartsch, 1948). By Station 7 (15.8 km below Ames), orthophosphate was the only parameter still significantly above Story City levels.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** DO fell below effluents, though sampling later in the day may have contributed to the decline.
- **Reservoir implications:** The proposed reservoir was expected to become eutrophic, with high plankton populations. The smallmouth bass population north of Ames would probably not persist in the reservoir because of lost reproductive habitat. About 1 km of tailwater could support a restricted sport fishery, which might require regulation.
- **Persistence:** The reach supports permanent fish populations that tolerate low flows, with the same species recorded over roughly 25 years (Paloumpis, 1956).
