# Digestive Tract Contents of Adult Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This short research note by [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Center for Environmental Studies and Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]]) reports the digestive tract contents of 34 preserved adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) collected from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. Contents were dominated by planktonic crustaceans, diatoms, filamentous algae and [[Detritus]]. The author concludes that the species is anatomically equipped for, and apparently pursues, a planktivorous habit, but that the presence of benthic material indicates that bottom feeding also occurs. Because the species is rare and endangered, the study relied on specimens already preserved for other purposes.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its numbers have declined sharply and it is considered imperiled, which has drawn considerable research attention ([[McAda and Wydoski]] 1980; [[Minckley]] 1983). Basic aspects of its life history, including diet and feeding mode, were poorly known. Sacrificing individuals to examine digestive tracts was considered undesirable, so the 34 specimens that became available as preserved material were used for this analysis.

Key morphological features discussed include long, fine [[Gill Rakers]], which earlier workers ([[Hubbs and Miller]] 1953) compared with those of lake-dwelling, presumably planktivorous suckers of the genus *Chasmistes*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and sampling.** Adult fish were collected by trammel net from shoreline areas of Lake Mohave in May 1980 and January through March 1984. Fish were killed and preserved in formalin. Standard length (SL) was measured to the nearest mm, and sex was determined by internal examination. The month-by-month sample sizes are partly illegible in the scanned source.

**Sample size.** Of the 34 digestive tracts, two were empty and were excluded, leaving 32 non-empty tracts for analysis. Animal food items were counted only in the 26 specimens collected in 1984 (two of which were empty), and numbers per milliliter were reported for 24 specimens from that year.

**Sizes.** Fish were considered representative of the Lake Mohave population ([[Minckley]] 1983; [[Bozek et al.]] 1984). Males averaged 431 mm SL, and females averaged 492 mm (range 411-530 mm).

**Laboratory method.** Each tract was rinsed in 5.0 mL of water, and 1.0 mL of the suspension was counted in a [[Sedgwick-Rafter Counting Chamber]] at 35-400x magnification. Frequency of occurrence was the percentage of non-empty tracts containing an item, and predominance was the percentage of tracts in which that item was the largest proportion of total contents, estimated by examining whole tracts. Together these measures were used to judge relative importance.

**Behavioral observations.** Earlier field observations, including those at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, describe fish "bouncing" along the bottom during forward movement ([[Minckley]] 1973). Fish were seen taking large mouthfuls of sediment, manipulating it, and expelling material through the opercles while apparently retaining food. Suckers also burrowed headfirst into sediment, sometimes to eye level, for several minutes.

## Key Findings

**1. Diet composition**
- Planktonic crustaceans, rotifers, diatoms, detritus and filamentous algae occurred in at least 44% of tracts.
- [[Bosmina]] sp. was the most abundant food item. Other cladocerans occurred, but only [[Daphnia]] sp. was reported as a notable component (72% of fish, as given in the scanned text).
- [[Rotifers]], benthic [[Ostracoda]], [[Copepoda]] and [[Chironomidae]] larvae were found in about 53%, 53%, 34% and 3% of tracts respectively. Except for rotifers, numbers were low.
- Among plant material, the diatom [[Fragilaria Crotonensis]] was found in nearly all tracts, and filamentous green algae were also common.
- [[Detritus]] and amorphous organic matter occurred in 56% of tracts. Inorganic material (for example, sand) occurred in 16% of fish.
- Benthic items such as ostracods and copepods occurred in about one-third of tracts but were predominant in none. Frequency and predominance therefore must be considered together.

**2. Feeding interpretation**
- The author concludes that gill raker morphology and diet indicate active planktivory.
- Digestive contents also included benthic material. It is unclear whether this material was selected as food or was ingested incidentally during burrowing or sediment excavation.
- Plankton such as *Bosmina* and *Daphnia* may also accumulate at the sediment-water interface after death, which complicates interpretation.

**3. Comparison with earlier studies**
- [[Allan and Roden]] (1978) reported razorback suckers feeding on zooplankton swarming near artificial lights in [[Lake Mead]] and Lake Mohave, and [[Minckley]] (1973) reported planktonic crustacean feeding in May in Lake Mohave.
- [[Dill]] (1944) examined four specimens from the [[Colorado River]] near Parker, Arizona, which were packed with silt rich in diatoms and filamentous algae.
- [[Banks]] (1964) found plant debris and aquatic insect larvae (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Diptera) in fish from the [[Green River]], Utah.
- [[Vanicek]] (1967) found mud or clay, chironomid larvae and plant stems in eight fish from the same river.

**4. Overall conclusion**
The study supports planktivory with a definite benthic component in the adult razorback sucker of Lake Mohave. The author notes that the mixed diet should inform management and conservation planning for this [[Endangered Species]].
