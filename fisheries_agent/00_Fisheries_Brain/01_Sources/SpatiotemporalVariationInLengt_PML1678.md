# Spatiotemporal Variation in Length-Weight Relationships of Endangered Humpback Chub: Implications for Conservation and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This study examines seasonal and long-term variation in the condition (weight at a given length) of adult [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endangered big-river fish. The largest population occurs in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and the nearby main-stem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The main stem is regulated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]], whereas the LCR retains a relatively natural hydrograph. Condition declined during the spring spawning season and recovered from summer through early winter. Main-stem fish recovered faster after spawning. Grand Canyon fish were heavier at length than fish from the other populations sampled, and LCR condition declined from 1978 to 1996. The authors recommend more frequent population-size monitoring, mark-recapture studies during spawning, and condition sampling in October–November.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is one of four endangered big-river fishes endemic to the Colorado River Basin. The others are the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. The species was first described in 1946 and listed as endangered in 1967.

Humpback chub persist in six populations:
- Five populations in the upper Colorado River basin above [[Lake Powell]]: Black Rocks, Cataract Canyon, Desolation–Gray Canyons, Westwater Canyon, and Yampa Canyon.
- One large population in Grand Canyon, comprising nine main-stem aggregations and one LCR aggregation.

The LCR, with base flow of about 6.5 m³/s, is the largest tributary of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon. Adults from the largest main-stem aggregation, near the LCR confluence, are the only ones that ascend the LCR to spawn. The degree of mixing between this aggregation and the apparently resident LCR aggregation is unclear.

**Diet:** No predam diet data exist. Postdam studies show a diet of mainly aquatic macroinvertebrates, with plant material, terrestrial invertebrates, and occasionally small fish and reptiles. The species appears to be a generalist, opportunistic feeder. It has a simple S-shaped gut and no [[Pyloric Caeca]], which are typical fat-storage organs in many fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime:** Since [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963, main-stem temperatures near the LCR confluence have ranged from 8 to 12 °C year-round. This is below the 16–22 °C range considered suitable for spawning and incubation. LCR temperatures range from 2 to 27 °C, and suitable spawning temperatures are reached in March. Because main-stem temperatures preclude reproduction, nearly all recruitment comes from LCR spawning.

**Food base:** Dam-related retention of particulate organic matter and cold, isothermal water eliminated most native macroinvertebrates from the main stem. Most instream primary and invertebrate production now occurs in the first 26 km below the dam. It is dominated by [[Cladophora Glomerata]], the non-native amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]], and chironomid midges.

**Hydrology:** The main stem is regulated, while the LCR still shows a relatively natural hydrograph and seasonal warming. The authors suggest dam-mediated changes, such as clear water and higher primary production, may have had a net positive effect on main-stem fish.

**Parasites:** The Asian fish tapeworm, [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]], was found in the Grand Canyon fish population. It can emaciate hosts, and the authors note it as a possible contributor to declining condition.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Sampling
- Fish were captured with hoop nets, trammel nets, and electrofishing. LCR sampling ran from July 1991 to December 1995, and main-stem sampling from January 1990 to November 1993.
- Each fish was weighed in grams and measured for total length (TL) in millimeters.
- Females were excluded because their weights fluctuate with egg expulsion during spawning. Only identifiable males and fish of indeterminate sex were analyzed.
- Only fish ≥200 mm TL were included, approximating the minimum size at maturity in Grand Canyon.
- Sample sizes were: LCR Powell Camp (1.31 km from confluence) N = 766; LCR Salt Camp (9–14.9 km from confluence) N = 1,159; main stem N = 845 (6.9 km upstream to 6.6 km downstream of the confluence).
- Upper-basin sample sizes were: Black Rocks N = 417; Cataract Canyon N = 20; Desolation–Gray Canyons N = 99; Westwater Canyon N = 137; Yampa Canyon N = 109.
- Analysis used [[Analysis of Covariance]] (ANCOVA), with log-transformed weight as the response, length as the covariate, and month, season, or river as the grouping factor. Comparisons were corrected with the sequential Bonferroni method (experimentwise α = 0.05).
- Predicted weight was calculated for a standard 300 mm TL adult. The authors used ANCOVA-based predicted weights rather than [[Relative Condition]] indices, which they considered statistically less robust.
- Repeated-measures models were not used because recapture rates (from [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]]) were low, so samples were treated as independent.
- Data from multiple studies (including [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] netting, 1978–1996) were pooled after tests showed no significant study effect (lower reach F = 1.91, P = 0.1672; upper reach F = 3.29, P = 0.0701).

### Seasonal Patterns
- Main-stem fish showed defined seasonal patterns, with highest condition in February–April and lowest in June or July.
- LCR fish were also in best condition in spring but showed a more irregular pattern, with lowest condition in fall (month effect, Grand Canyon F = 23.13; LCR F = 26.97; both P = 0.0001).
- Main-stem fish were at least 10% heavier than LCR fish in 20 of 22 months compared. No month showed LCR fish significantly heavier than main-stem fish at length.
- Main-stem fish regained condition faster after spawning. In 1992–1993, their condition rose during warm postspawning months, while LCR fish declined slightly (season × river interaction F = 12.09, P = 0.0005).

### Long-Term Trends
- Condition at the LCR confluence declined significantly from 1978 to 1996 (slope = −0.0037, df = 1,477, P = 0.0001). No significant autocorrelation was detected.
- Fish measured in 1990–1996 were 9–10% lighter at length than fish measured in the first seven years of the record (1978–1984).
- Post hoc comparisons from 1991–1993 showed higher condition in March–April than in June–August for Grand Canyon fish, and higher condition in March–April than in June–December for LCR fish.

### Population Comparisons
- Grand Canyon main-stem fish had significantly higher condition than LCR fish, even after accounting for seasonal differences (river effect F = 472.61, P = 0.0001).
- Length-weight relationships differed significantly among the eight locations sampled (F = 67.54; river–length interaction F = 60.81). Grand Canyon fish were the heaviest at length.
- Part of this variation is likely due to morphological differences among populations, and to differing sampling methods and observers.

### Discussion and Recommendations
- The authors attribute seasonal condition changes to spawning stress, metabolic changes associated with water temperature, and food availability. Dam effects may be favorable on the main stem but the LCR may be slower to recover due to low allochthonous input.
- The authors cannot explain the decline in LCR condition, which began in the 1980s. Possible causes include parasitism and changes in spawning timing.
- Recommended actions:
  - Mark-recapture studies during the spawning season to estimate population size and LCR residency.
  - Condition monitoring in October–November, when conditions are relatively stable, in addition to spawning-season sampling.
  - Standardized sampling across all populations, including content of somatic and reproductive tissues.
  - Population size estimates, which the authors consider the most direct measure of decline.
