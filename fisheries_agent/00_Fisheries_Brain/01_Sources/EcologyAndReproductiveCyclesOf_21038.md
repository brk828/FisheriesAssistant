# Ecology and Reproductive Cycles of the Introduced Gecko, Hemidactylus turcicus, in the Southern United States

#herpetology #gekkonidae #hemidactylus-turcicus

## Executive Summary

This study examines the ecology and reproduction of the introduced [[Mediterranean Gecko]] (*Hemidactylus turcicus*) in the southern United States, based mainly on a mark-recapture study of two five-story granite buildings on the [[Tulane University]] campus in New Orleans, Louisiana, conducted from October 1964 to June 1966. The authors report nocturnal activity, limited horizontal movement, a 1:1 adult sex ratio, a reproductive season from April to August, and a two-egg clutch laid in a nest of debris, which was the first record of nest building in the [[Gekkonidae]]. The authors suggest that [[Photoperiod]] (day length) is the most likely controlling factor of reproduction in New Orleans. The document is a reprint from *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 79, No. 1, January 1968), with authors affiliated with Texas Technological College and the University of Utah.

## Background & Description

The Gekkonidae are mostly tropical and warm-temperate lizards, predominantly Old World in distribution. Members are usually nocturnal or crepuscular, with vertical pupils, large eyes, expanded digital tips and no movable eyelids. The authors list two native genera and two species in the United States and four introduced species, including *H. turcicus*. The gecko had been recorded from Key West, Miami, Gainesville (Florida), New Orleans (Louisiana) and Brownsville (Texas) before this study.

In New Orleans, *H. turcicus* was common on old buildings, rock walls and burial vaults, with its highest densities in the city's above-ground cemeteries. On 30 September 1964, seven geckos were seen around storm drains on two large granite buildings. The authors state that they did not realise how large the population was until they began marking animals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Activity and microhabitat.** At night, geckos concentrated behind vertical storm drains and around windows. The drains ran from ground to roof and served as cover for vertical movement. During the day, the lizards retreated into crevices between granite blocks and into rotten or cracked wooden window frames. Activity began immediately after complete darkness and declined gradually after midnight. Geckos were seen in every month of the study, and one was seen moving on a building at an air temperature of 42 °F. The authors note that King (1958) reported an activity peak at 8 to 9 pm in Gainesville, whereas their data showed a less distinct peak, possibly because of small sample sizes.

**Movement.** Most recaptured geckos stayed in a small horizontal area. Vertical movements greater than 2.5 m could not be assessed, so the data do not estimate home range size. Two mature males changed buildings by crossing an open space of about 9.7 m, which the authors describe as the sort of movement expected when a species invades new areas.

**Diet.** Vision is the main sense used in feeding. Moving mealworms were eaten in the laboratory and dead ones were rejected. In May, tent caterpillars were the most prevalent food item, covering the ground and buildings. By July, ants and small beetles were most common.

**Competition and predators.** Only two other lizards occur in the study area: *Anolis carolinensis* and *Lygosoma laterale*. Both are diurnal, and *Lygosoma* is restricted to the ground, so the nocturnal gecko rarely overlaps with them in habitat or activity period.

**Disturbance.** The hurricane that struck New Orleans in September 1965 tore shingles from roofs, damaged drains and flooded cracks. The building population fell sharply, with a maximum of only five geckos seen per night afterwards. The authors suggest the storm's main effect was dispersal rather than mortality.

## Key Findings

**Population and movement**
- Of 265 animals marked, 106 (40%) were recaptured once, and the maximum number of recaptures for one animal was six (Table 1 counts: 49, 21, 12, 5 and 2 animals for two to six recaptures).
- 82% of singly recaptured geckos were found within 6 m of their initial capture site.
- The mean distance between initial capture and first recapture was 5.4 ± 9.7 (0–69.8) m. No correlation was found between distance moved and time since marking.
- Some geckos were not seen for over a year yet were recaptured within 75 cm of the original site, suggesting they spend much time on upper building surfaces.

**Sex ratio**
- Of 130 adults sexed, 49% were male, close to a 1:1 ratio. Juveniles were not sexed.

**Tail regeneration**
- No gecko fully regenerated its tail within the 60-day observation period. The most complete regeneration was a juvenile at 84% in 35 days.
- Regeneration rate was higher in juveniles than in males, and higher in males than in females, but the differences were not significant at the 95% confidence level.
- Relative tail length (tail length/snout-vent length) increased with age: 0.73 in two embryos, 0.94 (0.87–1.05) in juveniles of 25–34 mm, 0.96 (0.92–1.02) in animals of 35–44 mm, and 1.07 (1.01–1.21) in 15 adults over 45 mm.

**Reproductive season and maturity**
- The reproductive season extended from April to early August. The first female with large ovarian eggs was seen on 18 May, and the last with oviducal eggs on 8 August.
- Females matured in the winter or spring of their second year. The smallest male sexed measured 42.5 mm; size at male maturity was not determined.
- A copulating pair was observed on 1 June 1965. The authors infer that sperm are stored, since eggs are fertilised in the oviduct.

**Clutch and egg production**
- Each clutch contained two eggs, and about two months were needed to produce one clutch. Most females probably produce two clutches per season, and some may produce three.
- Ovulation occurs when follicles reach 8–8.5 mm in diameter.
- One female contained two eggs in a single oviduct; no female had two enlarged ova in one ovary.

**Nesting**
- Only one natural clutch was found: two eggs on the floor of an unused closet, in a cleared space about 25 mm across. Debris covered the eggs within 12 hours, forming a mound about 20 mm high.
- In a laboratory box of debris, all 12 females laid within one week and built nests. The authors describe this as the first observation of nest building in the Gekkonidae.
- Eggs were ovoid and hard-shelled, averaging 10.9 (10.4–11.7) mm long and 8.9 (8.5–9.9) mm wide. Egg-eating (oophagy) was not observed.

**Incubation and hatchlings**
- The average incubation period was 40 (37–45) days for 11 eggs, at about 31 °C in the closet.
- Hatchlings averaged 25.9 (25.0–27.7) mm snout-vent length.
- Hatchlings survived 30 to 33 days without food or water. Two of three died after 30 days and one after 33 days; the cause (starvation or dehydration) was not determined.

**Testicular cycle**
- Spermatogenesis began in late fall or early winter, when testes were smallest. By December, primary spermatocytes and maturing sperm were present.
- Sperm were packed in the epididymides by April, when a male was capable of inseminating a female.
- Spermatogenesis declined in July, and testes regressed rapidly in August.
- The cycle resembles that of *Uta stansburiana* and other temperate-zone lizards.

**Regulation of reproduction**
- The authors conclude that day length is probably the controlling environmental factor in New Orleans. Day length began shortening in June, while mean monthly temperature did not begin to fall until August, when testicular regression was completed.
- The authors note that no experimental evidence on light and temperature effects is available for this species.

**Introduction history**
- The authors could not determine whether the New Orleans population came directly from the Old World, from eastern seaboard populations, from the West Indies, or from several sources, but they suggest it was introduced more than once.
- The gecko can survive the most severe New Orleans winters.
