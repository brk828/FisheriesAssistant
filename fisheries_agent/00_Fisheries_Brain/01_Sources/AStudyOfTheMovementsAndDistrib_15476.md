# A Study of the Movements and Distribution of Adult and Juvenile Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

## Executive Summary
This 1994 [[U.S. National Biological Survey]] study plan outlines a 30-month telemetry-based investigation into the movement, distribution, and habitat preferences of adult and juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Lower [[Colorado River]] spanning Arizona and Nevada. The [[Lake Mohave]] population — monitored by [[Arizona State University]] for nearly 20 years — had declined from an estimated 60,000 fish (1988) to 25,000 fish (1992), with the majority of surviving adults believed to be over 35 years old and approaching the end of their natural lifespan. Natural juvenile recruitment is essentially absent (only 6 wild juveniles documented in 20 years), driving a large-scale stocking initiative by the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (10,000–50,000 hatchery-reared juveniles). The study's core goal was to use ultrasonic telemetry to determine whether suckers distribute randomly across the reservoir or exhibit geographic, seasonal, depth/thermal, and diel habitat preferences — data intended to refine stocking protocols and improve survival of released juveniles, which suffer heavy size-dependent predation from sunfish, largemouth bass, striped bass, and channel catfish.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Xyrauchen texanus*, a large-bodied catostomid distinguished by a keeled, "razorback" dorsal ridge behind the head.
- **Size classes referenced**:
  - Juveniles targeted for tagging: 30–35 cm (with 32–34 cm identified as the practical minimum for standard tag implantation).
  - Juveniles vulnerable to predation: <15 cm.
  - Adults: sufficient abdominal cavity volume to easily accommodate standard sonic tags without size constraints.
- **Anatomical notes relevant to surgical tagging**: The species possesses a "thick abdominal wall...well suited for surgical implantation," with sufficient abdominal cavity space in adults; juvenile abdominal capacity is more limited, requiring preliminary anatomical assessment using preserved specimens.
- **Behavioral/morphological maturation trend**: Juveniles become progressively less reclusive ("less reclusive as they mature"), suggesting an ontogenetic shift in predator-avoidance behavior rather than a purely morphological one.
- **Longevity**: Adults in [[Lake Mohave]] are estimated to live beyond 35 years, among the longest-lived catostomids documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat use**: [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]] primarily occupy the main reservoir body, congregating along wave-washed alluvial shoreline terraces for spawning (February–April). Minor spawning also occurs in the riverine portion of the reservoir.
- **Seasonal behavior**: During summer, large schools have been observed at the surface of the main reservoir and directly downstream of [[Hoover Dam]]; post-spawning dispersal patterns (dispersal vs. aggregation in deep water) are unknown.
- **Juvenile microhabitat**: Scuba observations in [[Davis Cove]] and [[Yuma Cove]] indicate juveniles conceal themselves in dense aquatic vegetation during the day and become active at night — a nocturnal, vegetation-associated behavior pattern that may not generalize from shallow coves to the open reservoir.
- **Thermal structuring of habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]] is divided into five ecologically distinct zones based on depth, morphometry, and thermal regime:
  1. **Lower Canyon** (Davis Dam to Mile 12) — deep, thermally stratified canyon reach.
  2. **Cottonwood Basin** (Mile 12–20) — broad, shallow, wave-washed basin.
  3. **Castle Cliff Basin** (Mile 20–38) — long, narrow secondary basin.
  4. **Interface Zone** (Mile 38–50) — warm/cold water interface where cold [[Hoover Dam]] releases dive beneath warmer reservoir water in summer.
  5. **Black Canyon** (Mile 60–62) — constantly cold (~12°C), riverine canyon reach.
- **Predation pressure**: Small razorback suckers (<15 cm) face substantial predation from non-native [[Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]], identified as a primary bottleneck to recruitment; survival is strongly size-dependent.
- **Comparable systems**: Related telemetry work on reservoir-dwelling razorbacks exists only from [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] (Ulmer 1987) and a Colorado River gravel pit near Grand Junction (McAda and Wydoski 1980); most other razorback movement research (Tyus 1987, Valdez and Masslich 1989, Creef et al. 1992) focused on riverine, not reservoir, populations.

## Key Findings
- The [[Lake Mohave]] razorback sucker population declined by ~58% between 1988 and 1992 (60,000 → 25,000 estimated fish), with an aging, largely post-reproductive adult cohort and near-total recruitment failure.
- Only 6 wild-produced juvenile razorback suckers were documented reservoir-wide over a 20-year monitoring period (4 in 1987, 2 in 1988, all from [[Eldorado Canyon]]), underscoring the near-collapse of natural reproduction/recruitment.
- Predation is strongly implicated as the principal recruitment bottleneck, with vulnerability concentrated in fish under 15 cm.
- Standard 6 g (16 × 60 mm) [[Sonotronics]] ultrasonic transmitters were deemed viable for adults and for juveniles ≥30–35 cm, based on abdominal cavity assessments in preserved specimens; smaller tags exist but trade signal strength and battery life for reduced size.
- Transmitter effective detection range was estimated at ~500–2,000 m (average ~1,000 m), variable with thermal stratification, wave action, and background noise — informing a reservoir-wide transect grid of ~90 listening stations on 1,600 m centers.
- The study proposed a dual-track survey design: (1) routine monthly reservoir-wide surveys recording fish location, body temperature, and depth, and (2) intensive individual fish tracking (19-week rotation) to characterize fine-scale diel and habitat-specific movement.
- Core hypotheses framed for statistical testing (chi-square, non-parametric comparisons, time-series regression) included: random vs. non-random geographic distribution, seasonal distribution shifts, thermal/depth habitat preference, riverine vs. lentic habitat preference, and diel/nocturnal shoreward movement.
- Findings were intended to directly inform a stocking protocol for the [[Native Fish Work Group]]'s planned release of 10,000–50,000 hatchery-reared juveniles, including optimal release timing, cover type, and avoidance of prolonged backwater releases that may attract roving predators like [[Striped Bass]].
- This work plan represents a cooperative effort among [[National Biological Survey]], [[Arizona State University]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[National Park Service]], with results intended to support the [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River]].
