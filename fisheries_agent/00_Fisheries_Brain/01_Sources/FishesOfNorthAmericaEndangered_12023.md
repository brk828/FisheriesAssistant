# Fishes of North America Endangered, Threatened, or of Special Concern: 1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-status

## Executive Summary

This update of the [[Deacon et al. 1979]] list, published by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) Endangered Species Committee, catalogs 364 North American fishes that warrant protection because of rarity. Compared with the 1979 list of 251 taxa, 139 taxa were added and 26 removed (16 for taxonomic or status corrections, 10 for extinction). No fish was removed because of successful recovery. Of the remaining listed taxa, 49 changed status (7 improved, 24 declined, 18 reclassified after new data). The authors conclude that recovery efforts are locally effective but are lagging behind the overall decline of the fish fauna, and they call for ecosystem-level conservation and long-term monitoring.

## Background & Description

The list covers freshwater and nonanadromous fishes from Canada, the United States (including Hawaii), and Mexico. Strictly marine fishes and distinct anadromous stocks were excluded. Definitions follow the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, with an added "special concern" category for taxa that could become threatened through minor habitat disturbance or that need more data.

Threat categories used in the list are:
1. Habitat destruction, modification, or curtailment
2. Overuse (commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational)
3. Disease
4. Other factors (hybridization, exotic introductions, predation, competition)
5. Restricted range

Taxonomic changes adopted include placing Pacific Basin trouts in *[[Oncorhynchus]]* following [[Smith and Stearley 1989]], and elevating *Cyprinella* to generic rank following [[Mayden 1989]]. The list contains 147 special concern, 114 threatened, and 103 endangered taxa, distributed across 22 Canadian, 254 U.S., and 123 Mexican occurrences. Appendix I gives per-state and per-province totals, with the highest counts in Nevada (43), California (42), Tennessee (40), Coahuila (28), and Chihuahua (28).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat degradation from [[Agriculture]], mining, industry, and urban development remains the dominant problem, and the threat factors have changed little since 1979. Introductions of exotic species and transplants beyond native ranges are also major drivers. Key examples include:

- **Acid rain:** About 100,000 lakes in Ontario and Quebec have been chemically altered. All wild stocks of the [[Aurora Trout]] were eliminated, and half the range of the [[Acadian Whitefish]] (Nova Scotia endemic) was lost.
- **Overuse:** Only 11 taxes (3%) are listed partly because of overuse, mostly salmonids, large catostomids, and catfishes.
- **Desert springs and ciénegas:** Desert fishes such as [[Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon*) and [[Springfish]] (*Crenichthys*) face habitat loss, water scarcity, and competition from non-native fishes such as *[[Gambusia affinis]]* and *Oreochromis*.
- **Large rivers:** Sturgeons and other large-river fishes depend on intact river systems; impoundments and dredging have eliminated much of the habitat of the [[Sicklefin Chub]].
- **Caves and groundwater:** The [[Alabama Cavefish]] is threatened by pollutants and a planned sludge disposal area within its groundwater recharge zone.

Listed fishes are concentrated in the southwestern U.S. and northern Mexico (Chihuahua, Sonora, Coahuila), and in the southeastern U.S. (Alabama, Tennessee, Georgia, and surrounding states). Midwestern states hold about 9% of listed fishes despite high diversity.

## Key Findings

**Overall list and status changes**
- Total: 364 taxa (251 from 1979, plus 139 new, minus 26 removed).
- Removed: 16 for taxonomic or status error, 10 for extinction; 0 for recovery.
- Status changes: 7 improved, 24 declined, 18 reclassified as data corrections.
- Only one taxon moved from "extinct" back to endangered: the [[Shoshone Pupfish]], rediscovered in 1986.
- Extinctions listed in Table 1 include the [[Longjaw Cisco]], [[Deepwater Cisco]], [[Blackfin Cisco]], and [[Blue Pike]]. These extinctions were mainly due to overfishing, habitat decline, and exotic introductions. Extinction details appear in [[Miller et al. 1989]].

**Family-level patterns**
- *Salmonidae:* 34 taxa, with 18 new listings. Eight are *O. clarki* subspecies and eight are *O. mykiss* subspecies; together they make up nearly 50% of listed salmonids. Of the salmonids, 72% are western and 13% are Great Lakes fishes.
- *Cyprinidae:* 96 minnows, 65% U.S. endemics, 21% Mexican, and 13% shared. Nearly 55% occur in southwestern and western desert streams. The genus *Gila* has 26 taxa, including 12 subspecies of *G. bicolor*.
- *Catostomidae and Ictaluridae:* Only 5 of 27 sucker taxa and none of 19 bullhead catfish species are listed at the subspecific level. 21 of 27 suckers are western.
- *Cyprinodontidae, Goodeidae, Poeciliidae:* 75 taxa, more than double the 36 in 1979. Only 6 (8%) occur in more than one state.
- *Percidae:* 50 darters, nearly all in the southeastern U.S.; 31 have restricted distributions.
- *Centrarchidae:* Only 8 sunfish taxa listed, indicating the family is less threatened.

**Case studies**
- *[[Apache Trout]]* (no change): Recovery efforts since about 1950s are compromised by brown trout contamination.
- *[[Gila Trout]]*: Downlisted from endangered to threatened.
- *[[Loach Minnow]]*: Range reduced to about 380 km from 2600 km historically; upgraded to threatened.
- *[[Yaqui Chub]]*: Abundant in Sonora; Arizona populations protected, so changed to threatened.
- *[[Gila Topminnow]]*: Reintroductions succeeded; downlisted to special concern.
- *[[Cape Fear Shiner]]*: One of three taxa moved from special concern to endangered after range and population declines.
- *[[Lost River Sucker]]*: Moved to endangered due to lack of recruitment over two decades.
- *[[Cyprinodon]] species of Laguna Chichankanab* (five syntopic species): Listed as threatened because of restricted range and introduction of *Oreochromis*.
- *[[Gambusia georgei]]*: Presumed extinct; not seen since 1983.

**Conservation recommendations**
- Manage for entire ecosystems and communities rather than single species, since this gives more lasting protection.
- Limit off-site translocation to cases where native habitat is lost.
- Establish long-term monitoring programs to provide baseline status data. Many 1979 status errors resulted from inadequate data.
- The authors note that without federal, state, and private efforts the list would be considerably longer.
