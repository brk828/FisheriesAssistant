# Statewide Survey of Arizona Anglers: 1986, 1989 and 1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Todd Pringle of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Fisheries Branch, presents results of statewide mail surveys of licensed anglers conducted in 1986, 1989 and 1992. The 1992 survey received responses from over 2,100 anglers. Its purpose was to understand angler behavior, preferences, spending and attitudes so that the Department could provide a variety of fishing opportunities and support responsive management and strategic planning.

Anglers fished an estimated 7.2 million user-days on Arizona waters in 1992, of which 2.5 million were for trout and 4.7 million for warmwater fishes. User-days have remained near 7.1 million since 1986 even as license sales declined, because individual anglers are fishing more days each year.

## Background & Description

Arizona anglers are a diverse group. Some pursue trout in high-mountain streams using artificial lures and flies, while others fish opportunistically for a variety of species with bait and lures. The survey followed earlier statewide angler surveys beginning in 1985 and informed the Department's [[Coldwater Fisheries Strategic Plan]], [[Warmwater Fisheries Strategic Plan]] and the [[Wildlife 2000 Strategic Plan]].

**Methods.** The target population was all people licensed to fish in Arizona in each survey year, stratified into eight license classes (resident and nonresident general fishing, combination hunting and fishing, Colorado River nonresident, 9-day and 5-day nonresident, and urban). Samples were drawn from license sales, with mailing lists generated from sales through the preceding months. Sample sizes were approximately 15,000 (1986), 8,000 (1989) and 7,000 (1992). Questionnaires were mailed with follow-up waves in 1986 and single mailings in 1989 and 1992. Response rates were about 50% (1986), 25% (1989) and 27% (1992). Nonresponse bias in 1986 was adjusted using regression extrapolation of responses across mailing waves, and the 1992 estimate of fishing participation was adjusted downward by 4 percentage points.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most anglers fish in or near home. Metropolitan residents of the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]] and [[Tucson Metropolitan Area]], who hold over 50% of the state's population, dominate local use. Fish Management Area 22 (adjacent to Phoenix and associated with the [[Salt River Project]] reservoirs) had the highest use at approximately 1.3 million user-days per year. Areas 19 and 25 also increased substantially. Use of [[Colorado River]] reservoirs by trout anglers fell from 211,000 user-days in 1986 to 77,000 in 1992 (-37%), and stream and river fishing pressure declined by about 26% since 1985.

Native fish management was a recurring topic. Species mentioned include the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Apache Trout]] and [[Desert Pupfish]].

## Key Findings

**Fishing effort and use**
- Total user-days: about 7.2 million in 1992 (trout 2.5 million; warmwater 4.7 million). User-days were 6.7 million in 1986 and 7.3 million in 1989 by the question 5 estimates.
- Mean days fished per angler rose from 17.5 (1986) to 22.7 (1992); median rose from 9 to 12 days. Anglers fishing more than 20 days increased, while those fishing fewer than 6 days declined, indicating a loss of occasional anglers.
- Largest increases in use were in city waters (+255%) and irrigation canals (+223%), linked partly to the Department's [[Urban Fishing Program]].
- Most anglers (about 75%) fished for more than one species; about 38% fished only one area.

**Target species and preferences**
- Trout and largemouth bass were the most preferred species in all three years, followed by channel catfish and striped bass. Trout were preferred by 34.6% of 1992 respondents.
- Percent of fishing days targeted: trout 34%, "anything that bites" 24%, largemouth bass 18%, striped bass 9%.
- Single-species anglers in 1992 included trout (16.3%) and largemouth bass (3.9%).
- Given a choice, 46% preferred four 12-inch trout over six 8.5-inch trout; anglers generally prefer fewer, larger trout.

**Fishing methods and attitudes**
- Bait and lure users (mixed) made up about 60% of anglers; bait-only anglers fell from 31% (1989) to 15% (1992); lure/fly-only anglers held near 18%.
- Belief that keeping fish is necessary for a successful trip rose from one-third (1989) to 54% (1992), mostly in the coldwater community. Overall about 43% agreed and 57% disagreed.
- On native fish, 37% favored not disrupting sport fish management, 26% favored doing more to protect native fish, and 37% were undecided. Asked whether native fish should be managed as sport fish, 31% said yes, 22% no, and 47% had no opinion.
- Willingness to buy a special use stamp for managed fisheries (e.g., Lee's Ferry, Becker Lake, Alamo Lake) was split: 41% yes, 43% no, 16% no opinion. For Lee's Ferry, 46% preferred current management, 29% a special use stamp, and 5% no harvest.

**Spending**
- Median spending per fishing day was $50; mean $57.27 (1992). Balt-only anglers spent least ($39/day); nonresidents spent more ($75 vs. $50 for residents).
- Estimated annual spending was $360 to $569 million, with an economic benefit to the state of approximately $548 million, based on the 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service survey figure of $79 per day.

**License sales and reasons for not buying**
- License sales declined roughly 18% from 1986 to 1992. Among those not planning to buy a license the following year, the top reasons were poor fishing, license too expensive and not enough spare time.

**Angler profile (1992)**
- Residents: predominantly male (76%), median age 36. Females were 24% of the fishing public. Residents accounted for about 94% of user-days (about 6.8 million), fishing large inland reservoirs and mountain trout lakes.
- Nonresidents: 84% male, median age 44, mainly from California, fishing the [[Colorado River]] and its reservoirs (about 430,000 user-days, 6%).
- Average years fished: 32 overall; 17 in Arizona, indicating many anglers are new to the state.
- Only 11% belong to a fishing club or organization. Key information sources were weekly newspaper fishing reports and local tackle shops.
- 38% had their license checked by an Arizona Game and Fish officer; 67% reported seeing an officer during a fishing trip.
