# Geographic Variations in Survival of Hybrids Between Etheostomatine Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae #Etheostomatinae #hybridization #speciation #reproductive-isolation #darters

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive study analyzed over 500,000 darter eggs from 1954 to 1965 to investigate geographic variation in hybrid survival among etheostomatine fishes (darters). More than 130 hybrid combinations were tested, with both reciprocals reared for 70 combinations. Key findings include: (1) darter hybrids were typically as viable or more viable than controls, demonstrating heterosis; (2) heterospecific eggs inhibit foreign sperm, especially after sperm has been active for more than ten seconds—a mechanism reducing chance hybridization in rapid-water spawning environments; (3) the first documented example of postmating reinforcement of isolation mechanisms was discovered in sympatric populations of [[Percina caprodes]] (logperch) and [[Etheostoma lepidum]] (greenthroat darter), where allopatric populations produced more viable hybrids than sympatric ones; (4) reciprocal hybrid crosses showed differential survival, with maternal effects predominating; (5) fecundity studies revealed that darters in species-rich areas produce more numerous, smaller eggs than those in peripheral, species-poor regions, suggesting adaptation to varying competitive pressures during larval stages.

## Background & Description

The subfamily Etheostomatinae (darters) comprises small, benthic freshwater fishes in the family [[Percidae]], distributed across North America from Texas to Canada. This study focused on multiple species across three genera: *Etheostoma* (advanced darters), *Percina*, and *Hadropterus* (primitive darters). The research employed controlled laboratory breeding experiments using stripped eggs and sperm to test hybrid viability across developmental stages: fertilization, eye pigmentation, hatching, prolarval completion, and postlarval survival.

**Species Studied (Primary Focus):**

- **[[Etheostoma spectabile]]** (orangethroat darter): Wide-ranging species (Texas to Michigan) with eggs averaging 1.22–1.57 mm diameter depending on locality. Laboratory survival up to 75% between 10–27°C. Spawns by burying in gravel.

- **[[Etheostoma lepidum]]** (greenthroat darter): Restricted to clear, spring-fed waters of Nueces, Guadalupe, and Colorado river systems in Texas. Eggs 1.28–1.56 mm. Survival 80% between 8–29°C. Attaches eggs to aquatic vegetation.

- **[[Etheostoma caeruleum]]** (rainbow darter): Ozark to Canada distribution. Eggs 1.47–1.82 mm. Survival up to 50% between 11–29°C.

- **[[Percina caprodes]]** (logperch): Canada to Texas. Eggs 1.31–1.74 mm, varying geographically. Survival up to 25% between 22–25°C. Produces 10–20 eggs per spawning act on stream bottom.

- **[[Hadropterus scierus]]** (dusky darter): Guadalupe River, Texas to central Indiana. Eggs 1.45–1.78 mm. Narrow thermal tolerance (22–27°C), survival up to 25%.

Additional species tested included [[Etheostoma tetrazona]], [[Etheostoma zonale]], [[Etheostoma blennioides]], [[Etheostoma punctulatum]], [[Etheostoma juliae]], [[Etheostoma radiosum]], [[Hadropterus shumardi]], and others.

**Methodology:**
Eggs were stripped into pans, fertilized with sperm from heterospecific or conspecific males, and reared at controlled temperatures. Survival was recorded at five developmental stages. Time-lapse fertilization experiments tested sperm viability by delaying egg exposure to sperm in water (typically 10–25 seconds at 15°C). Egg size measurements provided fecundity indices (larger eggs = fewer eggs per female).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Darters occupy diverse freshwater habitats, primarily riffles and runs in streams and rivers. Larval ecology varies: [[Etheostoma spectabile]] larvae are benthic, while [[Etheostoma lepidum]] and primitive darter larvae (e.g., [[Percina caprodes]], [[Hadropterus scierus]]) are free-swimming in upper water columns. Adults of all species are benthic. This ontogenetic habitat shift creates differential competitive pressures.

**Geographic Patterns in Egg Size and Fecundity:**
Egg size varied systematically with geography and species richness:

- **North-South Cline:** Northern populations (Missouri, Michigan) had smaller eggs (higher fecundity) than southern populations (Texas). Example: *E. spectabile* eggs averaged 1.22 mm in Missouri vs. 1.48–1.57 mm in Texas Guadalupe River.

- **East-West Cline:** Eastern populations had smaller eggs than western populations at similar latitudes. Example: *P. caprodes* eggs 1.35 mm in Missouri vs. 1.71 mm in western Colorado System (Edwards Plateau).

- **Species Richness Correlation:** Areas with many sympatric darter species (e.g., Missouri Ozarks) had smaller eggs (higher fecundity) than peripheral areas with few species (e.g., western Texas). This pattern suggests adaptation to larval competition intensity: high fecundity benefits species in low-competition (few-species) areas, while larger eggs with more yolk benefit survival in high-competition (many-species) environments.

**Reproductive Ecology:**
Darters are fractional spawners, breeding multiple times per season. Spawning sites vary: *E. spectabile* buries eggs in gravel, *E. lepidum* attaches eggs to vegetation, *P. caprodes* deposits eggs on substrate surface. Sperm viability in water is exceedingly short (≤25 seconds at 15°C), much shorter than other freshwater fishes, an adaptation to prevent hybridization in rapid-water spawning environments.

**Thermal Tolerance:**
Developmental temperature ranges varied among species and populations. Northern populations often tolerated higher temperatures than southern populations (e.g., Missouri *E. spectabile* optimal postlarval survival near 30°C vs. 26–27°C for Texas populations), reflecting rapid seasonal warming in northern latitudes.

## Key Findings

### 1. Hybrid Viability and Heterosis

**Intrageneric Hybrids (within *Etheostoma*):**
- 92 of 144 combinations (69%) successfully reared through larval stages.
- Posthatching survival indices: 82% above 100 (median 135).
- Total survival indices: 75% above 100 (median 112).
- Hybrids consistently showed heterosis (hybrid vigor) compared to controls.

**Intergeneric Hybrids (between primitive and advanced darters):**
- 53 of 85 combinations (62%) successfully reared.
- Posthatching survival: 75% above 100 (median 161–170).
- Total survival: 71% above 100 (median 118).
- Extreme heterosis in posthatching stages, especially when maternal parent had high laboratory survival.

**Interpopulation (Intraspecific) Hybrids:**
- 56% of control combinations successfully reared.
- Posthatching survival: 75% above 100 (median 116).
- Generally heterotic, but with notable exceptions (see reinforcement below).

**Phylogenetic Pattern:**
Any intrasubfamilial darter hybrid could be reared to adult size, regardless of phylogenetic distance. Hybrids between darters and other fish families died before hatching. This absolute threshold contrasts with gradual hybrid inviability seen in some other taxa (e.g., *Bufo*, *Drosophila*).

### 2. Gametic Inhibition and Sperm Viability

**Time-Lapse Fertilization Results:**
- Fertilization success with "fresh" sperm (≤10 seconds delay): ~97% of control rate.
- Fertilization with "tired" sperm (>10 seconds delay): median 57–75% of control rate.
- Interpopulation hybrids: 38% of indices below 100 (median 75).
- Interspecific hybrids: 37% below 100 (median 67).
- Intergeneric hybrids: 37% below 100 (median 57).

**Mechanism:**
Heterospecific eggs inhibit foreign sperm activity, especially after sperm has expended ~50% of its normal activity period. This mechanism is most effective against "wandering" sperm swept by currents to heterospecific spawning sites, not against behavioral mating errors (which would involve fresh sperm).

**Ecological Correlation:**
Primitive darters (*Percina*, *Hadropterus*) produce many eggs deposited on open substrate, with high exposure to stray sperm. Their eggs showed stronger inhibition of foreign sperm (median time-lapse index 28–38) than *Etheostoma* eggs (median 120), which are buried or attached to vegetation.

**Sperm Vitality Variation:**
- [[Percina caprodes]] sperm: 1.5× more vigorous than *Etheostoma* sperm, correlated with producing 10–20 eggs per spawn (vs. 2–7 for *E. spectabile*) and surface deposition requiring broader sperm dispersal.
- [[Etheostoma spectabile]] Brazos River population (allopatric, no sympatric congeners): exceptionally vigorous sperm, 1.5× that of other *E. spectabile* populations.
- [[Etheostoma lepidum]] Nueces River population (allopatric): reduced sperm vigor compared to sympatric populations, possibly correlated with large eggs/low fecundity.

### 3. Reinforcement of Isolation Mechanisms (Postmating)

**[[Percina caprodes]] × [[Etheostoma lepidum]] Hybrids:**
This combination provided the clearest evidence for reinforcement—the first documented example of postmating reinforcement.

- **Allopatric crosses** (both parents from outside each other's range): Never had indices <100; median posthatching survival 150.
- **One parent allopatric:** 29% of indices <100; median 138.
- **Both parents sympatric:** 49% of indices <100; median 101.

Statistical analysis (Table 20) showed allopatric populations had ~1.5× the probability of producing viable hybrids compared to sympatric populations (P < 0.01). This pattern held for both reciprocals and across multiple stream systems (Colorado, Guadalupe).

**Supporting Evidence:**
- [[Hadropterus shumardi]] × [[Percina caprodes]]: Similar pattern of reduced sympatric hybrid survival.
- [[Etheostoma punctulatum]] ♀ × [[Etheostoma spectabile]] ♂: Reduced sympatric survival.
- [[Etheostoma spectabile]] Brazos River (allopatric): High sperm vigor in absence of congeners.

**Mechanism (Population-Level Selection):**
Traditional reinforcement models invoke individual selection: individuals avoiding hybridization don't waste gametes. This cannot explain postmating reinforcement (gametes already "wasted" in zygote). The author proposes **population-level selection**:

1. Heterotic hybrids displace parental types in preferred habitats (documented in Guadalupe River *E. lepidum* × *E. spectabile* hybrid swarm).
2. Populations producing less viable hybrids retain more breeding adults in preferred habitats.
3. These populations produce more offspring in subsequent generations.
4. Larval dispersal spreads low-hybrid-viability genotypes to adjacent riffles.
5. Frequent natural catastrophes (floods, droughts in semi-arid Texas streams) repeatedly reduce populations to small sizes, amplifying selection for reduced hybrid viability.

This model requires: (1) stable adult populations with migratory young (documented), (2) genetic uniformity within riffles due to catastrophic population bottlenecks (inferred), (3) ecological overlap between hybrids and parents (documented).

### 4. Proximate Inhibition

An unexpected pattern emerged: hybrids between parents from **two different localities within the same stream system** often had **lower** survival than hybrids from identical localities or from different stream systems.

**Examples:**
- **[[Etheostoma lepidum]] ♀ × [[Etheostoma spectabile]] ♂ (Colorado River):** All crosses heterotic except those between two different Colorado localities (P < 0.001). Time-lapse fertilization also reduced for within-system crosses.
- **[[Etheostoma caeruleum]] × [[Etheostoma spectabile]]:** Same pattern in Loose Creek and White River (Missouri-Mississippi system).
- **[[Hadropterus scierus]] × [[Percina caprodes]] (Colorado River):** Reduced survival for within-system crosses.
- **[[Percina caprodes]] ♀ × [[Etheostoma spectabile]] ♂:** Reduced survival for within-Guadalupe and within-Colorado crosses.

**Interpretation:**
Proximate inhibition supplements **behavioral isolation mechanisms** that are highly effective in sympatry but break down when populations are mixed (e.g., by floods displacing upstream species downstream). Behavioral isolation is finely tuned to local populations; heterospecific individuals from nearby (but not identical) localities may not trigger full behavioral isolation, yet postmating barriers reduce hybrid success. This pattern is consistent with:

- Morphological and physiological races separated by short distances (documented in darters).
- Hybridization most likely when one species is rare (displaced by flood) and behavioral cues disrupted.
- Natural hybrid swarms occurring where upstream species washed into downstream species' range (e.g., *E. lepidum* into *E. spectabile* range in Guadalupe River).

### 5. Reciprocal Differences and Maternal Effects

**Intergeneric Hybrids:**
Hybrids with *Etheostoma* mothers consistently more viable than those with primitive darter mothers:

- **[[Etheostoma spectabile]] ♀ × [[Percina caprodes]] ♂:** Median posthatching survival 127.6 (vs. *E. spectabile* control) and 575 (vs. *P. caprodes* control).
- **[[Percina caprodes]] ♀ × [[Etheostoma spectabile]] ♂:** Median posthatching survival 92.6 (vs. *E. spectabile*) and 333 (vs. *P. caprodes*).

All 16 possible comparisons showed higher survival when *E. spectabile* was maternal parent vs. *E. lepidum* as maternal parent in crosses with primitive darters (P < 0.01).

**Interpretation:**
Reciprocal differences paralleled maternal parent survival rates, indicating strong **maternal influence** on hybrid viability. This likely reflects egg quality, yolk provisioning, or cytoplasmic factors. Primitive darters are difficult to rear in the laboratory (narrow thermal tolerance, low survival), and their eggs confer lower viability to hybrids.

**Ecological Correlation:**
*E. spectabile* × primitive darter hybrids more viable than *E. lepidum* × primitive darter hybrids.
