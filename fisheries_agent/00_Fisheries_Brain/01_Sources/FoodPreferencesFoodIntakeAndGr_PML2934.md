# Food Preferences, Food Intake, and Growth of the F1 Hybrid of Grass Carp × Bighead Carp

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Young, Monaghan, and Hartman (1983) compared the food preferences, feeding rates, and growth of an F1 hybrid of [[Grass Carp]] (female) × [[Bighead Carp]] (male) with those of pure grass carp. The hybrid was tested as a possible sterile alternative to grass carp for [[Aquatic Weed Control]]. The hybrid ranked food plants in a consistent order and ate less vegetation than grass carp. Its growth and feeding efficiency declined sharply at 30 °C in medium and large fish. The authors concluded that the hybrid needs at least twice as many fish as grass carp of the same size to achieve the same level of vegetation control, and they did not recommend it as a weed-control agent.

## Background & Description

- **Grass carp** (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) were imported from Asia to the United States for long-term aquatic weed control in temperate and southern waters. They are fast-growing herbivores that can consume more than 100% of their body weight per day. Their gut is short, about 2.25 times standard length, and they digest only a small fraction of the plant material they ingest, so they must eat large quantities of vegetation to sustain fast growth.
- **Escape concerns:** Fears that grass carp could escape and establish feral populations, which was realized in the Mississippi River drainage, led several states to ban the species. This prompted a search for sterile alternatives that could match grass carp's weed-control effectiveness.
- **Bighead carp** (*Aristichthys nobilis*) are zooplanktivores native to the Amur River basin. Both parent species have a diploid chromosome number of 48.
- **Hybrid origin:** Earlier work assumed all grass carp × bighead carp hybrids were triploid and therefore sterile. Later studies found that the cross produces both diploid and triploid hybrids. The authors' isozyme analyses, however, classified all tested fish as [[Triploidy|triploid]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design**

- **Food-preference trial:** Hybrids (15–20 cm total length, 33–76 g) were held in three 208-liter glass aquaria on single-pass water systems at 0.4–0.6 L/min. Plant choices were [[Najas guadalupensis]] (southern naiad), [[Ceratophyllum demersum]] (coontail), [[Elodea nuttallii]] (waterweed), and a [[Filamentous Algae]] complex (*Mougeotia*, *Oedogonium*, and *Spirogyra*). All were common in southern Illinois. Run 1 offered 35 g (wet weight) of each food to six fish per tank for six days. Runs 2 and 3 offered 70 g of the two most-preferred and the two least-preferred foods, respectively.
- **Feeding and growth trials:** Four hybrids and four grass carp were held together in each aquarium at 14, 22, and 30 °C, with a screen separating the two groups. Fish were fin-clipped for individual tracking. *Najas guadalupensis* was offered ad libitum for 20 days. Trials used three size classes:
  - Small: hybrids 77 g (SD 22), grass carp 106 g (SD 18), 208-L tanks
  - Medium: hybrids 272 g (SD 67), grass carp 230 g (SD 37), 1,100-L tanks
  - Large: hybrids 860 g (SD 36), grass carp 518 g (SD 45), 1,100-L tanks, run at 30 °C only
- **Water quality:** Water quality stayed acceptable throughout. Total ammonia-nitrogen, nitrite-nitrogen, and nitrate-nitrogen never exceeded 0.6, 0.05, and 1.4 mg/L, respectively. The lowest dissolved oxygen was 3.3 mg/L, lasting one day, in the small-fish 30 °C trial. Temperatures were held within about ±1 °C of target. Temperatures spanned the normal ranges of midwestern lakes in spring, summer, and fall.
- **Analyses:** Growth was compared by analysis of variance with LSMEANS, at α < 0.05.

## Key Findings

1. **Food preference hierarchy:** Hybrids consistently preferred plants in this order: filamentous algae > *Najas guadalupensis* > *Elodea nuttallii* > *Ceratophyllum demersum*. The order held whether foods were offered in groups of four or two. Less-preferred plants were eaten more when choices were restricted. Filamentous algae were fully consumed by day 5 or 6, depending on the run.

2. **Growth and feeding by temperature:**
   - Small fish: growth and feeding rose with temperature. Significant growth differences in small grass carp occurred only between 14 and 30 °C and between 22 and 30 °C.
   - Medium-sized hybrids: growth declined with temperature, and hybrids lost weight at 30 °C. These declines were significant relative to both lower temperatures.
   - Medium-sized grass carp: growth peaked at 22 °C, though no differences were statistically significant.

3. **Size effects:** At 14 and 22 °C, medium hybrids grew significantly faster than small hybrids. At 30 °C, medium and large grass carp grew significantly faster than small grass carp. The authors report no significant size differences at 30 °C for hybrids.

4. **Grass carp superiority:** Grass carp grew significantly faster than hybrids at every temperature and size group except medium-sized fish at 14 °C. Grass carp ate at least twice as much vegetation as hybrids in all cases except medium-sized hybrids at 22 °C, and they fed more efficiently.

5. **Poor hybrid performance at 30 °C:** Of the eight hybrids in the medium and large size groups at 30 °C, six lost weight and two gained weight, while grass carp in the same tanks gained weight. The authors argue these results reflect real differences, since both forms shared identical tank conditions.

6. **Gut length does not explain the gap:** Hybrids had a mean gut length of 2.61 (SD 0.39) times standard length, compared with 2.31 (SD 0.20) in grass carp. Despite the longer gut, hybrids fed less efficiently. The authors suggest less-developed [[Pharyngeal Teeth]] and a narrower gut in the hybrid, or differences in digestive enzymes, as possible causes.

7. **Conclusion on hybrid use:** Even under favorable temperatures, the hybrid needs at least twice as many fish as grass carp of the same size to achieve equivalent vegetation control. Pond studies by Osborne (1982) also found much lower hybrid feeding and growth. The authors conclude that the hybrid is a much less efficient herbivore than grass carp and is not recommended as a weed-control agent.
