# The Fishes of the Rivers Draining the Western Slope of the Cordillera Occidental of Colombia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Carl H. Eigenmann]] (Indiana University Studies, Vol. VII, 1920, Study No. 46) reviews the freshwater fish faunas of four rivers on the western slope of the [[Cordillera Occidental]] in Colombia: the [[Atrato River]] and [[San Juan River]], which lie across a low continental divide near Istmina, and the [[Dagua River]] and [[Patia River]] further south. The author uses species distributions to test whether the Atrato-San Juan valley served as a highway for fish dispersal between the Caribbean and Pacific slopes. He concludes that it did, though the San Juan fauna is less closely related to the Atrato than the Atrato is to the [[Magdalena River]]. The Patia shows a faunal link to the rivers north of it rather than to the [[Guayas Basin]] to the south.

## Background & Description

The rivers rise on the western Andes and flow west. The [[Atrato River]] turns north into the Caribbean, while the [[San Juan River]] turns south into the Pacific. Their headwaters meet at the [[Continental Divide]] near Istmina, which lies little more than 300 ft above sea level. Eigenmann notes that a general subsidence of about 300 ft would flood the Atrato valley and separate the Atlantic and Pacific by a ridge less than 5 miles wide. The [[Dagua River]] enters the Pacific at Buenaventura, just south of the San Juan mouth. The [[Patia River]] rises with the [[Cauca River]] on the Popayan plain and has cut a deep gorge through the western Cordillera.

The Atrato was already well documented because of 19th-century surveys for an interoceanic canal ([[Isthmus of Darien]] route). Eigenmann cites the Michler expedition reports and a letter from [[Theo Gill]] describing its fish collections. Gill identified nineteen genera of teleostean fishes, largely marine, and no detailed species list was published. Eigenmann's own collections (1913) and those of the Landon-Fisher Expedition, together with Spurrell, Carpenter, Henn, and Wilson, supply most of the data. Eigenmann also adds to Schott's Atrato collections the species *Prochilodus beani*, *Astyanax orthodus*, *A. atratoensis*, and *Charax atratoensis*, described earlier with Ogle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Atrato and San Juan faunas.** The two rivers together hold 112 known species, but only 31 (about 28%) are shared. Eigenmann records 72 species from the Atrato alone and 71 from the San Juan alone. The 31 shared species make up about 44% of the San Juan fauna. He groups the shared species by range:

- **Group A:** common both north and south of the San Juan (e.g., *Rhamdia wagneri*, *Hoplias malabaricus*).
- **Group B:** common north of the Atrato, reaching their southern limit in the San Juan (e.g., *Astyanax fasciatus*, *Brycon oligolepis*).
- **Group C:** common south of the San Juan, reaching their northern limit in the Atrato (e.g., *Brycon oligolepis*-related forms, *Parastremma sadina*).
- **Group D:** confined to the two rivers (e.g., *Nematobrycon amphiloxus*, *Lebiasina multimaculata*).

**Affinity with the Magdalena.** About 45% of Atrato species occur in the [[Magdalena River]], and roughly 10% more have close parallels there. About 90% of Atrato genera are found in the Magdalena. Eigenmann describes this affinity as Magdalenan in origin, and says it is comparable to the relationship between the Paraguay and Amazon basins. Several genera absent from the Magdalena are either marine (e.g., *Pristis*, *Thalassophryne*) or restricted to the Pacific side (e.g., *Lebiasina*, *Nematobrycon*).

**Barriers and dispersal.** The divide at Istmina has acted as a partial barrier. *Prochilodus*, *Leporinus*, and *Plecostomus* are abundant in the Magdalena and Atrato and in the Guayas, but absent from the San Juan, Dagua, and intervening basins. *Hoplias malabaricus* is widespread from the Atrato south, but is replaced by the closely allied [[Hoplias Microlepis]] both north of the Tuyra and south of the Patia. Eigenmann asks whether the latter evolved independently or was displaced.

**Vertical distribution.** Eigenmann notes that environment can matter as much as altitude. Within the Patia basin, the highest tributaries (around 5,000 ft and above) contain only *Astroblepus*, *Pygidium*, and *Bryconamericus*. Lowland fishes make up most of the fauna at 1,500 ft. Highland genera such as *Pygidium* and *Astroblepus* can descend to near sea level, and the reverse is also true to a lesser extent. He cites records from Lake Titicaca and Peru for much higher limits (*Orestias* above 15,000 ft; *Astroblepus* to 13,400 ft in Ecuador).

**Patia and Dagua.** The Patia fauna includes several ecological groups: high Andean forms, lowland species of marine origin (e.g., *Tylosurus fluviatilis*, *Thyrina colombiensis*, Gobiidae), and 25 strictly freshwater species living between brackish water and 3,000 ft. Of the 25, 68% also occur in the Dagua, San Juan, or Atrato. Twenty-two species, or 62% of the whole Patia fauna, are known from the Atrato, San Juan, or Dagua. Only one species, *Brycon henni*, links the Upper Cauca with both the Dagua and the Patia, and it is absent from the Atrato. Eigenmann concludes that the Cauca's contribution across the Cordillera Occidental is "all but nil," which also argues against Magdalena fishes crossing the high Cordilleras into the Orinoco basin.

## Key Findings

- **Dispersal route confirmed:** The Atrato-San Juan valley acted as a highway for fish dispersal, though the continental divide at Istmina has restricted some genera.
- **Shared species:** 31 of 112 known Atrato-San Juan species (about 28%) are common to both rivers. 42 San Juan species have not been recorded from the Atrato.
- **Magdalenan affinity:** About 45% of Atrato species, and about 90% of its genera, are shared with or related to the Magdalena fauna. The affinity is about as strong as that of the Paraguay to the Amazon.
- **Hoplias replacement:** *Hoplias malabaricus* is replaced by *Hoplias microlepis* north of the Tuyra and south of the Patia, which raises an unresolved question about speciation versus displacement.
- **Patia origin:** The Patia fauna shows a strong link to the rivers north of it (Atrato, San Juan, Dagua). It shows little link to the Guayas to the south.
- **Cauca isolation:** The Upper Cauca has contributed almost nothing to the Dagua or Patia fauna, with *Brycon henni* as the only fully shared lowland species.
- **Vertical zonation:** Highland genera (*Astroblepus*, *Pygidium*, *Bryconamericus*) dominate the upper Patia tributaries, while lowland forms dominate lower reaches. The boundaries are not sharp.
- **Sampling caveat:** Eigenmann stresses that the lists are not exhaustive. The Atrato's western tributaries, where *Pygidium* and *Astroblepus* are expected, had not been sampled. The large fauna reported for the Telembi reflects more thorough exploration there than in the lower Patia.
