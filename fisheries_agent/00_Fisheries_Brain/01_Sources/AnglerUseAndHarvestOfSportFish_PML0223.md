# Creel Survey of Lake Havasu Sport Fishery, September 1987 to August 1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae #creel-survey

## Executive Summary

A two-year roving creel survey of [[Lake Havasu]], a reservoir on the Colorado River, was conducted from September 1987 to August 1989 by BioSystems Analysis, Inc. for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CDFG). The study estimated angler effort, catch per unit effort (CPUE), harvest, fish size and condition, and angler demographics. Total angler effort was 154,896 hours in Year 1 and 171,971 hours in Year 2. Overall CPUE rose from 0.193 fish/hour in Year 1 to 0.235 fish/hour in Year 2. Estimated [[Striped Bass]] harvest was 25,135 fish in Year 1 and 36,011 in Year 2. The authors recommended developing a population model for the striped bass fishery.

## Background & Description

The study was prepared to support fishery management strategies on reservoirs such as Lake Havasu. It followed an earlier CDFG creel study (1984–1986) whose boat-ramp-based sampling was judged inaccurate and unrepresentative, leading CDFG to recommend an expanded [[Roving Creel Survey]]. The project was funded under the [[Dingell-Johnson Sport Fish Restoration Act]] (California F-51-R, Subproject V, Study 1, Jobs 1 and 2).

[[Lake Havasu]] is a storage and diversion reservoir formed by [[Parker Dam]] (completed 1938). It is about 62 km long, 0.1 to 0.4 km wide, and 9–10 m deep in places, and was divided into nine sampling areas. Threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) were introduced in 1953 as forage, and striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) were introduced in 1959, creating a heavily used striped bass fishery. Other important species include [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*). Minor species include black crappie, bluegill, green sunfish, smallmouth bass, carp, flathead catfish, and yellow bullhead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Striped bass were introduced into the Colorado River near Blythe, California, and spread rapidly. Stocking records were provided by CDFG, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) reportedly had not stocked fish in Lake Havasu in recent years. Survey methods likely underestimated largemouth bass effort and harvest, because these fish are often found in unsurveyed areas, and channel catfish and other species are often caught at night, which was not sampled.

Seasonal and monthly patterns were tied to weather, daylight length, and recreational events. Effort was lowest in winter and highest in the fall and spring. Cold winter water temperatures concentrated threadfin shad in southern coves, which influenced bait choice. A [[Striper Derby]] tournament in spring drew over 800 anglers in 1989 and contributed to high effort.

Management concerns included the effect of the 1983 flood on the fishery and the need for habitat improvement for largemouth bass. Brush piles had been placed in several coves, but their effectiveness had not been evaluated.

## Key Findings

- **Survey design:** Sixteen days per month were sampled, with eight randomly selected weekend and eight weekday dates. Two creel clerks conducted morning, midday, and evening counts plus angler interviews.
- **Effort:** Total effort was 154,896 hours in Year 1 and 171,971 hours in Year 2. The increase came mainly from spring of Year 2, when effort rose about 58%. Boat anglers accounted for 61% of effort in Year 1 and 66% in Year 2.
- **Species effort:** Striped bass anglers made up 60% of targeted effort in Year 1 and 65% in Year 2. Largemouth bass and channel catfish anglers made up a small share. About 18% of effort was not assigned to a target species.
- **Overall CPUE:** 0.193 fish/hour in Year 1 and 0.235 fish/hour in Year 2 (significant increase). Boat CPUE was consistently higher than shore CPUE.
- **Striped bass CPUE:** 0.233 fish/hour in Year 1 and 0.280 fish/hour in Year 2 (p = 0.01). Highest in summer and lowest in winter.
- **Other species CPUE:** Largemouth bass 0.424 fish/hour (Year 1) and 0.028 (Year 2); channel catfish 0.206 and 0.229 fish/hour. Small sample sizes made these estimates unreliable.
- **Harvest:** Total harvest was 38,046 fish in Year 1 and 66,000-range totals are not reported; the document gives 38,046 for Year 1 and 36,011 striped bass in Year 2. Estimated striped bass harvest was 25,135 (Year 1) and 36,011 (Year 2). Channel catfish harvest was 1,974 (Year 1) and 797 (Year 2). Largemouth bass accounted for about 15% of harvest in both years, though underestimated.
- **Bait type:** Cut fish was the most popular bait (69.7% of anglers in Year 1, 71.6% in Year 2). Shad had the highest striped bass CPUE in Year 2 (0.361 fish/hour), up from 0.097 in Year 1. Worms had the lowest CPUE in both years. Cut fish accounted for over 65% of striped bass harvest in both years.
- **Fish size:** Mean total length of harvested striped bass was 400.4 mm in Year 1 and 422.6 mm in Year 2 (significantly larger). Mean channel catfish length was also significantly greater in Year 2. No striped bass under 400 mm were recorded in either year. The document notes there is no size regulation for striped bass.
- **Angler demographics:** Anglers from Arizona and California were nearly equal in both years (about 45% each). Visitors from other states were more common in winter.
- **Angler satisfaction:** Most anglers rated their trip as poor. Satisfaction rose in summer, when CPUE was higher.
- **Recommendations:** (1) Develop a population model for the striped bass fishery to evaluate harvest rates and regulations; (2) study whether habitat limits largemouth bass populations and monitor existing brush piles; (3) repeat the same creel methods in future surveys to allow trend comparison, and study the effects of the 1983 flood.
