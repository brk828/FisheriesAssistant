# Early Life History of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave (1983-1984 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1984 package from [[Arizona State University]]'s Center for Environmental Studies to the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Endangered Species Office. It includes a cover letter, a corrected figure, and the *Early Life History: Interim Report on Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave* (May 1984). The study covers the 1983-84 field season and examines the reproductive biology, feeding ecology, and larval ecology of the [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The authors are [[Paul C. Marsh]] and Minckley. The letter states that all information is preliminary and tentative because the work is ongoing. The report also requests additional support for the 1984-85 season.

## Background & Description

- **Correspondence:** A May 18, 1984 letter from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) to Dr. James E. Johnson, Chief of the Endangered Species Office, enclosed the interim report as an annual report. The letter proposed discussing support at the 30 May 1984 meeting of the Lower Basin Endangered Fishes Committee in Boulder City. A follow-up letter dated May 21, 1984 enclosed Figure 1 ("Study Areas of Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada"), which had been accidentally omitted from the interim report.
- **Study objectives:** Field operations addressed three questions:
  1. Collection of adult specimens to examine reproductive biology and feeding ecology.
  2. Collection of potential vertebrate predators on razorback sucker embryos and larvae to examine food habits.
  3. Collection of larval razorback suckers to examine distribution, dispersal, and general ecology.
- **Field effort:** Six visits were made to Lake Mohave during the 1983-84 season. Collecting was concentrated at [[Cottonwood Cove]], [[Carp Cove]], [[Hammerhead Cove]], and [[Arizona Bay]], with additional effort along the main lake and shorelines.
- **Methods:** Adults were collected mainly by [[Trammel Netting]] and experimental gill nets. Larvae were collected by trap and dip net (day and night), timed series, and [[Plankton Tows]]. Predator stomachs were analyzed from carp, largemouth bass, rainbow trout, channel catfish, and other species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Adult collections:** Pre-spawning adults were collected in November 1983, ripe fish in January 1984, and post-spawning fish in March. Collections were timed to capture pre-, peak-, and post-spawning fish. Adults were preserved for gonad analysis and food-habit study at Arizona State University. Analyses were incomplete at the time of writing.
- **Predators:** Several hundred potential predators were captured from spawning areas and from habitats occupied by razorback sucker larvae. Dominant taxa included [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Green Sunfish]]. Preliminary gut analyses found insignificant numbers of larvae or embryos in the larger predators. Smaller fish from larval-rich areas (mainly threadfin shad and green sunfish) showed no evidence of consuming significant numbers of larvae.
- **Water level:** Lake Mohave was drawn down from a stable elevation of about 643-645 ft (February-March) to about 639 ft by mid-April 1984. This exposed shoreline habitat where larvae had been abundant.
- **Larval diet:** Larval food was dominated by [[Microcrustaceans]] (Cladocera and Copepoda) and early-instar [[Chironomidae]] larvae. Chironomids were absent from simultaneous plankton samples taken in areas of larval abundance.

## Key Findings

**Larval razorback sucker (based on examination of over 850 larvae):**

1. Larvae are concentrated in shallow water near adult spawning areas but also occur along shorelines and in mid-water where depth exceeds at least 10 m.
2. Larvae are positively phototactic at night and can be attracted to bright light.
3. Larvae remain active during daylight, distributed among shaded, submerged riparian vegetation and possibly other unknown areas.
4. Larvae do not enter the benthic substrate during daylight hours. They emerge at dusk or night.
5. Growth in shoreline and embayment areas is minimal. Mean total length did not increase significantly from February to April 1984, although maximum length did increase.
6. Larval hatch appears continuous, with new cohorts produced daily or at frequent intervals.
7. Survival from about 10 mm to 18 mm TL is extremely low (0.18 or less). The fate of larvae after reaching about 11 mm TL is unknown. They may disperse to unsampled areas or die from unknown causes.
8. The virtual disappearance of larvae by mid-April was preceded by about one week by the drawdown of Lake Mohave. The authors could not attribute the disappearance to the water-level change.

**Predation and diet:** Predation by the captured fish species did not appear to significantly affect larvae or embryos, though analyses were preliminary. Larval diet consisted mainly of microcrustaceans and chironomid larvae. Considerable additional data on larval food and plankton remained unanalyzed.

**Planned 1984-85 work:** Efforts will focus on determining the fate and survival of larvae through:
- Exclosure experiments in isolated backwaters and/or protected embayments
- Extensive embayment and open-lake sampling with larval trawls
- Deep-water netting with small-mesh (1 cm bar) nets
- Deep-water light trapping
- SCUBA observation of unsampled habitats
- Laboratory studies of larval ecology
- Continued collection of adult razorback suckers and other fish species

**Funding request:** Monitoring larvae requires nearly full-time field deployment from about November through April, the period of hatching through dispersal or disappearance. The request includes:
1. Housing at Cottonwood Cove, Nevada, for two persons (a federal trailer from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] was proposed)
2. A 23-foot boat slip at Cottonwood Cove (arranged at a nominal fee through the Cottonwood Cove Marina)
3. Salary for at least one full-time field biologist, either as a direct agency hire or administered through Arizona State University

The authors estimated the additional cost at $5,000.00 and planned to submit a formal request to the Bureau of Reclamation.
