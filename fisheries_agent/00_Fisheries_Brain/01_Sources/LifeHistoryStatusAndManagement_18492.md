# Modoc Sucker (*Catostomus microps*): Life History, Status, and Management in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game special publication (Inland Fisheries Endangered Species Program, Special Publication 80-6, November 1980, by Terry J. Mills) reviews the status of the [[Modoc Sucker]], a small catostomid endemic to northeastern California. The species was listed as rare under the [[California Endangered Species Act]] of 1970. The report estimates that only about 1,300 fish remain, in [[Hulbert Creek]], [[Johnson Creek]], and [[Washington Creek]] in Modoc County. The author concludes that the species should be reclassified as endangered. Its main threats are habitat loss from livestock grazing, water diversion, and channelization, together with [[Hybridization]] with the [[Sacramento Sucker]]. The report recommends a status change, a management plan, barrier completion, transplantation to new sites, and acquisition of private habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery and taxonomy:** The Modoc sucker was recognized as a distinct species in 1907 (Rutter 1908). It is one of four catostomid species found only within California. Its nomenclature has never been disputed. *Catostomus* refers to an inferior mouth, and *microps* means small eye.
- **Type material:** Rutter's description was based on three paratypes collected from [[Rush Creek]], Modoc County, on September 1, 1898.
- **Diagnostic characters:** The species is distinguished from other catostomids by dorsal ray count (usually 10, occasionally 11, rarely 12), lateral line scale count (79 to 89), and small body size (under 160 mm SL, or under 6.30 in.).
- **Similar species:** It closely resembles the Tahoe sucker (*C. tahoensis*). Compared with the Sacramento sucker, it is smaller, less robust, and has fewer dorsal rays and finer scales.
- **Coloration:** Martin (1972) described the back as greenish-brown to deep grey and olive, the sides as lighter, and the belly as cream to white. Three dark spots lie along the sides near the lateral line.
- **Morphology:** Martin (1972) found the jaws strongly adapted for scraping substrate, more so than in the Sacramento sucker, with heavier oromandibular musculature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic history.** The upper [[Pit River]] was once connected to the Klamath drainage in the late Pliocene. An early Pleistocene lava flow blocked that course and formed Lake Alturas, which later drained into the Sacramento Basin. The Modoc sucker was already present when the Sacramento sucker reached the upper Pit River. Its phylogeny has not been adequately studied, and it shows no close affinity to Klamath or Sacramento fishes.

**Distribution.** Outside the type locality, distribution was poorly known until the 1970s. Surveys by Ford (1977) found the species in eight streams (Table 2). Fifteen additional Pit River tributaries were surveyed with no Modoc suckers found (Appendix 1). A reported population in Dorris Reservoir was never confirmed.

**Habitat.** The species lives on the Modoc Plateau, a volcanic highland bounded by the Cascade Range to the west and the Warner Range to the east. Populations generally occur between 1,286 and 1,567 m (4,210 to 5,141 ft). Streams have moderate gradients and moderate to low flows through meadows. Riparian vegetation includes junipers, aspens, conifers, cottonwoods, willows, and chokecherries. [[Rush Creek]], [[Turner Creek]], and [[Ash Creek]] flow year-round. The others flow intermittently in summer and fall, with some sections drying to isolated pools.

**Habitat modifications.**
- *Livestock:* Overgrazing and streambank trampling are the primary cause of habitat damage. Pease (1965) reported that many upland meadows had been degraded by 1940. Lowered water tables and stream incision then turned former wet meadows into dry, sagebrush-covered grasslands. Upland meadows made up only 2% of Modoc County's land area.
- *Irrigation diversion:* Ford (1977) recorded 26 diversions, each ranging from 5 to 109 L/s (0.18 to 3.9 cfs), on Ash, Rush, Johnson, Turner, and Hulbert creeks. The diversion on [[Johnson Creek]] dewaters about 20% of that creek's available habitat.
- *Channelization:* Rush and Johnson creeks have been channelized (Table 4). Moyle (1976b) found total fish biomass in channelized Rush Creek sections to be under one-third that of unchannelized sections.

**Fish associations.** Common companions include speckled dace, rainbow trout, California roach, Pit sculpin, Sacramento sucker, and [[Brown Trout]], a predatory exotic. Occasional associates include the Pit-Klamath brook lamprey, Sacramento squawfish, hardhead, brown bullhead, and green sunfish.

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- Estimated population across eight streams in 1977 was 2,605 fish (Ford 1977). Only Hulbert, Johnson, and Washington creeks held pure Modoc suckers, with an estimated 1,250 fish in these creeks.
- The author considers the earlier estimate of fewer than 5,000 fish (Moyle 1974) generous, probably because it included hybrids. Abstract and Status sections report the current estimate as fewer than 1,300 fish.
- About 95% of pure Modoc suckers occur in streams within [[Modoc National Forest]].

**Life history**
- **Habitat preference:** The species is most abundant in low-summer-flow and intermittent streams, large shallow pools with mud substrate, partially shaded reaches, and clear water. It is absent from swift, high-gradient reaches and from channelized sections.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents of 14 fish were 47% detritus, 19% diatoms, 18% chironomid larvae, 10% filamentous algae, 4% crustaceans, and 2% insect larvae (Moyle and Marciochi 1975).
- **Age and growth:** The oldest fish examined was 5 years old and 280 mm SL. Adults rarely exceed 160 mm SL. The age-length relationship is length (cm) = 3.99 × age + 2.90. The author attributes the small adult size to preference for small, cool streams.
- **Spawning:** Spawning runs from mid-April to early June, with spawning lasting 3 to 4 weeks in each stream. Spawning occurs in the lower ends of pools over fine to medium gravel, at 13.3 to 16.1°C (55.9 to 61.0°F), in water about 15 cm (6 in.) deep. A female and usually three males spawn in a 6- to 8-second interval.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Nuptial males (mean 95 mm SL) are smaller than nuptial females (mean 111 mm SL). Males show a bright red-orange lateral band and body tubercles, which females rarely develop.

**Hybridization**
- Hybridization with Sacramento suckers is extensive and is linked to habitat degradation. Meadow incision and barrier erosion likely allowed Sacramento suckers to invade. Ditches from a reclaimed marsh may have provided passage into Rush Creek.
- Of 65 preserved specimens examined (26 Modoc, 25 Sacramento, 14 hybrids), Modoc and Sacramento suckers showed no overlap in six meristic and morphometric characters. Scales in the lateral line and dorsal ray count best separate them (Table 6).
- Hybrid ranges are 68 to 90 lateral line scales (mean 76.5) and 10 to 12 dorsal rays (mean 10.9).

**Essential habitat**
- The essential habitat comprises the eight creeks and their tributaries. Required features include pools, spawning and nursery areas, feeding areas, cover, adequate flow, and suitable temperature.
- Of the 20.5 km (12.6 miles) of stream inhabited by pure and hybrid suckers, 72% is privately owned and 28% is Forest Service land.

**Management status and plans**
- Washington Creek is the only population protected by an effective barrier against Sacramento sucker invasion. Potential barrier sites have been identified on Hulbert and Johnson creeks.
- Ford (1977) identified five potential transplant streams, including Butte Creek, Halls Canyon Creek, North Adin Pass Creek, Coffee Mill Gulch, and Howards Gulch Creek (Appendix 11). These require further evaluation.
- An interim recovery plan was in preparation. Its three major objectives are habitat protection and barrier construction, chemical rejuvenation and restocking of hybrid streams, and transplantation to expand range and provide a source of fish.

**Recommendations**
1. Reclassify the Modoc sucker from "rare" to "endangered" under the California Endangered Species Act of 1970.
2. Develop a management plan through the Forest Service interdisciplinary team approach, incorporated into the forest-wide land management plan.
3. Complete barrier evaluations for the eight essential habitats and improve incomplete barriers.
4. Evaluate all potential transplant sites and introduce Modoc suckers into several suitable locations.
5. Continue acquiring privately held essential habitat, especially upper Johnson Creek.
