# Key to Families of Bony Fishes of Inland North America and Species from Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Actinopterygii

## Executive Summary

This January 1969 document by W. L. Minckley, Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe, is a dichotomous identification key to the bony fish families of inland North America, with a species-level section for [[Arizona]]. It works through about 40 numbered couplets to reach a family, then gives species keys for the families that occur in the state. The text lists native and introduced species and notes which introduced populations are established, reproducing, or maintained only by stocking.

## Background & Description

The key begins with morphological contrasts used to separate families, including caudal fin structure (heterocercal, homocercal, abbreviate-heterocercal), mouth position, skeletal composition, scale type (ganoid, cycloid, ctenoid), fin position and ray counts, barbel number, and the presence or absence of an [[Adipose Fin]], pharyngeal teeth, and a [[Gular Plate]]. Families are keyed in sequence, with cross-references to later pages for detailed species keys. Several couplets note exceptions, such as the Amblyopsidae, whose anus position departs from the usual pattern.

Families covered in the species section include:

- **Acipenseridae** (sturgeons), with [[White Sturgeon]] listed.
- **Elopidae** and **Clupeidae**, with [[Machete]] (Pacific tenpounder) and [[Threadfin Shad]].
- **Salmonidae**, the largest keyed group for trout and char, with [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]].
- **Esocidae** ([[Northern Pike]]) and **Characidae** ([[Banded Tetra]]).
- **Cyprinidae** (minnows), about 25 species in Arizona.
- **Catostomidae** (suckers), with four introduced species and native forms marked in the key.
- **Ictaluridae** (North American catfishes), **Clariidae**, **Cyprinodontidae** (pupfishes and killifishes), and **Poeciliidae** (livebearers).
- **Mugilidae**, **Serranidae**, **Centrarchidae** (sunfishes), **Percidae**, **Cichlidae**, **Eleotridae**, **Gobiidae**, and **Cottidae**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is mainly taxonomic, but it records several conservation and ecological points:

- **Native versus introduced status.** Native Arizona species are marked with an asterisk in the key. Most salmonids in the state are introduced; the text says one, and possibly two, trout species are native, and that seven species are introduced, two of them maintained only by continued stocking.
- **Colorado River system.** Several records concern the lower [[Colorado River]]: the native [[Machete]] below Imperial Dam, the introduced [[White Sturgeon]] (1967), native [[Striped Mullet]], and introduced bait species. Several native cyprinids and catostomids are also tied to Colorado and Gila basin rivers.
- **Introduced bait and aquarium fishes.** Several exotics were introduced as bait or released from the aquarium trade, including [[Banded Tetra]], [[Convict Cichlid]], and [[Longjawed Mudsucker]]. Some are reported to be reproducing.
- **Reservoirs and lakes.** Introduced [[Northern Pike]] reproduce in intermediate-elevation lakes, and [[Largemouth Bass]] and other sunfishes are established.
- **Salinity and temperature limits.** The text notes that the longjawed mudsucker "most likely will not establish except in highly saline waters," and that the banded sculpin "may become established in colder waters below dams."
- **Identification difficulty.** The author warns that trout are hard to identify and that hybrids between some species are common. Life coloration must often be recorded. Identification of some pupfish and livebearer groups also requires good lighting and magnification.

## Key Findings

1. **Family-level key.** The document provides a stepwise key of roughly 40 couplets, with diagnostic traits for each family, cross-referenced to species keys on later pages.

2. **Native Arizona cyprinids and catostomids.** Native minnows and suckers are numerous and frequently keyed in detail, including:
   - [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]], and [[Middle Colorado Spinedace]].
   - [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Sonora Chub]], and [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila* species).
   - [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Yaqui Sucker]].

3. **Native trout are scarce.** The key includes [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo* species). The text separates them from introduced salmonids such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Coho Salmon]], [[Sockeye Salmon]], and [[Arctic Grayling]].

4. **No native catfish, with one possible exception.** The author states that no native catfishes are known from Arizona, except possibly [[Yaqui Catfish]], which "should have occurred" in San Bernardino Creek but was never collected there. Introduced catfishes include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Brown Bullhead]], and [[Black Bullhead]].

5. **Pupfishes and livebearers.** Three native kinds of *Cyprinodon* occur: [[Desert Pupfish]] (two subspecies) and the [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]]. The only native poeciliid is [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*). Introduced livebearers include [[Mosquitofish]], [[Guppy]], [[Green Swordtail]], and [[Sailfin Molly]].

6. **Established non-native sportfishes.** Introduced centrarchids, percids, and serranids are widespread. These include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[White Bass]].

7. **Notable single or occasional records.** The document includes several species recorded from few specimens or in limited circumstances: [[Walking Catfish]] (one specimen from a canal near Winterhaven, California), [[Spotted Sleeper]] (one specimen west of Yuma, California), and [[Convict Cichlid]] (from the Salt River remnants in Phoenix and Lake Mead's Overton Arm).

8. **Introduction status of major exotics.**
   - [[White Sturgeon]] was introduced into the Colorado River in 1967, and no reproduction had yet been detected.
   - [[Threadfin Shad]] are introduced and widespread below 4,000 feet.
   - [[Grass Carp]] is present in experimental ponds but is not keyed.

9. **Other noted families.** Cichlids are represented by the Central American [[Convict Cichlid]] and African Tilapias, with [[Tilapia mossambica]] described as the commonest African species. The author notes that Tilapia taxonomy is poorly understood, so no meaningful key is provided for them.
