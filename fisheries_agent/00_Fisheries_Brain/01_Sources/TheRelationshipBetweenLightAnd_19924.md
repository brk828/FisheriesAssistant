# Light and Photosynthetic Rate in a River Community: Implications for Water Quality Modeling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton-periphyton

## Executive Summary

This 1976 *Water Resources Research* paper by [[George M. Hornberger]], [[Mahlon G. Kelly]], and [[Richard M. Eller]] (University of Virginia) tests whether photosynthesis saturates or is inhibited at high light in natural flowing-water communities. Using free-water dissolved-oxygen measurements in the [[Mechum River]], the authors found a linear light-photosynthesis relationship on clear days and on days where highly variable radiation points were discarded. They propose that the saturation and photoinhibition reported in earlier work may be artifacts of [[Nutrient Depletion]] in enclosed containers, [[Light-Dark Bottle]] incubations and [[Microcosm]] experiments. If so, the authors argue, nonlinear light terms in [[Water Quality Modeling]] are inappropriate.

The paper contains no fish data. Its subject is the primary productivity of periphyton and phytoplankton communities.

## Background & Description

- **Focus:** the relationship between incident light and net/gross photosynthesis (primary productivity) in aquatic communities.
- **Standard view:** many [[In Situ Measurements]] and laboratory studies report that photosynthesis saturates or declines at high light, a pattern often called [[Photoinhibition]]. Models such as those of DiToro et al. (1971), Chen and Orlob (1972), Grenney et al. (1973), and O'Connor et al. (1973) include nonlinear light terms.
- **Free-water method:** productivity is estimated from diel changes in dissolved oxygen in the river, with the reaeration coefficient selected to yield a nonhysteretic light-productivity relationship. This is contrasted with [[Light-Dark Bottle]] methods, in which containment can introduce errors.
- **Study site:** the Mechum River, which drains a forested and farmed basin on the eastern edge of the Blue Ridge Mountains. Productivity was measured over a 1.5 km segment (exact value as OCR-scanned: "I} km"), between upstream station A and downstream station B.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Measurement period:** 15 weeks, March to September 1973 (108 complete days at station B and 66 at station A in the raw record).
- **Usable data:** 9 days at station A and 25 days at station B. Excluded data arose from oxygen-probe drift, equipment malfunction, maintenance, and storms and high flow. Storms scoured the river bed and washed out periphyton.
- **Efficiency range:** photosynthetic efficiencies in Table 1 ranged from 0.04% (September 19, station B) to 0.60% (July 30, station B). Values were consistent over runs of days with stable conditions.
- **Consistency example:** net productivity for August 9–13, 1973, was broadly repeatable day to day under similar conditions. Efficiencies on those days ranged from 0.22% (August 12) to 0.37% (August 10).
- **Calculation assumptions:** an assimilation coefficient of 1 (mol C per mol O₂), an energy fixation rate of 1.2 × 10⁶ gcal/mol C (Odum, 1971), and a mean river depth of 1 m.
- **Comparative rivers:** similar results were reported from five other rivers, and the linear relationship also held for carbon-uptake rates.
- **Scope:** no fish, invertebrate or species-level biological data are presented. The study concerns community metabolism.

## Key Findings

1. **Linear light response in river communities.** Net productivity increased linearly with incident radiation in the Mechum River, both on cloudless days and on days where points from highly variable radiation were discounted. The linear relationship held through the productive part of the year.
2. **Day classification.** Days were visually sorted into five classes: (1) unmistakably linear; (2) respiration changing in time; (3) enhanced efficiency at high light; (4) apparent photoinhibition; (5) rapidly fluctuating radiation. Most days fell into classes 1 or 5.
3. **Few anomalous days.** Only a small number of days showed photoinhibition (e.g., May 15, station B), upward bending (May 16, station A), or enhanced efficiency at midday. The authors could not explain these, but considered sensor or reaeration errors plausible.
4. **Respiration and nutrient explanations.** Two alternative explanations were offered for apparent saturation or afternoon decline: (a) respiration changes with dissolved oxygen concentration; (b) nutrient depletion in enclosed samples. Flowing water prevents local nutrient depletion (Whitford and Schumacher, 1961), so depletion should not cause photoinhibition in rivers.
5. **Nutrient-limitation simulation.** A Michaelis-Menten-based model of a closed light-dark bottle reproduced apparent light saturation (4-hour averages) and a surface afternoon decline (12-hour runs). The authors attribute both to nutrient limitation, not to a true light response. Apparent saturation and photoinhibition arose even with vertically homogeneous nutrients. A realistic nutrient increase with depth would instead predict photoinhibition at the surface.
6. **Modeling implication.** If reported saturation and inhibition arise largely from nutrient artifacts, then including nonlinear light or photoinhibition terms in [[Water Quality Modeling]] may give erroneous predictions. The authors state that such terms may be unnecessary.
7. **Proposed follow-up.** Concurrent nutrient and productivity measurements in containers were planned to test the hypothesis, and time-series analysis was being applied to the lack of photosynthetic response to short-term radiation changes.
