# Long-Term Effective Population Size of Three Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Garrigan, Marsh and Dowling (Arizona State University) applies a [[Coalescent Theory|coalescent]] maximum likelihood estimator of [[Long-Term Effective Population Size]] (N_e) to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from three federally endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Unlike [[Temporal Allele Frequency Methods]], which capture only recent drift, the coalescent approach reflects demographic history across much of a species' evolutionary past. The authors find three distinct outcomes: humpback chub retains historical equilibrium variation, razorback sucker shows a historically large, expanding population, and bonytail chub shows signs of a [[Population Bottleneck]] that may have erased much historical diversity. The authors argue these results match known natural history and are useful for [[Endangered Species Management]].

## Background & Description

**Effective population size.** N_e is the size of an idealized population experiencing the same [[Genetic Drift]] as the observed population. It is usually smaller than the census size because of variance in family size and temporal fluctuation in abundance. Low N_e increases the risk of inbreeding depression and reduces the capacity to adapt to environmental change, such as habitat modification, introduced competitors, predators and parasites.

**Method.** The study uses the [[FLUCTUATE]] program, which implements the [[Kuhner Method]]. It jointly estimates long-term female effective size (N_f) and the exponential growth rate (g) by reconstructing coalescent genealogies with [[Metropolis-Hastings Sampling]]. Test statistics include [[Tajima's D]], computed with DnaSP and tested by coalescent simulation with 1,000 replicates. Mutation counts, singletons and average pairwise differences were also calculated.

**Study species.** All three species are endemic to the Colorado River basin and federally listed as endangered:

- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): historically restricted to canyon reaches in and upstream of the [[Grand Canyon]]. It is the only one of the three with apparently self-sustaining populations. The largest population, in the [[Little Colorado River]], may contain as many as 10,000 adults.
- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): extirpated from most of its range and extremely rare in the wild. Captures in [[Lake Mohave]] are too few for census estimates.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): historically the most abundant of the three and basin-wide in distribution. The largest remaining population, in Lake Mohave, declined from perhaps hundreds of thousands to fewer than 10,000 fish in about 50 years.

Life span is about 20 to 50 years. Bonytail and razorback suckers have suffered near-total [[Recruitment Failure]], mainly from predation on early life stages, despite [[Captive Propagation]] programmes that began in the early 1980s and have distributed hundreds of thousands to millions of young. Authors suggest the longevity and recency of declines may have delayed loss of historical genetic variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The three species were historically widespread in the large streams of the Colorado River system. Declines followed [[Dam Construction]] and reservoir construction, water diversion and desiccation, and the introduction of [[Non-Native Fishes]] that prey on or compete with native species. Humpback chub declines are mainly due to localized reductions and extirpation of historically connected subpopulations, which reduces gene flow and immigrant haplotypes. Razorback sucker once occurred from Wyoming to the Colorado River delta in Mexico, and throughout the [[Gila River]] system; Native Americans and early settlers used it as food and fertilizer. Bonytail chub was functionally extinct in the upper Colorado River system by the 1980s.

Management history complicates interpretation. Bonytail were propagated from a few wild adults, and the hatchery-produced F1 fish were stocked into Lake Mohave from the early 1980s. The estimated effective founding size of this hatchery stock is 3.5 to 8.5 individuals ([[Hedrick et al. 2000]]), so the bonytail pattern could reflect a [[Founder Effect]], recent species-wide decline, or long-term population contraction.

## Key Findings

### Sampling and genetic data

| Species | Gene | Sample size (n) | Sites | Haplotypes/alleles | Mutations | Singletons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Humpback chub | ND2 | 18 | 790 | 5 | 6 | 1 |
| Bonytail chub | ND2 | 16 | 763 | 3 | 6 | 0 |
| Razorback sucker | Cytochrome b | 49 | 311 | 10 | 13 | 4 |

Tissue samples came from the Little Colorado–Colorado confluence in Grand Canyon (humpback chub), and from Lake Mohave (bonytail chub and razorback sucker). Variation was assessed by single-stranded conformation polymorphism (SSCP) and direct sequencing.

### Humpback chub: equilibrium variation retained

- Composite ND2 haplotypes were five, all transitions, none causing amino acid change.
- The most frequent haplotypes, AB and GB, occurred in 44.4% and 27.8% of samples.
- Average pairwise divergence was 1.54 nucleotide substitutions.
- Tajima's D was -0.3935 and non-significant, consistent with a constant-size population at mutation-drift equilibrium.
- Estimated N_f under size constancy was approximately 97,500. Under growth, N_f was approximately 148,966, close to the authors' rounded 150,000. The growth rate did not differ significantly from zero, so the two models did not differ significantly.
- Using a mutation rate of 2 × 10⁻⁸ per nucleotide (mammalian mtDNA), N_f was about 150,000. Authors note that teleost mtDNA may evolve more slowly, which would raise the estimate.
- The authors assumed equal sex ratios, so N_e = 2N_f.

### Bonytail chub: signs of recent bottleneck

- Only three ND2 haplotypes occurred in 16 individuals, and these were almost equally frequent.
- Average pairwise divergence was roughly double that of humpback chub. Six transitional mutations were found, none singletons, with one amino acid change.
- Tajima's D was significantly positive at 1.909 (p = 0.029), suggesting a population bottleneck or balancing selection.
- A bottleneck removes rare alleles, leaving relatively even frequencies among surviving divergent lineages.
- Coalescent estimates were N_f ≈ 62,000 with size change and N_f ≈ 89,500 under constancy. The growth rate was significantly negative, and the two estimates differed significantly.

### Razorback sucker: historically large, expanding population

- Ten cytochrome b haplotypes occurred in 49 individuals, with 13 mutations, four singletons and two amino acid substitutions.
- The most common haplotype, E, occurred in 59.2% of samples. Average pairwise divergence was 1.50 substitutions.
- Tajima's D was -1.46 (p = 0.058), marginally non-significant, but indicative of a trend toward excess rare mutations.
- The coalescent estimate was N_f ≈ 940,300 with size change, significantly higher than the constant-size estimate of approximately 669,000. The significantly positive growth rate and long external genealogy branches indicate an expanding population.
- Authors interpret this as a historically expanding population, consistent with a northward post-Pleistocene expansion ([[Dowling et al. 1996]]). The retention of rare alleles in the declining Lake Mohave population suggests the N_f estimate may be conservative if some variation has already been lost.

### Interpretation and methodological notes

- Humpback chub results are consistent with past connectivity across the Colorado River. Isolated Grand Canyon subpopulations may not yet have lost variation to drift.
- Coalescent estimators make more efficient use of sequence data than summary-statistic methods and have lower variance, which matters for small endangered-species samples.
- The probability that adding one sample would change the final coalescence time was 0.006 (humpback chub), 0.007 (bonytail chub) and 0.001 (razorback sucker).
- Coalescent estimates reflect long-term N_e, while temporal allele-frequency methods estimate current N_e. The authors argue the two approaches should be combined.
- The authors conclude that the three species illustrate the three possible outcomes of long-term N_e analysis: retention of equilibrium variation, expansion-related rare alleles, and bottleneck-driven loss. The method is useful for setting target N_e values in remnant populations.
