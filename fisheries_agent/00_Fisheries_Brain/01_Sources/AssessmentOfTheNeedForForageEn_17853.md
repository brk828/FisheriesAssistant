# Assessment of the Need for Forage Enhancement for the Benefit of Lake Powell Game Fish and Proposal for Rainbow Smelt Introduction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae #reservoir-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document comprises two related reports from the Lake Powell Fisheries Project (Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, 1987): (1) an assessment of forage fish needs in [[Lake Powell]] and (2) a formal proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) as a deepwater forage species. [[Lake Powell]], impounded in 1963, has experienced dramatic shifts in fish community structure over 24 years. The reservoir was originally envisioned as a "two-story fishery" supporting both salmonids and centrarchids. [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), introduced 1968-1969, became the sole pelagic forage base. [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), stocked beginning 1974, unexpectedly reproduced naturally and by the 1980s had severely depleted shad populations. By 1982-1986, striped bass and [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) exhibited poor body condition (K factor ~0.90-1.0) and stunted growth due to inadequate forage. The authors propose introducing rainbow smelt to occupy the cool hypolimnion (thermally partitioned from warm-water shad), provide year-round deepwater forage for walleye and striped bass, buffer predation on shad, and restore a balanced multi-species fishery. The proposal reviews smelt biology, potential benefits and risks, and precedents from other reservoir systems.

## Background & Description

**Lake Powell History and Stocking:**  
[[Lake Powell]] began filling in 1963 on the [[Colorado River]] (Utah-Arizona border). Initial stockings (Table 1) included [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), and [[Kokanee Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi*). Kokanee failed to establish; rainbow trout fishery was localized near the dam. Bass and crappie thrived as the reservoir filled and inundated brushy floodplain habitat. [[Threadfin Shad]] introduction (1968-1969) initially boosted all sport fisheries, creating a nationally recognized bass and crappie fishery in the early 1970s. [[Walleye]], present from pre-impoundment Colorado River stock, increased slowly and peaked in the early 1980s. [[Striped Bass]] fingerlings were stocked 1974-1978; natural reproduction was documented by 1979 in both the Colorado River inflow and within the reservoir proper. The striped bass sport fishery emerged in 1979 (coinciding with sexual maturity) and peaked 1982-1983 when the first naturally reproduced generation matured.

**Rainbow Smelt Biology:**  
*Osmerus mordax* is a slender, silver, anadromous fish (family [[Osmeridae]]) native to the North Atlantic coast (Labrador to New Jersey) with indigenous landlocked populations in New England. Landlocked smelt average 178-203 mm; maximum size ~356 mm in [[Lake Ontario]]. Growth: 40-50 mm first year, 117-183 mm by age 2-4. Smelt are schooling, pelagic, light- and temperature-sensitive fish inhabiting midwaters of lakes. They avoid temperatures >15°C, preferring 6-16°C, and concentrate at or below the thermocline in stratified reservoirs (often 90-125 ft depth). In [[Lake Oahe]] (South Dakota), smelt occupied the hypolimnion at 5-14°C, near bottom by day, rising to thermocline at night. Smelt cannot survive >21-26°C. They spawn in spring (March-May) in streams or on lake gravel shoals at 2-18.3°C (peak ~6°C), typically ascending streams <0.25 mile and halted by falls >1 foot. Females broadcast 10,000-43,000 adhesive eggs; incubation 8-60 days depending on temperature. Maturity at age 2-3 (minimum 127 mm). Spawning runs dominated by age-2 fish.

**Diet and Trophic Role:**  
Smelt are opportunistic planktivores feeding primarily on zooplankton (copepods, cladocerans, *Mysis*, *Daphnia*) and benthic invertebrates (amphipods, midge larvae). Larger smelt (>157-180 mm) occasionally consume small fish (including yoy smelt, alewives) but are not primarily piscivorous. Studies across the Great Lakes, New England, and Missouri River reservoirs confirm zooplankton dominates diet; fish predation is incidental and not a population-level threat to sympatric species. Smelt are "selective planktivores" preferring larger zooplankters when available, potentially restructuring plankton communities. Smelt are valuable forage for [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), other salmonids, walleye, [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), and bass species. In [[Lake Texoma]] (Texas-Oklahoma), smelt coexist with striped bass in a diverse forage complex including [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), [[Inland Silversides]] (*Menidia beryllina*), and shiners, supporting a balanced, trophy-sized striped bass fishery for >20 years.

**Distribution and Introductions:**  
Smelt colonized the entire [[Great Lakes]] within 25 years of introduction to [[Lake Michigan]] (1906-1912). They spread via the Chicago Canal into the upper [[Mississippi River]] by 1978 but did not establish in warm lower reaches. Smelt were introduced to [[Lake Sakakawea]] (North Dakota, 1971) and colonized downstream [[Lake Oahe]] (South Dakota), benefiting walleye, pike, and tailwater trout fisheries. Colorado stocked smelt in multiple small reservoirs (1970s-1980s) as trout forage. Nebraska introduced smelt to [[Lake McConaughy]] (1983-1985) for trout and striped bass forage, but establishment uncertain due to severe thermal/oxygen stratification. South Dakota stocked [[Pactola Reservoir]] (1982) for brown trout forage; reproduction documented but population still building.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Powell Fisheries Status (1987):**  
Angling pressure increased steadily since 1965 (Figure 2), yet catch rates remained near historic average of 0.4 fish/hour through 1985, indicating more fish harvested overall. [[Threadfin Shad]] populations exhibited a 3-year cycle (peaks 1978, 1981, 1984) but each peak progressively smaller due to predation (Figure 3). The expected 1987 peak failed to materialize; trawl samples collected no shad in most areas. Shad are confined to warm epilimnion (upper 10 m) in stratified reservoir, unavailable to cool-water predators (walleye, striped bass) below thermocline. Other forage (bluegill, green sunfish, red shiner, carp, crayfish, zooplankton, insects) support shorebound game fish but cannot sustain pelagic predators.

**Largemouth Bass:**  
Peak abundance 1972; population declined as flooded brushy habitat decomposed and reservoir filled to full pool (1980), eliminating annual inundation of new brush (Figure 4). Largemouth recruitment limited by lack of nursery cover and predation on yoy. Floods (1983-1984) flooded new brush, producing strong year classes and improved 1987 fishery. Population stabilized at lower level than peak years but remains better than most oligotrophic canyon lakes.

**Smallmouth Bass:**  
Introduced 1982 to supplement declining largemouth. Populations established at 12 locations with documented natural reproduction. Smallmouth utilize rocky substrate (abundant in Lake Powell) rather than brush, showing comparable growth to largemouth. Population building; beginning to contribute to sport fishery.

**Black Crappie:**  
Declined similarly to largemouth due to brush loss. Crappie more dependent on brush for nesting and yoy cover. Annual water fluctuation prevents terrestrial brush growth sufficient for next year's fry. Crappie confined to localized areas with suitable structure; will not regain early-1970s abundance.

**Walleye:**  
Population boomed late 1970s, peaked 1981 (one year after reservoir filled), then declined 1982-1983 due to shad shortage (Figure 4). Walleye difficult to catch in low-abundance years. Limited only by forage; sufficient spawning habitat available. Remain significant predator despite poor angler success.

**Striped Bass:**  
Rapid growth and abundant shad produced significant sport fishery 1979-1983. Average catch size 620 mm (24.4 in) 1979-1984. Physical condition (K factor) declined to ~1.0 in 1982 due to low shad (Figure 5). Shad peak in 1984 temporarily restored condition, but 1985-1986 shad failure caused condition to fall to new lows. Winter 1986-1987 die-off of adult striped bass observed lakewide. Average catch size declined to 555 mm (1985), 497 mm (spring 1986), 423 mm (fall 1986). Subadult striped bass maintained condition by foraging on zooplankton. Unlimited natural reproduction produced overcropping of shad; population dominated by small, stunted juveniles in marginal condition. Trophy fishery declining annually.

**Threatened and Endangered Species:**  
Endemic Colorado River "big river" fishes ([[Squawfish]] *Ptychocheilus lucius*, chubs *Gila* spp., [[Razorback Sucker]] *Xyrauchen texanus*) do not complete life cycles in reservoir. A few adults sampled; likely migrate into reservoir to forage before returning to river. No reproduction documented in reservoir.

**Thermal Stratification:**  
Lake Powell stratifies in summer. Hypolimnetic waters range 7.0-12°C, well within smelt's preferred range. Shad occupy warm epilimnion; walleye and striped bass restricted to cool hypolimnion. Thermal partitioning would separate smelt (hypolimnion) from shad and yoy game fish (epilimnion) during stratification, minimizing competition and predation on yoy. Winter mixing would allow some interaction, but most yoy would have grown beyond vulnerable size.

## Key Findings

**Assessment of Forage Needs:**

1. **Forage Limitation Confirmed:** Threadfin shad population severely depleted by predation, failing to produce expected 1987 cyclical peak. Pelagic shad population virtually absent. Striped bass and walleye exhibit poor body condition (K ~0.90-1.0) and stunted growth directly attributable to inadequate forage.

2. **Predator-Prey Imbalance:** Unlimited natural reproduction of striped bass (unexpected at introduction) created overcropping of shad. Striped bass population dominated by small juveniles (<20 inches) in marginal condition, foraging directly on zooplankton (indicating missing trophic link). Trophy fishery (>20 inches) declining; angler dissatisfaction increasing since 1985.

3. **Underutilized Primary Productivity:** Depressed shad population not converting available plankton to fish flesh. Sufficient nutrients exist to support larger forage base (as evidenced by 1984 shad peak, Figure 3). Juvenile striped bass consuming large zooplankton directly, bypassing forage fish trophic level.

4. **Management Options Evaluated:**
   - **No Action:** Would perpetuate current conditions—stunted striped bass, poor walleye fishery, stable but limited bass/crappie populations. Striped bass fishery would parallel declining [[Lake Mead]] situation.
   - **Regulation Changes:** Removing or increasing striped bass limits unlikely to significantly increase harvest (anglers already fail to catch limits). Reopening dam sanctuary area (April-May) could target pre-spawn adults for harvest.
   - **Commercial Fishery:** Philosophically inconsistent with state wildlife policy; technically difficult (selective netting impossible, conflict with recreation); economically questionable.
   - **Deepwater Forage Introduction:** Most promising option to restore balance.

**Rainbow Smelt Introduction Proposal:**

5. **Expected Benefits:**
   - Provide year-round deepwater forage for walleye and striped bass in hypolimnion (thermally partitioned from shad in epilimnion).
   - Divert predatory pressure from shad, allowing shad population to rebuild for littoral predators (bass, crappie).
   - Increase forage diversity and stability (when one species at population low, other likely increases).
   - Smelt grow larger than shad (up to 356 mm vs. shad ~150 mm), allowing striped bass to attain larger trophy size.
   - Potential spring dipnet/hook-and-line sport fishery during spawning runs (economic benefit during slow season).
   - More effective utilization of primary productivity via two thermally partitioned forage species.

6. **Biological Suitability:**
   - Lake Powell hypolimnion (7-12°C summer) matches smelt thermal preference (6-16°C).
   - Smelt occupy pelagic zone at/below thermocline, avoiding epilimnetic warm water (>15°C).
   - Spawning requirements met: smelt ascend streams <0.25 mile, halted by falls >1 foot (characteristic of Lake Powell tributaries); can also spawn on lake gravel shoals.
   - Diet (zooplankton, benthic invertebrates) minimally overlaps with shad (also zooplankton but in warm epilimnion during stratification).
   - Smelt not primarily piscivorous; predation on yoy fish minimal and thermally partitioned during critical summer months.

7. **Precedents for Success:**
   - **Lake Texoma** (Texas-Oklahoma): 35,600 ha reservoir with naturally reproducing striped bass since 1973. Diverse forage base (threadfin shad, gizzard shad, silversides, drum, shiners) supports balanced striped bass fishery with 30-50% of catch >500 mm (trophy size) despite overabundant 1982, 1985 year classes and 1981-1982 shad winter kill. Striped bass population stable, not depleting forage.
   - **Santee-Cooper Reservoir** (South Carolina): Landlocked striped bass since 1941, natural reproduction since 1950s. Diverse forage (threadfin/gizzard shad, alewife, blueback herring, centrarchids) supports successful striped bass, centrarchid, and catfish fisheries for >40 years. Described as "very successful, positive influence on total fish population ecology, worth millions of dollars."
   - **Missouri River System** (Lake Sakakawea, Lake Oahe): Smelt introduced 1971, colonized downstream reservoirs within 8 years. Beneficial to walleye, pike, and tailwater trout fisheries. Described by North Dakota biologist as "salvation of Missouri River system reservoirs." Smelt pass through tailwaters but do not establish (beneficial forage, no negative impact).

8. **Potential Risks and Mitigation:**
   - **Predation on Larval Fish:** Smelt can eat small fish/eggs when available. Risk minimized by thermal partitioning (smelt in hypolimnion, yoy in warm shallows during summer stratification). Winter interaction occurs after most yoy have grown beyond vulnerable size. Smelt often eat yoy smelt due to offshore availability.
   - **Competition for Zooplankton:** Some dietary overlap with shad and yoy game fish. In years of high forage abundance, plankton utilized more fully (currently underutilized). Smelt are "selective planktivores" preferring larger zooplankters, potentially restructuring but not eliminating plankton communities.
   - **Downstream Migration:** Fully expected and likely beneficial. Smelt will colonize downstream reservoirs (except possibly [[Lake Havasu]], which may lack strong thermocline). Summer temperatures below Havasu likely exceed smelt tolerance (>21-26°C), limiting downstream spread. Smelt in tailwaters benefit trout fisheries (Missouri River precedent).
   - **Upstream Migration:** Very unlikely. Smelt do not inhabit flowing waters except briefly at spawning; are weak swimmers; do not ascend streams with rapids or falls >1 foot; prefer non-silty water (opposite of Colorado/San Juan River tributaries). Will not threaten threatened/endangered fish in upstream refugia (White, Yampa, Little Colorado Rivers).
   - **Impact on Native Species:** Reservoir habitat already drastically altered from pre-impoundment conditions; native "big river" fishes cannot complete life cycles in reservoir. Smelt may provide forage buffer for remnant adult T&E fish, reducing predation by established exotic game fish. No further displacement expected.
   - **Disease:** Smelt host parasites (e.g., *Glugea hertwigi* white cysts). Proposal requires disease inspection by USFWS Fort Morgan, CO, prior to introduction. If clean adults unavailable, egg transfer would eliminate parasite introduction.
   - **Hybridization:** No potential; smelt cannot hybridize with any Colorado River species.

9. **Introduction Method:**
   - Preferred: spawning adults (readily adapt to new environment).
   - Alternative: egg transfers (if disease-free adults unavailable).
   - Smelt adapt to shore spawning in lakes lacking tributaries (documented in multiple systems).

10. **Monitoring Plan:**
    - Annual monitoring under existing DJ Project F-46-R, Job I (Forage Condition Study), Lake Powell Fisheries Project, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources.

11. **Regulatory Process:**
    - Proposal submitted to Colorado River Wildlife Council (CRWC)
