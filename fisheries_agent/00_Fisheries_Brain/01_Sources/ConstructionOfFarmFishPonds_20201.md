# Farm Fish Pond Construction in Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pond-aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This 1949 extension circular from the [[Alabama Agricultural Experiment Station]] (Alabama Polytechnic Institute, Auburn), written by assistant fish culturist J. M. Lawrence, is a practical construction guide for [[Farm Fish Ponds]] in Alabama. It covers site selection, watershed sizing, subsoil and seepage control, dam design, core trench excavation, drain and valve installation, spillway design, erosion protection, and stocking. Its central message is that ponds succeed or fail on the balance between pond size, [[Watershed]] area, and soil type, and on sound seepage-proof dam construction. The circular is framed as a guide for a program that was "only in its infancy."

## Background & Description

- **Pond inventory:** A 1946 survey counted 6,388 ponds in operation in Alabama.
- **Recent growth:** Over the previous 5 years, new ponds were built at a rate of 500 to 1,000 per year, totaling between 1,000 and 2,000 acres of water.
- **Potential:** An estimated 140,000 suitable sites exist on more than 200,000 farms in the state.
- **Expanded uses:** Beyond fishing, ponds served livestock watering, irrigation of vegetable crops, household and barn water, fire protection, forest fire fighting, log storage, and water for small sawmills and gins.
- **Pond types:** Most ponds are built by damming a natural hollow or draw (economical because three sides are natural). Flat stream bottoms require longer, costlier dams and a stream with enough fall to divert water.
- **Scope:** The circular addresses low earthen dams not exceeding 20 feet. Larger dams require an engineer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depth and fish habitat.** Recommended depth at the deepest point along the dam is 6 to 20 feet. The 6-foot minimum applies only to sites with an adequate, constant water supply. The 20-foot maximum reflects cost and biology: experiments showed that the [[Bluegill]], one of the most valuable pond fishes, seldom feeds in water deeper than 15 feet. Shallow water under 2 feet should be minimized, since the water level is adjusted or edges are deepened to eliminate it.

**Subsoil and seepage.** Clay soils are best because compacted, moistened clay particles swell and reduce seepage. Subsoil type also controls surface run-off from a watershed.

**Watershed and run-off.** Woodlands are recommended as watersheds. They are estimated to reduce surface run-off by about 50 per cent relative to pastures, owing to forest floor cover and high transpiration. Cultivated red-clay watersheds produce muddy ponds with poor fishing regardless of fertilization, so they should be terraced and planted to permanent cover.

**Regional drainage-area ratios** (drainage area per acre of pond; see [[Drainage Area Ratio]]):

| Region (Figure 1 area) | Soil and setting | Pasture ratio | Woodland ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Area 1 | Heavy clays, Black Belt and adjoining areas | 4 to 8 acres | 6 to 16 acres |
| Area 2 | Red clays (Piedmont) and clay loams (Limestone Valleys) | 5 to 20 acres (with semi-permanent or permanent stream or spring) | 10 to 40 acres (same condition) |
| Area 3 | Sandy surface soils over sand and gravel (Upper Coastal Plain, Appalachian Plateau, part of Lower Coastal Plain) | 10 to 20 acres (permanent stream or year-round spring only) | 20 to 40 acres |
| Area 4 | Deep sand, southern Alabama | Not stated as a ratio; natural or constructed ponds hold water where thick silt or organic layers seal the bottom | Not stated |

Exceeding the upper limits requires a [[Diversion Ditch]]. Ratios may be exceeded safely up to two times the recommended upper limit. Watersheds below the lower ratio will not supply enough water.

**Water supply sources.** The circular describes springs (open flowing springs and diffuse "springy ground"), [[Artesian Wells]] (aerated over gravel before entering the pond), and underground seepage for abandoned strip mines, quarries, and bar-pits.

**Flood-prone streams.** Streams draining large watersheds that flood after heavy rain are to be avoided, since they bring excess water and mud, both of which reduce fish production.

## Key Findings

**Site selection and survey**
- Pond water level is set with a surveying level, usually at 10 or 12 feet for the preliminary survey, and is later adjusted to topography and water supply.
- The watershed-to-pond ratio must be computed and compared with the recommended ratio for the soil type. Aerial photographs can be used to estimate watershed size.

**Clearing and timing**
- All trees, brush, and stumps in the dam site must be removed, since buried woody material can later cause leaks.
- Trees and brush on banks must be cut at least 15 feet back from the water line for public health regulations, or 30 feet if the owner plans to fly fish from the bank.
- Late summer or fall is the most desirable finishing period, when [[Hatchery]] bluegills are available. Winter is the least desirable.

**Dam design**
- Side slopes are a minimum of [[Two-to-One Slope]] on both upstream and downstream faces. Toe stakes are set at 2H on each side of the top width, so the total base is 4H + T.
- [[Freeboard]] is 2 feet for dams impounding 3 acres or less and 3 feet for larger impoundments exposed to wind waves.
- Topsoil is removed to at least 1 foot depth before the core trench is cut.

**Core trench**
- The [[Core Trench]] must extend the full dam length, reach from subsoil to water level, and be about 10 feet wide in impervious clay. In porous materials, width should be about twice the proposed water depth at that point.
- The trench must extend at least 3 feet into subsoil.
- Where the hollow is too boggy for equipment, [[Ditching Dynamite]] is cheaper than hand digging or dragline work.

**Blasting procedure**
- Holes are punched along the trench line, typically with an iron pipe punch about 6 feet long. Spacing is 15 inches (very wet soil, about 4 feet deep) or no more than 12 inches (moderately wet).
- Each hole is loaded with 2 or 3 sticks of 50 per cent ditching dynamite. Charges should not sit in water more than 2 hours before firing.
- The blasting cable must be at least 500 feet long, and no person may be within 500 feet of the blast.
- Muck and sand that remain can be removed by second and third blasts, or by pushing clay into the trench ends to force muck into a disposal channel.

**Drain pipe and valve**
- The [[Drain Pipe]] must be installed at the lowest point in the pond. Low spots must be filled and sloped toward the drain. The State Board of Health in Alabama requires a drain pipe on all constructed ponds.
- Preferred pipe types, in order: asbestos-cement (best, but needs special assembly equipment), then cast iron (durable and available, but inflexible and expensive).
- Concrete cut-off collars should be placed at about 15-foot intervals to prevent seepage along the pipe.
- A [[Gate Valve]] is the most reliable type. If joints are concrete-sealed, the valve should be on the upstream end to avoid leaks under pressure, which can wash out the dam.

**Spillway**
- Spillways must be wide enough that the heaviest floods pass no more than 3 to 6 inches deep, to prevent fish losses.
- For watersheds under 50 acres, width = (drainage acres ÷ 2) + 10 feet of safety margin. Example: 28 acres gives 14 + 10 = 24 feet.
- Solid rock is the preferred spillway foundation. Concrete spillways should be at least 4 inches thick and reinforced in freezing areas.
- A 12-inch overflow pipe can handle overflow from ponds up to 30 acres, with the upper end set 12 inches below water level.

**Erosion protection**
- Logs 10 to 12 inches in diameter and about 20 feet long, lapped and staked along the dam face, break waves on new dams.
- Riprap or a concrete apron 4 or more inches thick is used on large ponds.
- On dams of 300 feet or less, a heavy sod of grass is generally sufficient.

**Vegetation and edges**
- Dry, sodded banks are recommended over boggy edges, which breed water weeds, provide snake cover, and complicate mosquito control.
- [[Centipede Grass]] performed best, reducing pond edge upkeep by 60 per cent. [[Bermuda Grass]] ranked second and Zoysia a poor third. Kentucky bluegrass is not recommended, though it can be used in northern Alabama.

**Stocking and mosquito control**
- [[Gambusia Minnows]] should be added at 100 or more per acre as the pond fills, to control mosquitoes. They may be obtained from neighboring ponds or the County Health Officer.
- Floating trash should be removed within a week after filling.
- Bluegills are added in the fall or early winter, and [[Bass]] follow the next spring.

- Volume is calculated from cross-section areas (Table 1 by top width). In the worked example (12-foot top width, 30-foot intervals), the average cross section was 4.73 cubic yards, giving 47.3 cubic yards for one section.
- [[Diversion Ditch]] dimensions (Table 2) are given for 50 to 300-acre watersheds of hilly or rolling pasture or woodland, based on 25-year frequency for central Alabama. The 0.2-foot fall per 100 feet prevents scouring, and the 0.5-foot fall suits tight clay or rock bottoms.
