# San Marcos River Endangered and Threatened Species Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan, prepared by the [[San Marcos Recovery Team]] for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, addresses four federally listed species endemic to the [[San Marcos River]] ecosystem: the [[San Marcos Gambusia]], the [[Fountain Darter]], the [[San Marcos Salamander]], and [[Texas Wildrice]]. The stated goal is to secure the survival and eventual recovery of these species by protecting their natural ecosystem. The most serious threat identified is cessation of flow from the [[San Marcos Springs]] caused by overdrafting of the [[Edwards Aquifer]]. Other threats include habitat modification, urban pollution, flooding, and exotic species. The plan proposes an ecosystem-based approach with short-term emergency actions and long-term water-management actions. Downlisting to threatened is conditioned on assured river flow; delisting is not addressed in this phase.

- **Ecosystem context:** The San Marcos Springs are the second largest spring system in Texas. Records indicate they have never ceased flowing, though flow varies. The ecosystem supports a greater known diversity of aquatic organisms than any other ecosystem in the southwestern United States. Average annual spring flow is given as 4.46 m³/s (161.0 cfs).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and hydrology**
- The [[Balcones Fault Zone]] separates the Edwards Plateau from the Blackland Prairies and South Texas Plains. The San Marcos River flows about 110 km before joining the [[Guadalupe River]] near Gonzales.
- The upper river is a clear springrun 5–15 m wide and up to about 4 m deep.
- Spring water is thermally constant, varying by less than 1–2 °C in the headwaters. At the lower springrun, temperature ranged from 25.5 °C (August) to 20.4 °C (February). pH ranges 6.9–7.8.
- Lowest recorded monthly flow was 1.53 m³/s (54 cfs) in 1956. The lowest measured daily flow was 1.29 m³/s (45.55 cfs) on 15–16 August 1956. Maximum daily spring flow can exceed 8.33 m³/s (294.13 cfs).
- [[Comal Springs]] had the greatest mean discharge of any spring in the southwestern United States, with a mean of 7.8 m³/s (275.4 cfs) for 1928–1972. Its mean annual water temperature is 23.4 °C.

**Threats to the ecosystem**
- Groundwater overdrafting is the primary threat. Projections by the Texas Department of Water Resources and others suggest flow from San Marcos Springs could cease around 2000–2010. The Bureau of Reclamation projected a 50–75% probability of continuous flow by 2020.
- Urbanization increases flooding, erosion, pollution, and siltation, and reduces species diversity.
- Herbicide (Roundup) used along IH-35 bridge pilings may wash into the river. No gambusia have been taken at the type locality since spraying began.
- Five flood retardation structures planned by the Soil Conservation Service are expected to slightly benefit the system.

**Associated biota (salamander habitat)**
- Crayfish (*Cambarus* sp.), shrimp (*Palaemonetes* spp.), amphipods (*Hyallella azteca*), midge larvae, water mites, snails, leeches, and planarians.
- Predators include sunfishes, cichlids, stinkpots, bullheads, and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Exotic species of concern include *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Poecilia latipinna*, *P. formosa*, *Lepomis* spp., and *Sarotherodon* spp.

## Key Findings

### Species accounts

**[[San Marcos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia georgei*)**
- Described in 1969 by Hubbs and Peden; much less abundant than *G. geiseri* or *G. affinis*.
- Intensive collections in 1978–1979 yielded 18 *G. georgei* out of 20,199 *Gambusia* (0.09%). Collections in 1981/82 showed 0.06%.
- Current range is about 1 km between IH-35 and the USGS gaging station below Thompson's Island.
- Requirements: thermally constant water, quiet shallow water, muddy substrate without silt, partial shade, clean water, food, and protection from floods.
- Hybridization with *G. affinis* was noted. Collections in 1981–83 suggested hybrids may now be many times more abundant than pure *G. georgei*.
- In October 1984, an FWS memorandum called for an intensive survey. Edwards believed the species "may be extinct."

**[[Fountain Darter]] (*Etheostoma fonticola*)**
- Usually under 25 mm SL. Found only in the San Marcos and Comal Rivers.
- Prefers vegetated stream floors, especially *Rhizoclonium* mats.
- Population estimates: Schenck and Whiteside (1976a) estimated about 103,000 in the San Marcos River. A 1973 federal report gave about 1,000 with no method stated.
- Diet varies by size: copepods (<19.2 mm SL), dipteran and ephemeropteran larvae (19.2–29.5 mm), and ephemeropteran larvae (>29.6 mm).
- Mean fecundity was 19 (from 74 females with mature ova). Sex ratio was 1.39:1 (325 males, 234 females).
- Spawns year-round with two peaks (August and late winter–early spring).
- Extirpated from the Comal River after 1954. Reintroduction of 457 adults in 1975–76 produced a reproducing population.

**[[San Marcos Salamander]] (*Eurycea nana*)**
- A neotenic plethodontid that retains external gills and breeds in water. Adults reach about 59.6 mm total length.
- Listed by FWS as threatened and by Texas as protected nongame (threatened).
- Estimated 17,000–21,000 individuals in the uppermost Spring Lake (Tupa and Davis 1976).
- Population structure is stable: juveniles (<15 mm SVL) about 45%, larger juveniles 30–40%, mature males 10–15%, gravid females about 4%.
- Diet of 80 stomachs included amphipods and tendipedid larvae and pupae.
- Lab intolerance of temperatures ≥30 °C. Habitat water: pH 7.2, 21–22 °C, alkalinity 220–232 mg/l, conductance 510–535 µmhos/cm.

**[[Texas Wildrice]] (*Zizania texana*)**
- Restricted to a 2.4 km length of the San Marcos River headwaters within San Marcos city limits.
- First collected by Nealley in August 1892. Recognized as a distinct species by W. A. Silveus in 1932.
- Abundance dropped sharply. In 1976, plants covered about 1,131 m² of river habitat. None were found in Spring Lake.
- No seedlings were observed in the native habitat recently. Seeds were produced in cultivation (over 1,500 in 1975).
- Transplants into Salado Creek were destroyed by recreation and bulldozing. Nutria ate transplants in Spring Lake.

### Recovery objectives and strategy
- **Goal:** Secure survival and eventual recovery through protection of the San Marcos River ecosystem.
- **Downlisting criterion:** Assured flow in the San Marcos River within its natural cycle of variation.
- **Stepdown Outline (major tasks):**
  - 1.1 Monitor populations (gambusia and wildrice quarterly; darter and salamander twice yearly).
  - 1.2 Identify needs, including competition with exotics, food, disease, reproduction, survivorship, predation, aquifer characteristics, recreation impacts, watershed, and pesticide use.
  - 1.3 Manage habitats: establish guidelines, maintain captive stocks at Dexter and San Marcos National Fish Hatcheries, reduce pollution, augment aquifer recharge, control groundwater pumping, prepare wells to maintain flow, restore habitat, and remove exotics.
  - 1.4 Establish recreational-use guidelines.
  - 2.0 Obtain habitat management authority along the river.
  - 3.0 Law enforcement and agency consultation.
  - 4.0 Public education, including media releases and citizen participation.
  - 5.0 Recommend changes in listed status. Upon delisting, management would pass to the State of Texas.
- **Priorities:** Priority 1 actions prevent extinction, priority 2 maintain current status, and priority 3 provide for full recovery.

### Review comments (Appendix)
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Director's memo, 1983) said the format did not follow Recovery Guidelines and that the stepdown outline was too general. It also questioned whether delisting was feasible and asked for quantified, prioritized goals.
- The Ecological Effects Branch (EPA) requested information on watershed, soils, land use, climate, and pesticide use to assess "may effect" situations. It also asked for written notice of any translocation.
- The Edwards Underground Water District argued the plan was mostly technical rather than action-oriented. It said groundwater pumping control and recharge would not ensure species survival.
- Samuel S. Sweet argued the long-term aquifer problem was not adequately addressed and that the subterranean fauna deserved more attention.
- The final approval memo (June 1984) recommended the plan address immediate protection of the gambusia and tie threats to species needs.
