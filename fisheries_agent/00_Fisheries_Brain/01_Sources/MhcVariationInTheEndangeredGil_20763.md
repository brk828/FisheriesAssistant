# MHC Variation in the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #MHC-genetics #ConservationGenetics

## Executive Summary

This study examined variation at a class II [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC) gene (DRB locus) in the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] across the four remaining Arizona watersheds where the species persists: [[Bylas Spring]], [[Cienega Creek]], [[Monkey Spring]], and [[Sharp Spring]]. Contrary to prior findings based on [[Allozyme Variation]] (where only Sharp Spring was polymorphic), MHC analysis revealed substantial genetic differentiation among all four populations, with nine distinct alleles (*Pooc-1* through *Pooc-9*) identified overall. Population-specific patterns ranged from complete monomorphism (Bylas Spring) to five-allele polymorphism (Sharp Spring). The findings strongly support managing the four watersheds as distinct conservation units rather than relying on a single "best" source population (Sharp Spring) for reintroduction efforts, as previously recommended based on allozyme and fitness data.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), a small livebearing fish once abundant throughout the Gila River drainage, now federally and state-listed as **endangered**.
- **Decline drivers**: Habitat loss/fragmentation (loss of shallow-water habitat) and introduction of the non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
- **Current range**: Restricted to four isolated watersheds in Arizona — Bylas Spring, Cienega Creek, Monkey Spring, and Sharp Spring.
- **Prior genetic work**:
  - [[Allozyme Variation]] study (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985) found only Sharp Spring polymorphic (2 of 25 loci); the other three populations were monomorphic.
  - Fitness correlate studies (Quattro & Vrijenhoek 1989) suggested Sharp Spring had higher fitness than Monkey Spring, leading the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to use only Sharp Spring stock for reintroduction — though a later re-analysis (Sheffer et al., in press) found little fitness difference between populations.
  - [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) analysis using six-cutter restriction enzymes showed no variation among the four populations (low resolution).
- **Genetic marker examined**: A ~190 bp fragment of exon II of a class II [[MHC DRB Locus]] gene, examined via PCR, SSCP analysis, subcloning, and sequencing.
- **Mendelian validation**: Family segregation analysis (e.g., Cienega Creek crosses) confirmed that MHC allele inheritance followed Mendelian expectations, validating single-locus interpretation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- MHC genes are of major adaptive significance, particularly for **disease and parasite resistance**; low MHC variation is hypothesized to increase disease susceptibility in endangered populations — a key extinction risk factor.
- MHC variants can be maintained over very long evolutionary timescales (millions of years) due to balancing selection, in contrast with neutral markers like allozymes or mtDNA.
- **Population size/history context**:
  - **Bylas Spring**: Smallest population (only several hundred adults), experienced repeated bottlenecks in the 1980s, and went **extinct in 1989** (later reestablished from ASU stock).
  - **Monkey Spring**: Small, highly isolated population (few thousand adults); historically larger when a marsh existed at the site.
  - **Sharp Spring**: Historically large population (including adjacent upper Santa Cruz River), reduced in recent decades by introduced mosquitofish.
  - **Cienega Creek**: Continues to have large population numbers.
- The relationship between population history (bottlenecks, isolation, size) and observed MHC diversity supports a pattern where smaller, more isolated, bottlenecked populations show reduced allelic diversity.
- Findings do not fit the "starburst" model of divergence proposed by [[Minckley]] for these watersheds.

## Key Findings

### Allelic Diversity by Population
- **Bylas Spring**: Monomorphic — fixed for allele *Pooc-1* (Ho = 0.000).
- **Cienega Creek**: Two alleles (*Pooc-1*, *Pooc-5*) in near-equal frequency (0.500/0.500), in Hardy-Weinberg proportions (Ho = 0.600).
- **Monkey Spring**: Three alleles — *Pooc-6* (high frequency, 0.925), *Pooc-4* and *Pooc-7* (low frequency, 0.025 and 0.050), in Hardy-Weinberg proportions (Ho = 0.150).
- **Sharp Spring**: Five alleles — *Pooc-2* (0.325), *Pooc-3* (0.175), *Pooc-6* (0.050, low frequency), *Pooc-8* (0.350), *Pooc-9* (0.100). Significant **heterozygote deficiency** relative to Hardy-Weinberg expectations (χ² = 13.06, p < 0.01; Ho = 0.475 vs. He = 0.729).
- Only **two of six possible population pairs shared alleles**: Bylas–Cienega (share *Pooc-1*) and Monkey–Sharp (share *Pooc-6*).

### Sequence-Level Variation
- 19 of 75 examined amino-acid positions were variable.
- Putative **antigen-binding sites** showed higher variability (33.3%, 4/12 sites) than non-binding sites (23.8%, 15/63 sites) — consistent with balancing/diversifying selection acting on the peptide-binding region.
- Cienega Creek alleles (*Pooc-1*, *Pooc-5*) differ by 5 amino acids (positions 64–80).
- Monkey Spring alleles *Pooc-6* and *Pooc-7* differ by a single amino acid (position 80); *Pooc-4* differs substantially from both (8–9 amino acids) despite sharing residues at positions 31 and 33.
- Sharp Spring alleles *Pooc-2* and *Pooc-3* differ by only 1 amino acid (position 81); *Pooc-8* and *Pooc-9* are the most divergent alleles found, each carrying 4–5 unique amino acids not seen elsewhere, and are the only alleles bearing **synonymous (silent) substitutions** (suggestive of older evolutionary origin).
- Despite no shared alleles, Sharp Spring alleles *Pooc-2*/*Pooc-3* are sequence-similar to *Pooc-5* (found in Bylas/Cienega), differing by only 2–3 amino acids.

- Sharp Spring has highest amino-acid heterozygosity driven by divergent allele pairs, not allele number, though realized heterozygosity is only ~70% of expectation due to deficient classes (*Pooc-2/Pooc-8*, *Pooc-3/Pooc-8*).

### Population Differentiation
- Genetic distance (D) only computable (finite) for Bylas–Cienega and Monkey–Sharp comparisons (only pairs sharing alleles).
- **Fst** values were high and all statistically significant across pairs; lowest for Cienega–Sharp (0.24) due to high within-population heterozygosity in both.
- Nucleotide-weighted distance (**vT**) lowest for Bylas–Cienega (0.025); highest for Bylas–Monkey (0.095).
- Sequence-based **Nst** highest for Bylas–Cienega (0.50), lowest for Cienega–Sharp (0.25).
- Notably, Bylas Spring and Cienega Creek are the most genetically similar pair for MHC despite differing at two allozyme loci; conversely, Monkey Spring and Bylas Spring share allozyme alleles but **share no MHC alleles**.

### Phylogenetic Relationships
- Neighbor-joining tree of the nine alleles groups *Pooc-2*, *Pooc-3*, and *Pooc-5* in a tight cluster, and *Pooc-6* with *Pooc-7* in another cluster.
- Suggests localized allele generation via mutation within Sharp Spring (*Pooc-2*/*Pooc-3*) and Monkey Spring (*Pooc-6*/*Pooc-7*), but otherwise **no strong overall association between MHC allele identity and geographic locality**.

### Management Implications
- MHC variation shows **far greater population differentiation** than allozyme or mtDNA data — four of six population pairs share zero MHC alleles.
- This contradicts the earlier recommendation (based on allozyme/fitness data) that **Sharp Spring** stock alone should be used for range-wide reintroduction.
- Combined with geographic isolation and habitat differences among the four drainages, the authors recommend that **all four watersheds be conserved and managed as separate genetic units** to preserve adaptive MHC diversity and improve long-term reintroduction success and species survival.

### Methodological Notes
- MHC genotyping performed via PCR amplification of exon II (DRB class II), SSCP analysis, subcloning, and sequencing (Klenow-repaired fragments, pUC18 subclones).
- Allele nomenclature follows [[Klein Nomenclature]] (Klein et al. 1990).
- Family-based segregation analysis (Cienega Creek crosses) confirmed single-locus, Mendelian inheritance of MHC alleles.
- Heterozygote deficiency in Sharp Spring was consistent across two independent samples, suggesting a genuine biological phenomenon (e.g., cryptic population structure, selection, or possible null allele) rather than technical artifact.
