# Factors Influencing Poststocking Dispersal of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This management brief tests how hatchery rearing and release habitat affect the poststocking dispersal of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid that has declined to a largely unreproducing population in the [[Colorado River]] basin. Radio and sonic telemetry tracked fish released in three contrasting habitats: a mainstream reservoir ([[Lake Powell]]), a small side-canyon backwater on the [[Green River]], and a large backwater ([[Laughlin Lagoon]]) connected to the lower Colorado River. Fish were pond-reared or flow-conditioned, and some were site-acclimated before release. Release habitat strongly governed dispersal: fish released in the small backwater drifted far downstream, while fish in the reservoir and large backwater moved less. Flow conditioning reduced dispersal compared with pond rearing. Site acclimation had no detectable effect on short-term dispersal, although the authors consider that result inconclusive given small samples and short acclimation periods.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid endemic to the Colorado River basin. Its range has contracted, and it survives mainly as older adults in the upper basin and in the lower basin through stocking. Stocking programs have been hampered by poor survival of hatchery-produced fish, which often show physical and behavioral stress, disorientation, and starvation after release. The authors trace these problems to conventional culture methods that were designed for recreational fisheries.

The study compares several rearing and conditioning treatments:

- **Rearing source:** Fish came from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH, raceway-reared), [[Boulder City Municipal Ponds]] (BCMP, pond-reared), and Ouray National Fish Hatchery (ONFH, pond-reared) (Table 1).
- **Flow conditioning:** Some WBNFH fish were held for about 2 months in a 1.5 × 10 m enclosure with inflow of 0.1 to 1.0 m/s before release.
- **Site acclimation:** Fish were held in an enclosure or behind a barrier net at the release site for 2 to 7 days before release.
- **Telemetry:** Transmitters were attached externally in about 30 s, without anesthesia, because anesthesia impaired swimming performance. Sonic transmitters (coded, conical, 35–50 mm long) were used in the reservoir, and 40-MHz radio transmitters were used in the backwater tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Powell** is a large mainstream reservoir (65,000 ha) whose annual fluctuations typically exceed 15 m, which prevents submergent vegetation from establishing. Cover consists mainly of rock talus, tamarisk, and sparse flooded salt cedar.
- **Green River (Utah)** is a turbid river confined by steep canyon walls, with channel widths typically under 150 m and velocities of about 0.5 to 1.0 m/s. Backwater habitat is rare apart from small side eddies and side canyon embayments that flood during spring runoff.
- **Colorado River below Davis Dam** flows through a navigational channel into Laughlin Lagoon, a 30-ha backwater. About 75% of the lagoon is shallow and supports dense submergent (*Potamogeton*) and emergent bulrush (*Scirpus*) stands. The river there is confined and dredged, with widths typically under 150 m and currents below 1 m/s.
- The study area includes the confluence of the Green and Colorado rivers and the 26-km white-water reach of Cataract Canyon.

## Key Findings

**Reservoir test (Lake Powell):**

- Thirty fish were released. Fish were detected 145 times in the first month, and all had left the release site within 5 days.
- By days 3–4, fish moved toward shore, where 72% were usually found within 10 m of shore in vegetative or rocky cover.
- Acclimated fish dispersed an average of 3.3 km and reference fish 4.3 km. Maximum distances were 12.4 km (reference) and 11.1 km (acclimated).
- Movement had nearly stopped (<0.1 km/week) by week 3.

**Small-backwater test (Green River):**

- Sixty fish were tested in two groups of 15 per treatment. All left the release site within 5 days, and downstream movement averaged 5 km/day during the first week.
- Twenty fish (8 acclimated, 12 control) were still detected 2 weeks after release, yielding 70 contacts.
- Average dispersal was 69.5 km (N = 20), and drift was pronounced (>68 km/month).
- Six fish (4 acclimated, 2 control) swam upstream past the 54-km confluence into the Colorado River, and 9 of those 11 fish detected below the confluence were controls. Mean movement was 66 km for acclimated fish (maximum 126 km) and 71 km for controls (maximum 122 km).
- A Kruskal-Wallis test across the three release tests showed dispersal was greatest in the small backwater (χ² = 39.5, df = 2, p < 0.01).

**Large-backwater test (Laughlin Lagoon):**

- Fifty-eight fish were followed for one month, yielding 1,121 contacts. Twenty-one fish remained in the backwater for at least one month.
- Twenty-two fish (16 pond-reared, 6 flow-conditioned) moved downstream and 6 (5 flow-conditioned, 1 pond-reared) moved upstream.
- Most fish (95%) that entered the river were found in small backwaters by the end of week 4.
- Mean dispersal was 8.8 km for site-acclimated pond-reared fish and 6.6 km for reference pond-reared fish. For flow-conditioned fish, it was 3.6 km for site-acclimated and 0.2 km for reference fish.
- Maximum movement was 49 km downstream and 9 km upstream by week 4. Mean dispersal for this test was 7.6 km (N = 28).

**Cross-test comparisons:**

- Across tests, pond-reared fish dispersed a mean of 3.7 km in the reservoir (N = 19), 69.5 km in the small backwater (N = 20), and 7.6 km in the large backwater (N = 28).
- No statistically significant difference in dispersal was found between reference and site-acclimated fish at any site. Acclimation periods under 7 days had negligible short-term effects.
- Flow-conditioned fish dispersed significantly less than pond-reared fish (Wilcoxon two-sample test, Z = −2.298, P = 0.0216, N = 30).

**Interpretation and recommendations:**

- The authors interpret the strong drift of pond-reared fish in flowing water as evidence of poor performance in streams, consistent with physiological fatigue in pond-reared trout. Flow conditioning appears to improve swimming performance and reduce stress and chronic fatigue risk. Its effect in this study was to keep flow-conditioned fish near the release site, similar to wild-captured razorback suckers in earlier telemetry studies.
- Site acclimation results were inconclusive, possibly because of small sample sizes or short acclimation periods.
- The authors recommend testing longer flow-conditioning and acclimation periods, and winter releases, to determine whether performance and survival improve. Their conclusion is that flow conditioning is a promising stocking technique for this endangered species.
