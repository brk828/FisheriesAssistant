# Native Fishes of the Death Valley System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 monograph by [[David L. Soltz]] and [[Robert J. Naiman]], published as Science Series 30 by the [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]], synthesizes what was known about the native fishes of the Death Valley System: their geological and hydrological setting, evolutionary history, present distribution, natural history, physiology and conservation. The system supported 10 native fish species, one of which (the Ash Meadows killifish) was already extinct, and nine of which occur nowhere else on Earth. The authors argue that these fishes are the product of long isolation in desert springs and streams, that their survival depends on groundwater that is being pumped away, and that conservation requires protecting entire aquatic ecosystems rather than individual species.

## Background & Description

The book opens with an introduction on desert fishes as a model of evolution in North America, comparable in value to [[Darwin's Finches]] of the Galápagos. The Death Valley System fauna comprises three families, five genera, 10 species and 11 recognized subspecies:

- **Killifishes (Cyprinodontidae):** five pupfish species (genus *Cyprinodon*) and two killifish species (genus *Empetrichthys*, one extinct). Killifishes are small (2.5 to 10 cm) and distinguished from minnows by scales on the flattened head, an upturned toothed mouth and a rounded or square caudal fin.
- **Minnows (Cyprinidae):** three species, the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), the [[Owens Chub]] and [[Mohave Chub]] (both subspecies of *Gila bicolor*).
- **Suckers (Catostomidae):** one species, the [[Owens Sucker]] (*Catostomus fumeiventris*), reaching at least 40 cm.

Key pupfish taxa described include the [[Owens Pupfish]], [[Salt Creek Pupfish]], [[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (six subspecies, two now thought extinct), and the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], the smallest of roughly 25 pupfish species, rarely exceeding 2.5 cm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and hydrology.** The Great Basin contains more than 90 valleys separated by more than 160 mountain ranges. Death Valley is the hottest and driest part of the United States, with an average annual rainfall of 4.22 cm and an evaporation rate of about 380 cm per year. During the [[Pleistocene Epoch]], interconnected lakes such as [[Lake Manly]] (about 160 km long and 185 m deep at its height) allowed fish dispersal among basins. Lake Manly and other pluvial lakes receded after the last pluvial age, which ended about 8,000 to 12,000 years ago.

**Groundwater.** Present-day springs depend on interbasin groundwater moving through permeable carbonate rock. Water issuing from Ash Meadows springs is 8,000 to 12,000 years old. Major Ash Meadows springs discharge more than 2,700 hectare-meters annually, and up to 35% of groundwater reaching Ash Meadows may travel about 145 km from the [[White River Groundwater Basin]].

**Evolutionary isolation.** Pupfish populations have been isolated for intervals ranging from 400 to 4,000 years (Amargosa subspecies) to 10,000 to 20,000 years (Devils Hole and Owens pupfish). Three factors drive differentiation: selection pressure, length of isolation, and habitat and population size (genetic drift).

**Habitat types.** Fishes occupy constant-temperature springs (for example, [[Big Spring]] and [[Saratoga Springs]]), variable-temperature streams and marshes (such as the [[Amargosa River]] and [[Salt Creek]]), and thermal streams such as Tecopa Bore. Pupfish tolerate upper lethal temperatures of 40 to 44°C, and the Cottonball Marsh pupfish has been recorded in salinities up to 160 parts per thousand.

**Ecological interactions.** Pupfish are opportunistic omnivores that feed mainly on algae, detritus and high-protein invertebrates. Their social systems vary with habitat, from breeding territoriality in large springs to a non-aggressive social system in Devils Hole. Mosquitofish, largemouth bass and other exotics have displaced or eliminated native fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Population status (Table 2):** Of the 18 native taxa listed, none are unaffected by human activity. Only about five are considered healthy and undepleted; several are endangered, and the Ash Meadows killifish and at least three subspecies are extinct.
- **Four main threats:** (1) introduced exotic fishes, (2) pesticides, (3) diversion of surface water, and (4) groundwater mining, which the authors identify as the most serious.
- **Devils Hole:** Water levels were lowered by pumping from 1968 onward. The population fell from as many as 800 fish to a low of about 150 to 200 in winter, then recovered to nearly 400 after water levels stabilized. A 1976 [[U.S. Supreme Court]] decision in *Cappert v. United States* upheld federal authority to reserve groundwater for preservation of aquatic ecosystems.
- **Pahrump killifish:** Two of three subspecies are extinct; the third survives only in refugia outside Pahrump Valley after Manse Spring went dry in 1975.
- **Owens pupfish:** Declined from millions in 1916 to a few hundred by the 1930s; rediscovered in 1956 and saved by hand-carrying about 200 survivors to a new spring.
- **Refugia and transplants:** Transplanted Devils Hole pupfish did not establish reproducing populations in five outside springs; the Hoover Dam refugium population persisted but became larger and morphologically different.
- **Physiology:** Pupfish recover from hypersaline stress in about eight hours, versus 50 to 170 hours in steelhead and eels; Cottonball Marsh pupfish tolerated 130‰ for several weeks under gradual acclimation.
- **Productivity:** In a warm Tecopa stream, Amargosa pupfish yearly production was nearly 10 times that of any carnivorous fish population studied to date.
- **Conservation outlook:** The authors warn that many springs may be threatened or destroyed within 20 years, and call for a permanent philosophical and financial commitment to preserving intact desert aquatic ecosystems.
