# Fishes of Eagle Creek, Arizona, with Records for Threatened Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Marsh]] and colleagues documents the fish fauna of Eagle Creek, a second-order stream in southeastern Arizona, based on a survey in July 1987 and a review of earlier records. The stream holds nine native and 11 non-native fish species. It reports the first records of [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) from the stream, collected in 1985, and notes that [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) is known only from a single 1950 collection. Native fishes were abundant upstream, where non-native fishes were scarce, while downstream reaches with substantial exotic populations held few native fishes. The authors conclude that alien fishes likely affect the native fauna and that the loach minnow may still persist.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** Most native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]] in western North America face extinction. Spikedace and loach minnow are each the sole species of their genus, endemic to middle-elevation streams of the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. Both are federally listed as threatened and protected by Arizona and New Mexico.

**Survey history.**
- In 1950, [[Robert R. Miller]] (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology) collected fishes including loach minnow, the only known record of the species from Eagle Creek.
- Spikedace were not taken in 1950, nor in extensive sampling in the 1970s or early 1980s.
- In May 1985, a survey yielded a dozen juvenile spikedace (12 to 22.5 mm total length), the first records from the stream.
- An intensive July 1987 survey, including the 42-km central gorge accessible only on foot, found spikedace locally common in the canyon-bound reach but no loach minnows.

**Stream description.** Eagle Creek rises in mixed-conifer forest near 2800 m on the southern slopes of the [[White Mountains]]. It flows about 20 km through a grassy valley, then about 54 km through steep canyons to the [[Gila River]]. Its watershed covers about 2000 km².

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.**
- Gradient is 90 m/km or more in the headwaters, 4.9 m/km in the upper valley, and 7.4 m/km in the downstream canyons.
- For 1946 to 1969, mean discharge averaged 0.94 m³/s. Interbasin transfer from the [[Black River]] raised this 27% (to 1.2 m³/s), and withdrawals for municipal and industrial use at Clifton-Morenci reduced it 9% (to 0.86 m³/s).
- Annual mean discharge ranged from 0.21 to 2.74 m³/s; maximum daily discharge was near 600 m³/s in 1965.
- Peak flows occur twice yearly, from winter precipitation with spring snowmelt, and from summer [[Monsoon]] flash floods. The lowermost reach is often intermittent during droughts.

**Water quality (1976–77).**
- Dissolved oxygen was near 100% saturation year-round.
- pH ranged from 7.6 to 9.2.
- Total dissolved solids ranged from 134 to 324 mg/l.
- Water temperature ranged from near freezing in winter to about 24 °C in summer at high elevations. Lower reaches ranged from winter minima of 4 to 10 °C to summer maxima near 30 °C.

**Habitat and biota.**
- Substrate is dominated by cobbles and boulders, with sand at margins and silt in local pools.
- The green alga *Cladophora glomerata* can choke riffles after long low-flow periods. Vascular macrophytes are rare.
- Benthic invertebrates, mostly aquatic insect larvae and nymphs, are abundant and diverse.
- Riparian vegetation ranges from seepwillow and burrobush in narrow scoured canyons to gallery forests of cottonwood, willow, sycamore and walnut in wider reaches.

**Human pressures.** Eastern Arizona has been affected by heavy cattle grazing, open-pit copper mining and logging. Water transfer by pumping from the Black River via an aqueduct built in 1945, together with diversion dams, has also altered flows. A 5.0 m vertical-fall dam at the diversion structure acts as a fish barrier, separating upstream and downstream fish communities.

## Key Findings

**1. Species richness and composition.** The fauna comprises 20 recorded kinds of fishes: nine native (including razorback sucker) and 11 non-native. Five of the 11 non-natives were taken in 1987, and the ten species found in the 1987 survey include nine non-natives plus native fishes from each reach.

**2. Spikedace status.** Spikedace are present in Eagle Creek but were not captured at or upstream from Sheep Wash in 1987, although the species occurred abundantly less than a kilometre downstream. The authors conclude that an undetected population was already present when Miller visited in 1950. Absence from the 1950 collection may reflect the species' natural spatial fluctuations, and immigration from the Gila River or the Black River is considered unlikely.

**3. Loach minnow status.** Loach minnow has not been collected since the 1950 record, despite sampling of apparently suitable habitat with effective methods. The authors suspect it persists in low numbers in Eagle Creek and recommend monitoring to detect changes early.

**4. Native–non-native gradient.** Native fishes made up 98.2% of specimens in the upstream reach (Honeymoon to Sheep Wash), 97.8% in the Sheep Wash to P-Bar reach, and 91.0% in the P-Bar to diversion dam reach. Downstream of the diversion dam, native fishes made up only 23.2% of 418 specimens. Total species per reach were 6, 10, 10 and 14, with non-natives numbering 0 to 1, 4, 4 and 8. Native diversity remained at 5 to 6 species per reach, but native fishes were abruptly reduced at Honeymoon.

**5. Non-native fishes.** Rainbow trout, planted for sport fishing, occurred from Honeymoon to Sheep Wash. Red shiner, fathead minnow, yellow bullhead, channel catfish and smallmouth bass occurred between Sheep Wash and the diversion dam. Downstream of the dam, common carp, flathead catfish, mosquitofish and largemouth bass were added. Red shiner and fathead minnow likely came from baitfish releases. The origin of smallmouth bass is unclear, but the authors suggest it was pumped from the Black River via Willow Creek. Mosquitofish are implicated in declines of native fishes through predation and competition.

**6. Native trout.** The original Eagle Creek trout (*Oncorhynchus* sp.) was not collected in 1987 and may now be replaced by introduced rainbow trout. Its identity as [[Apache Trout]] or [[Gila Trout]] remains unresolved. Allozyme and mitochondrial DNA analysis of 22 fish from Chitty Creek in 1989 suggested that native trout may have been swamped by introduced rainbows. The authors recommend a common management goal for Chitty Creek, which they see as a candidate for preserving either native trout species.

**7. Razorback sucker reintroduction.** Stocking of razorback sucker began in the early 1980s as part of a recovery program. Two subadults (21.1 and 22.3 cm standard length) were caught in the impoundment above the diversion dam in August 1986. In July 1987, 10 individuals (47 to 72 mm SL; mean 58 ± 8 mm) were caught below Sheep Wash. These were presumed survivors of 33,750 juveniles (about 60 mm SL) stocked near the mouth of Willow Creek in June 1987.

**8. Management recommendations.**
- Monitoring to confirm the status of loach minnow and spikedace.
- Control of non-native fish introductions, including baitfish releases and unauthorized stocking.
- Development of a plan for native trout conservation in Chitty Creek.
- Early detection of further non-native introductions.

**9. Appended material.** The document also contains a book review of Charles F. Lummis's *Some Strange Corners of Our Country* (reprinted 1989), an account of the American Southwest's peoples and landscapes. It is unrelated to the fish study.
