# Gastric Evacuation in European Perch (Perca fluviatilis) Fed on Fish Larvae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by L. Persson (Institute of Limnology, University of Lund, Sweden; published in *Freshwater Biology*, 1981) examines how [[Water Temperature]] and [[Meal Size]] influence the rate of gastric evacuation in [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*) fed on small fish, mainly [[Bleak]] (*Leucaspius delineatus*), [[Crucian Carp]] (*Carassius carassius*) and [[Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*). Experiments were run at 16.5–21.7 °C on perch of two size classes (89–110 mm and 120–170 mm total length) collected from Lake Sövdeborgssjön, southern Sweden. Gastric evacuation was generally well described by an [[Exponential Decay Model]], with a constant instantaneous evacuation rate across most meal sizes. The results are intended to support estimates of food consumption rates in perch populations.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*), a piscivorous percid freshwater fish.
- **Context:** Earlier studies on gastric evacuation in piscivorous fish (e.g., [[Mckone 1971]], [[Schneider 1973]], [[Swenson and Smith 1973]]) reported contradictory findings on the effects of meal size and temperature. Some used second- or first-plus-second-order polynomial models, while others (e.g., [[Tyler 1970]], [[Elliott 1972]]) found exponential curves adequate.
- **Methods background:** Perch were caught by electrofishing, acclimatised for 3–4 weeks, and fed live prey. Stomach contents were removed by stomach-pumping at set intervals, dried at 65 °C for 48 h, ashed, and weighed as organic dry weight. Pre-experiment starvation lasted 12–48 h, depending on temperature.
- **Size classes:** Perch smaller than 90 mm never fed on fish in the study lake, and perch larger than 170 mm formed a very small part of the population, which shaped the choice of the two size classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Perch in Lake Sövdeborgssjön fed mainly on fish, with small perch (under 90 mm) feeding on invertebrates. Previous work on perch feeding on invertebrates (e.g., [[Persson 1979]]) was used for comparison.
- Gastric evacuation of fish prey is slower than that of invertebrates such as [[Gammarus pulex]] and chironomids, indicating a higher energy value per meal and a longer digestion time for large prey.
- The instantaneous evacuation rate was similar between the two perch size groups and across different prey types, but prey type (fish vs. invertebrate) affected evacuation.
- Temperature governs digestion speed, so seasonal temperature variation directly affects estimated food consumption in perch populations.

## Key Findings

- **Temperature effect:** The relationship between the instantaneous evacuation rate (R) and temperature was described by an exponential function. R values were 0.117–0.129 per hour at 16.5–20.0 °C, and up to about 0.200 at 21.7 °C for the smallest meal (17.3 mg), as shown in Table 2 (n = 10 per experiment).
- **Meal size effect:** For meal sizes of roughly 21 mg up to about 75 mg organic dry weight, R was constant, and gastric evacuation followed an exponential curve. At larger meals (above about 75 mg, e.g., 121.3 mg), a lag phase appeared early in digestion, and R-values were lower (about 0.136–0.200 in some fits).
- **Size class differences:** Perch of 89–104 mm and 121–162 mm showed similar R-values at 21.7 °C (about 0.24–0.26) for meals up to about 75 mg. The threshold meal size at which a lag phase appeared was smaller for the smaller perch.
- **Model fit:** Exponential curves gave good fits (coefficients of determination mostly 0.75–0.97). A second-order polynomial with an added lag term (Eqn 3, of the form R = a·(t − b)^c fitting lag phases) was proposed as an improvement for large meals, with an empirical justification based on the lag phase.
- **Multiple meals:** In experiments with two or three meals, calculated stomach contents from the multi-meal equation (Eqn 2, after Elliott 1972) matched observed values reasonably well, though some estimates for later meals were underestimated (e.g., calculated 21.0 mg at 8 h vs. actual 20.7 mg).
- **Methodological caution:** Weighing errors may underestimate the amount of food remaining in the stomach, especially for small residues, so the author recommends dry weight or organic dry weight measurements rather than wet weight.
- **Ecological significance:** The study supports using exponential evacuation models to estimate food consumption rates of perch, while noting that fish prey and meal size can alter the evacuation curve.
