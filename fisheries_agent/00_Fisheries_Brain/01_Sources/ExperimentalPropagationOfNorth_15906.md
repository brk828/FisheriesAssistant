# Experimental Propagation of Northern Pike at the St. Marys Fish Farm

## Executive Summary
This 1954 investigational report from the Ohio Division of Wildlife documents experimental artificial propagation trials of [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) conducted at the St. Marys Fish Farm. The study aimed to develop hatchery techniques suitable for producing pike stock adaptable to southern Ohio conditions, extending the species' range beyond its native northwestern Ohio distribution. Breeders were sourced from three populations: wild-trapped fish from the [[Dildine Area]] of Lake Erie, holdover breeders from [[Magee Marsh]] (trapped 1953), and fish hatched/reared on-site at the farm. Researchers tested five distinct fertilization/stripping methodologies across 22 hatchery jars, along with parallel natural reproduction trials in ponds. Results revealed significant variability in egg survival (0–60%) tied to fertilization method, use of a clay separating agent, and water temperature during incubation.

## Background & Description
- **Egg density**: Water-hardened eggs numbered **56,083 eggs per quart** (measured via 35 mL sample counts); egg size remained essentially constant between introduction to hatchery jars and hatching.
- **Breeder size ranges** (natural reproduction ponds): Females 20–35 inches; males 18–24 inches.
- **Fry development**: Mouths do not open until approximately the **third day** post-hatch, at which point exogenous feeding (entomostracans) becomes necessary.
- **Fingerling growth**: Seined fingerlings averaged 14 inches by May 14 (early-season sample); final harvest fingerlings averaged 7 inches (July drainage, likely reflecting a later cohort or measurement discrepancy in source data).
- **Recovered stock**: Pond-reared fry recovered in October averaged **12 inches** in length, with a total yield of 554 lbs from ~150,000 stocked fry (150 individuals recovered).
- Natural spawning pond fish (straw substrate trial) averaged **15 inches** at recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning substrate**: Natural reproduction trials used floating straw to simulate the debris substrate typical of pike spawning habitat in marsh systems, mimicking conditions in areas like [[Magee Marsh]] and Lake Erie marshlands.
- **Thermal spawning range**: Spawning was observed across a water temperature range of **40–51°F** in pond settings with adult breeders.
- **Larval feeding ecology**: Fry were fed **entomostracans** (zooplankton) seined from fertilized ponds beginning around day 3. Notably, no choking incidents occurred in 1954, contrasting with 1953 trials where **Daphnia magna** caused choking mortality in pike fry — suggesting prey-size selection is a critical constraint in early larval rearing.
- **Habitat use post-stocking**: Fry were released into fertilized ponds with established entomostracan crops; forage base (minnow fry) was supplemented two weeks post-stocking to sustain growth.
- **Predation/competition pressure**: In natural reproduction ponds, depletion of appropriately-sized minnow forage likely led to **cannibalism of smaller pike fingerlings** by larger conspecifics, limiting overall fingerling yield.
- **Temperature sensitivity**: A drop of 15°F within 18 hours substantially reduced fry numbers in both pond-stocked and natural reproduction settings, indicating high sensitivity of larval pike to rapid thermal shifts.

## Key Findings

### Fertilization Method Comparison
Five stripping/fertilization methods were tested, with survival to hatching stage as follows:
1. **Moist pan, milt directly into eggs**: 50% survival
2. **Moist pan, milt into water then poured over eggs**: 28.7% survival
3. **Stripped into water, milt into eggs, stirred**: 20% survival
4. **Stripped into water, milt into eggs, not stirred**: 46% survival
5. **Moist pan, milt collected via water-filled burette**: 22% survival

The single best-performing jar (Refill Jar #4, Method 4 variant without post-fertilization washing) achieved **60% survival**, suggesting that eliminating the customary wash step, or the separating-agent effect, may have influenced outcomes more than the stripping method itself.

### Clay Separating Agent
- A **Minford silt clay solution** was tested as an anti-clumping agent in 4 jars.
- Results were inconsistent: some clay-treated batches were discarded due to irreversible clumping (Jars #2, #16), while others achieved a 15% hatch (Jars #3/#4 combined).
- Mixing clay-treated and untreated eggs (Jar #16) resulted in total clumping failure and discard.
- **Conclusion**: Clay was not a reliable solution and may have introduced additional variability rather than controlling for it.

### Temperature and Incubation Period
- Initial stripping occurred at water temps of **39°F** (March 23–24).
- Temperatures fluctuated between 38–55°F over the incubation period, with a notable 16°F swing over 14 days and an 11°F rise in just 26 hours (April 6–7).
- Of 19 hatched jars: 6 hatched in 14 days at ~43°F average; 8 in 14 days at 44.5°F; 4 in 7 days at 52°F; 1 in 6 days at 54.7°F.
- **Higher water temperatures correlated with shorter incubation periods AND higher survival**, contradicting an assumption that faster incubation increases mortality risk.
- Highest survival (60%) occurred in a 7-day incubation at 52°F (Refill Jar #4).

### Fry Mortality and Rearing
- Heavy fry mortality occurred on the **sixth day post-hatch**: ~75% mortality in one rearing creel versus ~75% survival in an adjacent creel sharing the same holding box water supply — indicating high variability in unidentified stressors (possibly genetic origin, handling, or microhabitat conditions within creels).
- Fry derived from **St. Marys Farm-reared breeders** showed the best survival rates among rearing creels.
- Egg-shell removal from newly hatched fry proved logistically impossible at scale; elodea substrate in creels only partially prevented fry from settling into debris.

### Pond Stocking Outcomes
- ~150,000 fry (excluding St. Marys-origin fry) were stocked into two fertilized ponds with established entomostracan populations.
- A rapid 15°F temperature drop within 18 hours significantly reduced fry numbers shortly after stocking.
- October drainage recovered **150 pike averaging 12 inches**, totaling 554 lbs — demonstrating pond-rearing as a viable, if lossy, production method.

### Natural Reproduction Trials
- **Straw-substrate trial** (0.1-acre pond): Wind/wave disruption of the straw substrate reduced viable egg exposure, yet still yielded 6 pike averaging 15 inches — equivalent to a production rate of **150 fish/acre**, deemed promising for further study.
- **Breeder-stocked ponds** (6 ponds, 2 females + 8 males each, sourced from three breeder populations): Spawning confirmed at 40–51°F. Final harvest (July 20) yielded 138 total fingerlings (60 from St. Marys-origin breeders averaging 7 in.; 78 from other sources at similar size) — a modest but confirmed demonstration of viable **natural pond reproduction** for the species.

### Overall Assessment
The report concludes that while artificial jar-hatchery propagation is feasible and controllable, natural pond reproduction is also achievable and merits further investigation as a lower-intervention alternative. Both approaches face significant fry mortality bottlenecks linked to temperature instability, forage size/availability mismatches, and intraspecific predation among juvenile [[Northern Pike]].
