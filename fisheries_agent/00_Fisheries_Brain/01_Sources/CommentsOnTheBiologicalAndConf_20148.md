# Metropolitan Water District Comments on the Lower Colorado River Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1997 comment letter from the [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]] (Metropolitan) to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Reclamation) on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Biological Opinion (BO) for Lower Colorado River operations and the Lake Mead to International Boundary reach. Metropolitan is a member of the [[Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (MSCP) Steering Committee. It generally supports the consultation's geographic scope, five-year timeframe, and Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) and Measures (RPMs), but disputes whether operations jeopardize the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. It argues that the BO's "catastrophic" characterization of habitat loss rests on incorrect assumptions, supported by new survey data. The letter also challenges the BO's use of the environmental baseline, its reliance on a non-final prospectus, and several factual and table errors.

## Background & Description

The letter is a formal correspondence, not a research study. It was signed by Debra C. Man, Chief, Planning and Resources, and includes a detailed enclosure of specific comments. Metropolitan relies on the Colorado River for water supply and hydroelectric power in Southern California.

The species at issue are:

- **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]**, the central species of concern. It is a listed riparian songbird whose status drives the jeopardy question.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, a native endangered catostomid, discussed in relation to recruitment, predation, and reintroduction.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**, an endangered cyprinid, discussed mainly in terms of recovery planning and stocking size.

Relevant governing frameworks include the [[Endangered Species Act]] and its implementing regulations (50 CFR § 402.14(g)(4)), the [[Environmental Baseline]], and the [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter addresses several habitat types and reservoir dynamics:

- **Lake Mead Delta**: Willow stands that the BO called "catastrophic" to lose sit on the lake bottom, which is periodically inundated. Metropolitan notes these trees did not exist about five years earlier, when the area had been dewatered after a long dry period.
- **Lower Grand Canyon**: Dense riparian and wetland habitat, including Gooding's and coyote willow, extends above the Lake Mead Delta.
- **Virgin and Muddy River drainages**: Flycatchers were believed extirpated here, but new sites have been found, including agricultural drainages near Mesquite.
- **[[Topock Marsh]]**: Flycatcher nesting is associated mainly with saltcedar.
- **Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu**: Sites of current stocking and grow-out programs for native fishes.

Metropolitan argues that reservoir-bed habitat is ephemeral and that permanent protection of about 1,400 acres is more effective long term. The letter also describes the [[Lower Colorado River]] as heavily shaped by dams and non-native predators, including [[Non-Native Fish]] that prey on larval and juvenile native fish.

## Key Findings

**1. Flycatcher habitat is larger than the BO assumed.**
- Aerial surveys reported in a Hualapai Tribe January 1997 report estimated about 3,070 acres of riparian and wetland habitat above the Lake Mead Delta in 1994, extending more than 20 miles along both banks.
- The BO referenced about 4,500 acres. Metropolitan says that "at least several thousand acres" of existing and potential habitat were not considered.
- Surveys focused on accessible perimeters likely underestimate actual use.

**2. Flycatchers are nesting in new and previously unrecorded areas.**
- Lower Grand Canyon surveys located one nest on the Hualapai Reservation (one young, two eggs, three adults nearby, implying another nest), plus two additional pairs.
- The BO recorded only two territories in all of Nevada from 1993 to 1996. Ongoing [[San Bernardino County Natural History Museum]] surveys under Reclamation contract found 12 pairs at four sites on the Virgin and Muddy rivers. These pairs produced 7 young or fledglings and 11 eggs.
- At Topock Marsh, about 18 to 22 pairs were observed, with seven nests producing eight young or fledglings and eight eggs.

**3. Survey protocol may miss birds.** Some nests were first located only after a fourth visit, after which a researcher following the standard protocol would have stopped searching. Some sites were located in mid-July.

**4. Breeding occurs outside the "ideal" habitat.** Birds are breeding and fledging in saltcedar and other habitats, not only the willow/cottonwood type the BO assumed.

**5. The "catastrophic" label and jeopardy finding are challenged.** Metropolitan argues that the two assumptions behind the label are incorrect: that replacement habitat is unavailable and that only willow supports successful nesting. It asks Reclamation to forward this information so the Service can amend the BO. The RPA provisions 5, 7, and 11 and RPM 1 are said to already require protection or restoration of about 1,400 acres.

**6. Scope of consultation.** Under 50 CFR § 402.14(g)(4), a BO should address the proposed action and cumulative effects. Metropolitan argues the BO wrongly expanded scope by including past actions, such as pre-ESA dam and reservoir construction, in the environmental baseline. This made the RPA disproportionate. The Service relied on a 1994 draft handbook that had not been finalized, and existing regulations should prevail.

**7. Reliance on a non-final prospectus.** The Service used its February 1995 preliminary *Colorado River: Lower Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus* rather than the official [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] or the draft [[Razorback Sucker Recovery Plan]]. The prospectus was held in abeyance and, according to Metropolitan, was not peer reviewed by native fish recovery teams.

**8. Razorback sucker issues.**
- The BO attributes poor recruitment to "too little or too much water." Metropolitan cites the Service's own 1994 critical habitat document, which identifies non-native predation and competition as the most important limiting factor.
- Native Fish Work Group staff harvested more than 100,000 wild larvae in Lake Mohave in 1996–97, most of which fall prey to non-natives.
- Past reintroductions in the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers failed, partly because fish were too small. Current efforts grow fish to 10–12 inches to reduce predation, aiming to preserve the gene pool rather than create self-sustaining populations. Metropolitan notes that neither recovery plan calls for self-sustaining mainstem populations.

**9. Factual and table corrections requested.**
- **Figure 3 (flood control line):** The BO gives 450.5 feet, but the figure shows 446.4 to 450.0 feet, with the top of dam at 450.5 feet.
- **Table 1:** Reclamation's discretion to "renegotiate" downstream contracts should be limited to requesting renegotiation, since these contracts are permanent. Lake Mohave item (3) should refer to requesting that the Secretary of Energy seek renegotiation.
- **Page 91:** The BO gives an average Lees Ferry discharge of 15,297 acre-feet, higher than the stated long-term average of 14,400 acre-feet. Metropolitan says both values are incorrect and asks that the averaging period be stated.
- **Page 45 header:** "Response to the Proposed Action" conflicts with the paragraph's purpose as a review of past effects, many of which (dam construction, reservoir creation) were not discretionary.
- **Table 12:** Shows entitlements rather than 1993 usage for some users, despite the text describing the table as summarizing 1993 uses.

**10. Overall position.** Metropolitan finds the BO generally credible and scientifically defensible, and it supports the five-year consultation period, which allows time to finish the MSCP before Reclamation reinitiates consultation in 2002. It recommends that Reclamation coordinate closely with the MSCP Steering Committee on all required assessments.
