# Strip Count Method for Estimating Fish Densities and Habitat Use in Lake Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 methods paper, published in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* (Vol. 1, No. 2), tests the [[Strip Count Method]] as a way to estimate fish densities and habitat use in a small lake. The study was carried out in [[Lake Opinicon]], Ontario, using snorkel-based [[Diver Counts]] at three inshore sites with different physical and biological characteristics. The authors asked whether the method is applicable, whether its results are repeatable, and whether it gives an accurate estimate of population numbers at a given time.

The method proved repeatable for total fish numbers and for the ratio of the commonest species to total fish, but repeatability depended on habitat. It was excellent in small weedy sites, good on gravel bottoms, and only fair in open sandy bays. Morning-to-afternoon and day-to-day comparisons were broadly similar but were statistically different in many cases according to the [[Mann-Whitney U Test]] at α = 0.05. The authors conclude the method has considerable potential for studying habitat specialization, community structure, and relative abundance in small lakes.

## Background & Description

**Origins of the method.** The [[Line Transect Method]] was first used in plant ecology and later became a major tool in game and avian biology. [[Brock (1954)]] and [[Bardach (1959)]] first applied it to fishes in tropical reef communities. Bardach (1959) stated that visual counts were "the least biassed and best census method" under prevailing conditions, yet the authors note that the strip count method had not been adequately tested by ichthyologists.

**Why this approach.** Plot counts and selective netting in Lake Opinicon were too limited in scope. [[Capture-Mark-Recapture]] methods were judged inappropriate because fish move freely in and out of study areas.

**Known problems addressed.** The paper reviews four general problems with strip counts:
1. Variable detectability among species and sizes.
2. Defining strip boundaries without elaborate preparation, which was handled through angle-of-approach formulae.
3. Avoiding double counts. Workers usually note the direction a disturbed animal flees.
4. Repeatability, which had rarely been tested in wildlife studies.

**Study organisms.** Lake Opinicon's fish are relatively fearless, and all 18 species can be identified underwater. Most fish concentrate in marginal shallows at depths of 0.75-2.25 m. Fin-clipping showed that many [[Bluegill]] and [[Rock Bass]] remain in restricted areas for long periods. Different habitat types, such as sandy shallows, dense weedbeds, gravel shoals, and rock shelves, hold different species and size classes. Some species, such as [[Blackchin Shiner]] and young [[Bluegill]], keep to dense weedbeds and avoid exposed rocky areas or deeper open water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Counts were made in early September 1975, when fish were still in their summer habitats and minimally territorial. Three inshore sites were chosen, and count strips were laid out in water 1.3-1.9 m deep, the depths of maximum fish density:

- **Weedy inlet (Cottage Bay):** A sheltered enclave about 15 m across with dense weed growth on soft mud. Dominant fish were [[Blackchin Shiner]] and young-of-year [[Bluegill]] (40-80 mm). A few other species appeared intermittently.
- **Gravel area:** A weed-free strip about 50 m long and 3 m wide, with a steep drop-off to 3-4 m. Dominant fish were medium to large [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and [[Rock Bass]] (100-140 mm).
- **Sandy bay:** A shallow sandy area about 25 m across with only 10% weed cover and high exposure to turbulence. Dominant fish were medium to large [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]], with some young [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Banded Killifish]].

**Field protocol.** A 1.5 m wide strip was found to be optimal. Quarter-inch yellow nylon rope anchored by yellow painted bricks at 3 m intervals marked the edges. The observer swam down the centre using a mask and snorkel and counted fish 1.0-1.5 m ahead, across an arc of 0.75 m on either side of the midline. Fish were sorted into size groups (40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 mm) with a measuring device. Results were periodically checked by [[Seine Netting]].

**Timing.** Five consecutive counts were made per series, with 10-minute intervals to let fish settle after the observer passed. Counts were taken on five successive mornings and afternoons, at 0830-0930 and 1600-1700, which correspond to peak feeding times of several species (see [[Feeding Periodicity]]).

**Age structure.** The paper gives length-by-age data for [[Bluegill]] (for example, age 0 at 25-45 mm and age VI at 134-156 mm), [[Pumpkinseed]], and [[Rock Bass]]. These year classes shift in abundance among habitats, so recording several size categories is essential.

## Key Findings

**1. Repeatability of counts varies with habitat.**
- *Weedy site (mornings):* Total fish across five repeated counts on successive mornings had means of 40.8, 57.4, 51.0, 44.8, and 43.4, with SDs of 2.63, 4.96, 3.57, 1.72, and 1.11. Variation was below the 10% SD-to-mean criterion. Three species (blackchin shiner, bluegill, and small largemouth bass) made up about 95% of individuals.
- *Gravel site:* Only three of five morning series met the 10% criterion for total numbers. Pumpkinseed-to-bluegill ratios had SD-to-mean values below 10% in all series.
- *Sandy site:* Variability was high, with morning means of 38.4, 38.6, 6.0, 23.2, and 14.8 and only one SD-to-mean ratio meeting the 10% criterion for total numbers. Counts declined progressively from count 1 to count 5 on two days, which the authors attribute to fish moving freely out of the open strip.

**2. Internal consistency was high.** The [[Spearman Rank Correlation Test]] showed no significant difference (α = 0.05) in total numbers across counts 0 to 5 within series. Among the ratios of major species to total fish, only 1 of 140 cases differed significantly. The authors interpret this as evidence of no [[Observer Effect]].

**3. Morning and afternoon, and day-to-day, counts differed significantly.** Series were within the same general range but were statistically different by the Mann-Whitney U test (α = 0.05).
- Morning vs. afternoon, same day: total fish differed significantly on 5 days at the weedy site, 2 days at the gravel site, and 5 days at the sandy site. Bluegill-to-total ratios differed on 3, 5, and 4 days, respectively.
- Between days (six pairs, three morning and three afternoon): total fish differed significantly on 5 pairs (weedy), 4 (gravel), and 4 (sandy). Bluegill-to-total ratios differed on 4 pairs at each site.
- The differences showed no clear pattern related to weather. The authors conclude they reflect random day-to-day variance.

**4. Species-level variability.** The less common species were consistently more variable than dominant ones. Blackchin shiners were more consistent than bluegills, which are larger and less restricted to shallows. The ratio of large to medium bluegills varied considerably at the gravel site.

**5. Conclusions and recommended uses.** The authors draw three broad conclusions:
- The method gives good repeatability over short time intervals, with the level depending on habitat and community.
- Total numbers and species composition are broadly comparable at equivalent times on successive days, but these differ significantly when tested.
- The method can provide a fair density estimate where toxicants and mark-recapture are not applicable. It is also useful for studying fish movements relative to feeding grounds, [[Habitat Specialization]], and [[Community Structure]].

**6. Recommendations for open habitats.** For extensive homogeneous areas such as the sandy bay, the authors suggest wider strips (two or three observers swimming in parallel) or more strips per site to improve reliability.
