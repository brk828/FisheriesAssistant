# Amazon Mollyfish: Clonal Heredity and Research at Marquette University

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mollies

## Executive Summary

The Amazon molly is a female-only fish whose offspring are essentially identical twins within each clone. Its breeding system raises a basic question about how the population originated and how heredity is controlled. [[Rezneat Darnell]] and [[Peter Abramoff]], associate professors of zoology at [[Marquette University]], run one of three major U.S. research projects on the species. Their work combines ecology, genetics, [[Immunology]], and anatomy, and it is supported by a [[National Science Foundation]] grant. The researchers aim to determine whether the species began as a hybrid, what role the male plays in reproduction, and what causes differences between clones. The source also argues that the species is valuable as a vertebrate model for heredity and tissue compatibility.

## Background & Description

- **Unusual reproduction:** No male [[Amazon Molly]]s exist. Females mate with males of several species, including guppies, mosquitofish, platyfish, sailfin mollies, and [[Liberty Molly]]s, but the offspring retain the mother's heredity.
- **Discovery:** The species was recognized only in the 1930s, when a biologist found that Rio Grande collections contained two kinds of females and only one kind of male. Research on the species is described as comparatively recent.
- **Hybrid hypothesis:** Anatomically, the Amazon molly lies roughly halfway between the [[Sailfin Molly]] and the Liberty molly. Researchers suspect it originated as a hybrid of these two species and survived, which would be unusual. Laboratory crosses of Liberty and sailfin mollies produce both males and females, so they do not reproduce the Amazon molly.
- **Comparison with other animals:** The source notes a beetle in which sperm activates reproduction but contributes nothing to heredity. The source stresses that beetles are not vertebrates, so the Amazon molly is more relevant to human biology.
- **Origin of the project:** Darnell first encountered the species during doctoral dissertation research in a Mexican river. Abramoff introduced him to immunological techniques, which he judged useful for studying the species. The project combines anatomy, genetics, and immunology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic range:** The species occurs in three rivers from the Texas-Mexico border southward: the [[Rio Grande]], the [[Rio Tamesi]], and the [[Rio Soto La Marina]].
- **Clonal structure:** The Rio Grande contains about 11 known clones, each a group of identical relatives. Darnell and Abramoff are investigating whether the southern rivers contain different clones and whether Rio Grande clones recur there.
- **Environmental influence on offspring:** Offspring from a clone found in Liberty-inhabited waters appear somewhat more like Liberty mollies, while those in sailfin waters resemble sailfins. The Amazon molly usually mates with the Liberty in Mexican rivers and with the sailfin in the Rio Grande.
- **Field timing:** Collecting trips to Mexico occur each spring because the species is [[Live-Bearing Fish]], so females are often pregnant. March and April bring less danger from crocodiles and snakes, and rivers have not yet risen with spring floods that would scatter the fish.
- **Field methods:** Fish are caught with nets, kept alive in plastic bags, and sorted at motel-room laboratories. Some are bled for blood samples, some are preserved for anatomical study, and others are shipped live to the Marquette aquarium for [[Tissue Transplantation]] experiments.

## Key Findings

- **Heredity is controlled by the female:** Darnell reports that the female controls heredity "apparently completely or almost completely." A working hypothesis is that offspring are identical regardless of the father's species.
- **Clonal differences remain unexplained:** Offspring within a clone are essentially identical, yet clones differ from one another. Two explanations are under study:
  - A simple [[Mutation]] of genes that altered heredity at some point.
  - Occasional transfer of whole pieces of male chromosomes during reproduction. Darnell leans toward this view but states that neither theory has been proven.
- **Transplantation as a test:** Tissues from identical twins are interchangeable, so a successful graft indicates that donor and host are essentially identical. A fin graft from a [[Black Molly]] was rejected by its host, as shown in the source illustration. Rejection usually occurs within a few weeks, while confirming a successful graft takes longer.
- **Immunology adds sensitivity:** Anatomy and grafts suggest the fish are essentially identical. Immunological analysis of blood samples, led by Abramoff, is intended to detect subtler differences that traditional methods miss.
- **Research goals:** The team hopes to determine:
  - Whether the Amazon molly originated as a hybrid.
  - What role the male plays in reproduction.
  - The origin of clonal differences.
  - The basis of tissue and organ compatibility or incompatibility in transplants.
- **Scientific value:** Because the species is a vertebrate, the researchers argue it allows study of heredity in an animal "not too distant from us." Darnell also notes that natural accidents or breakdowns can reveal processes that normal function does not.
- **Related species mentioned:** Other mollies and relatives cited in breeding trials include the [[Guppy]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Platyfish]]. Their hybrid relationships with the Amazon molly remain part of the investigation.
