# Life History of the Blue Rockfish (Sebastodes mystinus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sebastodes

## Executive Summary

This study by J. H. Wales, prepared at the [[Hopkins Marine Station]] of Stanford University, outlines the life history of the [[Blue Rockfish]] (*Sebastodes mystinus*) in [[Monterey Bay]], California. Work began in summer 1929 and included a year of regular sampling from June 1930. The author used [[Scale Analysis]] as the main method of age determination, supported by [[Length Frequency Analysis]] and a [[Tagging Study]]. Environmental data came from the [[Skogsberg Hydrography]] survey.

The author concludes that scale features are a difficult and unreliable basis for aging this species. The likely cause is the irregular [[Shallow Water Temperature]] regime, in contrast with the seasonal rhythm of deeper-water rockfish. Age determination was feasible only for the first four year classes, and only with length-frequency support. The paper also reports maturity, spawning season, and fecundity.

## Background & Description

The blue rockfish, also called bluefish or blue rockcod, is a member of the genus *Sebastodes*. Wales notes that it is less commercially important than many congeners, but it was easy to obtain in sufficient numbers for a life-history study. The author chose it as a preliminary step toward a wider survey of the genus. The common name "blue rockfish" replaced the department's former official name "priestfish" because it better matches popular usage.

Specimens were taken mainly by hook and line from a skiff off the rocks at Hopkins Marine Station, on Mussel Point, about 1.2 miles west of Monterey. Net and multi-hook rockcod lines performed poorly. The most successful gear was a short pole with a line of equal length and one or two hooks, baited with squid, blue rockfish, sefiorita (*Oxyjulis*), or sardine. The author notes this method was time-consuming and probably selective, so the catch was largely of one year class. Some commercial specimens from Monterey fish markets were also included.

Sampling and tagging totals:
- 280 fish tagged in September 1929.
- 770 fish tagged in summer and fall 1930 (1,050 total).
- 17 recaptures (1.6 percent) in total.

Fish were measured by fork length, scale-sampled, and tagged with non-corrosive metal fish tags on the operculum. Scale readability was about 98 percent for younger age classes, but many scales from older market fish could not be read.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study rests on a contrast between deep and surface waters in Monterey Bay. [[Skogsberg Hydrography]] (1936) showed that deep water temperatures follow a distinct annual rhythm. Surface temperatures, by contrast, fluctuate irregularly, and show less similarity between years than deep temperatures do (Figure 2 of the source). Because the blue rockfish is a shallow-water species, Wales argues that its growth mirrors this irregular environment. This would explain the marked differences in scale features and growth among year classes.

Wales compares this with commercially important congeners that live in deeper water, whose otoliths show well-defined rings. He argues that if bony structures record a metabolic rhythm, then the differing behavior of these structures points to temperature as the fundamental factor driving that rhythm. The author also notes that no season is characterized by food scarcity in the Monterey region.

Habitat and behavior:
- Fish of all ages were found in 30 to 40 feet of water.
- Individuals from about two years old usually occur deeper, sometimes at the surface and sometimes down to 50 fathoms.
- The species is gregarious but does not form compact schools.
- Juveniles appear in tidepools in May and July, but scarcity there suggests most young are in the rocky subtidal zone, where hook-and-line capture is difficult.
- Diet includes small crustaceans, crabs, and fine red and green algae. Young and adults appear to eat similar food.

## Key Findings

### Growth and tagging
- Average monthly growth of tagged fish was 2.46 mm. Assuming uniform seasonal growth, this equals about 29.52 mm (1.5 inches) per year for the first three year classes.
- Scale circuli increased by 4.34 per month, or about 52.08 per year. Per-class monthly increases were 5.7 (class 1+), 4 (class 2+), and 4.9 (class 3+). Wales considers the class 3+ value probably unrepresentative.
- Of 1,050 tagged fish, 17 were recaptured. Seven from 1929 were retaken 1 to 9 months and 25 days after release. Ten from 1930 were retaken within five months. Wales judged the recapture sample too small and too varied for strong conclusions, but considered it useful as a check on the scale and length data.
- No definite movement information was obtained from tags.

### Age classes and scales
- Seven year classes were examined. The first four (0+ to 3+) could be recognized with confidence.
- The smallest group, class 0+, was estimated at slightly over six months old and probably derived from the previous spawning season.
- The dominant group in 1930 (modal length 135 mm, range 113 to 148 mm) was class 1+.
- Classes 2+ and 3+ were assigned by scale ring counts (95 and 150 rings, at 175 mm and 200 mm respectively in June 1930).
- Age determination beyond class 3+ was not possible because annuli were often absent or poorly defined.
- The normal pattern was one annulus per year, but scales sometimes showed three rings and sometimes none. Annuli could be single or paired, and secondary rings sometimes confused aging.
- The causes of annulus formation were not determined.
- In class 1+ scales examined in June 1931, 31 percent had previously formed annuli. Annulus formation appeared to occur in March, April, and May.
- No differences were found between male and female scales.

### Population dynamics
- Class 1+ remained the most abundant group for 13 months of regular sampling.
- In April 1931, a younger class appeared in small numbers. It was much less numerous than the same-age class had been a year earlier, which Wales takes as evidence of strongly variable mortality among year classes.

### Maturity and reproduction
- Males produce sperm at about three years. Most males are probably mature by age four. Males are mature in the third and fourth years, and females in the fourth and fifth.
- Females probably do not mature before four years, and usually not before five.
- Spawning extended through November, December, and January. Running sperm was first seen in late October.
- Fertilization is internal. Copulation was not observed in this or any other *Sebastodes* species.
- Oocyte diameters ranged from 0.03 to 0.24 mm, and ripe eggs were about 0.35 mm.
- A 405 mm female taken 28 November 1930 contained an estimated 524,000 embryos. Embryo development was described in stages, from colorless to pale yellow oil globules through hatching.
- The planktonic larval stage was not observed. The youngest fish seen were about five months old.

### Fishery and value
- The commercial catch consisted largely of fifth-, sixth-, and seventh-year fish, averaging about 300 mm.
- Commercial importance is limited because of the species' unattractive coloration. The flesh is white and of good flavor.
- Most catches at Monterey were by recreational fishers rather than for markets.

### Sampling summary (Table 2, 1930–31)
- Class 1+: 667 specimens.
- Class 2+: 70 specimens.
- Class 3+: 250 specimens.
- A larger-fish group: 1 entry, with a footnote noting 111 specimens and a largest individual of 415 mm.

### Summary conclusions (author's numbered summary)
1. Fish were collected near Monterey, California.
2. Scale study and length frequencies were the main methods of age determination. Sampling limitations made length frequencies unreliable.
3. Circuli increase rate was a valuable supplement to periodic length measurements.
4. Tagging was a reasonably effective check on age estimates.
5. Scales of the first four classes were readable, and the paper's annulus criteria follow from this.
6. Scale annuli are unreliable for aging older fish, so age determination needs length-frequency analysis as well.
