# Persistence and Stability of Fish and Invertebrate Assemblages in a Repeatedly Disturbed Sonoran Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Gary K. Meffe]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] assesses the persistence and stability of two animal assemblages, fishes and benthic invertebrates, in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a flash-flood-prone Sonoran Desert stream in Arizona. Persistence (presence or absence of taxa, measured by a turnover-based index) and stability (constancy of taxon rankings, measured by [[Kendall's W]]) were both evaluated against a long record of natural floods. Fish assemblages were highly persistent over several decades and resisted even the most severe floods. Benthic invertebrates were decimated by frequent, intense flooding after July 1977 but recovered quickly. Absolute abundances varied by orders of magnitude, while the general structure of both assemblages stayed relatively constant.

## Background & Description

The authors frame their analysis around [[Connell and Sousa (1983)]] criteria for ecological stability. Persistence is the continued presence of species through potentially devastating events. Stability is the relative constancy of abundances, which may arise from resistance (unchanged relative abundances under disturbance) or resilience (rapid return to the prior state). The study addresses three questions:

1. Are assemblages persistent over the long term (several generations) and/or the short term (consecutive sample periods)?
2. Are assemblages stable, with relative numbers of individuals unchanged by disturbance?
3. If disturbed, are assemblages resilient, with decimated populations recovering to former levels?

The authors treat the fish and invertebrate data sets as independent. They note that differing sampling protocols and generation times preclude direct comparison between the two groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** Aravaipa Creek is an unregulated desert stream with a native fish fauna of five minnows (Cyprinidae) and two suckers (Catostomidae). The stream has been studied since the 1940s and has been largely undisturbed by people, which makes it a rare near-pristine system in the American Southwest. It has three physiographic reaches: an upper reach of about 10 km, a 17 km deep canyon, and a lower reach of about 14 km that sinks into the stream bed. Riparian vegetation includes cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*) and willow (*Salix gooddingii*, *S. exigua*) gallery forests.

**Flood regime.** Flash floods are rapid in onset and short in duration. Summer modal discharge is 0.14–0.28 m³/s, while recorded maximum instantaneous discharges since 1920 reach 566 and 1,982 m³/s, thousands of times the modal flow. Between 1966 and 1981 there were at least 10 summer floods with daily mean flows above 30 m³/s and seven winter floods above 100 m³/s. About 50% of annual flow passes in 6% of the time, and 25% in less than 1% of the time. Floods can raise the water surface more than 10 m in the canyon, uproot gallery forests, and remobilize bedload.

**Sampling.** Fish data cover up to 14 sampling years between 1943 and 1979, compiled from percentage occurrences, since count data were not available for all years. Seines (3.2–6.4 mm mesh) were used before 1974, and electrofishing of blocked segments from 1974 to 1979. Invertebrates were collected with a Surber-type sampler (929 cm², 228 µm mesh) at 1–4 month intervals. The study covered 26 collections over 40 months (January 1975 to April 1978), with observations extending to December 1978. The sampling period spans several generations, which the authors note is required to avoid mistaking long-lived individuals for stability.

## Key Findings

**Fish persistence and stability**

- Seven native fish species persisted. No species were gained or lost over the long-term record (1943 through 1984), and fish persistence was above 0.9 in all reaches.
- Persistence index (PR) values: upper reach 0.965, lower reach 0.908; the canyon value is only partly legible in the source, but the text reports all reaches above 0.9, decreasing from upper to lower sections.
- The three most common species (longfin dace, desert mountain sucker, and Sonoran sucker) were present in every collection in every reach. Roundtail chub was occasionally absent from the upper reach.
- Kendall's W for fish rankings was significant in all sections (upper W = 0.634; canyon W = 0.705; lower W = 0.831; all p < .001), indicating stable relative rankings.
- Fish populations resisted even the most severe flood on record, in October 1983, when instantaneous discharge peaked at 1,982.2 m³/s and more than 50% of the riparian gallery forest was destroyed. Samples taken about two weeks before and after the flood were qualitatively similar, and all seven species persisted in roughly the same proportions.
- Fish population fluctuations are partly attributed to intrastream movement and habitat selection. For example, Pantosteus clarki remained most abundant in the canyon, and Agosia chrysogaster dominated the lower reach.

**Benthic invertebrate persistence and stability**

- Of the taxa analyzed, only Chironomidae were present in all 26 samples. Other taxa underwent repeated local extirpation and recolonization.
- Persistence index values: January 1975 to April 1978, 0.852; before July 1977, 0.926; after June 1977, 0.697. Persistence was high until the period of frequent, intense flooding.
- Kendall's W for invertebrate rankings (12 most abundant taxa of 52) was significant for all 26 samples combined (W = 0.461, p < .001) and within seasons: winter (W = 0.632, p < .005), summer (W = 0.775, p < .01), and autumn (W = 0.709, p < .025). Spring was also significant at p < .01.
- The 12 taxa made up 94.7% of 166,508 individuals collected.
- Absolute abundances fluctuated greatly. Single spates reduced invertebrate numbers by as much as 99% in sampled habitats, yet rankings generally returned to preflood states.
- Life history traits support rapid recovery: generation times of 14–21 days for several genera, year-round reproduction, rare diapause, and aerial adults present at all times. Even so, much of the assemblage was damaged by severe flooding; by late in the study, most taxa except baetid mayflies, plecopterans, and some chironomids were absent.

**Resilience and comparisons**

- Invertebrates were classed as semipersistent and nonresistant, but resilient. Taxa reduced or eliminated by floods generally reappeared at former densities.
- Similar invertebrate recovery was reported for [[Sycamore Creek]], Arizona, where numbers fell by an average of 86% in floods and returned to preflood levels within 14 days if not further disturbed.
- In [[Ozark Stream]] (Arkansas), following the worst catastrophic flood of the century, the fish assemblage recovered to a near-preflood condition within 8 months.
- Fish persistence in an [[Indiana Stream]] assemblage studied by [[Grossman et al. (1982)]] was comparable to Aravaipa Creek, with PR values of 0.837 (spring), 0.947 (summer), and 0.937 (autumn).

**Methodological conclusions**

- The authors argue that a single analytical tool (such as Kendall's W) or a single framework (an equilibrium versus nonequilibrium dichotomy) is insufficient. A strict concordance analysis would have indicated stable assemblages unaffected by flooding, which misses the major invertebrate declines late in the study.
- Invertebrate conclusions are provisional, because most taxa were identified only to family or genus.
- All persistence values are minimum estimates, since taxa may have been present but undetected in a given sample.
