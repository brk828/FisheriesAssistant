# Factors Affecting the Success of Gila Topminnow Introductions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This first annual report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], monitors introductions of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] onto [[USDA Forest Service]] lands in Arizona. In 1982, 64 sites on four National Forests were stocked under a tripartite [[Memorandum Of Understanding]]. Monitoring in June and July 1983 classified 31 sites (48.4%) as successful, 27 (42.2%) as unsuccessful, and 6 (9.4%) as unknown. Successful introductions were associated with smaller [[Drainage Area]], lower [[Elevation]], higher [[Water Temperature]], and particular [[Cover Type]] conditions. A [[Discriminant Analysis]] built on four variables correctly classified 80% of sites. The authors recommend revised [[Introduction Site Selection Criteria]] for future stocking.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small, live-bearing poeciliid that is omnivorous and feeds heavily on detritus. It is listed as endangered. Historically it occupied almost all kinds of aquatic habitat in southern Arizona (Minckley 1973, as cited). Its recovery depends on establishing new populations, which led to this monitoring effort.

Stocking stock came from the [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]] pond in Pinal County, which originated from Monkey Springs in Santa Cruz County. Some fish were transplanted to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. Twenty-four additional introductions were made in June 1983. These were excluded from the analyses.

Site selection criteria were developed by Forest Service biologists for non-fishery field staff. They focused on abiotic factors such as size, flow, depth, perennial water, temperature, elevation, flooding, barriers and access. Only one biological criterion was included: presence of predators or competitors.

The report also includes a [[Gila Topminnow Survey Form]] in its appendix, used to record habitat, water quality, substrate, cover and fish collection data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Topminnow habitat is highly variable. Meffe et al. (1983), as cited in the report, found extant populations in a wide range of conditions and suggested that present habitats represent only a minimum of the historical range. Aquatic vegetation, including algal mats, is a common component of topminnow habitat.

Key threats and causes of failure include:

- **[[Flooding]]:** The main cause of failure in larger stream habitats with large drainage areas. Floods caused scouring, debris piling and bank instability.
- **[[Desiccation]]:** Most dried completely, while others declined through summer water loss.
- **Water development:** Vegetation removal, deepening and similar alterations.
- **Livestock trampling:** Damage during low water periods.
- **Predatory non-native fishes:** Mainly a problem in ponds. The [[Western Mosquitofish]] is a competitor with topminnow in the region.

Cienega Creek, a relatively large drainage with frequent flash floods, supports a natural population. Collins et al. (1981) documented the loss of an introduced population in [[Tule Creek]] to flooding ten years after an apparently successful introduction.

## Key Findings

### Introduction Outcomes

- 64 sites were stocked in 1982; 58 were monitored.
- 31 sites (48.4%) were successful, 27 (42.2%) unsuccessful, and 6 (9.4%) unevaluated.
- For the 50 sites used in analysis, 30 were successful (60.0%).
- Unevaluated sites included several tanks and springs. One site was restocked in 1983 after the original reach proved non-perennial.
- Population size was visually estimated only for successful introductions.

### Success by Habitat Type ([[Habitat Type]])

| Habitat Type | Sites | Successful | Success Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pond (spring or runoff fed tanks) | 17 | 12 | 70.6% |
| Artesian well | 3 | 3 | 100% |
| Spring stream | 20 | 13 | 65.0% |
| Stream | 9 | 2 | 22.2% |

Spring-fed ponds and streams had the highest success. Streams had the lowest, likely due to flooding risk. No trough sites were stocked.

### Success by Cover Type ([[Cover Type]])

- Aquatic plants: 23 of 32 sites successful (71.8%).
- Terrestrial vegetation: 1 of 2 (50.0%).
- Abiotic cover: 4 of 9 (44.4%).
- No cover: 1 of 7 (14.3%).

Aquatic vegetation was an important habitat feature for successful introductions.

### Success by Substrate Type ([[Substrate Type]])

- Silt: 15 of 23 sites successful. Silt was the most common substrate, but its share of successful sites did not differ significantly from its share of all sites.
- Sand and bedrock had success rates of about 60%. Gravel had a rate of about 50%.
- Both concrete (stock trough) and metal (above-ground tank) sites failed.

### Physical and Chemical Comparisons

Successful and unsuccessful sites were compared using t-tests.

| Variable | Successful | Unsuccessful | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Drainage Area]] | 0.8 ± 0.5 km² (trace to 6.1) | 5.3 ± 1.8 km² (trace to 17.5) | Significant |
| [[Elevation]] | 970 ± 45 m (599–1465) | 1071 ± 60 m (587–1682) | p < 0.001 |
| [[Dissolved Oxygen]] | 8.4 ± 0.6 ppm (6.4–10.0) | 7.0 ± 0.5 ppm (0.8–10.8) | Not significant |
| [[Water Temperature]] | 24.1 ± 0.7 °C (17.0–33.0) | 21.0 ± 0.9 °C (13.0–27.0) | p < 0.001 |
| Conductivity | 514 ± 39 µmho/cm (112–1000) | 465 ± 58 µmho/cm (75–1090) | Not significant |

- pH did not differ significantly between groups.
- Thirteen successful and four unsuccessful sites had zero drainage area (artesian wells, windmill-fed ponds, perched springs) and were excluded from drainage computations.
- Drainage area was the strongest discriminator. The authors caution against using it alone to judge site suitability.

### Discriminant Analysis

- A stepwise analysis selected four variables: drainage area, elevation, cover type and dissolved oxygen.
- A single function separated the groups (canonical correlation 0.73).
- Overall classification accuracy was 80%: 26 of 30 successful sites and 14 of 20 unsuccessful sites were correctly classified.
- Classification functions were derived for each group. Each site is assigned to the group with the higher score.
- The authors note that dissolved oxygen was a single measurement and may be unreliable, and that the function needs validation on future sites.

### Reasons for Failure

Flooding was the main cause of failure in larger streams. Desiccation, water development, livestock trampling and predatory non-native fishes (mainly in ponds) also contributed.

### Recommended Site Selection Criteria

Revised criteria in Table IX include:

- Drainage area, elevation, stream flow and gradient
- Pond surface area and depth
- Channelization (little or none preferred)
- Habitat composition (complex and heterogeneous)
- Cover (aquatic vegetation present)
- Other fishes (native and nonpredatory only)
- Water quality (meets state surface water standards)
- Water source (perennial, with snails such as *Physella*, *Planorbella* or hydrobiids present as indicators)
- Developmental potential (low or none)
- Location (within the [[Gila River Drainage]])

### Recommendations

1. Use the Table IX criteria to identify new sites and reassess unsuccessful ones.
2. Continue annual monitoring, collecting quantitative habitat and fish population data.
3. Monitor all other introduced and natural topminnow populations using the survey form.
4. Evaluate unsuccessful and potential sites against the criteria.
5. Stock or restock approved sites that meet the criteria.
6. Do not stock sites that do not contribute to recovery under the approved recovery plan.

### Data Notes

The source text contains minor inconsistencies. Site totals are given as 31 successful in the results but 30 in the analysis. The text says 2 of 7 no-cover sites held topminnow, while Table V shows 1. Values reported here follow the tables where text and tables differ.
