# Paleocene to Holocene Paleodeltas of the Ancestral Colorado River Offset by the San Andreas Fault System, Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-paleogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1996 *Geology* paper by Jeffrey L. Howard (Department of Geology, Wayne State University) reconstructs the early history of the [[Colorado River]] using fluvial-deltaic rock records from the [[Los Angeles Basin]], the [[Ventura Basin]], and the [[Salton Trough]]. The author proposes that a Colorado paleoriver drained into the Los Angeles basin during much of the Cenozoic, before roughly 300 km of dextral displacement on the [[San Andreas Fault]] moved those deposits far to the northwest. Late Miocene fault movement and rifting in the Salton Trough then rerouted the river into the proto-[[Gulf of California]], and Pliocene to Holocene deltaic filling migrated southeastward along the fault. The paper is a geological reconstruction; it contains no fish or biological data.

## Background & Description

- **Research question:** There is no agreement on the early history of the Colorado River, partly because the origin of some sedimentary units is disputed and pre-late Miocene rocks are rarely exposed upstream of the San Andreas fault.
- **Approach:** Chronostratigraphic and palinspastic review, plus new paleogeographic reconstructions built on the hypothesis that a well-developed Cenozoic succession of Colorado paleoriver deposits lies west of the San Andreas fault.
- **Terminology:** "Paleoriver" denotes an ancestral drainage that is genetically related to, but not necessarily the same shape as, a modern river.
- **Relevance to theory:** The model constrains the magnitude of regional Cenozoic displacement and bears on the [[Suspect Terrane Theory]] (Gastil, 1991).
- **Key stratigraphic units:** [[Sespe Formation]], [[Topanga Formation]], [[Puente Formation]], [[Imperial Formation]], [[Bouse Formation]], and the [[Gypsum Canyon Quarry]] conglomerate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses paleo-drainage geography, sediment provenance and estuarine facies rather than living organisms. Environmental context includes:

- **Freshwater-marine estuary:** Strontium isotope data suggest the basal [[Bouse Formation]] limestone formed in a freshwater environment, while contemporaneous [[Imperial Formation]] rocks were marine. The author interprets this as a salt-wedge (freshwater) estuary.
- **Volcanism and lava dams:** Volcanism began in the Salton Trough about 35 Ma and was widespread in southwestern Arizona between 26 and 20 Ma. Lava dams may have ponded the ancestral drainage, and their breaching produced torrential floods (clasts up to 2 m reported at Gypsum Canyon).
- **Basin and Range extension:** Late Miocene block faulting (rifting beginning about 8 Ma with development of [[Death Valley]]) buried basin-axis deposits in grabens and disrupted the [[Amargosa Paleoriver]].
- **Sea-level influence:** A mid-Oligocene unconformity in the Sespe Formation is probably of eustatic origin, and the author suggests the resulting base-level drop may explain an upper Paleogene regional unconformity in Arizona.

## Key Findings

1. **Upper Paleocene to middle Miocene fluvial-deltaic rocks** in the Los Angeles and Ventura basins were deposited by a Colorado paleoriver before about 300 km of dextral displacement on the San Andreas fault.
2. **Provenance evidence:** Eocene conglomerates contain far-traveled metavolcanic, quartzitic and granitoid clasts, including rhyolitic clasts linked to Mesozoic magmatic arc sources in southern Arizona. Cretaceous to lower Paleocene conglomerates were locally sourced.
3. **Gypsum Canyon facies:** The author reinterprets this boulder conglomerate as channel-axis material deposited by the Colorado paleoriver during Topanga time. The absence of host-rock (Sespe) clasts indicates a first-cycle origin. The deposit marks the last known position of the paleoriver before its diversion from the Los Angeles basin.
4. **Late Miocene displacement:** About 60 km of right slip occurred on the San Gabriel–San Andreas fault between 12 and 6 Ma. This displaced the paleoriver southeastward toward the embryonic Salton Trough.
5. **Timing of diversion:** An upsection change to local provenance in the [[Puente Formation]], dated at about 9 Ma, may record the river's departure. Volcanic rocks of 9–10 Ma associated with the Imperial Formation constrain its arrival at the head of the Salton Trough.
6. **Stream capture:** The Imperial Formation represents a Pacific embayment into a proto-Gulf of California, which is inferred to have captured the Colorado paleoriver. The author suggests late Miocene offset created the valley that became San Gorgonio Pass.
7. **Pliocene to Holocene filling:** Incorporating 240 km of right slip, the model proposes the Salton Trough filled progressively southeastward as a migrating deltaic depocenter, with the early Pliocene river located in the Coachella Valley. Pliocene Imperial Formation deposits are generally of Colorado River origin.
8. **Amargosa–Colorado paleoriver:** Pliocene [[Bouse Formation]] strata, a prograding paleodelta, outline a drowned estuarine embayment. The author proposes that lowering of the headwaters from about 8 Ma, driven by Death Valley rifting, defeated the Amargosa paleoriver. Headward erosion then created the modern river and [[Grand Canyon]].
9. **Gila–Colorado paleoriver:** The Gila paleoriver may have developed during the Eocene, with a pre-late Miocene origin indicated by its antecedence to about 13 Ma Basin and Range faults. Gila gravels beneath the 3 Ma Sentinel Basalt suggest an integrated drainage by the early Pliocene.
10. **Tapeats-like clasts:** Quartzite clasts in Eocene Los Angeles conglomerates are tentatively matched to the Tapeats Sandstone. Their source is inferred to be miogeoclinal rocks in the Mojave Desert, not the ancestral Grand Canyon, which had eroded only to the Supai Formation before the middle Miocene.
11. **Reinterpreting "missing" deltas:** The paper argues that exposed fanglomerates prove nothing about a throughgoing river, since basin-axis deposits lie in the subsurface. Overlap of younger far-traveled gravels can create the illusion of initial deposits of a through-flowing drainage.
12. **Regional consistency:** Well-integrated Eocene paleodrainage in the Colorado Plateau and Oligocene to middle Miocene fluvial gravels in the Transition Zone are at least broadly consistent with the model. The author states that little direct evidence for the paleoriver exists east of the San Andreas fault.
13. **Significance:** If correct, the Los Angeles basin holds an important sedimentary record of Cenozoic tectonic unrooting in the Basin and Range region.

**Limitations noted by the author:** The model relies on indirect evidence, and the volume of sediment produced by lava-dam breaching versus base-level change is unknown.
