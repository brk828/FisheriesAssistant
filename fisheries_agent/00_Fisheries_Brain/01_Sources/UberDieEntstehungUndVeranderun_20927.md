# Origin and Alteration of Color Patterns in the Genus Xiphophorus (Pisces): I. Qualitative Changes After Interspecific Crossing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This genetics study by [[Claus Dieter Zander]] examines how pigment-pattern genes of [[Xiphophorus]] livebearers change their expression when moved into other species or subspecies through hybridization. Zander crossed nine recognized Xiphophorus taxa, producing nearly all 72 possible hybrid combinations, and tested twelve [[Macromelanophore Genes]] and eight [[Pterinophores|Pterinophore]] genes. Expression was either intensified (up to tumor formation) or weakened (up to complete loss). The author argues these changes are best explained by [[Polygenic Modifiers]] that are specific to each color gene, not by monogenic [[Repression Genes]] as proposed by [[Anders Hypothesis|Anders]] and [[Brewer Hypothesis|Brewer]]. The paper also proposes a two-type modifier model and discusses how color patterns and their modifier systems may have evolved.

## Background & Description

The Xiphophorus pigment studies began with [[Bellamy]] (1922) and were continued by [[Gordon]] and [[Kosswig]]. The nine known species, some split into populations or subspecies, can be crossed, and their F1 hybrids are mostly fertile, allowing further backcross generations. Artificial insemination was used where natural mating failed.

Two pigment cell types build the patterns: black [[Melanophores]] and [[Pterinophores]] (xanthoerythrophores and their precursors). Black spots are mostly controlled by genes on the sex chromosomes that are allelic or pseudoallelic. Only *X. helleri* and *X. montezumae* carry autosomal melanophore genes. Yellow, orange and red patterns depend on pterinophore genes, usually tightly linked to a melanophore gene. Melanophore genes in the platys are named for their pattern (e.g., Sp = spotted, Sd = spotted dorsal, Sr = striped, N = nigra), and the paper retains the traditional nomenclature rather than the new names proposed by Kallman and Atz (1967).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The studied populations come from freshwater habitats in eastern Mexico and Central America. Source localities include the Laguna St. Tecla and Rio St. Catarina (Rio Grande system, Coahuila and Nuevo Leon), the Rio Purification and Rio Soto la Marina (Tamaulipas), the Rio Sabinas and Rio Choy, Rio Coy and Rio Axtla ([[Rio Panuco Basin]], San Luis Potosi and Tamaulipas), the Rio Jamapa and Catemaco Lake (Veracruz), the Belize River, and the Rio Lancetilla (Honduras). Zander's 1967 biogeographic work places the evolutionary origin of the genus in the Rio Panuco basin. The paper treats the [[Platy]] group (gordoni, couchianus, xiphidium, variatus, maculatus, milleri) and the [[Swordtail]] group (helleri, montezumae, pygmaeus) as the main taxonomic division, and it links modifier systems to this division.

## Key Findings

**Species and genes surveyed**
- Nine Xiphophorus taxa were studied, including subspecies and populations.
- Pattern expression was recorded in F1 and, in some cases, F2 and backcross (F1R, F2R) hybrids. Only animals at least one year old were compared.
- Twelve macromelanophore genes and eight pterinophore genes were analyzed. Of the eight pterinophore genes, three belong to *X. maculatus*, two to *X. p. nigrensis*, and one each to *X. m. montezumae* and *X. m. cortezi*.
- *X. gordoni* and *X. couchianus* carry no tested macromelanophore or pterinophore genes.

**Changes after crossing**
- Gene expression changed in most crosses. Changes ranged from intensification to tumor formation, or from weakening to complete suppression.
- *X. maculatus* genes: N is intensified in *X. helleri* hybrids, but it is absent in *X. montezumae* and *X. pygmaeus* hybrids. Sd and Dr are linked, but they do not always change together. Sr and Ar also change independently in most crosses.
- *X. milleri* Sr: intensified in most F1 crosses, reduced with *X. maculatus* and *X. variatus*. Linkage analysis with *X. xiphidium* showed that this gene lies on a chromosome homologous to the platy Y chromosome, and it is probably an allele of the other platy macromelanophore genes. Table 2 (one backcross family of 98 offspring) split cleanly into Sp and Sr classes (62 and 36), with no animals carrying both genes or neither.
- *X. variatus* P: largely unchanged in foreign genomes, making it the most stable gene reported.
- *X. helleri* intensifies most foreign genes. *X. maculatus* weakens most: only two pterinophore genes of *X. p. nigrensis* were intensified, while eleven other genes were weakened.
- The linked Sc–Dfl genes of *X. m. cortezi* and the Sr–Rl genes of *X. m. montezumae* behave like the other linked pairs. Table 3 (one family of 98 offspring) split into two classes (50 and 48).

**Modifier systems**
- Repression-gene hypotheses (Brewer 1952; Anders and Kallman 1965; Anders 1967) do not explain the data. The paper's six arguments include variable expression within one cross, and the lack of intensification in some backcrosses.
- Zander proposes two modifier types for pterinophores: *intensifiers* (which increase the number of pterinophores) and *transformers* (which convert yellow G-xanthophores to red xanthoerythrophores). Transformers are present only in platys and *X. helleri*. *X. pygmaeus* and *X. montezumae* lack them and reduce foreign red patterns to yellow.
- Modifier action is region-specific. The gene sets the pattern center, and modifiers present in that region determine expression.

**Free amino acids**
- Anders and Kallman (1965) linked free amino acid levels to the number of intensifying modifiers. Zander reports exceptions: *X. xiphidium* has the lowest free amino acid content, and *X. m. cortezi* has the highest, exceeding *X. helleri*. Anders (1967) reported 11.4 percent exception results.

**Evolutionary conclusions**
- Modifier systems are species-specific and diverged alongside morphological differentiation.
- Pattern genes may share a common ancestral gene, and the paper proposes a balanced polymorphism. Excess intensification by mutation could cause melanosis, so matching of color genes and modifiers is required.
- The evolution of pterinophore transformers and of the Sd and N modifiers agrees with the platy versus swordtail division.
- The author concludes that *X. helleri* gained specific modifiers during its evolution, which is more plausible than assuming *X. maculatus* acquired repression genes.
