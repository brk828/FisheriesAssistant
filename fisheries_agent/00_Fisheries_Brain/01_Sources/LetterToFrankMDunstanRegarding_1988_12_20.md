## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from Dean A. Hendrickson (Arizona Game & Fish Department) to Frank M. Dunstan of the National Audubon Society addresses a management dispute concerning the population of pupfish inhabiting **Finley Tank** on the Audubon Society's Research Ranch. The core issue is the uncertain genetic provenance of the tank's [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] stock, which management agencies argue may have been genetically contaminated through undocumented historical mixing in captive/aquaria stocks. The letter advocates for removing the potentially non-pure pupfish from Finley Tank and replacing them with the federally endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) as part of a broader recovery strategy under the 1973 Endangered Species Act. The document is notable for its explicit discussion of subspecies taxonomy, genetic sampling limitations, and differing recovery strategies for geographically restricted versus widely extirpated taxa.

## Background & Description
- No new morphological data are presented; rather, the letter explicitly **cautions against relying solely on morphological or biochemical comparisons** to establish stock purity: "regardless of how the fish in Finley Tank stack up in a series of morphological or biochemical tests when compared to Quitobaquito animals, we just do not know what may have transpired in the original stock aquaria."
- Two recognized subspecies are referenced taxonomically:
  - *Cyprinodon macularius eremus* — [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]], endemic to Quitobaquito Springs.
  - *Cyprinodon macularius* (nominal desert form) — [[Desert Pupfish]], historically widespread in the lower Colorado River basin, including the [[San Pedro River]].
- The letter raises the taxonomic question of subspecies validity itself, noting that "purely academic opinions regarding the reality of subspecies tend to trivialize differences" — implying morphological/taxonomic boundaries may be debated even among specialists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Finley Tank**: An artificial impoundment on the National Audubon Society's Research Ranch, currently stocked with pupfish of uncertain lineage (labeled non-native/possibly hybridized [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] stock).
- **Quitobaquito Springs**: Sole native habitat of *C. m. eremus*, located in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument; described as currently "stable, with no imminent threats of destruction." No evidence this subspecies ever occurred outside this single spring system.
- **Desert Pupfish habitat context**: Historically distributed across the lower Colorado River basin; extirpated from over 90% of former range, now surviving only in a handful of peripheral sites.
- **San Pedro River**: Cited as the historic type locality for [[Desert Pupfish]], now extirpated from that system; the Research Ranch's proximity to the San Pedro is highlighted as an ideal reintroduction site.
- **Proposed additional reintroduction sites**: Impoundments in **Post Canyon** and **O'Donnell Canyon** on the Research Ranch are suggested as suitable alternative pupfish habitats.
- Contrast in recovery ecology:
  - *Quitobaquito pupfish* recovery strategy = habitat stability at the single natural site + captive backup stocks (no redistribution to new habitats).
  - *Desert pupfish* recovery strategy = active restocking into multiple stable sites within historic range, given extensive habitat loss.

## Key Findings
- **Genetic uncertainty drives management decisions**: Agencies (AGFD, USFWS, Desert Fishes Recovery Team) argue Finley Tank fish cannot be confirmed as pure *C. m. eremus* due to undocumented history in source aquaria stocks.
- **Asymmetric conservation value**: Because genetically "pure" captive stocks of Quitobaquito pupfish already exist and the wild population at Quitobaquito Springs is stable, the Finley Tank stock (of uncertain purity) is deemed "of little value at this time" relative to the recovery needs of the far more imperiled [[Desert Pupfish]].
- **Genetic sampling limitations discussed**: The letter presents a nuanced argument regarding allozyme and mitochondrial DNA analyses — noting that failure to detect "significant differences" between Finley Tank and Quitobaquito samples would **not** prove genetic purity, given known limitations of these techniques in capturing total genomic variation. Conversely, detection of unique alleles/haplotypes in Finley Tank fish **would** constitute strong evidence of contamination.
- **Legal/regulatory framework**: Actions are framed under obligations of the 1973 Endangered Species Act and its amendments, with USFWS Office of Endangered Species and the Desert Fishes Recovery Team acting as key advisory/regulatory stakeholders.
- **Divergent species-level recovery philosophies**: The letter articulates a clear conservation principle — recovery strategy must match the ecological/distributional status of the taxon (restricted endemic vs. widely extirpated species), rather than applying uniform stocking policies.
- **Proposed management action**: Renovation of Finley Tank — removal of non-native/questionable pupfish stock and replacement with genetically confirmed [[Desert Pupfish]] — is proposed as a flagship recovery action, with potential expansion to Post and O'Donnell canyon impoundments.
- **Stakeholder/ownership note**: Land ownership of Finley Tank (Mrs. Ariel Appleton) is identified as a key factor requiring approval before any renovation could proceed, alongside coordination with Research Ranch Scientific Directors Drs. Jane and Carl Bock.
- Discussion scheduled for the **Desert Fishes Recovery Team** meeting, January 26–27, 1989.
