# Endangered Woundfin and Water Management in the Virgin River, Utah, Arizona, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries journal article (Vol. 13, No. 1, January–February 1988) by James E. Deacon examines whether [[Woundfin]] and other endangered or native fishes can coexist with water development in the [[Virgin River]] basin. The [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] project was completed in 1985 after a non-jeopardy [[Biological Opinion]], but its flow requirements were repeatedly violated in summers 1985 and 1986. Water quality collapsed in 1985 when the river sank into sink holes, and native fish reproduction failed. Exotic [[Red Shiner]] came to dominate the Arizona–Nevada reach, and woundfin declined by more than 60% of their remaining range since 1983. The author concludes that compatible coexistence is possible but requires early, continuous endangered-species involvement in planning.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River drains about 15,000 km² across southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southern Nevada, and supplies water for environmental, irrigation, municipal, industrial, recreational, and limited hydropower uses. It is the only remaining natural habitat for the endangered woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), the proposed-endangered [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), and the rare [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*). Other native fishes include [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Most land is administered by the [[Bureau of Land Management]], Forest Service, or National Park Service; private land is under 10%.

Irrigation diversions and livestock grazing expanded during 1860–1910. Diversions at Quail Creek, Washington Fields, Mesquite, and Riverside can remove the entire low-season flow, though high flows remain relatively unrestricted and springs plus irrigation return flows sustain reduced reaches.

Several water projects were proposed in this period. The [[Allen-Warner Valley Energy System]] (coal-fired power plants, coal-slurry pipelines, and a multipurpose reservoir) was abandoned after the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service determined it would jeopardize woundfin. The planned [[Warner Valley Reservoir]] would have held about 67.9 million m³ (maximum 1,034 ha surface area per the text as printed, with an estimated yield of 40.2 million m³/year). Estimated construction costs were about $32 million for Warner Valley and about $20 million for Quail Creek, which exceeded its estimate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biological Opinion conditions (Quail Creek):** The 1982 [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] opinion found the project unlikely to jeopardize woundfin or the endangered hedgehog cactus. Key conditions included:
- Minimum flow of 2.58 m³/s (86 cfs) below La Verkin Power Plant unless natural flows were lower.
- Supplemental releases from Quail Creek Reservoir to keep flow at or above 2.85 m³/s at Washington Fields Diversion.
- Preserved flood frequency, with reduced high-flow volume.
- Slight water-quality effects, since diverted water would no longer dilute saline flows from [[Pah Tempe Springs]].
- A long-term woundfin study.

**Flow violations:** Flows at the Virgin gage were consistently below 3.54 m³/s in summers 1985–86. Flow was below the 2.58 m³/s requirement for 112 days (8 June–7 October 1985) and 108 days (2 June–22 September 1986), by the author's estimate.

**Water quality:** In late May or early June 1985 the entire flow disappeared into sink holes below the Hurricane/La Verkin Bench/Quail Creek diversion. Pah Tempe Springs discharge rose from about 0.3 m³/s to an estimated 0.85–1.2 m³/s. Conductivity increased across the Utah segment, temperature rose, and turbidity fell. Water quality over more than 50% of the best woundfin habitat did not meet the Environmental Assessment conditions. The sink holes were filled with rock in autumn–winter 1985, and conditions largely recovered in 1986.

**Table 1 (specific conductance, µmhos/cm):** Above La Verkin, conductance ranged 850–6,600 before 1985 versus 9,400–10,900 in June–December 1985. Other reaches showed similar elevations in 1985, with recovery in 1986.

**Red shiner invasion:** The exotic [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was first recorded in Utah in April 1984 below Washington Fields Diversion, likely after high flows in summer 1983 allowed upstream movement through the Virgin Gorge. It dominated the population by 1986 and is considered a probable vector for the Asian tapeworm in the Virgin River. Near Beaver Dam Wash it made up 65% and 86% of catches in 1984 and 1985, having been under 10% for most of its roughly 30-year presence. Prolonged low flow without flash floods (1984–85) favored its dominance.

## Key Findings

- **Quail Creek Reservoir** was completed in early 1985, but flow requirements were violated for 112 days in 1985 and 108 days in 1986.
- **Native fish reproduction** failed in the Utah segment in 1985. Mean sizes were the largest on record, and most species recovered somewhat in 1986.
- **Woundfin abundance** declined since 1983 across most of its range. Only the roughly 10 km Quail Creek–Washington Fields segment remained approximately stable.
- **Parasitism** in woundfin rose after 1985. Tapeworms were absent in 1985 samples above Quail Creek but common downstream, with a high infection incidence in October 1985 and February 1987.
- **Woundfin population size** has declined dramatically in more than 60% of its remaining range since 1983.
- **Cost comparison:** The author argues that virtually all water-quantity goals of the Warner Valley project could be met by Quail Creek at a much lower cost, and that the reduced debt reflects the woundfin-driven decision.
- **Management recommendations:** Endangered species must be integrated into all planning phases. Water quality and fish monitoring must be continuous. Flow requirements may need negotiation or litigation to be precisely defined. Each reservoir and diversion will likely alter native fish communities in unanticipated ways.
- **Compatibility:** Coexistence of endangered fishes, irrigated agriculture, municipal supply, and recreation in southwestern Utah is possible, but the author concludes the woundfin and roundtail chub face greater jeopardy than before Quail Creek was built, and that future conflicts require restraint, cooperation, and legislation.
