# Sustainability of Western Aquatic Ecosystems: Symposium Report to the Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This report, organized and edited by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] at Arizona State University (17 February 1997), compiles five independent reviews prepared for the Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission. Each review examines one component of western aquatic systems and concludes that these systems are degraded and must be repaired to become sustainable. The five components are physical landscapes and channels, riparian ecosystems, nutrients and primary productivity, aquatic food webs, and [[Native Fishes]]. The contributors describe a common pattern of dam construction, water diversion, groundwater pumping, grazing, mining, agriculture and non-native species introduction. They argue that these pressures have fragmented river systems, reduced biodiversity, and made current water use unsustainable in arid and semi-arid regions. The editor notes that the conclusions of all five contributions are unanimous and unequivocal.

## Background & Description

The symposium was based on the premise that information must precede informed decisions. Its authors approached the state of western surface waters through six interwoven categories: watersheds, channels, riparian zones, primary producers, secondary producers, and native fishes. Watersheds were treated within each of the other accounts rather than separately. Native fishes were included as a sensitive indicator of overall ecosystem health.

The report cites several laws as the policy framework. These include the [[Clean Water Act]] (objective: to "restore and maintain the chemical, physical, and biological integrity" of the nation's waters), the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1899, the [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]], and the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The authors argue that physical integrity has received far less attention than chemical and biological integrity, despite its legal standing.

The report notes that the West's hydroclimate is non-stationary. Shifts between [[El Niño and La Niña]] patterns and multi-decade hydroclimatic periods make extremes of flood and drought unavoidable. Engineers' assumption of [[Stationarity]] therefore fails to describe the system accurately.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical landscape and channels.** Human activity has changed western rivers over the last century and a half. Grazing increased runoff and erosion. A 20-year U.S. Geological Survey study in western Colorado found grazed watersheds produced about a third more runoff and, in some cases, twice as much erosion as ungrazed ones. Forest clearcutting increased water and sediment yield, and urban and off-road vehicle use also altered runoff. Beaver, once widespread, were essentially exterminated by about mid-nineteenth century, removing the small dams that had moderated flood peaks. The most extensive change came from large dams. By 1984, 70 structures with capacities above one million acre-feet had been built, storing more than 271 million acre-feet in western rivers. Channel narrowing downstream of dams, such as a roughly 30% width decrease near Grand Junction, Colorado, has reduced habitat for endangered fishes.

**Riparian zones.** Riparian systems are described as the land-water interface linking aquatic and terrestrial systems. Changes in flow have altered their structure. Flow regulation along the Middle Rio Grande reduced wetlands from about 52,000 acres in 1918 to 7% of that area (3,671 acres) by 1989. Saltcedar invasion is linked to disconnected floodplains and falling water tables.

**Nutrients and productivity.** Many western streams are nitrogen-limited, so nitrogen standards may matter more for natural productivity than phosphorus standards. Effluent-dominated urban rivers can experience algal blooms that push pH above 9.5, exceeding water quality standards. Irrigated agriculture consumes most western water, and in most western states agriculture accounts for near 90% of total water consumption. Groundwater declines of more than 30 m have occurred in the Rio Grande Valley near Albuquerque.

**Aquatic food webs.** The contributors describe food webs in which energy passes from algae and detritus through invertebrate grazers to long-lived predators, including fishes. Floods are essential to these webs. Periodic scouring floods reset successional communities and benefit fish indirectly by sustaining invertebrate prey. Dams and hydropeaking strand small fishes and invertebrates. Altered flows also degrade spawning gravels for salmonids.

**Native fishes.** Of 810 native North American freshwater fishes, 170 (21%) live west of the Rocky Mountains and 600 (74%) east of them. The Colorado River basin has 32 native fishes, 22 of them (69%) endemic. More than 60 non-native species occur in the Colorado basin. At least 40 North American freshwater fish kinds have gone extinct in the last century, and at least 100 additional western native species are considered threatened, endangered or of special concern.

## Key Findings

**Physical system.** Western rivers are now segmented into zones dominated by storage, erosion or uncertain change. Equilibrium among water, sediment, energy and landforms no longer exists. The authors state that the western fluvial system "is not now natural, and may never be natural again." Dam-induced changes are measurable and far larger than projected effects of global climate change. Some reservoirs store about 325% of the average annual water yield of the entire country, and in many western systems storage has reached 400 to 500% of average annual yield.

**Restoration by flood releases.** A controlled flood from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] began on 26 March 1996 at about 45,000 cubic feet per second (about 1,274 m³/s as printed in the source). Its goal was to restore sandbars in [[Grand Canyon]], which about 22,000 river runners use each year. Preliminary results indicated the goal was accomplished.

**Sediment monitoring decline.** Sediment monitoring is expensive, at about $30,000 per year for a single station. The number of sediment sampling sites in the Colorado River basin has declined sharply since about 1960. Agencies lack the data needed for adaptive management.

**Wild and Scenic Rivers gap.** The U.S. has about 5.1 × 10⁶ km of stream channels, with 17% under reservoir waters. Human activities have altered all but about 2%, and the Wild and Scenic Rivers System permanently protects only about 0.3%. The Colorado Plateau and Basin and Range provinces hold 13.7% of U.S. land but only 3.8% of protected rivers. The Great Plains hold about 20% of the nation's surface but only 2.8% of protected rivers.

**Agency fragmentation.** Federal water agencies are organized by function rather than geography. The Army Corps of Engineers handles flood control, the Bureau of Reclamation handles water supply, the Natural Resources Conservation Service handles agriculture, and the Fish and Wildlife Service handles fish and wildlife. The report recommends a commission to reorganize agencies around watersheds, a policy of "flexible federalism," and a stronger role for watershed councils. Hydropower should remain part of dam operations but not hold primacy over wildlife, recreation and physical integrity. Relicensing should account for ecosystem restoration.

**Recommended management actions.** The reviews converge on several measures:
- Allow periodic flood flows and minimize daily flow fluctuation.
- Consider dam decommissioning case by case, with a cost-benefit analysis that values ecosystem services and plans for sediment release.
- Expand instream flows and protect riparian corridors.
- Reduce subsidies to irrigated agriculture and reform water law to reward stewardship.
- Establish sediment quality standards, because sediments carry more contaminants than water.
- Protect floodplains by excluding or regulating development on them.
- Integrate monitoring around ecosystem quality, including invertebrates, which are poorly known taxonomically.

**Native fish status.** Only about 4.0% of federally protected aquatic species with recovery plans have shown significant recovery. None had been downlisted from endangered to threatened, and the only delistings came from extinction. Non-native species have replaced native fish assemblages in many systems. In the lowermost Colorado River mainstem, the native fauna is replaced by aliens except for two marine species entering from the sea.

**Policy conflicts and examples.** The [[Virgin River]] case shows how the Endangered Species Act raised biodiversity concerns, leading to a 1996 water-rights agreement that provides essentially natural flow through [[Zion National Park]]. The report warns that the surrounding plans remain heavily skewed toward water development. [[Habitat Conservation Plans]] are criticized as long-term agreements that often rely on owner-generated data. Restoration of inflows to [[Mono Lake]] in 1993 resolved threats to waterfowl food supplies after diversions had lowered lake levels by 14 m. The [[Kesterson Reservoir]] selenium disaster caused embryonic deaths in about 40% of nests in one 1983 assessment, with 20% of nests containing deformed chicks.

**Food web and fish conclusions.** Eliminating flooding and natural flow variation harms food webs and fish. Non-native species, particularly bullfrogs, predatory fishes and introduced mysid shrimp, have caused declines of native frogs, fishes and food webs. Restoring more natural hydrologic and geomorphic conditions can tip competition back toward native species.

**Unanimous conclusion.** The editor states that "Aquatic systems in the American West are broken and must soon be fixed if they are to again be sustainable." The authors argue that the continued fragmentation of management, combined with unsustainable water use, leaves only a narrow window for effective restoration.
