# Highway Coordination (FW-16-R-6, WP3, J3)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wildlife-management #Bovidae #desert-ecology #freshwater-fisheries

> **Note:** This source is a wildlife/land-use coordination report, not a fish-centric study. Its primary focus is [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]]-highway interactions in Arizona, with a brief secondary mention of a planned freshwater fishery. Content is synthesized accordingly rather than forced into a fish-only framework.

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department completion report (August 1966–March 31, 1967) documents highway project review and wildlife mitigation efforts under the Land and Water Projects Investigations program. Of 75 highway projects reviewed (8 interstate, 24 primary, 25 secondary, 18 urban secondary), fencing and habitat recommendations were submitted for 15 projects. The report's central ecological concern is chronic mortality of [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] trapped within right-of-way fencing along Interstate 10 in the Plomosa Mountains, and a highway realignment near the [[Bill Williams River]]/[[Colorado River]] corridor to protect bighorn habitat in the Aubrey Hills. A secondary item describes early-stage planning for a fisheries management plan tied to a proposed 89.2-acre borrow-pit lake in Winslow, Arizona, associated with Interstate 40 construction.

## Background & Description

While this is not a fish morphology study, the report provides detailed age- and horn-morphology-based mortality data for [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]]:

- **Affected age/sex class:** Rams aged 3–5 years were disproportionately represented in fatal fence entrapments.
- **Horn morphology and mortality mechanism:** In this age class, horns have not yet curved sufficiently past the head; this morphological feature allows fence wire to slip up behind the neck and under the horn tips when the animal attempts to crawl through fencing, preventing retraction of the head and causing fatal entrapment.
- Older and younger rams were observed entangled but were able to escape (with lacerations), indicating horn curvature progression reduces entrapment risk with age.

No fish morphological data are present in this source; the proposed Winslow lake (89.2 acres) has no fish species identified yet, as the fisheries management plan was still in formulation at the time of reporting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat corridor conflict:** Interstate 10 bisects the Plomosa Mountain range (mileposts 26.5–30.5) east of Quartzsite, cutting across a historically important [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] migration route.
- **Fencing as ecological barrier:** Existing five-strand barbed-wire right-of-way fencing on both sides of the divided highway creates an entrapment zone; sheep enter the median via box culverts under the roadway but become trapped attempting to cross the divided highway surface, sometimes jumping onto concrete culvert wing walls.
- **Highway realignment for habitat protection:** The Parker-Topock Highway (US 95) alignment was shifted to bypass critical bighorn habitat in the Aubrey Hills, reflecting proactive habitat conservation in highway planning.
- **Recreation-driven habitat pressure:** Completion of the Parker-Topock Highway is expected to intensify recreational use along the adjacent [[Colorado River]] corridor, increasing future wildlife-human conflict potential near the [[Bill Williams River]] bridge.
- **Access management:** Recommendations limited public vehicular access to two designated recreation areas (Larned Landing and Hi Isle Campground) on State Parks land, with all other access roads slated for obliteration to reduce habitat disturbance.
- **Aquatic habitat creation:** The proposed 89.2-acre Winslow borrow-pit lake, formed incidentally by Interstate 40 construction, represents a novel artificial aquatic habitat under joint development by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, City of Winslow, and Greer Engineering Company for future sport fisheries use.

## Key Findings

1. **Project volume:** 75 highway projects processed in this period (vs. 103 previously), with a marked drop in interstate projects (8 vs. 38 previously) due to reduced federal matching funds.
2. **Mitigation recommendations:** Fish and wildlife provisions (primarily standard game/fish fencing per Manes 1965) were recommended for 15 of 75 projects (2 interstate, 11 primary, 2 secondary).
3. **Bighorn sheep mortality crisis:** Nine rams died in Interstate 10 right-of-way fencing during 1965–1966, representing 11% of all sheep legally taken in Arizona and 8% of the total desert bighorn harvest in the continental United States for the same period (Russo 1967, pers. comm.).
4. **Mortality mechanism:** Fatalities concentrated in 3–5 year-old rams whose horn curvature was insufficient to prevent fatal wire entanglement during escape attempts — this age class is ecologically important as replacement stock for older rams removed by sport hunting.
5. **Engineered mitigation solution:** A joint Arizona Game and Fish Department–State Highway Department fencing plan specified attaching 56-inch net-wire fencing to existing barbed-wire right-of-way fencing (bottom edge ≤4 inches from ground, top at 5 feet), extending to culvert wings and medians, with removal of boulder piles or terrain features usable by sheep to bypass the fence. This was designed to funnel bighorn sheep movement through existing box culvert underpasses.
6. **Funding approval:** Bureau of Public Roads approved interstate funding for the fencing retrofit on April 14, 1967, with completion anticipated within two months.
7. **Habitat-protective realignment:** The Parker-Topock Highway (US 95) was successfully realigned to avoid critical bighorn sheep habitat in the Aubrey Hills, and access road consolidation was agreed upon to protect the Colorado River recreation corridor near the Bill Williams River bridge.
8. **Emerging fisheries opportunity:** Formulation of a fisheries management plan began for an 89.2-acre borrow-pit lake in Winslow, Arizona, to be created as a byproduct of Interstate 40 construction — an example of incidental aquatic habitat creation from highway infrastructure projects.
9. **Procedural recommendation:** The report recommends establishing a formal inspection procedure during and after highway construction to verify that recommended fish and wildlife provisions (e.g., fencing, culvert design) are actually implemented as specified.
