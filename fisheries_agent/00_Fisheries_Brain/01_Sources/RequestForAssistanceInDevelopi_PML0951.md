# Razorback Sucker Interagency Rearing Proposal, Lake Mohave (1989 Bureau of Reclamation Letter)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1989 letter from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado Regional Office (Regional Director Edward M. Hallenbeck) to Dr. W. L. Minckley. It asks for help in forming an interagency effort to examine a [[Native Fish Rearing Facility]] for [[Razorback Sucker]] populations in the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The letter reports that [[Lake Mohave]] sucker age data suggest there is no natural recruitment, and that the populations are at serious risk. It recalls earlier spawning work in a Lake Mohave backwater and proposes a barrier-protected rearing area to raise young fish past their most vulnerable size before release. Reclamation requested a response by November 15, 1989.

## Background & Description

- **Species of concern:** The letter discusses the razorback sucker, a large catostomid. The scan refers to it as "back sucker," which has been corrected here.
- **Study context:** Several agencies had been working on the species for a few years, with Reclamation providing funding and participating in the efforts.
- **Age evidence:** Age data for suckers (and related fish) collected from Lake Mohave were cited as indicating an absence of natural recruitment.
- **Policy basis:** Reclamation had recently adopted a fishery policy that emphasizes maintaining or enhancing fishery resources on its reservoirs. It had also signed memorandums of understanding with the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and other agencies to cooperate on such work.
- **Recipients:** Identical letters were sent to Dr. Paul Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies, Arizona State University), Alan O'Neill (National Park Service recreation area superintendent), and Michael Spears (Regional Director, Fish and Wildlife Service, Albuquerque).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave backwater history:** Studies by Dr. Paul Marsh and Dr. W. L. Minckley tested whether the species could spawn and rear young in an isolated area of Lake Mohave. A backwater was renovated and stocked with spawning suckers. The suckers spawned successfully, and the water-level conditions at the time were favorable.
- **Predation failure:** Later reservoir elevations reconnected the backwater to the main reservoir. This allowed predator fish to enter, and the young suckers quickly disappeared through predation.
- **Threat level:** The letter states that time is running out for these populations and that the species' survival depends on action now.

## Key Findings

1. **No natural recruitment:** Age data from Lake Mohave suckers suggest no natural recruitment is occurring.
2. **Successful spawning, failed rearing:** Captive-spawned suckers reproduced in a renovated backwater. Predators eliminated the young after the backwater was reconnected to the reservoir.
3. **Proposed solution:** Build a permanent or temporary barrier to create an isolated, predator-free maintenance rearing facility. Raise fish to a size less susceptible to predation, then release them into Lake Mohave to replenish the wild population.
4. **Feasibility:** Reclamation considers that the expertise, resources, and policy mandate exist to save the populations, and it is requesting cooperation from the Fish and Wildlife Service, the National Park Service, and state fish and wildlife agencies.
5. **Next steps:** Reclamation asked for a response by November 15, 1989. If the response was favorable, Reclamation would coordinate with the recipients to discuss methods and initiate the program. The concept had already been discussed informally among staff.
