# Natural History of Arizona - Biology 300 Lecture Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecology #natural-history #biomes

## Executive Summary

These lecture notes from Biology 300 (Natural History of Arizona) taught by W. L. Minckley provide a comprehensive introduction to natural history, ecology, and the biological diversity of Arizona. The course defines natural history as the observational and experimental study of living organisms at the individual level, their interactions with each other, and their relationships with living and non-living components of their environment under natural conditions. The notes cover foundational ecological concepts including species definitions, taxonomy, community structure, ecosystem function, and the climatic and topographic factors controlling plant and animal distributions. Arizona is presented as an exceptionally diverse state containing multiple biomes ranging from warm deserts to montane tundra, with over 2000 plant species and at least 800 vertebrate species. The lectures emphasize the relationship between climate (temperature and moisture), latitude, altitude, and the resulting biotic communities, applying C. Hart Merriam's life zone concept to Arizona's varied landscapes.

## Background & Description

The course instructor, W. L. Minckley, brings 30 years of field experience in North American desertlands from Mexico to the high cold deserts of eastern [[Washington]] and [[Oregon]]. Natural history is defined as primarily observational science, with experimental approaches secondary, conducted in natural environments rather than removing organisms to laboratories. When natural observations are impossible, the goal is to duplicate nature in controlled laboratory settings.

[[Arizona]] occupies a position between 31-37 degrees north latitude and 109-115 degrees west longitude in the American Southwest. The state measures approximately 400 miles east-west and 400 miles north-south, bordered by [[Utah]] (north), [[New Mexico]] (east), [[Sonora]] (south), and [[California]] and [[Nevada]] (west). The [[Colorado River]] forms most of the western boundary, draining approximately one-seventh of the continental United States.

The state's topography divides into three major physiographic areas: the [[Colorado Plateau]] in the north (standing at 6000-7000 feet with higher peaks), a central transition zone of highly variable relief separated by the [[Mogollon Rim]], and southern basin-and-range country characterized by parallel mountains and adjacent valleys. Elevations range from over 12,000 feet near [[Flagstaff]] to near sea level at [[Yuma]].

Major river systems include the [[Colorado River Basin]] (with tributaries including [[San Juan River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Virgin River]], [[Bill Williams River]]) and the [[Gila River Basin]] dominating southern Arizona (including [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], [[San Simon River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Santa Cruz River]]). Minor basins exist along the Mexican border, and closed basins include [[Wilcox Playa]] and [[Hualapai Red Lake]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lectures establish fundamental ecological vocabulary and concepts:

**Species Concepts**: Species are defined both reproductively (groups that interbreed successfully among themselves but not with other groups) and ecologically (organisms occupying a definite niche within a habitat and community). Reproductive isolation can be physiological, mechanical, behavioral, or geographic. Species in wild populations are simpler to define than in domesticated organisms where artificial selection creates diverse forms (e.g., domestic dogs from [[Chihuahua]] to [[Great Dane]]) that remain interfertile.

**Ecological Hierarchy**: 
- **Environment**: The sum of abiotic and biotic factors influencing organisms, ranging from immediate factors (cover, local temperature, day-length, soils, nutrients, water) to distant influences (climatic conditions from northern Pacific weather disturbances)
- **Habitat**: Places providing food, shelter, and resources allowing species to complete their life cycles
- **Community**: Collections of interacting animals and plants operating within habitats
- **Niche**: An organism's occupation within the system, including producers (plants deriving sustenance from sunlight), consumers (herbivores, detritivores, carnivores), and decomposers
- **Ecosystem**: Arbitrary, human-delimited collections of communities operating as functional systems with inputs (solar energy, nutrients, water) and outputs

**Taxonomic Framework**: The Linnaean hierarchical system groups organisms by presumed relationships: Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Subphylum → Phylum. Only species represent natural units testable by interbreeding success; higher categories are artificial constructs for human communication convenience. Scientific names (Genus + species + namer) use Latin or Latinized Greek to ensure universal communication.

**Climatic Controls**: Global and local climate patterns control plant and animal distributions through the interaction of latitude, altitude, and topography. The thermal lapse rate approximates 1°C per 100 m elevation and 1°C per 100 km latitude. Solar energy input is highest at the equator, with short-wave radiation absorbed by solid surfaces and re-radiated as long-wave radiation trapped by atmospheric gases ([[Carbon Dioxide]], water vapor, dust), creating a greenhouse effect. Projected increases in CO₂ from fossil fuel combustion over the next 100 years could raise atmospheric temperature 2-3°C, sufficient to shift [[Payson]] from pine forest to woodland or grassland and reduce North American wheat production by 20% (30% in Russia).

**Atmospheric Circulation**: Earth's rotation creates predictable air movement bands. Without rotation, hot air would simply rise at the equator and sink at the poles. The [[Coriolis Effect]] produces distinct zones: equatorial regions (hot and wet), 23-35 degrees latitude (warm and dry doldrums), 45-75 degrees latitude (cool and wet frontal mixing zones), and polar regions (cold and dry). These patterns create global biome distributions but are modified locally by topography.

**Succession and Climax**: Vegetation develops through predictable seral stages toward climax communities that persist until major climatic changes occur. Early invaders prepare areas for later species, with the final climax community resisting change. Disclimax communities are maintained at successional stages by overriding forces (human agriculture, fire, grazing). Examples include [[Aspen]] groves preceding coniferous forest and grasslands maintained by fire (natural and Native American-set) and [[Bison]] grazing.

## Key Findings

**Arizona's Biological Diversity**: The state contains exceptional habitat diversity reflected in over 2000 plant species and at least 800 vertebrate species. This diversity results from Arizona's remarkable climatic and topographic variation, containing warm desertlands, extensive cool forests, montane tundra at highest elevations, and large expanses of temperate woodlands and prairies at intermediate elevations. The state is not solely desert despite common characterization.

**Human Population Pressures**: Arizona and the sunbelt region are experiencing rapid human population growth, perhaps more than any comparable continental area. Climate factors including general absence of snow in lowlands and high percentage of warm, sunlit days attract increasing human influx. This expansion creates continuing and increasing stresses on natural habitats and native plants and animals. The course advocates for multiple use of lands, waters, and their biological inhabitants while documenting how habitats and biotas have changed under human development influences over recent generations.

**Historical Context**: Spanish discovery occurred in the 1500s, with Spanish and Mexican governance for over 300 years without significant settlement. Detailed exploration and settlement began approximately 150 years ago. Until the 1930s-1940s, the state remained largely open and undisturbed except for extensive cattle ranching and limited low-desert agriculture. Spanish explorers found evidence of major Indian cultures, especially in vast irrigatable valleys, where populations had numbered in the hundreds of thousands in the [[Salt River]] and [[Gila River]] valleys, developing extensive irrigation networks later utilized by settlers.

**Major Biomes of Northern Hemisphere**:

1. **Ice/Far North**: Essentially no terrestrial life; organisms depend directly or indirectly on the sea
2. **Tundra**: Circumpolar, cold, dry, treeless, flat terrain dominated by mosses, lichens, sedges; characterized by permafrost with top layers defrosting 2-3 months annually; low productivity but high individual numbers of few specialized species; remarkably abundant insects supporting great numbers of birds
3. **Taiga**: Northern coniferous forest (30-60 degrees N); most biomass in trees and detritus with little productivity usable by animals; relatively small animal populations of large cellulose-feeding ungulates, pine-adapted organisms, and insects
4. **Deciduous Forest**: Oaks, beech, birch, chestnut producing nuts and fruits; high diversity; supports deer, bison, woodland ungulates, and smaller animals; highly productive summers with diverse storable seeds and fruits
5. **Prairies**: Highly productive, isolated from oceanic moderation, more arid; grasses provide remarkably available food source with high silica content requiring special teeth; supported remarkable animal biomass including bison, horse, antelope, and predators
6. **Subtropical Deserts**: Diverse with no single dominant vegetation; warm and dry; patchwork of communities; special fauna of rodents and reptiles with nocturnal habits and desiccation-avoidance adaptations
7. **Tropical Forests**: Very warm and wet; dominated by trees holding biomass and nutrients; many habitat layers increasing diversity; abundant amphibians, herbivores, insects, and insectivorous birds

**Arizona Altitudinal Zonation**: Following C. Hart Merriam's late 1800s work on [[San Francisco Peaks]], Arizona exhibits distinct altitudinal life zones:

- **Montane Tundra** (above 11,500 feet north-facing slopes): Differs from arctic tundra by lacking permafrost; unstable soils, wet climate with runoff, high winds, cold, low productivity; mosses, lichens, grasses, sedges, small shrubs; few animal species due to isolation and recent glaciation; represents relict communities from earlier stages
- **Timberline**: Transitional zone at upper tree limit; trees show special adaptations to severe wind, cold, and water scarcity (runoff); gnarled, twisted forms including [[Bristlecone Pine]]
- **Coniferous Forest**: Closed canopy; semi-humid to humid, temperate to cold climate; [[Spruce]]-[[Fir]] forests followed downslope by [[Yellow Pine]] forests (some of world's most extensive), sometimes mixed with broadleaf deciduous trees
- **Woodland**: Open canopy allowing sunlight penetration to ground with considerable understory; semi-arid to semi-humid, warm or cold; [[Pinyon Pine]]-[[Juniper]] woodlands very extensive but largely modified by grazing; [[Encinal Woodland]]; [[Saguaro]]-[[Palo Verde]] habitats qualify as woodlands
- **Chaparral**: Semi-arid, warm to cold; low vegetation rarely exceeding 6 feet, principally evergreen; may develop closed canopy shading out other plants; represents fire disclimax with recovery from roots, nutrient release, and some seeds requiring fire for germination; extremely long-lived plants; few adapted animals
- **Grasslands**: Semi-arid, warm to cold; most Arizona grasslands are short-grass or prairie type; includes upland marshes; savannah represents grassland punctuated by trees, transitional to woodland
- **Desertscrub**: Short trees, shrubs with tiny leaves, chlorophyllous bark, winter and drought deciduous, protective spines or poisons preventing injury and water loss; extreme deserts are vegetationless or have special plants like cacti with most body underground, or annual plants

**Gradient Concepts**: Biotic communities occur in regular zones globally and occupy specific altitudinal zones on mountains, influenced by the same climatic factors (hot-cold, wet-dry gradients). Exposure differences (north-south, east-west facing slopes) significantly affect community distribution, relating to latitudinal patterns.

**Course Philosophy**: The goal is to implant appreciation of diversity, understanding of how diversity develops and is maintained, and recognition of basic biological diversity's importance to human activities. Students should absorb sufficient information to understand, appreciate, and act upon environmental issues in Arizona and elsewhere. The course relates Arizona to other changing areas worldwide, touching on broad environmental problems including acid rain and greenhouse effect, though other classes address these issues in depth.
