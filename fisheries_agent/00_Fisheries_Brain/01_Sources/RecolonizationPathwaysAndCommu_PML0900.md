# Recolonization Pathways and Community Development After Disturbance in Desert Stream Macroinvertebrates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-insects #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1980 Arizona State University doctoral dissertation by Lawrence James Gray examines benthic macroinvertebrate responses to drought and flooding in [[Sycamore Creek]], a lowland Sonoran Desert stream in central Arizona. The study covers species composition, [[Life-History Adaptations]], recolonization pathways, [[Community Development]] after disturbance, and [[Secondary Production]]. No fish are reported; the document concerns aquatic insects and the wider invertebrate community.

The work argues that frequent flash floods and drying select for rapid development, aerial dispersal, and behavioral avoidance rather than dormancy. Aerial adults act as the resistant life stage, recolonization proceeds mainly by air, and secondary production rates are among the highest recorded for freshwater habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Sycamore Creek rises in the Mazatzal Mountains (elevation 2,146 m) and flows 65 km to the [[Verde River]] (430 m). Lowland vegetation includes Sonoran desertscrub, with riparian sycamores, cottonwoods and mesquite.
- **Climate:** Mean annual precipitation is 51 cm at mid-basin and 23 cm at the Verde River; 55% falls in winter, and the remainder in summer [[Monsoon Thunderstorms]].
- **Hydrology:** Floods are defined as discharge exceeding 1.0 m³/s. They occur on average five times a year (range 1 to 15). Winter floods affect the whole basin; summer floods are local and usually last only hours.
- **Temperature:** Water temperature rarely exceeded 10 to 30 °C; microhabitat differences of 5 to 12 °C occurred.
- **Taxonomic scope:** 99 aquatic insect taxa and 104 total macroinvertebrate taxa were recorded from July 1977 to November 1979. Life histories were studied for 17 insect species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Disturbance regime:** Transpiration greatly exceeds precipitation, creating short-lived habitats. The author frames the system as a model for non-equilibrium community dynamics ([[Disturbance Ecology]]).
- **Island biogeography:** Springs, stock tanks, temporary pools and intermittent channel segments are treated as islands, allowing testing of the [[MacArthur-Wilson Model]].
- **Faunal origin:** The fauna mixes Neotropical and Nearctic elements. Most species are restricted to the arid American Southwest; widespread Nearctic taxa such as Helicopsyche borealis and Simulium species also occur.
- **Comparison streams:** Similar desert streams studied include Aravaipa Creek, Tornillo Creek and Pinto Creek.
- **Dormancy:** Desert species generally lack dormant stages. Only Capnia arizonensis (egg diapause) and Tabanus dorsifer (larval diapause) were confirmed.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and life histories (Chapter III)**
- 99 aquatic insect taxa (Ephemeroptera 9, Plecoptera 2, Trichoptera 11, Coleoptera 31, Hemiptera 10, Diptera 28, Odonata 5, Megaloptera 1). Non-insects were also present, with Oligochaeta, Gastropoda and Acari.
- Mayflies, small dipterans and Graptocorixa serrulata: rapid development (egg to adult roughly 1 to 3 weeks; mayflies 8 to 14 days), continuous reproduction, no dormancy.
- Aquatic beetles (Dytiscidae, Hydrophilidae): 4 to 8 weeks development, reproduction largely restricted to spring and after summer rains.
- Helicopsyche mexicana: about 50 days total development (1,100 degree-days). Polycentropus halidus: about 46 days.
- Abedus herberti: about 80 days (1,440 degree-days); the only known iteroparous aquatic insect in the stream.
- Tabanus dorsifer: about one-year generation time with a six-month larval diapause.

**Drought and flood adaptations**
- Drought: habitat selection by ovipositing adults and rapid development.
- Floods: behavioral avoidance, rapid development and seasonal reproduction. Flood losses averaged 86% for all taxa except hemipterans and adult beetles.
- Flood frequency and tolerance shaped [[Recolonization]] and species richness, and the rarity of dormancy made aerial adults the key resistant stage.

**Recolonization (Chapter IV)**
- Nearly two-thirds of total taxa recolonized by aerial pathways in both seasons. Drift contributed most of the rest; upstream and vertical movements contributed little, except vertical movement after winter floods.
- Few taxa actively drifted during stable flow: 5 taxa made up 95% of drift individuals.
- Drift-to-upstream ratio was 2.5 in non-flood periods versus 1.5 to 4.6 after floods.
- Colonization pits tested the MacArthur-Wilson model: 17 taxa colonized successfully in 48 days. An equilibrium was reached between immigration and emigration for both reproductive and all taxa, at about 6-7 and 9-10 taxa respectively, although the equilibrium was not dynamic for reproductive taxa.

**Community development (Chapter V)**
- Nine floods reduced total macroinvertebrate numbers by 86% and biomass by 81%.
- After single floods, density and biomass returned to pre-flood values within 1 to 3 weeks; after frequent floods, recovery took 5 to 7 weeks.
- Collectors and predators dominated trophic structure. Scrapers were uncommon, and leaf shredders were absent from mainstem habitats.
- Predators reduced prey populations; spring beetle reproduction led to prey regulation.
- Species richness depended on adult populations and flood frequency, supporting the [[Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis]] in part.

**Secondary production (Chapter VI)**
- Mainstem production exceeded 1,100 kg dry weight/ha/year for primary consumers and 100 kg/ha/year for secondary consumers.
- Primary consumers processed at least 18% of gross photosynthesis; secondary consumers ingested 18 to 28% of primary consumer production.
- Sycamore Creek production is exceeded only by Speed River, with about five times higher production on a substrate-volume basis.

**Summary and conclusions**
- Succession was not clearly demonstrated. Macroinvertebrate communities behaved as "pioneer" types, with community properties arising from individual life-history traits.
- Aquatic and terrestrial life stages suggest these insects may be an important energy and nutrient link between desert aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, including riparian birds.
