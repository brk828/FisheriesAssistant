# Tilapia as a Biological Control Agent and Water Quality Policy Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a multi-part extract, likely from a 1970s conference or policy publication. It contains three components. The first is a continuation of an American Fisheries Society (AFS) statement on water quality standards for chronic pollution effects in fish. The second is a technical paper on using [[Tilapia]] species as a biological control agent for aquatic weeds in irrigation and drainage systems, from the Division of Biological Control, University of California-Riverside. The third summarizes recommendations of the [[National Commission on Water Quality]] under the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments]] of 1972. A literature cited list is also included. The source text is heavily affected by OCR errors, so some values below are approximate or omitted where illegible.

## Background & Description

The AFS statement addresses standard-setting for chronic pollution in fisheries. It argues that water quality standards must protect fishery products for human consumption and protect all beneficial uses of water.

The tilapia paper focuses on two species:

- **[[Tilapia Zillii]]** (*Tilapia zillii*): the most widely used species, applied chiefly to control higher aquatic weeds in irrigation canals and drainage ditches.
- **[[Tilapia Mossambica]]** (*Oreochromis mossambicus*): also used for aquatic weed control in some small lakes and ponds, and for mosquito and midge control in golf course ponds.

The National Commission on Water Quality was created to assess the effects of the 1972 Act and recommend legislative direction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weed control in canals and ditches.** Tilapia were introduced outside their native range, and in some areas their populations are restricted to thermal discharges, but in others they support sport and commercial fishing. Culture conditions and water temperature strongly constrain their use. Tilapia control of weeds was described as most effective when combined with mechanical methods, though fish alone also gave excellent control in some trials.

**Weed preferences.** [[Southern Naiad]] (*Najas* sp.) and [[Chara]] sp. were preferred food, while other weeds, including [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum spicatum*), could still develop into nuisance growth when tilapia were used alone.

**Rice fields.** In rice culture, tilapia were proposed to control weeds and mosquito larvae. Harvesting tilapia at rice harvest could recycle nutrients and reduce fertilizer use. A mixed culture of tilapia species controlled rice weeds better than either species alone.

**Mosquito and midge control.** Tilapia were used with reduced pesticide applications to control mosquito and midge larvae in golf course ponds. Control was partly indirect, through eliminating weeds that serve as habitat and through consumption of larvae and pupae.

**Introduction risks.** Introductions of non-native fish are generally controlled by state fish and game agencies. The authors note that where such species are already established, they may provide a useful biological tool to replace less acceptable methods.

**Water pollution context.** The water quality standards discussion addresses chemical residues in fish, including the toxaphene studies on [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Brook Trout]] growth and bone composition (Mehrle and Mayer, 1975a, b), and the effects of sewage treatment plant effluents on fish (Tsai, 1975).

## Key Findings

### 1. Tilapia as a biological weed control agent
- *T. zillii* is the most extensively applied species for controlling higher aquatic weeds in irrigation canals and drainage ditches.
- In some trials, excellent weed control was achieved with fish alone at an initial stocking rate as low as 1,500 fish per hectare.
- Results were less reliable in some other situations. Integrated biological and mechanical control was used in these cases.
- The authors conclude that tilapia alone, or combined with mechanical methods, appears to be economically feasible and can reduce energy and herbicide use.
- A key limitation is that tilapia cannot survive cold winter temperatures. Control is therefore mostly limited to regions with warm climates, and winter costs of raising fish in heated water must be considered.

### 2. Culture and production
- District personnel reared *T. zillii* in a pond with supplemental commercial catfish pellets, maintaining water temperature between 26 and 29 °C for about 10 months of the year.
- *T. mossambica* produced approximately 180 lb per acre in 3 months during 1975. This was a ninefold increase over the number of fish stocked.

### 3. Policy and management recommendations on tilapia
- Introductions of non-native fish should be closely controlled by state fish and game agencies.
- Where non-native species are already present, they may offer useful management tools.

### 4. AFS water quality standard recommendations
- Standards must ensure fishery products are safe for humans, domestic animals, and fish-eating birds.
- Standards should be set low enough to protect all beneficial uses.
- Where chronic effects are unproven, interim standards should be based on the probability of risk, although reaching consensus on probability levels is difficult.
- All water quality standards should be provisional, with a defined method for adjustment as new information becomes available.
- An independent advisory group, including representatives from industry, regulatory agencies, universities, and citizen groups, should review new information and recommend adjustments.
- Communication between scientists, regulators, industry, and the public must improve. Confusing regulatory structures are causing public frustration.
- Ecological education should be improved at every level of the school system.
- Comment deadlines for proposed standards published in the *Federal Register* are often too short. Supporting data should be made easily available to any group wishing to respond.

### 5. National Commission on Water Quality recommendations
- The Commission was tasked with assessing the 1972 Act and recommending legislative direction for pollution control.
- Recommendations include a compliance date (partly illegible in the source, but apparently around 1977) for uniform effluent requirements.
- Federal financing for priority treatment needs was recommended at not less than $5 billion per year, for a specified period of five to ten years. The upper limit is illegible in the source.
- Other recommendations address discharge permits, reuse of water, selective certification of control plans for both point and nonpoint sources (including irrigated agriculture), and the federal construction grants program.
