# Drift of Larval Fishes in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 study by John G. Carter, Vincent A. Lamarra and Ronald J. Ryel of the [[Ecosystems Research Institute]] examines [[Larval Fish Drift]] in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. Two independent studies were run: a 1983 survey near [[Parachute Creek]], Colorado, assessing a midchannel water intake, and a 1984 survey at the [[Palisade Site]] on [[Plateau Creek]], Colorado, assessing a shoreline hydropower project. Five native fishes dominated the drift, and larval fish drift was tied to water temperature, discharge, shoreline position and time of day. The authors frame the findings as baseline information for managing water withdrawals, flow modification and hydropower in the basin, where the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] is a concern.

## Background & Description

Drift of larval fishes had not been documented for the upper Colorado River before the [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]] (1981). Earlier work by Banks (1964) and Vanicek (1967) and the belief that young Colorado Squawfish moved little from spawning sites had suggested otherwise. [[Tyus et al 1982]] proposed that larval squawfish drift from suspected spawning areas to downstream nursery areas. [[Haynes et al 1984]] and [[Radant et al 1983]] used small-mesh drift nets in the Colorado, Yampa and White rivers.

The study used five gear types at each site:
- Large-mesh seines (adult and juvenile composition)
- Small-mesh seines, dip nets and light traps (non-drifting larvae and young-of-the-year)
- Drift nets (drifting larvae)
- Tow nets (midchannel surface drift, Parachute only)
- Boat electrofishing (Palisade only)

Drift nets were 560-micron Nitex mesh, with a 30.48 × 45.72 cm opening and a 3 m length tapering to a collection cup. Water volume was calculated from the mean of initial and final velocities at the net mouth. Larvae were identified using [[Snyder 1981]] and staged as [[Protolarvae]], [[Mesolarvae]], [[Metalarvae]] or juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water temperature was the main cue for drift timing. Larvae first appeared when water reached about 16°C during a rapid warming trend. Most drift occurred as temperatures rose to roughly 18–21°C during the falling limb of [[Spring Runoff]]. Drift declined when midchannel temperatures exceeded about 20°C, and the authors could not tell whether fish avoided these temperatures or whether fewer fish were available to drift.

Discharge also mattered. At Parachute, a peak of 804 m³/s on June 26 fell to about 284 m³/s by July 20. Peak drift came during a week when discharge was about 271 m³/s. At Palisade, drift rose as discharge fell from about 525 to 230 m³/s.

Habitat zone influenced sampling. Drift density was nearly seven times higher along the shoreline than in the midchannel surface zone, suggesting that most larvae drift near shore. The midchannel benthic zone was not sampled because of high velocity and excessive debris, so its role remains unknown.

## Key Findings

**Species composition**
- Five native fishes (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, roundtail chub, speckled dace, mottled sculpin) made up about 87% of the drift catch overall.
- At Palisade, the five native species made up 90% of the shoreline drift catch.
- Three non-native species (white sucker, fathead minnow, green sunfish) were in drift at low numbers. White sucker was the only one forming a significant share, at 8.6% of Palisade drift.
- At Parachute, 13 species were captured, and 8 were found as larvae in drift. At Palisade, 14 species were encountered, and 8 were found in drift.
- Shoreline and midchannel surface drift were similar in species composition. Four species (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace, white sucker) made up most of the drift, at 78.5% of midchannel samples.

**Density and timing**
- Shoreline drift density was nearly seven times greater than midchannel surface drift (p = 0.026, randomization test of paired differences of weekly means).
- Parachute (1983): about 72% of drift occurred in a 3-week window (July 25 to August 15). The highest mean weekly density was 0.062 fish/m³, in the week of July 25–31. The abstract gives about 65% for the combined two studies.
- Palisade (1984): mean weekly density peaked at 0.082 fish/m³ in the fourth week. A second pulse in late August and early September reached 0.047 fish/m³ and could not be explained by temperature or discharge. The authors suggest it reflects a split spawning event.
- Drift lasted 9 to 10 weeks, from early July to early September.

**Diel patterns** (see [[Diel Drift Pattern]])
- Palisade: densities were highest from midnight to 0400 hours (0.023 fish/m³) and from 2000 hours to midnight (0.021 fish/m³), together about 60% of drift. The lowest density was between 1600 and 2000 hours. ANOVA was significant (p < 0.01), with the two maxima differing from the four minima.
- Parachute: highest density was at 2000–2400 hours, and lowest at 1600–2000 hours (0.0088 fish/m³). No significant differences were found, which the authors attribute to turbidity, sediment load and sampling variation across the 9 weeks.
- The authors read the night-time peaks as evidence that larvae are photosensitive and can enter or leave surface drift actively.

**Vertical distribution**
- Paired shoreline nets (16 samples per level) showed 0.014 fish/m³ in the upper 30 cm and 0.009 fish/m³ from 30 to 90 cm. The difference was not significant, which suggests uniform mixing to at least 90 cm along the shoreline.

**Length and developmental phase**
- Flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, roundtail chub and speckled dace drifted most in the mesolarval phase. Their abundance in shoreline seine hauls rose as they reached late mesolarval and metalarval stages, while drift numbers fell.
- White sucker showed a similar mesolarval drift pattern. Fathead minnow showed no clear length-related drift.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- Protolarvae, hatched from eggs on gravel bars, stay in quiet interstitial spaces. Mesolarvae, with absorbed yolk sacs and developed mouths and guts, emerge and enter the current. Metalarvae and juveniles, with full fins and musculature, escape drift and move into shoreline habitat.
- Temperature during spawning and egg development, not only at hatching, appears to control drift timing.
- The authors note that fish in drift may be partly controlled by where and when they can use shoreline habitat, which matters for planning intakes, flow regimes and hydropower.
- The study is a baseline. The authors recommend quantitative sampling of the midchannel benthic zone before the role of deep, high-velocity habitat can be judged.
