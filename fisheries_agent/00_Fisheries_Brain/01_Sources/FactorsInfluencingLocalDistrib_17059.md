# Factors influencing local distribution of Cyprinodon variegatus (Pisces: Cyprinodontidae)

## Executive Summary
This study by F. Douglas Martin (1972) investigates the local distribution of **[[Cyprinodon variegatus]]** (sheepshead minnow), a euryhaline cyprinodontid with an exceptionally broad salinity tolerance (0.08‰ to over 120‰). Across 120 localities from Texas to Florida, the study tested whether water chemistry (Ca, Mg, Na, K, Cl⁻, alkalinity, dissolved oxygen) or biological factors (vegetation, predators, competitors) best explained the species' patchy distribution in low-salinity waters. The key finding is that chemical factors play a minor role, while **biological exclusion by primary freshwater fish, especially [[Centrarchidae|centrarchids]]**, appears to be the dominant control on the species' penetration into fresh water, likely mediated by competitive interactions rather than predation.

## Background & Description
The paper is ecological/distributional rather than morphological, but notes the following behavioral-morphological/ecological traits relevant to niche partitioning:
- **Territoriality**: Males establish and defend territories, actively challenging or repelling intruding fish (including known predators such as *Fundulus grandis*), rather than fleeing.
- **Habitat use**: Juveniles and females exhibit cryptic behavior—burying in soft substrate or forming compact schools in shallows when threatened.
- **Vegetation association**: Individuals transit through beds of rooted vegetation (*[[Thalassia testudinum]]*, *Syringodium filiforme*) but do not use them as cover from predators; territories are typically established in unvegetated areas.
- **Osmoregulatory tolerance**: Demonstrates one of the broadest salinity tolerances among fishes, with breeding populations documented from 0.08‰ to 97.3‰.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Distribution pattern**: *C. variegatus* is reliably present in shallow, low-wave-action habitats with salinity above 10‰, but is frequently and inconsistently absent from similar habitats below 10‰, despite being physiologically capable of breeding at salinities as low as 0.08‰.

**Chemical factors (largely ruled out)**:
- Dissolved oxygen ranged 4.0–9.6 ppm in the field; lab fish tolerated 2.3–12.1 ppm with no behavioral effect — DO is not a limiting factor.
- Ion concentrations (Na, K, Ca, Mg) and ion ratios (Na+K/Ca+Mg; Na/K; Mg/Ca) showed extreme values at only ~10–15% of localities where the species was absent — chemistry explains only a small minority of absences.
- Calcium levels, previously implicated by [[Breder 1934]] in freshwater invasion by marine fishes, were not supported as a major limiting factor here; the author suggests **total osmotic pressure** may be more relevant than individual ion levels (cf. [[Loeb and Wasteneys 1915]] on divalent/monovalent cation balance and osmoregulation).

**Biological factors (primary drivers)**:
- **Vegetation avoidance**: Significant negative association with *Thalassia testudinum* and *Syringodium filiforme* beds (p < 0.0001, χ² = 35.410), though the species can be abundant among freshwater vascular plants (*Scirpus* sp., *Najas* sp.) in the absence of competitors/predators.
- **Predator relationships**: 
  - All potential piscine predators combined: significant non-overlap (p < 0.03).
  - Restricting to size-appropriate/piscivorous predators only: stronger non-overlap (p < 0.001).
  - Marine predators at salinities >15‰: **positive association** (no exclusion; Fager 1957 test).
  - Freshwater predators (*[[Ictalurus]]*, *[[Aphredoderus]]*, *[[Cichlasoma]]*, centrarchids) at salinities 0.07–2.0‰: strong non-overlap (p < 0.0001, χ² = 20.284).
- **Primary freshwater fish** (all taxa) at low salinity: strong non-overlap (p < 0.0001, χ² = 18.708) — identified as the **key limiting factor** for freshwater penetration, with competition (not predation) as the likely mechanism.
- **Species richness pattern**: Localities where *C. variegatus* occurs typically support fewer than four other fish species, consistent with a niche strategy of exploiting physiologically harsh/variable environments where competitors cannot persist.

**Breeding ecology**:
- Sudden salinity reduction experimentally triggered spawning: 10/10 tanks with reduced salinity (~10‰ drop from 29.1‰ baseline) contained eggs vs. 2/10 controls (p < 0.0005, χ² = 13.334) — implicates osmotic shock/rainfall pulses as a breeding cue.
- Field spawning observed across 0.08–63.1‰; newly hatched young found up to 90‰+ (spawning not directly observed there).
- Interannual variation in breeding timing (first ripe females/juveniles) at Corpus Christi bays correlated with rainfall patterns (1966 vs. 1967), with more even/heavy rainfall associated with larger, more stable populations.

## Key Findings
1. **Chemical factors are largely non-limiting**: Ca, Mg, Na, K, ion ratios, alkalinity, and dissolved oxygen do not substantially constrain local distribution in most situations (~85–90% of absence localities had "acceptable" chemistry).
2. **No strong association with rooted vegetation**: The species avoids seagrass beds (*Thalassia*, *Syringodium*) but is not excluded by freshwater vascular plants when competitors/predators are absent.
3. **Predation is not the primary exclusion mechanism**: Marine predators show positive association with *C. variegatus*; only freshwater predators/primary freshwater fish show strong exclusion, suggesting **competition**, not predation risk, drives the pattern.
4. **Primary freshwater fish (especially centrarchids) are the principal barrier** to *C. variegatus* invasion of fresh water — the central conclusion of the study.
5. **Rainfall-driven salinity pulses** appear to act as a breeding trigger, linking hydrology to reproductive timing and population dynamics.
6. Findings challenge or refine earlier hypotheses by [[Breder]] (calcium-limitation) and [[Odum]] (chloride-dependence), proposing that **total osmotic pressure** and **interspecific competition** are more parsimonious explanatory factors than single-ion physiological limits.
