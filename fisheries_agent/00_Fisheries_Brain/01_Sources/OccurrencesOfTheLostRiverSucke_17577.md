# Occurrences of the Lost River Sucker and Shortnose Sucker in Northern California

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #species-distribution #endemic-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1965 report documents the first confirmed occurrences of [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) in California waters, adding both species to the state's freshwater fish fauna. Previously known only from the [[Klamath Lakes]] drainage in Oregon, specimens were collected from [[Copco Lake]] in [[Siskiyou County]] and [[Boles Creek]] in [[Modoc County]] between 1955 and 1956. The report provides meristic data, morphological descriptions, and historical accounts of indigenous fisheries that once harvested these large suckers from [[Tule Lake]] and [[Lower Klamath Lake]] before extensive reclamation drained these habitats in the early 1920s.

## Background & Description

The [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) belong to the family [[Catostomidae]]. Both species were originally described by Cope (1879) from the [[Klamath River Basin]].

**Lost River Sucker morphology:**
- Large size, reaching 3 feet as adults
- Long, slender head with slight hump on upper snout surface
- Terminal, oblique mouth
- Lips bearing minute papillae in some specimens
- Triangular gill rakers with generally unarmed, entire edges
- Large scales up to 12 mm at widest diameter

**Shortnose Sucker morphology:**
- Comparatively small head
- Prominent hump on short snout formed by premaxillary spines (variable among specimens)
- Oblique mouth with thin lips lacking papillae
- Triangular gill rakers with free margins fringed with fine teeth
- More cylindrical body than [[Lost River Sucker]]

**Meristic data for Lost River Sucker** (3 specimens from [[Copco Lake]], California Academy of Sciences No. 26304):
- Total length: 26.3-30.3 inches
- Standard length: 575-663 mm
- Weight: 6.4-11.1 pounds
- Dorsal rays: 11-12
- Anal rays: 7-8
- Pectoral rays: 15-17
- Pelvic rays: 10-10
- Lateral-line pores: 82-88
- Gill rakers: 32-32
- Vertebrae: 51 (29+22)

**Meristic data for Shortnose Sucker** (1 adult female from [[Copco Lake]], California Academy of Sciences No. 26305):
- Total length: 19.3 inches
- Standard length: 430 mm
- Weight: 2.6 pounds
- Dorsal rays: 11
- Anal rays: 7
- Pectoral rays: 16-16
- Pelvic rays: 10-10
- Lateral-line pores: 79-79
- Vertebrae: 46 (27+19)

Fecundity estimates for two [[Shortnose Sucker]] females (19.3 inches fork length) from [[Copco Lake]]: 36,763 and 56,217 eggs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and habitat:**
The species' ranges were previously documented only in the [[Klamath Lakes]] drainage in Oregon. Gilbert (1898) stated that *Deltistes* were resident in deeper waters of [[Upper Klamath Lake]] and [[Tule Lake]] during most of the year.

**California collection localities:**
1. [[Boles Creek]], [[Modoc County]] (May 15, 1955; May 10, 1960): Mature [[Shortnose Sucker]] observed in apparent spawning migration. [[Boles Creek]] is an intermittent tributary of [[Willow Creek]], entering [[Clear Lake Reservoir]]. [[Lost River]] originates from [[Clear Lake Reservoir]], flows north into Oregon, re-enters California, and discharges into [[Tule Lake]].

2. [[Copco Lake]], [[Siskiyou County]] (first collected November 20, 1956): Storage reservoir on [[Klamath River]] near Oregon border. Collection site was gently sloping beach with maximum depth of 6 feet, bottom of silt and coarse gravel with submerged logs. Both species noted in routine beach seine haul. Later sampling with beach seines and gill nets usually revealed both species, particularly in fall. No young or spawning activity observed in [[Copco Lake]].

**Spawning behavior:**
[[Lost River Sucker]] provides spring sport fishery around margins of [[Upper Klamath Lake]], Oregon. Fish locally called "mullet" are caught by snagging with grabhooks during spawning runs near springs and lower reaches of tributary streams.

**Age determination challenges:**
Scale analysis of several specimens from [[Copco Lake]] yielded no definite age conclusions. Usual annual growth characters (cutting over, packing of circuli, irregularities) were confusing and variable. Some scales lacked bands of spaced circuli; peripheral areas were jumbled. Distinct concentric bands on opercular bones showed no relationship to scales from same specimens.

**Biogeographic relationships:**
The genus *Chasmistes* also occurs in two remnant Pleistocene lakes of the [[Great Basin]]: the [[June Sucker]] (*C. liorus*) in [[Utah Lake]] (Bonneville drainage) and the [[Cui-ui Sucker]] (*C. cujus*) in [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada (Lahontan drainage). Close relationships of genera like *Chasmistes* and *Siphateles* in upper [[Klamath Drainage]] and [[Great Basin]] suggest past connection with interior basin.

**Historical fisheries and habitat loss:**
[[Klamath Indians]] and [[Modoc Indians]] historically took and dried [[Lost River Sucker]] in large numbers. Klamath Indians called *Deltistes* "T'swam" and *Chasmistes* "Xooptu."

Long-time residents confirmed [[Lost River Sucker]] once populated both [[Tule Lake]] and [[Lower Klamath Lake]] in California:
- Lewis Foulke observed large suckers and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerti*) taken from mouth of [[Lost River]] around 1919 using set lines and snagging gear
- John McKay stated [[Lost River Sucker]] formerly populated [[Sheepy Lake]] (western extension of [[Lower Klamath Lake]]) and spawned in [[Sheepy Creek]], a short spring-fed stream
- Local residents took large numbers in spring from "Sucker Hole" using chicken wire traps and snag gear for human consumption and hog feed
- Fishery continued until before 1924, when thousands of acres of lakes and marshland in [[Lower Klamath Lake]]-[[Tule Lake]] area were drained and converted to farmland
- Reclamation of [[Sheepy Lake]] resulted in heavy fish mortality, including large suckers
- [[Lower Klamath Lake]] (including [[Sheepy Lake]]) was later reflooded after futile farming efforts
- Joseph Allen observed a few large fish in [[Sheepy Creek]] in spring 1960, suggesting possible remnant spawning population

**Current status concerns:**
Carl E. Bond reported difficulty obtaining [[Shortnose Sucker]] specimens from [[Upper Klamath Lake]], suggesting possible population decline from formerly abundant status reported by Cope (1879).

## Key Findings

1. **First California records:** [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) documented in California for first time, adding both species to state's freshwater fish fauna.

2. **Collection data:** [[Shortnose Sucker]] first collected from [[Boles Creek]], [[Modoc County]] on May 15, 1955. Both species first collected from [[Copco Lake]], [[Siskiyou County]] on November 20, 1956.

3. **Size ranges in Copco Lake:**
   - [[Lost River Sucker]]: 19.3-27.4 inches fork length, 4.7-11.1 pounds (3 specimens)
   - [[Shortnose Sucker]]: 17.3-20.1 inches fork length, 2.6-3.1 pounds (4 specimens)

4. **Taxonomic verification:** Specimens verified by Carl E. Bond (Oregon State University). Three *Deltistes* and one *Chasmistes* deposited at California Academy of Sciences with complete meristic data provided.

5. **Morphological distinctions:** [[Lost River Sucker]] distinguished by larger size (up to 3 feet), terminal oblique mouth with papillae on lips, and unarmed gill rakers. [[Shortnose Sucker]] has smaller head, cylindrical body, thin lips without papillae, and gill rakers fringed with fine teeth.

6. **Reproductive data:** Two female [[Shortnose Sucker]] (19.3 inches fork length) contained 36,763 and 56,217 eggs respectively.

7. **Age determination difficulties:** Scale analysis inconclusive for age determination in both species. Annual growth characters confusing and variable; opercular bone bands showed no relationship to scale patterns.

8. **Historical abundance and decline:** Both species historically abundant in [[Tule Lake]] and [[Lower Klamath Lake]], supporting indigenous and settler fisheries until 1920s. Extensive lake drainage and reclamation for agriculture caused heavy mortality and apparent extirpation from California portions of range except [[Copco Lake]] and possibly remnant population in [[Sheepy Creek]].

9. **Spawning observations:** [[Shortnose Sucker]] observed in apparent spawning migration in [[Boles Creek]] (May 1960). No spawning activity or young observed in [[Copco Lake]] despite regular occurrence of both species, particularly in fall.

10. **Nomenclatural history:** [[Lost River Sucker]] originally described as *Chasmistes luxatus* (Cope 1879), later *Catostomus rex* (Eigenmann 1891), then *Deltistes luxatus* (Seale 1896), recently designated *Catostomus luxatus* (American Fisheries Society 1960).

11. **Biogeographic significance:** Presence of *Chasmistes* in [[Klamath Drainage]] and [[Great Basin]] lakes suggests historical hydrographic connections between these systems during Pleistocene.

12. **Utilization:** Flesh described as somewhat coarse but pleasant flavor. [[Lost River Sucker]] provides spring sport fishery in [[Upper Klamath Lake]], Oregon, where fish are snagged during spawning runs.
