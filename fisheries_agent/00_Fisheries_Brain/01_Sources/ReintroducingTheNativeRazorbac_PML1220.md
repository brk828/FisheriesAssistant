# Reintroducing Native Fishes: Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This document, a bilingual (English and Spanish) U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service paper by James E. Johnson, Endangered Species Biologist, describes the first year (1981) of an effort to reintroduce the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] into historic habitats of the [[Colorado River Basin]] in Arizona. The approach was chosen because the species' adults could not be stocked directly and their spawning in the wild was questionable. Adults were taken from [[Lake Mohave]] and spawned at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, producing roughly 20,000 young for the 1981 year class. Stockings in June, July and September 1981 placed about 15,100 juveniles in tributary and mainstem sites on the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]. Only one stocked fish had been recaptured by the time of writing. The paper also frames the project as a possible model for other native fish recovery, tied to a [[Memorandum Of Understanding]] (MOU) with the state.

## Background & Description

**Species:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid-type native fish of the Colorado River system. The document does not give its family.

**Historical range:** In Arizona the species historically inhabited the Colorado, Gila, Salt, Verde and San Pedro rivers and Tonto Creek (Minckley and Deacon 1968; Minckley 1973). Its distribution was generally below 4,000 feet elevation. Razorback suckers disappeared from the Gila River basin in the mid-1950s.

**Current status:** The [[Lake Mohave]] population consisted of large, old adults apparently unable to reproduce successfully. In the upper Colorado River basin, more than two years of intensive field work by the Service's Colorado River Fish Project yielded fewer than 40 specimens, all old fish.

**Reintroduction methods considered:**
- (1) Stocking adult fish directly. This would reduce predation by non-native fish but depends on a large source of healthy adults that could spawn successfully in the release waters. Neither condition could be met.
- (2) Moving adults into a hatchery, spawning them there and stocking their offspring. This was chosen.

**Hatchery operations:** On January 15, 1981, 135 adult razorback suckers were collected from Lake Mohave and shipped to Dexter National Fish Hatchery. Mean total length was 57.7 cm (range 50.0–59.0 cm) and mean weight was 2.6 kg (range 1.4–4.2 kg). Many fish were ripe at collection. Spawning followed [[Toney (1974)]] methods. About 80% of the adults were blind in one or both eyes, and many adults and eggs were lost during handling. Approximately 20,000 young were eventually produced for the 1981 year class.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline:** [[Habitat Alteration]] was identified as the principal cause of decline throughout the Colorado River basin, especially in lower basin areas such as the lower Gila and Salt rivers, which are now almost totally dry. Habitat change may explain only part of the loss above the uppermost dams on the Salt, Gila and Verde rivers. Estimated usable habitat above these dams included:
- About 60 miles of stream above [[Horseshoe Reservoir]] on the Verde River
- About 40 miles above [[Lake Roosevelt]] on the Salt River
- At least 20 miles above [[San Carlos Reservoir]] on the Gila River

No data explain the disappearance from these upper reaches. The author notes that the habitat must have been unsuitable at the time of loss, but the true cause may never be known.

**Predation:** Predation by non-native fish, including [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), was a major threat. Predation on newly stocked razorback suckers was observed almost immediately after the June stocking.

**Upper basin:** Preliminary data suggest that losses in the upper Colorado River basin (Utah and Colorado) may have been at least as extensive as in the lower basin. Those states had not yet agreed to stocking agreements at the time of writing, though New Mexico signed an MOU on October 20, 1981, to stock fish into the [[San Juan River]].

**Reintroduction philosophy:** The author calls the stocking approach the "[[Johnny Appleseed Approach]]" (Johnson 1980a), in which the organism itself tests whether habitats are suitable. This works only if potential habitat remains and large numbers of offspring can be readily obtained. Environmental effects were expected to be few to nonexistent because only historic habitats were targeted.

**Site selection:** Tributary streams were chosen first because they probably mimic natural nursery habitat and host fewer large predatory fish. The sites were [[Eagle Creek]] (Gila River drainage), [[Cherry Creek]] (Salt River), and [[West Clear Creek]] and [[Oak Creek]] (Verde River). Mainstem sites were also used for the later stockings.

## Key Findings

**1. Reintroduction strategy.** The hatchery-based method was chosen over direct adult stocking, which was constrained by the lack of healthy, spawning-capable adults.

**2. Stocking results, 1981.** Three stockings put roughly 15,100 young razorback suckers into Arizona waters:

| Date | Number stocked | Mean length | Mean weight | Sites |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| June 30 and July 1, 1981 | 7,000 | 8.1 cm (range 5.9–11.3 cm) | 6 g | Eagle Creek, Gila River (Guthrie), Cherry Creek, West Clear Creek, Oak Creek |
| September 8–9, 1981 | 8,100 | 12.6 cm (range 7.9–17.7 cm) | 19.5 g (range 4.5–60.7 g) | Gila River (Guthrie, below Bonita Creek confluence), Salt River (Horseshoe Bend, Highway 288 Bridge), Verde River (Childs) |

The Gila River mainstem site (Guthrie) was used because the planned nursery stream, Bonita Creek, had too low a flow.

**3. Recapture.** Only one of the 15,100 fish stocked in 1981 had been recaptured by the time of writing: a 17.1 cm individual found in an irrigation ditch near Salome, Arizona, on November 11, 1981.

**4. Lake Mohave genetic stock.** About 4,000 young fish were planned for stocking into Lake Mohave in early 1982. This is a separate effort to maintain a genetic stock for future recovery, because the existing Lake Mohave fish are aged, senescent and largely non-reproductive. Estimates of their age vary: McAda and Wydowski (1980) estimated a maximum of 9 years for upper-basin fish but questioned their findings, while Minckley (1973) suggested 30–45 years for Lake Mohave fish. The author proposed that a stocking program through 1990 could provide genetic material through at least 2020.

**5. Monitoring.** The program was to be evaluated annually for 15 years. The 1981 fish were marked with [[Tetracycline Marking]] (Weber and Ridgway 1962). Lake Mohave stockings were to have a pelvic fin clipped and a liquid-nitrogen brand applied to the wound to prevent regeneration. Future stockings were to be marked with a [[Coded Wire Tag]], a nose tag commonly used for salmonids.

**6. Policy outcome.** The MOU between the Service and Arizona was intended to lead to re-establishment of razorback suckers in the Gila River drainage. The MOU requires the Service not to list the species while reintroduction continues and appears successful. This "in lieu of listing" approach was seen as breaking a deadlock that had blocked earlier proposals and as a way to allow reintroduction of other listed fishes under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**7. Related reintroductions.** Soon after the razorback sucker agreement, the Service, Arizona and the U.S. Forest Service signed a similar agreement to reintroduce the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), with planting to begin in early 1982. In October and November 1981, about 4,500 young [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) were also stocked into Lake Mohave (the count is partly garbled in the scan).

**8. Broader message.** The author argues that reintroducing native fish need not end sport fishing or other activities, and that native fish are a valuable public resource worth conserving.
