## Executive Summary

This paper (De Vos, Snoeks & Thys van den Audenaerde, 1990) documents a historical case study of catastrophic faunal turnover in [[Lake Luhondo]], Rwanda, following the introduction of [[Tilapia]] species. Prior to 1934, the lake's fish fauna was dominated by three native cyprinid species: a small, abundant barbel (*[[Barbus neumayeri]]*, previously described as *Barbus luhondo*) and two larger, less common cyprinids, *[[Barbus microbarbis]]* and *[[Varicorhinus ruandae]]*. Between 1935 and 1938, colonial farmers and later the Belgian colonial authorities introduced young *Tilapia nilotica* (now *[[Oreochromis niloticus]]*) from Lake Bunyoni, Uganda, likely accompanied by an accidental co-introduction of *[[Haplochromis]]*. By 1952, a limnological survey confirmed the two large cyprinid species had vanished entirely from the lake, and by the 1980s the smaller *B. neumayeri* had become extremely rare, surviving only in small tributary streams. The authors conclude that the Tilapia introduction — and possibly the concurrent accidental introduction of Haplochromis — drove the native cyprinid fauna to local extinction or near-extinction, challenging the prevailing assumption that Tilapia introductions are ecologically benign.

## Background & Description

- **[[Barbus neumayeri]]** (syn. *Barbus luhondo* Pappenheim & Boulenger, 1914): A small-bodied barbel; noted by original 1907 collectors as not growing to a larger size. Previously treated as a subspecies of *Barbus kerstenii* (Greenwood 1962), now considered a junior synonym of *B. neumayeri*.
- **[[Barbus microbarbis]]** (David & Poll, 1937): Known from a single holotype specimen (MRAC 41847). Exhibits morphological characters intermediate between *Barbus ruasae* and *Varicorhinus ruandae*, raising the possibility that it represents a hybrid rather than a valid species, per Banister (1973).
- **[[Varicorhinus ruandae]]** (Pappenheim & Boulenger, 1914): A larger cyprinid species, originally described from the Mkunga locality near Ruasa in the Mukungwa drainage (the outlet of Lake Luhondo).
- **[[Tilapia nilotica]]** (*Oreochromis niloticus* strain): The introduced cichlid, locally named "samaki" (Swahili for "fish," not Kinyarwanda) — linguistic evidence corroborating its introduced, non-native status.
- **[[Haplochromis]] sp.**: Juvenile morphology reportedly easily confused with juvenile Tilapia by non-specialists, which is central to the hypothesis of its accidental co-introduction.
- Additional native species noted with restricted microhabitat morphology/ecology: *[[Clarias liocephalus]]* (small catfish) and *[[Amphilius uranoscopus]]* (small catfish restricted to cold, fast-flowing rivulets), and *[[Pseudocrenilabrus multicolor]]* (small cichlid restricted to weedy marginal creeks/pools).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake system**: [[Lake Luhondo]] and neighboring [[Lake Bulera]], Rwanda, part of the Nyabarongo/Mukungwa drainage system (Congo basin via Nyabarongo).
- **Pre-1934 baseline**: Cyprinid-dominated assemblage; no Tilapia or Haplochromis present in either lake. *B. neumayeri* was very common in the open lake; *B. microbarbis* and *V. ruandae* were less common large-bodied cyprinids.
- **Tributary refugia**: *B. neumayeri* now persists only in small inflowing tributaries (e.g., Kabaya River) rather than the main lake body — indicating a habitat contraction/refuge effect following the introduction event.
- **Post-introduction assemblage (by 1952 and today)**: Dominated by introduced *Tilapia nilotica* and *Haplochromis* sp.; native large cyprinids (*B. microbarbis*, *V. ruandae*) locally extirpated from the lake proper, though *V. ruandae* persists in effluent rivers of the Nyabarongo system.
- **Water quality shift**: Verbal reports suggest a historical shift from clear water with abundant submerged vegetation (still characteristic of Lake Bulera) to comparatively turbid water (transparency 30–50 cm) with reduced submerged vegetation in Lake Luhondo — a possible contributing/co-occurring ecological factor, though causally unconfirmed.
- **Fisheries context**: No significant traditional fisheries targeted the native cyprinids before Tilapia introduction, ruling out overfishing as a driver of cyprinid decline. European gillnet fisheries developed only after the successful Tilapia introduction.
- **Anthropogenic hydrology**: The Rwasa Dam (built 1982) altered the Mukungwa outflow, but this postdates the major faunal changes and is deemed not relevant to the observed cyprinid decline.

## Key Findings

1. **Documented species turnover**: Historical collections (1907, 1934) versus modern surveys (1980–1986) demonstrate that Lake Luhondo shifted from a cyprinid-dominated fauna to a Tilapia/Haplochromis-dominated fauna within roughly two decades of Tilapia introduction.
2. **Introduction timeline**: Young *Tilapia nilotica* were transported from Lake Bunyoni, Uganda (where they had been introduced in 1927/1930) to Lakes Luhondo and Bulera between 1935–1938, initially by private farmers and later formally sponsored by Belgian colonial authorities.
3. **Accidental co-introduction hypothesis**: Since no *Haplochromis* were ever deliberately introduced, the authors argue juvenile Haplochromis were unintentionally transported along with young Tilapia stock due to visual similarity between juveniles of the two genera.
4. **Extinction/extirpation of native cyprinids**: By H. Damas's 1952 limnological survey, large cyprinids (*B. microbarbis*, *V. ruandae*) had already disappeared from the lake and were forgotten by local populations; *B. microbarbis* (or its parent species/hybrid) is now considered extinct in Lake Luhondo.
5. **Taxonomic uncertainty**: *B. microbarbis* status remains unresolved — it may be a valid but critically rare species known from a single specimen, or a hybrid between *V. ruandae* and an unidentified *Barbus* species.
6. **Rejection of alternative causal explanations**: Overfishing and dam construction (Rwasa Dam, 1982) are both ruled out as causes, since the cyprinid decline predates both factors substantially.
7. **Broader conservation implication**: This case directly contradicts the common assumption that Tilapia introductions are ecologically neutral, aligning instead with Welcomme's (1988) findings on Tilapia-driven native fish replacement (e.g., Cameroon's Noum marshes), and serves as a cautionary example against unregulated Tilapia translocation in tropical freshwater systems.
