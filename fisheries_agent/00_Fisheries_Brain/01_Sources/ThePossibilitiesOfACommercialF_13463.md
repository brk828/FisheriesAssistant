# Commercial Fishery Potential in TVA Impoundments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1944 journal article by [[Clarence M. Tarzwell]] of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] assesses whether the main-stream reservoirs of the [[Tennessee River]] can support a commercial fishery, particularly for coarse fishes, to supply protein during wartime food shortages. The author argues that impoundment has increased fish productivity, that the sport fishery is declining as coarse species increase, and that legalized netting would both yield food and control competing rough fish. Recommendations include expanding commercial fishing immediately under discretionary state management.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Wartime meat scarcity and disruption of marine fisheries (submarine warfare, fleet diverted to patrol) increased reliance on freshwater resources. The [[Great Lakes]] were partially depleted.
- **Reservoir system:** The [[Wilson Reservoir]] was created in 1924 and taken over by the TVA in 1933. The TVA planned 9 main-river and 14 storage reservoirs, with about 600,000 acres of combined area. The nine main-stream reservoirs were to cover about 440,000 acres and roughly 6,800 miles of shoreline along a 635-mile stretch of river.
- **Multipurpose operation:** Reservoirs serve flood control, navigation, power and national defense. Winter drawdowns occur during the non-growing season, and [[Guntersville Reservoir]] fluctuations are limited to 2 feet.
- **Legal context:** The Alabama Legislature's 1935 act prohibited netting in impoundment waters, so commercial fishers were limited to setlines and snaglines. Netting was prohibited in all forms there, and no closed season existed in Alabama before 1943.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Productivity:** Fish populations were large in the reservoirs. Fyke-net catches in [[Wheeler Reservoir]] in its fifth impoundment year averaged about 9 times those in the former main river at the Watts Bar site, and over 10 times those in the [[Holston River]].
- **No decline with age:** Wilson Reservoir remained productive after 18 years, and its 1940 yield was the highest of the four lower reservoirs. The author attributes the difference from irrigation reservoirs to rapid water turnover, nutrient inputs (especially phosphates from farmland), and multipurpose operation.
- **Habitat loss:** Impoundment largely eliminated mussel beds and reduced stream species such as rock bass, Kentucky bass, certain redhorses and minnows.
- **Backwater ecology:** Shallow protected backwaters function mainly as sunfish nurseries. Shallow flats hold more legal game and panfish per unit volume than deeper areas.
- **Species shift:** Coarse species such as [[Common Carp]] and [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] gained dominance, with [[Black Crappie]] and [[White Crappie]] surging in the catch. The author links this to competition with juvenile game fish and to protection of coarse species from netting, not to predation.
- **Fish meal inputs:** Gar, shad and offal from edible species could be processed into fish meal and oil.

## Key Findings

**1. Fishing pressure and yield**
- Sport and getline fishers took an average of 25 pounds per acre in 1940 across Guntersville, Wheeler, Wilson and Pickwick, versus 1.8 pounds per acre in the Great Lakes (1939 data).
- The commercial census estimated over 1,200,000 pounds were taken by commercial fishers in 1940, and about 5 million pounds total (all fishers) in the four lower reservoirs.
- Yields by reservoir: Pickwick 15 lb/acre (3rd year), Wilson 32 lb/acre (16th year).
- Over 1,200,000 fisherman-days were recorded; over 60 per cent were bank fishers, who mainly caught coarse fish.

**2. Creel census trends (sport fishing)**
- Wheeler catch per hour rose from 0.4 (1939) to 0.9 fish (1941); average catch per person rose from 2.6 to 4.8 fish.
- Game fish share of the Wheeler catch fell from 28 per cent (1939) to 20 per cent (1940) to 9 per cent (1941).
- Panfish share rose from 42 to 60 to 86 per cent over the same years.
- Wilson game fish comprised 3 per cent of the sport catch in 1940 and 1941.

**3. Backwater population studies (Wheeler Reservoir, 1941)**
- Five backwater areas totaling about 34.3 acres were isolated with barrier seines and treated with rotenone.
- 282,852 fish weighing 19,765 pounds were recovered, excluding Gambusia and brook silversides.
- Average population: 8,246 fish weighing 576 pounds per acre. Peak density reached 831 pounds per acre in waters only 2 to 3 feet deep.
- Coarse fishes made up 82.5 per cent of total weight; game fishes 3.0 per cent, panfishes 9.6 per cent, food fishes 4.9 per cent.

**4. Recruitment and stocking**
- Sunfish fingerling-to-legal ratio was 142:1 overall (277:1 in Upper Railroad Pond).
- Over 126 million fingerlings would be needed to raise legal sunfish 10 per cent in Wheeler, so stocking is considered impractical.
- Bass fingerling ratios fell from over 100:1 in spring to 10:1 by September, indicating high mortality. The author recommends stocking legal-sized bass the year before impoundment.

**5. Commercial potential**
- Middle Railroad Pond held about 708 pounds per acre of total fish, about 428 pounds marketable.
- Powerline Slough held 723 pounds per acre, about 375 pounds marketable.
- A sustainable yield of 50 pounds per acre was judged conservative, equal to about 22 million pounds annually for the lower reservoirs. Including gar and gizzard shad would raise the total about 50 per cent.
- A spoonbill fishery on Wilson produced over 600,000 pounds in 11 months (39 pounds per acre).

**6. Gear evaluation**
- Setlines and snaglines (the only legal gear) are selective for catfish and spoonbill but yield too little coarse fish for control. Snagline spoonbill fishing produced about a million pounds annually.
- Fyke nets are selective for crappie and bluegill and are not effective for coarse fish. Wings raised catches (for example, 15.9 to 23.7 fish per set in Middle Wheeler).
- Large-mesh gill nets below dams were effective.
- Seines were the best method for carp and coarse fish, with 92 hauls showing coarse fish at 81 per cent of catch weight, though only one-fourth of fish were marketable size.
- Trammel nets, otter trawls, purse seines and trap nets remain untested.

**7. Markets and processing**
- Experimental fish meal from about 7,000 pounds yielded 13 pounds meal and 15 pounds oil per 100 pounds of fish and waste. The meal was about 55 per cent protein.
- Canned carp compared favorably with salmon in fish cakes and salad.

**8. Recommendations**
- Legalize all forms of netting, with state conservation commissions holding discretionary powers over gear, mesh size, timing and location.
- Harvest gar and gizzard shad along with coarse fish to prevent their dominance.
- Adopt closed seasons, stabilized water levels during spawning, and pre-impoundment adult stocking to favor game fish.
- Expand the commercial fishery on TVA main-stream reservoirs immediately to supply protein during the war.
