# Spikedace and Loach Minnow Study Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #species-interactions #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This proposal, submitted by Arizona State University to the Arizona Game and Fish Department in July 1986, outlines a one-year study to evaluate potential interactions between imperiled native [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda fulgida]]) and [[Loach Minnow]] ([[Tiaroga cobitis]]) versus introduced [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis lutrensis]]) in the Southwest. Both native species are endemic to the [[Gila River]] basin and have experienced substantial reductions in geographic range and population size. The study emphasizes comparative trophic relations and microhabitat use across three major river systems ([[Gila River]], [[Verde River]], [[San Francisco River]]) to describe potential for interactions among target species. The research integrates survey collections with instream manipulative experiments to quantify differences and similarities in resource use, employing univariate and multivariate statistical techniques. The proposal requests $58,350 for the 12-month study period, with potential for continuation over four years.

## Background & Description

[[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] represent monotypic genera endemic to streams of the [[Gila River]] basin of southwestern North America. [[Spikedace]] is a midwater inhabitant of turbulent zones associated with inflow of riffles into pools, while [[Loach Minnow]] typically occupies shallow riffles with gravel/cobble bottoms and seasonally profuse growths of algae. Each species has experienced substantial reductions in geographic range and population size stemming from stream alterations and interactions with introduced fish species.

[[Spikedace]] was listed as threatened by the U.S. Department of Interior (1986) under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. An identical listing was proposed for [[Loach Minnow]] (USDI 1985). Once widely distributed in the [[Gila River]] basin above Phoenix, [[Meda fulgida]] by 1986 was known from only a few localities in Arizona (upper [[Verde River]], [[Aravaipa Creek]], and [[Eagle Creek]]). In New Mexico it occurred in few scattered localities in the [[Gila River]] basin above Redrock. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] occurred in Arizona only in [[Aravaipa Creek]], the upper [[White River]], and the [[Blue River|Blue]] and [[San Francisco River]]s above their confluence.

[[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis lutrensis]]) is a stream-adapted minnow introduced and established in the mainstream [[Colorado River]] either by accident or as forage. The species dispersed or was transported as baitfish into the [[Gila River]] system, and by the 1960s was invading upstream. Minckley and Deacon (1968) first noted disappearance of both [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] where [[Red Shiner]] became abundant. Ranges of [[Red Shiner]] and the two indigenous species remained almost mutually exclusive; the endemics progressively retreated to ever higher elevations as the shiner extended its range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Stream discharge in southern Arizona and New Mexico is typically low in autumn and spring-early summer and high in late summer and winter; flood conditions in the last two seasons preclude effective studies. The proposal notes that native fish abundance and diversity in the [[Gila River]] basin are inversely correlated with abundance and diversity of exotic species. However, it remained unknown whether presence of exotics was the direct cause of the decrease in native faunas, or whether both exotic and native faunas were simply responding independently in different ways to altered environments.

Alarming declines in southwestern fishes began with physical alterations of streams for agricultural/industrial and power development, coupled with unbridled human population growth in arid western North America. Lotic-adapted native fishes do poorly and soon disappear from impoundments, and regulated flows downstream from reservoirs similarly tend to extirpate or reduce their populations. In contrast, modification and stabilization of erosive streams in this region seemingly enhance introduced fishes.

The proposal identifies four broad categories of potential biological interactions between [[Red Shiner]] versus [[Spikedace]] and/or [[Loach Minnow]]:

1. Direct competition for similar resources
2. Interference by [[Red Shiner]] with foraging activities or microhabitat use of the native species
3. Lowered reproductive success by the native species due to spawning interference or predation by [[Red Shiner]] on eggs or larvae
4. Community-level shifts in resource use among native species in the presence of [[Red Shiner]]

## Key Findings

**Proposed Research Objectives:**

1. Determine if [[Loach Minnow]] and/or [[Spikedace]] interact directly with [[Red Shiner]] and other exotics and elucidate the nature of any such interactions (e.g., predation, competition, interference)
2. Describe and quantify the extent of any interspecific interactions (e.g., extent of dietary overlap, extent of predation mortality, reproductive success, habitat displacement)
3. Elucidate and quantify physical and chemical habitat requirements and preferences of all life history stages of native species, especially [[Meda]] and [[Tiaroga]], as well as those of exotics
4. Describe and quantify spatial and temporal aspects of occurrence of any interspecific and/or habitat interactions
5. Relate findings to the pattern of historic decline of the native species and construct hypotheses regarding the mechanisms by which the decline has occurred
6. Suggest management methods which would act to curtail or reverse present trends toward decline of native fish faunas

**Study Design:**

Three major study sites were proposed:
- Mainstream [[Gila River]] (apparent, ongoing, and progressive displacement of both native species by [[Red Shiner]])
- [[Verde River]] (progressive replacement of [[Spikedace]] by [[Red Shiner]] until extreme headwaters were reached, wherein a long-term co-occurrence had been recorded)
- [[San Francisco River]] (apparent, ongoing, and progressive replacement of [[Loach Minnow]])

**Methodology:**

Field studies would begin within the first four weeks after project initiation, with sampling of all known habitats of [[Spikedace]] and/or [[Loach Minnow]] to obtain an "instantaneous" data base for population sizes, distributions, and basic ecology. Another collection would be made six months later to monitor changes, obtain seasonal comparisons, and confirm residency of populations.

Three different kinds of collecting sites would be established within each study reach:
1. General sites for limited numbers (20 individuals per species) of threatened species and larger samples of [[Red Shiner]] and associates (minima of 30) for analysis of food habits
2. Three stream segments 50 to 100 m long in each study reach, each separated by 100 to 600 m, mapped for habitat types, depths, currents, substrate distribution
3. Instream species manipulations through construction of paired enclosures including foods, microhabitats, and other resources typical for each target species

Fish samples would be closely linked with collections of potential foods (benthic and drifting algae and invertebrates). Triplicate samples of benthic invertebrates would be taken at each sampling site by 1.0-m, 1.0-mm-mesh kick nets. Drifting invertebrates and other potential foods would be sampled at midday and near midnight to assess diel variation.

Microhabitat use would be evaluated by direct observation (snorkeling), seining, or electrofishing. Underwater observation by snorkeling would provide information on precise habitat relations (position relative to physical and biological features) of each species. During times of turbidity, fishes would be sampled by limited-space seining or electroshocking from 50 to 100 small (not to exceed 15 m²/area), homogenous areas.

Instream species manipulation experiments would use split habitats and/or differentially treated individual pools to quantify effects of [[Red Shiner]] on [[Spikedace]] and/or [[Loach Minnow]]. Discrete habitats of 10 to 20 m lengths would be blocked upstream and downstream and split longitudinally by open-mesh screening. Natural levels of fish abundance would be maintained, with [[Spikedace]] and/or [[Loach Minnow]] on one side and one or both native species plus [[Red Shiner]] on the other.

**Data Analysis:**

Patterns of food and microhabitat partitioning or niche overlap would be statistically assessed and ordinated by both univariate and multivariate techniques. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on geographic location and season would be conducted for each species. Principal component analysis (PCA) would provide estimates of dietary differences for native species syntopic and allotopic to shiner. Niche breadth and overlap indices would be calculated following established methods.

**Budget:**

Total contract cost: $58,350
- Personnel: $22,514 (including Graduate Research Assistant and two Field/Laboratory Technicians)
- Operations: $6,750 (field and laboratory supplies, computer charges, equipment maintenance)
- Travel: $7,014 (mileage and per diem)
- Equipment: $2,595 (current meter and options)
- Subcontracts: $5,500 (consultant services)
- Indirect costs: $13,977 (31.5% of direct costs)

**Timeline:**

The proposal requested a starting date of December 1, 1986 (rather than September 1, 1986 as specified in the RFP) to allow emphasis on the reproductive season for data collection. Field collections, observations, and experimentation would occur February-May (June), with data analysis May-July (August), and final report preparation August-October (November), with final report transmittal by December 31, 1987.
