# Late Pliocene-Early Pleistocene Fish Fauna of the Lake Chapala Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #fossil-fishes #paleoclimate

## Executive Summary

This paleontological study describes a seven-species fish fauna in six families from late Pliocene-early Pleistocene deposits at the [[Jocotepec Quarry]], a gravel pit about 5 km west of Jocotepec, Jalisco, in the [[Lake Chapala Basin]] of the [[Mesa Central]], Mexico. Six species still live in the basin today; the seventh, *Micropterus relictus*, is a new, extinct large black bass described from a dentary and premaxilla. The authors infer that the fauna lived in shallow lake-margin or stream water, and that the regional climate since the early Pleistocene was probably not much different from today. The centrarchid record also extends the known southern range of the family well beyond its recent limits.

## Background & Description

The study is framed by the strongly endemic fish fauna of the uplifted Mexican Plateau, whose southern portion, the Mesa Central, is considered a probable center of origin for several fish groups. [[Lake Chapala]] is the largest lake in Mexico (about 80 km long and 20 km wide). Its basin was formed by graben faulting and uplift during the mid-Pleistocene, which tilted and broke the sediments of a pre-uplift lake known as the [[Chapala Formation]].

The fossils were collected in 1969, 1971 and 1972 by several collectors, including C.D. Barbour, R.J. Douglass, T. Cavender, R.R. Miller, F.H. Miller and N.A. Neff. The Jocotepec section is a roughly 15 m exposure of unconsolidated, coarse, water-rounded volcanic sediment with at least two erosional surfaces. It includes cross-bedded [[Pumice Conglomerate]], laminated lacustrine shale, and an ochreous sand containing basalt boulders. Fish fossils occur at many horizons in the middle and lower parts of the section. The site also yielded a frog or toad, a turtle, a bird, *Mammuthus*, *Neotoma* and *Sigmodon*. The horse genus *Nannippus* suggests a Pliocene age, while the rest of the fauna correlates with the late Pleistocene lake-floor fauna.

Seven fish species in six families are documented, based on material housed in the University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology (UMMP):

- **Moxostoma** (Catostomidae): one large sucker, close to 500 mm standard length, possibly from the subgenus *Scartomyzon*; not identified to species.
- **Algansea tincella** (Cyprinidae): a moderate-sized minnow reaching 15 cm, a lake-dwelling form.
- **Yuriria alta** (Cyprinidae): a large minnow reaching 35 cm, widely distributed in Mesa Central lakes and streams.
- **Ictalurus dugesi** (Ictaluridae): a catfish that is abundant in Chapala today; the species was documented from multiple pectoral spines and mandibles.
- **Chapalichthys encaustus** (Goodeidae): a quiet-water goodeid, now among the most abundant goodeids in Lake Chapala.
- **Chirostoma lucius** (Atherinidae): a silverside; ten *Chirostoma* species occur in Lake Chapala today.
- **Micropterus relictus** (Centrarchidae): an extinct new species described by Cavender and Smith.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sedimentary record contrasts two phases. The Jocotepec section represents a period of uplift, erosion and deposition, with fast-moving water, high relief and intense volcanism. By contrast, the sediments north of Chapala are mostly fine, well-sorted material deposited in quiet water. The Jocotepec sediments are interpreted as stream and lake-margin deposits at the periphery of the ancient Chapala basin, with fractured layers indicating uplift before and during deposition.

Whether pluvial episodes or changes in drainage explain the terraces north of Chapala remains debated. Diatom stratigraphy from [[Lake Texcoco]], about 100 km south of Chapala, shows persistent diatom species and thus suggests that northern climatic changes were not strongly reflected in central Mexico.

The authors use the [[Uniformitarianism]] principle to infer fossil ecology from the close morphological similarity between the fossil and living fish. Only slight changes were detected in *Algansea tincella*, *Yuriria alta* and *Ictalurus dugesi*. The lack of refuges for the endemic fauna is taken to show that climate stayed within the fishes' tolerances, with the extinction of *Micropterus* possibly reflecting stress on a peripheral northern-family population rather than a regional climate shift.

The living Lake Chapala fauna is described as relatively depauperate for such a large subtropical lake. Together with high endemism, this suggests that dispersal limits, reinforced by geographic barriers and distance from source faunas, have shaped the biogeographic history. Coastal-lowland fishes are excluded by both geographic and ecological factors.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna and habitat:** Seven fish species in six families were identified. Six still live in the basin; the extinct *Micropterus relictus* is the only exception. The fauna is interpreted as lake-margin or stream fish, and the authors infer that more than one community is represented at the site.
- **Climate stability:** The authors conclude that the climate of the Mesa Central since the early Pleistocene was probably not drastically different from today, based on the survival of the fauna in place and on the absence of refuges for reinvasion.
- **New species:** *Micropterus relictus* Cavender and Smith, n. sp., is a large black bass (maximum size near 600 mm standard length, probable weight over 5.5 kg). The holotype is an incomplete right dentary (UMMP 62499), and the paratype is an incomplete right premaxilla (UMMP 62500). Its diagnosis relies on 14 osteological characters, and its shares many features with *M. dolomieui*.
- **Primitive traits:** Several *M. relictus* characters, including the premaxillary process, the parasphenoid flexure and the dentary pit, are interpreted as primitive for Centrarchidae.
- **Biogeographic significance:** *Micropterus relictus* is the only fossil centrarchid known from outside the family's natural range. Centrarchids were previously thought to reach only as far south as the Rio Conchos basin on the Plateau, so this record indicates a stronger affinity between the Mesa Central fauna and the North American fauna than previously suspected.
- **Geological interpretation:** The authors propose that changes in drainage, rather than only pluvial episodes, may explain the Chapala terraces. Their 10 summarized stratigraphic points include the proposal that the cross-bedded pumice conglomerate could serve as a marker bed between adjacent basins.
- **Ichthyological identifications:** The fossils are referred to species based on osteological characters. The *Ictalurus dugesi* material is considered to represent one species, and the authors synonymize *I. dugesi* and *I. ochoterenai* following Alvarez (1966).
