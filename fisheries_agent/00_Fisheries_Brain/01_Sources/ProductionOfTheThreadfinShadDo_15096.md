# Production of the Threadfin Shad, Dorosoma petenense (Gunther)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae #forage-fish #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This study examined the production efficiency of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in experimental ponds at varying stocking densities (100–1,600 adults per acre) to determine optimal rates for maximum forage production. Total production ranged from 84.5 to 290.0 pounds per acre, with net production from 36.4 to 169.0 pounds per acre. The number of young per surviving parent decreased dramatically from 1,392 at 100 adults/acre to 82 at 1,600 adults/acre, indicating density-dependent reproductive suppression. Threadfin shad spawned from May (76°F) through August when stocked in April. Comparisons with [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) showed similar growth rates but gizzard shad spawned two months earlier. When stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), young-of-the-year threadfin shad were completely eliminated by predation.

## Background & Description

The [[Threadfin Shad]] has been widely introduced outside its natural range as supplemental forage for piscivorous game species in large reservoirs. However, evaluation of these introductions has been difficult due to challenges in detecting population changes in large water bodies. This study used small experimental ponds (0.25–1.0 acre) to assess threadfin shad production potential and suitability as forage.

**Spawning Biology**: Previous research established that threadfin shad spawn in spring and early summer. In Oklahoma, rapid ovarian development began in March at 53.4°F, with spawning commencing mid-April at 67°F and peaking by mid-May at 75°F (Shelton, 1964). Multiple spawning periods have been documented—May and September in California (Kimsey et al., 1957), with evidence that both adults and young-of-year can spawn by July. Fecundity estimates range from 6,700–12,400 eggs for 4-inch fish (Burns, 1966) to approximately 22,000 mature ova for 6-inch specimens (Kilambi and Baglin, 1969).

**Growth**: In [[Lake Havasu]], California, threadfin shad grew approximately one inch per month until reaching three inches. At first annulus formation, lengths ranged 1.8–3.4 inches. Few individuals exceed two years of age or seven inches in length (Parsons and Kimsey, 1954).

**Diet**: Threadfin shad are planktivorous, feeding equally on phytoplankton and zooplankton. Miller (1961) identified protozoa, microcrustacea, and several genera of Chlorophyta and Chrysophyta as important food items. Beers (1965) listed nonfilamentous green algae and rotifers as most important, with organic debris most abundant volumetrically.

**Temperature Tolerance**: Temperatures below 41°F are typically fatal (Parsons et al., 1954; Strawn, 1965), making this the primary limiting factor for establishing populations outside the natural range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Forage Value**: Threadfin shad constitute significant portions of piscivorous game fish diets (Table 1 in original). For [[Largemouth Bass]] 6.0–10.0 inches, threadfin shad comprised 72.5% of stomach contents by volume in California (Goodson, 1965). For larger bass (>12 inches), the proportion was 67.9% in Kentucky (Anderson, 1966). [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*) 6.5–11.9 inches consumed 47.2% threadfin shad. [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) 21–24 inches had 94% threadfin shad in their diet (Goodson, 1964). Threadfin shad are particularly important during fall and winter months.

**Population Dynamics in Natural Systems**: Following introduction of 575 three-inch threadfin shad into 40,000-acre [[Lake Martin]], Alabama in August 1957, population studies 18 months later estimated 248 million fish weighing 1.784 million pounds—a dramatic expansion. In [[Lake Havasu]], California, 1,020 threadfin shad introduced in 1954 populated the entire [[Colorado River]] from Davis Dam to the Mexican border within 18 months (Kimsey et al., 1957).

**Relative Abundance with Gizzard Shad**: In Alabama waters (146 population studies, 1949–1963), where both species co-occur, gizzard shad predominate. In rivers, threadfin shad averaged 9.2 lbs/acre (1.01% E-value) versus gizzard shad at 42.6 lbs/acre (5.89% E-value). In impoundments, threadfin shad averaged 44.1 lbs/acre (9.72% E-value) versus gizzard shad at 208.4 lbs/acre (41.51% E-value). Both species are more abundant in reservoirs than rivers.

## Key Findings

**Experimental Methods**: Adult threadfin shad were collected from [[Chewacla Creek]] (tributary of [[Tallapoosa River]]), [[Tallapoosa River]] near Tallassee, and [[Coosa River]] below [[Lake Jordan]] dam. Fish were transported in aluminum tanks with compressed oxygen, using tricaine methanesulphonate (MS-222) at 1 gram per 12 gallons and 0.5% salt to reduce mortality. Mortality rates were 4–9% with anesthetic and salt versus ~90% without treatment over 5-mile distances.

**Fecundity**: Six threadfin shad collected April 25, 1965 from Chewacla Creek (6.6–7.0 inches total length) contained estimated egg counts of 14,860–46,720 eggs (mean 22,314 eggs). Ovaries comprised 2.31–18.54% of body weight. Eggs per gram of ovary ranged 5,089–18,032.

**Optimal Stocking Density**: 
- At 100 adults/acre (67 surviving): 93,253 young produced (1,392 young/parent)
- At 200 adults/acre (138 surviving): 48,974 young produced (355 young/parent)
- At 800 adults/acre (556 surviving): 54,510 young produced (98 young/parent)
- At 1,600 adults/acre (1,172 surviving): 96,481 young produced (82 young/parent)

Linear and quadratic regression equations were significant (P<0.010):
- Linear: Y = -96.58 + 93,059.58X
- Quadratic: Y = 91.53 - 11,084.32X + 6,579,353.38X²

Where X = 1/(number of surviving parents per acre) and Y = number of young per parent.

**Spawning Phenology**: Threadfin shad stocked in April spawned from May (76°F) through August. Eggs were found on spawning mats June 1, 17, 22, and July 12, 1966. Fry were present until approximately the second week of August. First spawning occurred in the second week of May at ~76°F water temperature.

**Growth Rates**: Growth was slow at population densities of 70–290 lbs/acre. Shad hatched in May and early June reached maximum 3 inches by mid-October, though the majority were 1–2 inch fish. No apparent difference in growth rate occurred at different population densities when stocked alone. Young threadfin shad and gizzard shad grew at approximately the same rate (Table 6 in original).

**Fertilization vs. Supplemental Feeding**:
- **F-565** (fertilized, 1,000/acre): Net production 36.4 lbs/acre, cost $0.11/lb, 46,440 young/acre, 46.8% adult survival
- **F-1565** (fed Auburn No. 2 pellets, 1,000/acre): Net production 108.6 lbs/acre, cost $1.31/lb, 28,004 young/acre, 57.6% adult survival

Pelleted feed was not utilized by small shad but may have contributed to adult growth and spawning frequency. Benefits did not justify costs. Fertilization alone was more cost-effective ($0.02–0.11/lb vs. $1.31/lb with feeding).

**Threadfin vs. Gizzard Shad** (E-366 vs. F-666):
- Threadfin: 200/acre stocked, 84.5 lbs/acre standing crop, 70.0 lbs/acre net production, 72% adult survival, 48,974 young/acre
- Gizzard: 208/acre stocked, 179.2 lbs/acre standing crop, 85.5 lbs/acre net production, 98.1% adult survival, 14,008 young/acre

Gizzard shad spawned two months earlier (eggs found April 7 at 60°F) but both species showed similar growth rates. Gizzard shad had higher adult survival and standing crop but produced fewer young per acre.

**Predation Experiments**:

1. **With Flathead Catfish** (E-765): Threadfin shad (664/acre) stocked with [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) fry (2,000/acre). Standing crops: 107.6 lbs/acre shad, 47.5 lbs/acre catfish. Only 8.5% catfish survival. F/C ratio 2.27, Y/C ratio 1.52 (lower limits of desirable range). During February 1966 freeze (-3°F, 3-inch ice for 4 days), approximately 20 of 106.8 lbs/acre threadfin shad survived.

2. **With Largemouth Bass and Fathead Minnows** (F-266): 400 threadfin shad/acre, 100 3-inch largemouth bass/acre, 1,000 [[Fathead Minnows]] (*Pimephales promelas*)/acre. Threadfin shad standing crop 69.6 lbs/acre (28% survival). Bass net production 37.0 lbs/acre (58.1% harvestable size ≥0.4 lb). F/C ratio 2.8, Y/C ratio 1.7. Fathead minnows nearly eliminated (0.64 lb/acre recovered).

3. **With Largemouth Bass, Bluegill, and Fathead Minnows** (F-665): 500 threadfin shad/acre, 140 largemouth bass/acre, 1,500 bluegill/acre, 1,000 fathead minnows/acre. Young-of-year threadfin shad completely eliminated by predation. Only 16.8 lbs/acre adult shad recovered (32.8% survival). All largemouth bass reached harvestable size (0.71 lb average) in 229 days. F/C ratio 1.75, Y/C ratio 0.66 (characteristic of overcrowded piscivorous species). Fathead minnows also eliminated (0.30 lb/acre recovered).

**Conclusions**: Optimal stocking rate is ≤100 adults/acre for efficient brood stock utilization. Threadfin shad cannot withstand combined predation pressure from largemouth bass and bluegill in small ponds. While they contribute to bass growth, young-of-year are eliminated before the following spawning season. Threadfin shad are unsuitable for commercial production of piscivorous species in small ponds due to vulnerability to predation and slow growth rates. Fertilization is more cost-effective than supplemental feeding for threadfin shad production.
