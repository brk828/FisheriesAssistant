# Conservation and Management of the Freshwater Turtles of Cuatrociénegas, Coahuila, México

#herpetology #chelonia #emydidae #trionychidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document is a research-fellowship application and its review file, submitted to the [[Wildlife Conservation Society]] (WCS) [[Research Fellowship Program]] in 1995. The applicants, four Mexican biologists based at Mexico's national university, request USD 19,920 for a one-year survey and conservation program for three endemic freshwater turtles in the [[Cuatrociénegas Basin]], Coahuila. The taxa are [[Terrapene coahuila]], [[Apalone ater]] and [[Trachemys scripta taylori]]. The proposal combines a basin-wide census with three strategies: in-situ semi-captive breeding, removal of the invasive [[Kinosternon flavescens]], and water-management habitat restoration.

The file also contains an external review by Wendell L. Minckley (Arizona State University, dated 5 September 1995). Minckley supports the project's need but judges it too broad. He recommends funding two core components and restructuring the rest. Supporting materials include the WCS review form, the suggested proposal outline, program guidelines, the investigators' curricula vitae, and a letter of endorsement from the Faculty of Sciences, UNAM.

## Background & Description

**Terrapene coahuila** (Coahuilan box turtle, "bisagrita") was described in 1944. Brown (1974) produced the only ecological study, based on surveys from 1964 to 1967. Captive breeding has been reported at the [[Jersey Wildlife Preservation Trust]], the Dallas Zoo, the Bronx Zoo, and Guadalajara Zoo (the last being established at the time of writing).

**Apalone ater** (black softshell) was described as *Trionyx ater* by Webb and Legler (1960) from eight males and one female at Poza Tío Cándido, the type locality. Its taxonomic status has been contested:
- Webb (1962) regarded it as a relict population that may be going extinct.
- Winokur (1968) accepted its morphological uniqueness, while noting that the rugose rear carapace margin is the only character that always separates it from *emoryi*.
- Morafka (1977) considered it a race or morph of *A. spinifera*.
- Smith and Smith (1979) placed it in synonymy with *emoryi* as an extinct subspecies, reporting intermediates and apparently breaking-down differential characters.

**Trachemys scripta taylori** has been studied for taxonomy, morphometrics and hybridization, but its ecology, reproduction and captive breeding are essentially unknown. Investigators observed carapace malformations in some specimens, which they attribute to high incubation temperatures or to genetic drift. (The budget page refers to *T. s. venusta*, while the introduction names *taylori*; the proposal does not resolve this.)

**Prior conservation action:** No in-situ turtle conservation had been carried out before the proposal. The basin was declared a protected area in December 1994. The applicants' own long-term work on the three species was unfunded for about ten years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** The basin covers about 1,500 km² and is spring-fed, with ponds and marshes in a porous karst system within the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. The proposal notes that the basin is well watered relative to other Chihuahuan sites.
- **Threats:** Habitat drainage, illegal trade, a severe drought over the previous three years in northern Mexico, and the recent invasion of *Kinosternon flavescens*. Genetic drift is suspected in some localities.
- **Habitat dynamics:** Minckley, with 37 years' experience in the basin, reports that marshes ranging from a few square meters to over a square kilometer repeatedly dry and refill. Turtles follow water levels, persisting in moist depressions or underground refugia during dry periods. He describes the system as "dynamic," with no average conditions.
- **Water management:** Canals cut below water level drain pools and lower adjacent water tables. This affects the karst "plumbing" and downslope habitats, and creates surface channels that may spread invasive species such as the spiny softshell.
- **Biodiversity:** The proposal states that about 50% of the basin's fishes are endemic and highly habitat-restricted, and that almost 100% of its molluscs are endemic and seriously affected by water loss.
- **Population decline:** Brown (1974) reported 40 to 60 *T. coahuila* per marsh acre. The applicants observed similar densities in 1985. A marsh was almost dry and no individuals were found in early May, which suggests that population is probably extinct.

## Key Findings

**Proposal content**
- Objective 1: Survey and assess the status of the three taxa and their habitats, using mark-recapture in stratified quadrants, with population, mortality, survivorship, growth and sex-ratio estimates.
- Objective 2: Establish semi-captive breeding colonies in situ at Rancho Orozco (type locality for *A. ater*), a neighborhood, and the town of Cuatrociénegas. Surplus hatchlings would be released to reinforce wild populations.
- Objective 3: Remove or cull *K. flavescens* from critical localities, especially type localities of *T. coahuila*.
- Objective 4: Restore ponds and marshes by controlling outflow structures, redeviating channels and halting unnecessary water extraction. The applicants state that more water is extracted than local agriculture requires.
- Methods include back-flushing for stomach contents, non-lethal blood and tissue sampling, radio-tracking, and temperature experiments on sex determination in *Trachemys*.
- Education: a local school environmental program, a field biology course at UNAM, articles and media outreach, and ecotourism as an alternative income source.

**Expert review (Minckley, 5 September 1995)**
- The project is important and timely. Minckley questions whether it is too broad unless it is part of a larger CITES effort.
- Restoring water levels by controlling outflows is the most important component. Minckley argues the basin-wide water-table effects are more serious than the pond-level effects.
- Genetic work is needed. Captive breeding should not start without a well-planned genetic survey, and small-population genetic theory must be applied with care in a long-lived species whose populations move with water levels.
- *Apalone ater* needs a thorough ecological, morphological, genetic and demographic assessment. Minckley estimates this would cost about as much as the entire requested budget.
- Alien species removal may work in isolated places but is not an overall strategy. Isolating refugia is a better option.
- Minckley recommends funding two parts: (1) a basin-wide survey of all aquatic habitats using quantifiable techniques, and (2) development of control structures to prevent canal connections that dry habitats and lower water tables.
- He doubts the radio-tagging component fits a broad conservation program. If it is retained, he advises long-lived transmitters and multi-year study to capture climatic variation.
- He recommends the committee contact the investigators and have them rewrite the selected sections in detail. He offered to advise.

**Program fit and eligibility**
- WCS awards are capped at USD 20,000, with a median of USD 5,000. Grants may not fund salaries, permanent field stations or overhead.
- Preference goes to regions such as South America and Central America. The guidelines state that WCS does not support research in North America, which may conflict with a project in Mexico.
- Deadlines are 1 February, 1 June and 1 October, with five copies of the proposal required. Minckley's review arrived after the 25 August 1995 cutoff, though he had obtained the staff's permission for the delay.

**Investigator qualifications**
- Four Mexican biologists (Oscar Sánchez-Herrera, Francisco Soberón-Mobarak, Adrián Cerda-Ardura, [[Rolando Mendoza-Trejo]]) have worked in herpetology and conservation biology. Cerda has a diploma in endangered-species management from the Jersey Wildlife Preservation Trust. Soberón and Cerda presented on *T. coahuila* at a 1992 SSAR meeting.
- The UNAM Faculty of Sciences endorsed the project on 31 May 1995, offering laboratory, collection and administrative support. It stated it could not fund the project itself.
