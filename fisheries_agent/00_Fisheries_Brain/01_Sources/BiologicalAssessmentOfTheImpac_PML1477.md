# Biological Assessment of Water Intake Impacts on Federally Listed Fishes, Telephone Cove, Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment, prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) on 18 January 1995, evaluates potential impacts of constructing and operating the [[McAlister Intake Facility]] (Great Life Subdivision) at [[Telephone Cove]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. It was prepared for Stovall Engineers, Inc. and the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]], administered by the National Park Service, to support its responsibilities under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The assessment addresses four federally listed endangered fishes: [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Lake Mohave is designated [[Critical Habitat]] for bonytail and razorback sucker. The author concludes that the intake project is unlikely to affect the species of concern. Humpback chub and Colorado squawfish are considered extirpated from the reservoir, and the project's low water velocities and small footprint are not expected to entrain or harm bonytail or razorback sucker. Recommendations include timing construction for late summer to early autumn, on-site monitoring, and specimen-handling protocols.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Lake Mohave is a mainstream [[Colorado River Basin]] impoundment formed by [[Davis Dam]] on the Arizona-Nevada border. Davis Dam construction began in 1942, and the reservoir was largely complete by 1951. The reservoir reached the tailrace of [[Hoover Dam]] in May 1951. The original operating level of 195.0 m was raised in 1954 to 197.2 m to ensure rated power generation. Key morphometry includes:

- Maximum depth: 42 m; mean depth: 19.5 m
- Length: about 108 km; maximum width: 6.4 km
- Surface area: approximately 115 km²; volume: about 2.3 km³
- Mean total dissolved solids: about 700 mg/L

The lake is described as a "run of the river" reservoir. Its only continuous inflow is the Colorado River through Hoover Dam, and there are no major diversions.

**Target species.** All four species are native to the Colorado River basin and are specialized, large-bodied fishes whose populations have declined sharply since basin development.

- **Bonytail:** Maximum about 65 cm TL. Historically common in the river reach now inundated by Lake Mohave. Listed as endangered in 1980.
- **Humpback chub:** Maximum about 50 cm TL. Its largest remaining population is in Grand Canyon. Listed as endangered in 1967.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Maximum about 1.8 m TL, making it North America's largest minnow. Listed as endangered in 1967.
- **Razorback sucker:** Approaching 100 cm TL. Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining wild population. Listed as endangered after an earlier threatened proposal was withdrawn.

**Project.** The McAlister Intake Facility will withdraw an estimated 44,000 gallons per day (0.0691 cfs) of freshwater. Design assumptions include an 8-inch inlet pipe, a 7.5 hp pump, a 6-inch outlet, and a static head of about 870 ft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Lake Mohave is slightly alkaline (pH near 8). Its major ions are sulfate, carbonate, and chloride, with sodium, calcium, magnesium, and potassium as cations. Phosphorus is extremely low and limiting (<0.015 mg/L), nitrogen is moderate (0.2–0.5 mg/L), and silica is abundant.

**Fish fauna.** Before reservoir formation, the mainstream Colorado River supported nine native species. Carp and channel catfish were the first introduced species, stocked in the late 1800s. After Lake Mead was created, many non-native fishes were stocked for sportfishing. Table 1 lists about 23 non-native taxa (including salmonids, centrarchids, and bullheads) alongside the native species. Its status categories are abundant, common, uncommon, rare, extirpated, and indeterminate. Bonytail and razorback sucker still persist in the area, while humpback chub survives mainly far upstream in Grand Canyon.

**Substrate and hydrology.** The basin is dominated by sedimentary clay, silt, and sand of the Chemehuevis Formation, mixed with alluvium from the Black, Eldorado, and Newberry mountains.

**Species status in Lake Mohave:**

- **Bonytail:** Spawning was observed over a gravel shelf in the early 1950s. No successful reproduction has been documented since then, apart from one individual captured in the late 1980s. Adults have been captured sporadically in March and consistently in May since 1988, including some survivors of USFWS reintroductions and at least one natural recruit.
- **Razorback sucker:** Adults congregate in autumn upstream along shorelines and in Arizona Bay before spawning from November to May over clean, wave-washed substrate. Netting surveys have run since 1974. Few young are found, and the population is represented mainly by old adults.
- **Humpback chub and Colorado squawfish:** Neither is known to persist in Lake Mohave. The only humpback chub record is prehistoric, and the Colorado squawfish records are anecdotal and predate Davis Dam.

**Site-specific data for Telephone Cove:**

- Inlet velocity of 0.207 ft/s (6.3 cm/s) falls to 0.012 ft/s (0.4 cm/s) within the first foot from the inlet.
- Beyond 3 ft (91 cm), velocity is virtually nil (0.002 ft/s, or 0.06 cm/s or less).
- Electrofishing in Telephone Cove (1988–1991) captured no razorback suckers. No larvae were found in winter-spring collections in 1993–1994.
- Substrate and bottom topography at the site do not appear optimal for razorback sucker staging, spawning, feeding, or resting.

## Key Findings

- **Humpback chub and Colorado squawfish:** The project is not expected to affect either species. Both are considered extirpated from Lake Mohave, their nearest populations lie far upstream, and neither species' critical habitat includes any part of the lake.
- **Bonytail:** The project is unlikely to harm this species. Recruitment is minimal, and the very low inlet velocities are too slow to entrain fish of this size. The author attributes this to the typical size and swimming ability of adults, citing Grabowski et al. (1984).
- **Razorback sucker:** The project is unlikely to affect the population. Any disturbance to critical habitat is expected to be "innocuous and temporary," limited to short-term bottom disruption and turbidity increases in shallow water. No constituent elements or selection criteria under USFWS (1993a) are expected to be affected.
- **Recommended timing:** Construct the intake during late summer to early autumn, when adult bonytail and razorback suckers are dispersed in deeper, offshore waters and larvae are absent. This minimizes disturbance risk.
- **Monitoring:** A trained observer should be on-site periodically during installation. Monitoring should combine surface observation with other methods, and a photographic record should be kept.
- **Mortality protocol:** If any bonytail or razorback sucker dies, specimens should be frozen immediately, or tissues removed and frozen. Materials should be transferred as directed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and deposited in an appropriate institutional collection.
- **Reporting:** A summary report should be provided to state and federal resource management and regulatory agencies and to other interested parties.
