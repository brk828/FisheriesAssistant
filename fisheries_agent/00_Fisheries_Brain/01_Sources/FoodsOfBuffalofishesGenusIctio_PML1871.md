# Foods of Buffalofishes (Genus Ictiobus) in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictiobus

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by Mincklev, Johnson, Minckley, and Weitkamp (Arizona State University) examines the diets of three buffalofishes (genus *Ictiobus*) in [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]] on the [[Salt River]] in central Arizona. Guts from 541 fish were analyzed: 428 [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (240 from Roosevelt, 188 from Apache), 57 [[Black Buffalo]] and 56 [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (both from Apache). The authors found that all three species feed mainly on crustaceans and other planktonic organisms, but that detritus, sand, and benthic organisms in the guts indicate a near-bottom feeding habit. The black buffalo fed most strongly on the bottom, with the introduced [[Asian Clam]] as its main volumetric food. The paper also places the Arizona results within the wider literature on buffalofish diets.

## Background & Description

Buffalofishes are large catostomid-type fishes with a deep, laterally compressed body and a large, protrusible mouth. The paper focuses on three species that coexist in the Salt River reservoirs:

- **[[Bigmouth Buffalo]]** (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*): an enlarged heart and mouth and fine, numerous gill rakers, which are features associated with plankton feeding. This is the most upstream-distributed of the three species in the Salt River system and is presumed to be native or at least naturally present in the drainage.
- **[[Black Buffalo]]**: benthic in feeding habit; the paper notes that few prior studies of its diet exist.
- **[[Smallmouth Buffalo]]**: benthic but with broader use of diatoms and dipteran larvae; a higher proportion of ostracods and a larger share of sand suggest feeding in shallow bays and along shorelines.

The Salt River reservoirs were impounded for irrigation and hydroelectric use and for the Phoenix metropolitan area. They were stocked with fish from elsewhere, including [[Threadfin Shad]] introduced as forage. The authors note that these reservoirs are unusually biologically simple, since few native organisms persist, so the buffalofish community is comparatively well studied there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** Roosevelt Lake, the largest and most stable of the reservoirs, and Apache Lake, lying downstream, were sampled from roughly September through the following year. Canyon Lake, just downstream of Apache, had been drained, exposing a previously unidentified sponge, which was later found in Apache Lake and was treated as a diatom-like source of error.

**Reservoir morphology.** The paper includes a table of dam and basin dimensions (dam length, maximum and mean width and depth, surface area, volume, and basin slope) for each reservoir, with development dates in the early and mid 20th century.

**Collection methods.** Fish were taken with sill nets, trammel nets, and a 220-volt electrofishing apparatus; some were gill-netted. Entire visceral tracts were preserved in 70% isopropyl alcohol. Food was examined microscopically from aliquots on slides, with independent counts by different observers and discrepancies reconciled.

**Quantification.** Results were expressed as percent frequency of occurrence, percent total number, and percent relative volume. Relative volume was estimated from the intersections of a grid on an ocular micrometer covered by each food category. Detritus and sand percentages were visually estimated independently by two or more persons.

**Seasonal pattern.** Algae were important year-round, far more so in Apache Lake than Roosevelt Lake. Diatoms, mainly planktonic genera such as *Melosira* and *Asterionella* (plus some aufwuchs forms), were dominant. Chlorophyta, mostly free-floating filamentous forms, were common in autumn. Cyanophyta and Pyrrhophyta were least important.

## Key Findings

**Gut content composition**

- Crustaceans, principally [[Cladocera]] and [[Copepoda]], were the dominant animal foods for all three species and by volume in most periods.
- *Bosmina* was the dominant cladoceran genus in guts, followed by *Daphnia*. Copepods were mostly calanoid (limnetic) types, but they were often larger and contributed more relative volume than cladocerans.
- Minor animal groups included protozoans (a shelled amoeba), bryozoan statoblasts, molluscs (*Corbicula*, veligers and rare juveniles), chironomid larvae, water mites, ostracods, and fish eggs, probably of threadfin shad.
- Rotifers were most common in Apache Lake fish. Their loricate forms digest quickly and are fragile, so they are likely underrepresented.

**Bigmouth buffalo**

- Detritus (fine leaves, twigs) and inorganic material (fine sand) were prominent in the guts, indicating activity on or near the bottom. In the September–October period in particular, detritus averaged 24.4% of relative volume in Apache Lake fish and 25.7% in Roosevelt fish.
- The authors report that detrital and inorganic components occurred in nearly 100% of fish from both lakes.
- Individual fish varied widely; some guts were filled with cladocerans and others with copepods, which suggests either heterogeneous plankton dispersion or selective feeding by individuals.

**Black buffalo**

- Feeds mostly from the bottom. The introduced clam was the major volumetric component of the diet.
- Clams eaten ranged from juveniles to small adults, from under 1.0 mm to more than 15.0 mm across the valves.
- Organic detritus made up about 30% of relative volume and sand about 10.3%. Neither was strikingly higher than in bigmouth buffalo.

**Smallmouth buffalo**

- Sand reached 20.6% of the diet, suggesting shallow-water or shoreline feeding near inflows.
- Ostracods were an important numerical component and occurred at a higher frequency than in the other two species.
- Its guts showed the greatest diversity of food items in all periods, consistent with inshore feeding.

**Comparison with other studies**

- Johnson (1963) examined 201 adult bigmouth buffalo from two Saskatchewan lakes. Young fish (under 46 mm standard length) ate substantial cladocerans and copepods, and older fish (over 500 mm) ate about 58% cladocerans and copepods, 26% chironomids, and 4% molluscs. Johnson described feeding as a "bouncing" motion near the bottom.
- Scidmore and Woods (1960) found a substantial share of sand in juvenile Minnesota bigmouth buffalo, but cladocerans and copepods were again the main foods.
- Moen (1954) reported Iowa lakes with micro-crustaceans as the most important food, plus significant phytoplankton and detritus.
- In Lewis and Clark Lake and the Missouri River, Walburg and Nelson (1966) and McComish (1967) found planktonic crustaceans made up nearly 100% of bigmouth gut contents, suggesting stronger limnetic feeding than in Arizona. In the smallmouth buffalo, zooplankton made up 32% of total volume in 271 adult and subadult fish, and these authors concluded the species was opportunistic and fed mainly along shallow shorelines.

**Conclusion.** Despite local differences, the authors conclude that all three species feed on crustaceans and other plankton but also depend heavily on near-bottom habitat. Their mouth positions and the swirling of fine sediments by fish moving in small schools make bottom feeding efficient. The authors consider limnetic feeding on plankton unlikely for black buffalo.

**Methodological caveat.** Several small or fragile organisms (rotifers, some algae, fish eggs) were probably underestimated, and the 100x magnification used may have missed some diatoms.
