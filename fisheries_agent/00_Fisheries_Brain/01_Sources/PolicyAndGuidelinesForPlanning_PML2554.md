# Policy and Guidelines for Planning and Coordinating Recovery of Endangered and Threatened Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) document, dated May 1990, sets out the [[Recovery Planning Policy]] and procedures used to plan and coordinate the recovery of species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It revises and supersedes earlier recovery-planning guidance in response to the 1988 Amendments to the Act, a December 1988 General Accounting Office report on recovery management, and the Service's wish to make the guidance more useful and consistent with current policy.

The guidelines emphasize measurable recovery criteria, trackable recovery tasks, and realistic estimates of time and cost. They also define public review requirements, annual reporting of species status and expenditures, post-delisting monitoring, and the division of responsibilities among Regional Directors, lead Regions, and the Division of Endangered Species. The document is aquatic-relevant mainly through its marine and freshwater species scope and the fish and mussel examples in its appendices.

The document is a policy and guidance manual, not a research study. It contains no original field data. Its numerical content consists of procedural deadlines, priority scales, and the example recovery plans in the appendices.

## Background & Description

**Legal basis.** Section 4(f) of the Act (16 U.S.C. 1531 et seq.), as amended, directs the Secretary of the Interior and the Secretary of Commerce to develop and implement recovery plans for listed animals and plants unless such plans would not promote conservation. The Service administers the Act for birds, terrestrial, and freshwater species. The [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] is generally responsible for most marine species, with exceptions including the West Indian manatee, sea turtles on land, and sea otters, which fall under the Service.

**Definition of recovery.** Recovery is the process by which the decline of a listed species is arrested or reversed and threats to its survival are neutralized, so that long-term survival in nature can be ensured. The goal is maintenance of secure, self-sustaining wild populations with minimum necessary investment of resources.

**Structure of the document.** The guidance is organized into four sections (Introduction; Recovery Plan Preparation and Processing; Reporting and Tracking; References) and several appendices:
- Appendix I: [[Recovery Plan Format]], including title page, disclaimer, literature citation, executive summary, and plan outline with an implementation schedule.
- Appendix II: Organizing the recovery effort, covering [[Recovery Teams]], Service biologists, species recovery coordinators, recovery outlines, and [[Lead Region]] assignments for about 150 listed species.
- Appendix III: Public involvement, including sample newspaper notices, press releases, a model [[Federal Register]] notice, a transmittal letter, public meeting guidance, and a distribution list.
- Appendix IV: Service policies relating to recovery, covering captive propagation, habitat protection, hybrids, relocation, reintroduction, termination of efforts, experimental populations, the listing and recovery priority system, and NEPA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidance addresses species whose recovery depends on habitat, water quality, and ecosystem function. Several examples in the appendices concern aquatic taxa:

- **[[Ozark Cavefish]]**: threatened, with 14 known populations in Missouri (7), Oklahoma (3), and Arkansas (4), historically in 24 caves. Principal threats are habitat loss and declining water quality from urbanization, declining bat populations that supply energy to caves, and low reproductive rates. The recovery objective is delisting.
- **[[Fassett's Locoweed]]**: a threatened plant of sandy shorelines of landlocked seepage lakes, relocated at four of eight historical stations in a 1988 survey. Shoreline development is the largest threat.
- Lead-Region tables list dozens of aquatic species, including chubs, darters, pupfish, shiners, sturgeon, trout, woundfin, and numerous freshwater mussels (pearly mussels, pigtoes, pocketbook), and marine species such as sea turtles, manatee, and the [[Southern Sea Otter]].

The document also notes that species' ecosystems should be described in habitat sections, including water quality and flow regimes where relevant.

## Key Findings

- **Measurable criteria are required.** Recovery plans must state precise, measurable criteria, and must identify research needs that allow objective determination of recovery.
- **Four mandatory elements.** Every plan must (1) identify recovery tasks to the maximum extent feasible, (2) estimate the time frame to recovery, (3) estimate the total cost for all Federal, State, and private parties, and (4) set measurable criteria for delisting or downlisting.
- **Public review.** Section 4(f) requires public review of all new or revised plans before approval. A Federal Register notice opens a formal comment period, with newspaper notices and public meetings as appropriate.
- **Reporting.** Regions report species status biennially to Congress (first report due October 1990). Species expenditures are reported annually by January 15 under Section 18. Recovered species must be monitored for at least five years after delisting.
- **Priority systems.** The Species Recovery Priority System ranks species 1 to 18 (with a C-suffix for conflict with development), based on threat, recovery potential, and taxonomic distinctness. The Recovery Task Priority System assigns tasks Priority 1 (prevent extinction), 2 (prevent significant decline), or 3 (other actions for recovery). The systems are guides, not rigid formulas.
- **Lead Regions.** A lead Region is assigned for each species, must submit a recovery outline within 60 days of the final listing rule, and coordinates with other affected Regions. Assignments are made by mutual agreement.
- **Updates versus revisions.** Minor updates (schedule changes, completed tasks) need no public review. Substantial revisions require public involvement.
- **NEPA.** Recovery plan development is categorically excluded from NEPA. Individual federal actions implementing plan tasks may require NEPA analysis.
- **Experimental populations.** Experimental populations may be established outside current range under Section 10(j), with relaxed Section 9 and possibly Section 7 restrictions. Formal designation is required for reintroductions treated as experimental.
- **Hybrids and relocation.** Hybrids are not protected under the Act. Listed species are not relocated outside historic range without Director approval.
- **Genetic and taxonomic priorities.** Monotypic genera receive priority over species in the ranking system, and the Service declined to use ecological significance as a substitute for taxonomy, citing difficulty quantifying it.

Note: The document also includes a 1983 Federal Register notice on listing and recovery priority guidelines, with public comments and Service responses, reproduced in Appendix IV.
