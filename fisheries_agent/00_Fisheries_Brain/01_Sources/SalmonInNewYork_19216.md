# Salmon Fishery Development in New York Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fisheries-management #landlocked-salmon #sockeye-salmon

## Executive Summary

This 1954 report by Chief Aquatic Biologist John R. Greeley summarizes the rapidly-growing salmon fishery program in New York State lakes, involving two distinct species: [[Landlocked Salmon]] (Atlantic salmon, *Salmo salar*) and [[Red Salmon]] (Pacific sockeye, *Oncorhynchus nerka*). The program, initiated in 1944, has expanded from initial trials at [[Schroon Lake]] to state-wide operations. Fall 1953 netting operations captured a record 309 landlocked salmon for spawn collection, with particularly strong populations in [[Lake George]], [[Schroon Lake]], and Clear Pond. Red salmon populations remain experimental but show promise in [[Green Lake]] (Onondaga County) and several other waters. The report emphasizes three management factors: the fish biology, angler participation, and the management program itself.

## Background & Description

### Atlantic Salmon (Landlocked)

Only one species of [[Atlantic Salmon]] exists scientifically, though resident freshwater populations are termed "landlocked salmon." New York tests demonstrate that Atlantics from sea-run parents stocked in lakes produce fish indistinguishable from those of landlocked parentage. This is the largest and most important salmon type in New York lakes, with supply progressively increased over several years. [[Lake George]] produces the largest landlocked salmon of any New York lake.

### Pacific Salmon (Red Salmon)

Of five Pacific salmon species, only [[Red Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*)—variously called sockeye, blueback, kokanee, and little red fish—is involved in the New York program. Tests indicate fish reach approximately two pounds, considerably smaller than landlocked salmon. Despite large teeth, adults feed on tiny [[Plankton]] organisms, unlike most game fish which only consume plankton when young. This feeding behavior necessitates very small lures. Red salmon are not protected by New York law and may be angled at any time, any size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Preferences

**Summer Distribution**: Red salmon occupy deep, cold water and may range well above bottom. Surface water temperatures drive vertical distribution patterns.

**Spring and Fall Distribution**: When surface water is cold, fish range in shallows and at surface, making them accessible to shore anglers.

### Angling Methods

**Landlocked Salmon**: Methods commonly known among New York sportsmen; standard salmon angling techniques apply.

**Red Salmon**: Require specialized light tackle approaches:
- **Trolling**: Metal wobblers and red beads followed by small hook with small worm piece
- **Still Fishing**: Single salmon egg effective in early spring
- **Fly Casting**: Smaller artificial flies successful during spring/fall shallow-water periods
- **Tackle Requirements**: Limber rods essential to prevent hook tear-out in delicate feeders

### Population Distribution

**Landlocked Salmon Waters** (with evidence of survival or establishment):
- [[Lake George]] (highest abundance, largest fish)
- [[Schroon Lake]] (high abundance)
- Clear Pond, Essex County (high abundance)
- [[Upper Saranac Lake]]
- [[Cranberry Lake]]
- [[Chateaugay Lake]]
- Long Pond, Franklin County
- [[Blue Mountain Lake]]

**Red Salmon Waters**:
- [[Green Lake]], Onondaga County (highest abundance, adult spawners collected)
- Little Green Pond, Franklin County (adult spawners)
- [[Lake Luzerne]] (adult spawners)
- [[Loon Lake]], Franklin County (immature fish)
- [[Chapel Pond]] (immature fish)
- [[Caroga Lake]] (immature fish)

## Key Findings

### 1953 Spawning Operations

**Landlocked Salmon**: Fall netting efforts achieved excellent success with **309 fish captured**—a record catch for the program. Upturn in numbers especially pronounced in [[Lake George]]. Abundance also high in [[Schroon Lake]] and Clear Pond (Essex County). Few salmon taken in [[Upper Saranac Lake]]. Evidence of at least some survival documented in [[Cranberry Lake]], [[Chateaugay Lake]], Long Pond (Franklin County), and [[Blue Mountain Lake]].

**Red Salmon**: Experimental spawn-taking operations secured few eggs, but these proved excellent quality, indicating possibility of expansion if populations continue increasing. Adult red salmon secured at [[Green Lake]] (Onondaga County), Little Green Pond (Franklin County), and [[Lake Luzerne]]. Immature red salmon netted in [[Loon Lake]] (Franklin County), [[Chapel Pond]], and [[Caroga Lake]].

### Management Program Status

The salmon project took concrete shape in 1944, initially selecting [[Schroon Lake]] as study area. Program now operates on enlarged, state-wide scale, expanding as warranted by fish populations, angler success, and management capabilities. Test netting serves as primary population assessment method—either brief gill net checks or intensive campaigns to secure salmon breeders for egg supply.

### Research Needs

Management biologists request angler-supplied information, particularly:
- Scale samples (scraped from captured fish)
- Length measurements
- Date of capture
- Locality details
- Other relevant data

Such information particularly important in waters where experimental stocking remains on trial basis. Angler exploration in lakes with documented immature red salmon populations desired from management perspective.

### Future Outlook

**Landlocked Salmon**: Results sufficiently encouraging to warrant expansion of planting program. Supply in various lakes progressively increased.

**Red Salmon**: Too early to predict long-term future in New York lakes. If supply can be built up and anglers solve the problem of getting these delicate feeders to strike readily, red salmon may become established as light tackle sport fish for small, deep lakes.

### Three-Factor Management Framework

1. **The Fish**: Biology, feeding behavior, size potential, and species-specific characteristics
2. **The Fishermen**: Angler participation, catch success, method development, and information reporting
3. **The Management Program**: Research, propagation, applied management, test netting, and adaptive expansion based on results
