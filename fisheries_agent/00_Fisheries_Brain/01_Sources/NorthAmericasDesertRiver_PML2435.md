# Regulation and Ecology of the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a multi-part review by [[J.A. Stanford]] (University of Montana Biological Station) and [[J.V. Ward]] (Colorado State University) on the physiography, hydrology, limnology, riparian ecology, and fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] in the American Southwest. The authors argue that flow regulation by dams and diversions has transformed a sediment-laden, seasonally flooding river into a series of thermally and chemically altered reservoirs and tailwaters. They conclude that native, largely endemic fishes are incompatible with regulation and exotic species, and that water shortages will likely preclude environmental flows for fishes.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River drains a large part of the American Southwest, including the [[Mojave Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]], and passes through the [[Grand Canyon]]. Snowmelt from the southern Rocky Mountains feeds the basin. The river's total basin area within the United States is stated as 629,000 km², with about 9,500 km² more in Mexico. Its virgin average flow at [[Lee Ferry]] was estimated at 19.35 km³/year for 1914–1965. Historically the annual discharge was very low relative to basin area, with the document describing the basin as one of the driest river basins in the world.

Key historical events include:
- Settlement and diversion of water beginning in the late 1800s; canals were delivering water to southern California by 1903.
- Legal division into Upper and Lower Basins at Lee Ferry.
- Construction of [[Hoover Dam]] to form [[Lake Mead]] (impounded 1935).
- Construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to form [[Lake Powell]] (1963).
- The 1905–1907 flooding of the [[Salton Sea]] (then a Salton sink) when Colorado River water broke through irrigation canals.

Physiographically, the basin was shaped by Mesozoic and Cenozoic orogeny, uplift of the [[Colorado Plateau]] to 1500–2500 m, and incision by the river, which exposes much of the region's geologic history. Pluvial lakes such as [[Lake Bonneville]] and Lake Lahontan existed in the Great Basin, but Lake Bonneville was never connected to the Colorado system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and sediment.** The pre-regulation annual hydrograph peaked in spring with snowmelt, with flows of 2,400–5,600 m³/s in the Grand Canyon. Pre-regulation flows at the delta averaged about 19.0 km³/year (602 m³/s), with a recorded peak of about 7,100 m³/s (22 January 1916) and a minimum below 10 m³/s. The virgin river was described as the greatest sediment conveyor of any river system in the world. Regulation has reduced flows to a near-constant release (about 425 m³/s from Lake Powell, eliminating the freshet), reduced the median suspended sediment concentration in the Grand Canyon by a factor of 3.5, and dewatered most of the lower Gila River and delta.

**Geomorphology.** Pre-regulation side-flow floods built sandy pools separated by rapids. Post-regulation, rapids are growing more turbulent, sand bars and terraces are no longer reworked, and the channel has stabilized.

**Water quality.** Salinity increases from the headwaters (under 10 mg/l) to the Lower Basin, where sulphate constitutes over half of the total dissolved solids (TDS) at the delta. Pre-regulation TDS at the delta was about 380 mg/l; present values exceed 825 mg/l. Salton Sea salinity was about 36,000 mg/l in 1907 and has since increased by an order of magnitude.

**Temperature.** Pre-regulation desert reaches rarely dropped below 5–10°C in winter and seldom exceeded 30°C in summer. Regulated reaches are cool in summer and warm in winter; flows from dams reduced mean mainstream temperature by 10–15°C in some reaches.

**Riparian vegetation.** The authors describe six riparian plant community classes, from montane deciduous scrub (above 2,000 m) through cottonwood-willow forest, subtropical deciduous woodlands (mesquite bosques), subtropical evergreen forest (California fan palm), marshes, and desert canyon scrub. The riparian zone ranges from a few metres in narrow canyons to over a kilometre on the lower mainstem floodplain.

**Invasive species.** [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*), introduced in the 1800s, has invaded nearly all but montane elevations and now dominates or co-dominates many reaches, aided by post-regulation flows.

**Fish fauna.** Of 33 native species in nine families, 36 are described as endemic, reflecting Quaternary pluvial and interpluvial history. Many native fishes were isolated in desert springs and tributaries, and 51% of intermountain native fishes occurred in only one sub-basin. Regulation has allowed over 100 exotic fish species to establish, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Striped Bass]].

## Key Findings

- **Hydrologic transformation:** Regulation has eliminated the spring freshet, reduced sediment loads, and fully regulated flows except in small tributaries. Water allocations are based on an overestimate of virgin flow at Lee Ferry.
- **Water deficit:** Consumptive use is currently allocated more water than the basin produces. The authors project depletion deficits by the year 2000 regardless of further development.
- **Salinity:** Salinity has increased dramatically through irrigation, evaporation, and dissolution of marine sediments; the document notes that Lake Mead has experienced decreasing TDS since 1972.
- **Reservoir limnology:** Lake Powell is phosphorus-limited; phosphorus loading into Lake Powell is estimated at 5,250 tonnes/year, with only 229 tonnes exiting through Glen Canyon Dam. Lake Mead phosphorus sedimentation decreased 93.5% after Lake Powell's construction.
- **Lake Mead fishery decline:** Threadfin shad populations have declined, and predatory game fish have suffered. Striped bass were reported in poor condition, possibly due to food limitation and overexploitation.
- **Endemism and extinction risk:** Many native fishes are endangered, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Fewer than 8,000 specimens of [[Gila Trout]] remained in the native habitat. Only one viable population of [[Desert Pupfish]] is noted (Quitobaquito Spring).
- **Exotic displacement:** Exotics compete with and prey on natives, and hybridization (e.g., rainbow × cutthroat trout) has further reduced native integrity.
- **Management outlook:** The authors argue that stream regulation can serve as a large-scale experiment on ecosystem processes, but that water shortages will likely preclude allocation of flows for fishes and natural ecological attributes. Some endemic fishes may survive in hatcheries, though reintroduction to former ranges is likely to fail.
