# Diverse Types of Young in a Single Species of Fish: The Gizzard Shad

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

Hubbs and Whitlock (1928) document two strongly contrasting forms of young [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) collected around July 1, 1927 from neighbouring streams near Fort Smith, Oklahoma. Young from a muddy backwater of the [[Arkansas River]] had swollen, macrocephalic heads, large eyes and stunted tails, while young from an overflow pond of the [[Poteau River]] were slender with smaller heads and longer, deeper tails. Meristic counts did not differ between the forms, and no parasites were found. The authors attribute the divergence to direct environmental effects, most plausibly the heavy silt load ingested during flood conditions, rather than to genetic racial differences. The abnormality decreases with size, a pattern interpreted as compensatory growth regulation. The paper also warns against using body proportions of young fish as racial or specific characters.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Part of a systematic study of the fishes of Oklahoma by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], with [[Stanley C. Whitlock]] as co-author. Published in the *Papers of the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts and Letters*, Vol. X (1928; published 1929).
- **Specimens:** Several hundred young in the lower part of a 22.4 to 75 mm total length range (excluding caudal fin) from the Arkansas River series, plus a Poteau series of similar sizes collected at the same time, a few miles away.
- **Measured characters:** Total length, length of head, width of head, length of eye, length of tail (origin of anal fin to base of middle caudal rays) and least depth of the caudal peduncle, measured with dividers to 0.1 mm.
- **Preservation:** Formaldehyde, later alcohol.
- **Analytical tools:** Growth curves fitted with logarithmic equations, a composite [[Character Index]], and a percentage-of-abnormality measure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feeding:** Adults and young feed in open water. Arkansas River young had guts packed with fine inorganic material (clay, mica, silica). Pyloric caeca were also distended with this material, and only about one particle in a hundred was a desmid or diatom. Poteau young contained mainly entomostracan remains.
- **Habitat use:** Young tend to spend their infancy in cut-off ponds, where temperature and water chemistry may differ sharply from the main channel.
- **Flood conditions:** The Arkansas specimens were collected during the great floods of 1927, when the water was very silty.
- **Parasites:** Careful examination of specimens of different sizes from both series revealed no parasites, which rules out parasitization as the cause (contrast with Hubbs, 1927).
- **Migration:** The rare atypical individuals in each series were probably recent migrants from the other stream.
- **Adult ecology:** Adults are free-roaming river fishes, so sharp racial differentiation between nearby populations is not expected.
- **Hypothesised mechanism:** Heavy inorganic roughage may have caused differential inhibition along the cephalocaudal growth gradient of the young fish, disturbing the normal relative size of anterior and posterior parts ([[Developmental Gradients]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Two extreme forms:** Arkansas young are teratological-looking, with long, wide, swollen heads, large eyes and stunted tails. Poteau young are wafer-like, with shorter, narrower heads, smaller eyes and longer, deeper tails.
2. **No meristic difference:** In specimens of about 65 mm, slightly more than 300 gill-rakers were counted on the first arch in each series. Anal rays ranged from 30 to 36 in each series. Mean vertebral count was 50.0 in both series (Arkansas: 49 = 2, 50 = 9, 51 = 2; Poteau: 49 = 1, 50 = 11, 51 = 1).
3. **Intermediate populations:** Young from other localities were intermediate between the two extremes.
4. **Environmental cause:** Differences are attributed to direct environmental action, most likely silt-laden water and altered diet.
5. **Convergence with size:** The character index for the two series converges rapidly. At about 80 mm, only a slight average difference, if any, persists.
6. **Character index:** The composite index differs by more than 100 per cent between the smaller specimens. No single character differs by more than 30 per cent.
7. **Compensatory recovery:** Percentage of abnormality fell from a high of 15 to 27 per cent to below 7 per cent at 80 mm. This is interpreted as compensatory regulation of growth, comparable to tail growth compensation in rats (Sumner, 1915).
8. **Growth curves:** Dimensions do not scale linearly with total length during early growth. Curves approach straight lines at larger sizes. This contradicts the linear-growth generalisation of Crozier and Hecht (1914) and Hecht (1916).
9. **Verdigris River sample:** Young from the Verdigris River (50 to 100 mm) were intermediate at small sizes but maintained a higher character index in larger fish. They were modified less but showed less recovery. The authors link this to faster growth.
10. **Taxonomic caution:** The authors question the two proposed subspecies of *Signalosa* (Weed, 1925), which were based on young fish whose differences are no greater than those produced environmentally in *Dorosoma*. They advise caution in using proportions of young fish as racial or specific characters.
