# California Desert Conservation Area Plan: Summary Booklet

#desert-ecology #land-use-planning #bureau-of-land-management

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management (BLM) booklet summarizes the California Desert Conservation Area (CDCA) Plan, the long-range land-management plan required by Section 601 of the [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] (FLPMA) of 1976. The Plan covers a 25-million-acre CDCA, of which the BLM administers 12.1 million acres. It balances economic, scientific, educational, and recreational uses against the protection of environmental, cultural, and aesthetic values. The Plan was signed by Secretary of the Interior Cecil Andrus on December 19, 1980, and concurred in by Secretary James Watt on April 2, 1981.

The document does not address fish or aquatic systems. Its ecological content concerns desert vegetation, wildlife, wild horses and burros, and rangeland, and it describes the Plan's management framework (multiple-use classes, Plan Elements, and Areas of Critical Environmental Concern).

## Background & Description

The CDCA lies in parts of three desert subprovinces: the [[Mojave Desert]], the [[Sonoran Desert]], and a small part of the [[Great Basin]]. Much of the region receives less than 10 inches of rainfall annually, against potential evaporation of seven or eight feet. Landforms include valleys, bajadas, alluvial fans, sand dunes, washes, and dry lakebeds.

Land use has changed over time. Native American groups created village sites, quarried stone, flood-irrigated land, and maintained trade trails. Later uses included emigrant routes, mining, railroads, livestock grazing, and irrigated agriculture in the [[Imperial Valley]]. After World War II, the Desert attracted settlers, military training, and recreation. The inventory behind the Plan cost $6 million and used methods from literature review to satellite sensing. The Plan drew 14,000 written comments on the 1980 draft and final environmental impact statement documents.

The Plan rests on four management principles set by FLPMA:
- **Multiple use:** combining resource uses to serve human needs.
- **Sustained yield:** maintaining regular production from renewable resources.
- **Maintenance of environmental quality:** protecting the resources that sustain life.
- **Good neighbor concept:** respecting private landowners, military installations, tribes, and other agencies and governments.

The Plan is a framework for a 20-year period, and it is amended through a formal review process.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and soils.** Desert soils are often protected only by a natural crust, known as "desert pavement," and sparse vegetation. Disturbance exposes thin soils to severe erosion.

**Plant adaptations.** Most desert plants are annuals that persist as seeds until rain. Perennials have dual root systems, succulent water storage, and drought-deciduous leaf loss, as in the [[Ocotillo]]. Animals rely on behavior, such as restricting activity to cool hours, and some store water, as the [[Desert Tortoise]] does in bladder sacs.

**Water.** Seeps, springs, and other surface-water sources are rare, and plant and animal life concentrates around them. This makes them highly important to the ecosystems.

**Vegetation.** The Plan manages plant resources on two levels. The floristic perspective covers rare and endangered species and "unusual plant assemblages." The vegetation perspective covers productivity and the forage and other resources they supply. Notable features include the [[Joshua Tree]] stands of Pipes Canyon and wetland and riparian areas.

**Wildlife.** Soils, topography, vegetation, and climate create over 100 habitats. Some, such as oases and sand dunes, support species found nowhere else. Wildlife populations have declined since the mid-1800s, several species have disappeared from the Desert, and a few are extinct. Species-level plans address the [[Desert Tortoise]] and [[Bighorn Sheep]].

**Wild horses and burros.** Populations are growing at 10 to 15 percent annually. Non-native burros cause overgrazing, water pollution, riparian loss, and soil erosion.

**Human pressures.** The Desert holds over 500,000 residents in 100 communities, along with mining, power generation, military bases, pipelines, and extensive vehicle routes. Ongoing pressures include urban growth, recreation, and energy demand.

## Key Findings

**Multiple-use classes.** Nearly all of the 12.1 million BLM acres are assigned to one of four classes:

| Class | Purpose | Acres (thousands) | % of BLM lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| C (Controlled) | Wilderness recommendations | 2,099 | 17.3 |
| L (Limited) | Protects sensitive values | 5,883 | 48.5 |
| M (Moderate) | Controlled balance of use and protection | 3,336 | 27.5 |
| I (Intensive) | Concentrated uses such as mining and motorized recreation | 499 | 4.1 |
| Unclassified | Under review | 314 | 2.6 |

**Areas of Critical Environmental Concern (ACECs) and Special Areas.** The Plan designates 75 ACECs covering over 600,000 acres, which are priority protection areas but not "lock-up" designations. It also identifies 14 Special Areas, including the East Mojave National Scenic Area.

**Plan Elements.** The Plan has 12 elements. Key decisions include:
- **Cultural resources:** Over 14,000 prehistoric and historic sites have been recorded, and the Plan estimates more than 100,000 exist. About 35 percent have been lost, and roughly 1 percent a year continue to be lost. Protection covers 47 of the 75 ACECs, and 66 desert sites are listed on the [[National Register of Historic Places]].
- **Native American values:** Consultation identified sensitive values, about 80 percent of which fall in Classes C and L. Thirteen ACECs were designated for these values.
- **Vegetation:** 91 vascular plant species are potentially rare, threatened, or endangered. Forty-one unusual plant assemblages have been rated for disturbance sensitivity.
- **Wildlife:** 27 ACECs and 59 habitat management areas target wildlife habitat.
- **Wild horses and burros:** About 1,000 horses and over 10,000 burros were counted. Management covers 17 of 22 areas, with reduction targets of about 280 horses and 2,750 burros, and the first priority is reducing burros.
- **Livestock grazing:** About 75,000 sheep and 14,000 cattle graze roughly 4.5 million acres across 54 existing and 15 proposed allotments. Grazing is reduced 25 percent on "fair" range and 50 percent on "poor" range. A minimum of 350 pounds per acre of forage is required in crucial [[Desert Tortoise]] habitat before turnout. Allocations total about 74,700 animal unit months (AUMs), with 6,000 more possible from reactivated allotments.
- **Wilderness:** 5.7 million acres in 138 Wilderness Study Areas were found to have wilderness characteristics. The Plan recommends 2.1 million acres in 45 locations as suitable for Congressional designation, and finds 3.6 million acres nonsuitable.
- **Land tenure:** Land exchanges with the State of California and the Southern Pacific Land Company are planned. About 300,000 unclassified BLM acres may be sold, exchanged, or leased.
- **Recreation:** Recreationists log over 15 million visitor use days a year, and 85 percent of visitors come from southern California. About half a million acres in Class I are open for vehicle play.
- **Motorized-vehicle access:** The network totals about 45,000 miles of routes. Routes in Class L and ACECs are to be designated as "approved" by the end of 1982, and interim rules apply until then.
- **Geology, energy, and minerals:** A valuation of 25 of 46 known mineral deposits estimated the mineral estate at over $200 billion. The Desert produces 15 percent of U.S. talc, 10 percent of gypsum, and 5 percent of iron, and most U.S. borax and most of the world's rare earths.
- **Energy and utility corridors:** The Plan provides 16 planning corridors, 2 to 5 miles wide, for transmission lines of 161 kilovolts or more, aqueducts, large pipelines, and communication cables. About 3,500 miles of transmission lines and 12,000 miles of oil and gas pipelines already exist.

**Implementation and cost.** Implementation depends on annual Congressional appropriations. Costs must rise gradually to $1.50 per acre per year over three years. Amendments will be considered annually for the first five years. The Plan's goal is to avoid any net diminishment of environmental, cultural, and aesthetic values.

**Projected outcomes for the Year 2000.** The Plan projects that wildlife populations will improve or stabilize, rangelands will be in good condition or better, and cultural resource loss will be greatly reduced. It also expects a rational motorized access system, new energy facilities including geothermal, wind, and solar plants, and southern California's population to reach about 16.5 million.
