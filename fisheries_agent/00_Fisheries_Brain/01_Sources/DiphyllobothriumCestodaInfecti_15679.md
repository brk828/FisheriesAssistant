# Diphyllobothrium Infections in Salmonids from Three Washington Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #diphyllobothriidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by [[Becker and Brunson]] (Journal of Wildlife Management, Vol. 31, No. 4) examined larval *Diphyllobothrium* cestodes in stocked salmonids in three western Washington lakes (Goodwin, Shoecraft, and Silver) from June 1963 to June 1966. The authors concluded that the plerocercoid larvae were the most important parasite affecting survival of stocked [[Rainbow Trout]], with infections acquired mainly in summer, chronic mortality linked to warm water, and one severe epizootic in Goodwin Lake in winter 1963–64. Direct control was judged largely infeasible, so the paper discusses indirect, biology-based management options.

## Background & Description

Fishery biologists in Washington observed that salmonids stocked in several popular lakes acquired heavy burdens of immature cestodes, and the sport catch was far below expectations from the number of fish released. Substantial mortality was suspected. The study had four objectives: to document significant mortality in infected salmonid populations, to present data on how salmonids acquire plerocercoids, to discuss practical control measures for managers, and to review the literature on diphyllobothriid infections in salmonids.

The parasite is a pseudophyllidean cestode whose plerocercoid (larval) stage lives in the internal organs of teleost hosts. Adults occur mainly in the intestines of piscivorous birds, chiefly gulls (*Larus* spp.), with the herring gull the most frequently recorded North American host. The larvae were not identified to species; the authors noted that *Diphyllobothrium* taxonomy is confused and that the Washington plerocercoids belong to the body-folded, cuticular bristle group that includes *D. cordiceps*.

The life cycle passes through copepod first intermediate hosts ([[Cyclops]] or [[Diaptomus]]), which become infective to fish in about 14 days. Growth of plerocercoids in fish is strongly temperature dependent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling:** 1,053 salmonids and about 80 fish of other species were examined from the three study lakes. Gill nets were set at 1–2 month intervals, with target collections of 10 spring-plant and 5 fall-plant fish per species.
- **Stocking:** Rainbow trout were stocked twice yearly, as spring fingerlings and as fall legal-sized fish (about 8 inches). Young coho were spring-planted, and cutthroat and brook trout were examined only in Goodwin Lake in 1964–65. Stocking numbers ranged from roughly 6,000 to 200,300 fish per plant (see Table 1 in source).
- **Reference lakes:** 352 rainbows from Canal, Crater, and Upper Goose lakes (Grant County, 1964–66) were free of larval diphyllobothriids.
- **Temperature:** Mean June–September subsurface (0–20 ft) temperatures were 58–60 °F in 1963, 64–66 °F in 1964, and 65–67 °F in 1965. Mean November–March temperatures were 42–45 °F.
- **Host feeding:** Copepods (*Diaptomus* and *Cyclops*) dominated trout diets, and rainbows fed more on zooplankton than cutthroat, brook, or coho.
- **Definitive hosts:** Gulls and other piscivorous birds were present on the lakes most months, but birds were not examined for the parasite.

## Key Findings

1. **Infection timing:** Plerocercoids were acquired mainly in summer, when subsurface water temperatures peaked. Under normal conditions, fall plants had much lower infections than spring plants sampled at the same time.
2. **Host differences:** Rainbow trout in Goodwin Lake generally had heavier infections than rainbows in Shoecraft and Silver lakes in every season. Lacustrine coho had distinctly lower intensities than rainbows. Cutthroat and brook trout had only slightly lower intensities (one season of data), but these smaller species suffered heavier losses and disappeared from collections by March 1965.
3. **Epizootic:** In winter 1963–64, Goodwin Lake fish reached average intensities of 78 plerocercoids per fish in spring-plant rainbows, 140 in fall-plant rainbows, and 38 in spring-plant coho (February). The cause was not determined and the event was not repeated in 1964–65 or 1965–66.
4. **Chronic mortality:** Losses were linked to warm temperatures during the first spring, summer, and fall after planting, when larvae developed quickly in small fish. Infection levels were not cumulative, which suggested ongoing losses of heavily infected fish.
5. **Size and damage:** Infections had greater effects in young fish. Infected fingerlings were especially vulnerable to predation by larger trout and piscivorous birds. Large rainbows aged 2–3 years sometimes carried massive inactive infections with no apparent harm. Damage depended on larval size, activity, and location, not only on counts.
6. **Pathology:** Small larvae encysted in the serosa of the stomach, pyloric caeca, intestine, and mesenteries. Larger larvae occurred free or unencysted in the liver, spleen, kidney, or heart, sometimes causing peritonitis, hemorrhage, or ascites.
7. **Control assessment:** Direct control was judged infeasible. Gulls are protected under federal migratory bird law, and chemical treatment with rotenone or toxaphene would not eliminate zooplankton or prevent reintroduction by gulls.
8. **Indirect management options:** The authors recommended considering fall stocking of legal-sized trout (lower winter infection, larger fish for winter angling), extending the fishing season into winter, using coho (lower infection) where appropriate, and possibly introducing alternative food organisms (mysids, amphipods, decapods) to reduce copepod dependence. They noted that costs and angler preferences may outweigh these gains.
9. **Conclusion:** No method, direct or indirect, appeared fully effective. The authors argued that ecological factors driving epizootics remain poorly studied and need to be understood before effective management can be designed.
