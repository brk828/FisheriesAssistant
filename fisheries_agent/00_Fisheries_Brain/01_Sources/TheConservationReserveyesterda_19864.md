# The Conservation Reserve Program: Yesterday, Today and Tomorrow

#grassland-ecology #conservation-policy #range-management

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-203, May 1991) collects papers from a 1991 Society for Range Management symposium held in Washington, DC. It was organized by the [[Great Plains Agricultural Council]] and the [[Society for Range Management]] task forces on the [[Conservation Reserve Program]] (CRP). The document addresses terrestrial grassland and agricultural policy rather than fish or aquatic taxa, so this note is tagged to that domain. Its central question is what will happen to roughly 34 million enrolled acres when the first 10-year contracts expire from 1996. The authors agree that history, economics, and policy design will largely decide whether the land stays in grass. They also document large gains in erosion control, wildlife habitat, and water quality.

## Background & Description

The proceedings was edited by Linda A. Joyce, John E. Mitchell, and Melvin D. Skold. It contains eleven papers, ranging from historical overviews to economic, environmental, and wildlife analyses. The Great Plains are defined as the treeless steppe lying west of the 98th Meridian and east of the Rocky Mountains. About half of CRP land is in this region.

Key contextual points from the papers:

- **Historical land use:** Laycock (Univ. of Wyoming) traces a sequence of cattle ranching, homesteading under the [[Homestead Act]] of 1862, and plowing that set up the [[Dust Bowl]]. The [[Soil Bank Program]] of 1956 was the first official "Conservation Reserve Program." Its peak enrollment was about 28.7 million acres in 1960–61, and most of its grass land was later plowed.
- **Post-Dust Bowl measures:** Land Utilization Projects of about 6 million acres were bought under the [[Bankhead-Jones Act]] and later managed as [[National Grasslands]]. A large [[Shelterbelt]] planting of 223 million trees on 30,000 farms (1935–1942) was largely removed in the 1970s.
- **Legislation:** The 1985 [[Food Security Act]] created CRP, and the 1990 [[Food, Agriculture, Conservation and Trade Act]] continued it and added new programs (see below).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soil and water.** Blackburn, Newman, and Wood used the [[WEPP Model]] and the Wind Erosion Equation across 17 western states plus Minnesota and Missouri. Crop-management erosion in the Great Plains and Northwest ranged from 6 to 152 tons per acre per year. Under CRP, water erosion at all modeled sites was estimated at less than 1 ton per acre per year, and wind erosion was 1 ton per acre or less. Evapotranspiration rose at all sites, and runoff fell at most. Deep percolation declined except at two high-rainfall sites (Doniphan County, Kansas, and Clinton County, Missouri, above 30 inches of precipitation). Model validation for cropland was incomplete, so the authors stress that relative comparisons are more reliable than absolute values.

**Wildlife.** Schenck and Williamson argue that CRP can greatly improve grassland habitat, drawing on the Soil Bank experience. In South Dakota, pheasant numbers rose from 4–6 million to 8–11 million with 1.8 million acres of Soil Bank cover. Nationally, 88% of CRP cover is herbaceous, and native grass dominates only in Kansas. Trees cover about 2.2 million acres (6%). Restored wetlands, floodplains, and filter strips make up less than 2% (572,436 acres). Early [[Wildlife Habitat Monitoring]] by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service rated pheasant nesting habitat at 65% of optimum, but cottontail habitat showed virtually no improvement because few woody plantings were established.

**Haying and grazing.** Emergency haying during the 1988 drought was a major disturbance. An estimated 7.7% of 1986–87 contract acreage was hayed nationally, and Wisconsin reached 45%. Hays and Farmer describe CRP fields as a possible "[[Ecological Trap]]" for nesting birds. Schenck and Williamson recommend periodic disturbance on 3- to 5-year intervals and leaving untouched patches.

## Key Findings

**1. Program scale and cost.** Enrollment reached almost 34 million acres through nine signups (about 8% of 400 million acres of U.S. cropland). Annual rent was about $1.7 billion, averaging $49 per acre (Bjerke). Net federal cost was estimated at $8.5 billion, from $18.2 billion in payments minus $9.7 billion in projected savings to the [[Commodity Credit Corporation]]. Berlinger and Knapp put the national net CRP expense at $2.0–6.6 billion.

**2. Benefits.**
- Erosion reduction of about 656 million tons per year, cutting cropland erosion by over one-fifth (Bjerke).
- Economic Research Service estimates: soil productivity $1.2 billion, wind erosion $0.4 billion, cleaner waters $1.3–3.9 billion, wildlife hunting $1.9–3.1 billion, and timber present value $3.3 billion.
- Ribaudo et al. estimated about $10 billion in benefits for a fully implemented 45-million-acre CRP, with wildlife habitat (40%) and surface water quality (37%) the largest shares.
- Langner estimated $3.8 billion in net present value of added recreational days over 14 years.

**3. Central Great Plains.** Colorado, Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma, and Wyoming hold about 22% of enrollment (7,576,229 acres under contract) and deliver a 154,961,573-ton annual erosion reduction, about 24% of the national total. Total annual rent is $365,217,677, and 5,154,900 acres of commodity base have been retired, including 3,182,229 acres of wheat base.

**4. Post-CRP outlook.** Estimates of the share of land that stays in grass vary widely:
- Myers and Harris cite Soil Bank retention of about 20%.
- Heimlich and Kula estimate 15–30% retention. At mean variable values, livestock farmers had a 77% probability of keeping CRP in grass versus 44% for crop farmers.
- Berlinger and Knapp estimate that about 80% could revert to cropland without policy change.

**5. Landowner options (Harris).** Surveyed producers gave these expectations:
- Plow out and return to row crops: 60–70% of producers, the most popular option.
- Grazing or haying: 20–40% of acreage.
- Contract extension: 50–75% of current contracts, if offered.
- Partial conversion with grass strips: 10–15% of producers.
- Long-term easements: under 5%.
- Sale or development: under 2% of acreage.

Harris also reports that 54% of producers had not finalized post-CRP plans, and that 60% identify economics as the main factor.

**6. Root causes of the policy risk (Laycock).** Laycock identifies several design flaws:
- Crop base was left attached to CRP land, which he calls the largest single driver of future plowing.
- [[Sodbuster Provisions]] applied to CRP land, even though it had been in permanent cover.
- Rapid implementation (about 18 million acres in the first year, over 25 million a year later) strained seed supplies and local businesses.
- No research funds were provided, and a proposed joint research program with a claimed return of "perhaps exceeding 10 to 1" was not funded.

**7. Economics of grazing (Heimlich and Kula).** Adjusted for CRP yields and excluding government payments, 1987 cattle returns on CRP land would have exceeded grain returns. Grain prices or payments would need to fall about 15% to equalize returns. Trade liberalization under [[GATT Negotiations]] and projected declines in export demand for wheat are cited as possible drivers.

**8. 1990 Farm Bill changes.** The farm bill added:
- Protection of crop base for an extra 10 years if conservation cover is kept, with limited haying and grazing allowed.
- The [[Wetlands Reserve Program]] (goal of 1 million acres).
- The [[Environmental Easement Program]].
- The [[Water Quality Incentives Program]] (goal of 10 million acres, payments capped at $3,500 per person per year).
- An Environmental Conservation Acreage Reserve Program umbrella with a 40–45 million acre goal by 1995.

Heimlich and Kula argue that this only defers the decision to 1995.

**9. Research gaps (Bartlett and Mitchell).** A questionnaire sent to 214 people produced 62 responses (29%). It was not designed as a statistically valid sample. Key results:
- 81% said erosion data and models were inadequate for landscape-scale prediction.
- 86% said information on county or state economic impact was inadequate.
- 77% wanted more effort on economic impacts.
- 83% considered cover crop selection important; only 49% felt seedbed techniques were adequately understood.
- Only one-third monitored CRP seedings.

**10. Synthesis.** The symposium concludes that CRP has met its erosion and water-quality goals, but that most benefits depend on keeping land in grass after 1996. Laycock sees the main risk as a lack of policy resolve, and Myers argues that the net cost to government is much below gross outlays. Several papers call for coordinated monitoring, economic research, and better linkage between research and decision makers.

**Note:** The final paper (Luttschwager and Higgins, on emergency haying and duck production) is truncated in the source. Only its abstract is available, which reports that CRP generally raises net farm income but may cause small decreases in local business economies.
