# Legal Opinion on ESA Status of Hybrid Offspring from Two Endangered Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-law #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1993 memorandum from the Office of the Solicitor, U.S. Department of the Interior, clarifies that hybrid offspring whose parents are both listed as endangered or threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) are not protected under the Act. The opinion addresses a specific case involving hybrid wolves (crosses between [[Red Wolf]] and [[Gray Wolf]]) and reaffirms the 1977 legal interpretation that hybrids defeat the ESA's primary purpose of conserving pure genetic heritage of endangered species.

## Background & Description

The memorandum responds to a request from the Special Agent in Charge, Region 5, seeking clarification on ESA status of a hybrid wolf with both parents listed as endangered or threatened. The document traces the evolution of legal interpretation:

- **May 18, 1977**: Initial opinion concluded hybrids were covered by ESA, defining "hybrid" as offspring of two animals where each parent is from a different species and at least one parent is from an endangered or threatened species
- **August 2, 1977**: Opinion reconsidered based on additional information from [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]; concluded hybrids of listed species were not subject to ESA protection
- **September 21, 1993**: Current memorandum reaffirms 1977 interpretation applies to hybrids of two listed species

The legal analysis emphasizes that the ESA's overriding purpose, based on legislative history, is to conserve and protect the genetic heritage of endangered species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memorandum recognizes that hybridization itself is considered a threat to species conservation. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] has historically taken the position that hybridization may constitute a threat sufficient to support listing as endangered or threatened (40 Fed. Reg. 29863-64).

The opinion cites *Connor v. Andrus* (453 F.Supp. 1037, 1041, D.Tex. 1978), which acknowledged hybridization between [[Mexican Duck]] and [[Mallard]] as a danger to the Mexican duck, alongside habitat destruction.

The genetic argument presented states that while hybrids between two endangered species (e.g., [[Red Wolf]] × [[Gray Wolf]]) contain genetic material entirely from listed species, breeding two such hybrids would not produce purebred red wolves and purebred gray wolves. Therefore, the pure genetic stocks of the parent species would not be conserved through hybrid protection.

## Key Findings

1. **Legal Status**: Hybrids whose parents are both listed species under the ESA are not covered by the Act's protections (16 U.S.C. §§ 1531 et seq.)

2. **Rationale**: Treating hybrids as protected would defeat the ESA's objective because hybrids would receive the same legal protections and conservation measures as purebred specimens, while not actually conserving the pure genetic heritage of either parent species

3. **Genetic Conservation**: The genetic heritage of endangered species is not conserved by protecting hybrids, as breeding hybrids does not regenerate purebred populations of the parent species

4. **Policy Consistency**: Extending ESA protections to hybrids would not promote Congressional purposes in enacting the legislation, which focuses on preserving distinct genetic stocks

5. **Hybridization as Threat**: The Service's position that hybridization constitutes a threat to species supports the conclusion that hybrids should not receive ESA protection, as protecting them would perpetuate the threat rather than mitigate it
