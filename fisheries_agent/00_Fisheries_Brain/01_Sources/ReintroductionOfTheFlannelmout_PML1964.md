# Reintroduction of the Flannelmouth Sucker in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines a [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) population that colonized the tailwater below [[Davis Dam]] on the lower [[Colorado River]], Arizona–Nevada, between 1999 and 2002. The species was historically rare in this reach. Sampling used larval light traps, trammel nets, [[Pit Tagging|PIT-tagged]] mark-recapture, and visual boat surveys. Two independent population estimates were produced: a mark-recapture estimate of 2,286 fish (95% CI 1,847–2,998) and a visual-survey estimate of 6,794 fish. The population was dominated by adult spawners, with recruitment estimated at about 15% per year. The authors conclude that altered river conditions below the dam support natural reproduction, in contrast to the failed recovery of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]].

## Background & Description

*Catostomus latipinnis* is a catostomid sucker native to the upper [[Colorado River]] Basin and its tributaries, where it historically reproduced. Its presence in the lower main stem below Davis Dam was previously uncommon. The study was conducted within an 80-km reach, with the upper 40 km below the dam receiving most of the sampling effort. Sampling sites included the [[Laughlin Lagoon]] area, about 15 km downstream of the dam, and the [[Fort Mohave Ruins]] area, about 25 km downstream.

The authors report that the reach was reached by the species through natural colonization after dam construction. They speculate that upstream water development altered habitat (for example, making shallow, warm, turbid conditions more tributary-like) in ways that favored this species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and dam effects.** The authors argue that water development is usually harmful to native fish, but conditions below Davis Dam were readily used by spawning flannelmouth suckers. Similar colonization of dam tailwaters has been reported elsewhere (for example, [[Lee's Ferry Tailwater]] and the [[White River]] in Colorado). Cool tailwater releases from [[Lake Mohave]] also affected aging, because they prevented sharp annuli from forming.

**Community context.** Larval samples were dominated by non-native and common taxa, including [[Common Carp]] among the larger fish. Cyprinids and centrarchids were the most abundant larval groups. The authors note that physical habitat changes and nonnative predators have severely reduced seven of the nine native fish species of the lower Colorado River. The flannelmouth sucker is an exception that tolerates the altered conditions.

**Comparison with Razorback Sucker.** More than 12 million small [[Razorback Sucker]] were stocked since 1980 with little measurable survival. Current efforts stock larger fish to improve survival, and spawning has been observed, but natural recruitment is not yet documented.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**

- Floating larval light traps, deployed after dusk and retrieved after dawn, collected more than 8,000 fish larvae during 2000 and 2001. Trammel nets, larval nets, and boat-based visual surveys were also used.
- Over the study, more than 9,000 larval fish and 9,095 larger fish were captured in total. Flannelmouth suckers were a small share of the larval catch, and their larvae were less common than many other species.
- Fish longer than 20 cm were marked with [[Pit Tagging|PIT tags]]. Pectoral fin rays and vertebrae were collected for [[Age Determination]], because they showed clearer annuli than otoliths.

**Distribution and abundance**

- Trammel net catch per unit effort was about 50 times higher in the upper 40 km below the dam (3.81 fish/100 m²) than in the 40–80 km reach (0.08 fish/100 m²).
- Two large spawning congregations (more than 200 fish each) were observed at the same locations during the study: one near Laughlin Lagoon and one near the Fort Mohave Ruins.
- Sixty-nine percent of captured suckers were taken within 100 m of the lagoon entrance, and 73% of those were females.
- Adults were commonly seen in the main channel, while spawning fish were found in shallow areas adjacent to spawning concentrations.

**Population structure and age**

- Size distributions were similar across 1999, 2000, and 2001. Fewer than 5% of captured fish were immature, and most were spawning adults.
- Nearly all (94.8%) suckers collected were adults longer than 35 cm.
- Age data from 119 vertebrae and fin rays showed that 73% of fish were 8–13 years old, with most growth occurring by age 6.
- The oldest sucker was 24 years old, younger than a 39-year-old fish reported by [[Minckley]] (cited in Scoppettone and Vinyard 1991).

**Population estimates**

- Mark-recapture: 715 fish were PIT-tagged between 1999 and 2001. Eight field surveys in 2002 captured 329 suckers, of which 115 were recaptures. The estimated population was 2,286 (95% CI 1,847–2,998). Marked fish were predominantly mature females (73%).
- Visual surveys: 301 suckers were observed in the upper 40 km reach, none in the 40–80 km reach. Density averaged 8.6 fish/ha, giving an estimate of 6,794 fish (8.6 fish/ha × 790 ha). The authors consider this more realistic than the mark-recapture figure, which they suspect is biased low, but no replicates were taken for statistical analysis.
- Both approaches indicate that the population had grown well beyond the number initially stocked.

**Reproduction and recruitment**

- Each mature female can produce up to 15,000 eggs per year and may spawn for more than two decades.
- Recruitment into the spawning cohort (ages 3–4) averaged slightly more than 15% per year (range 9.4–31.3%) over the four-year study.
- The authors calculate that about 15% annual recruitment could replace the spawning cohort if adult mortality is low. Their estimate is low compared with short-lived species (above 50%), but adequate for this long-lived species.
- Earlier work by [[Weiss et al. 1998]] attributed poor recruitment at the Paria–Colorado confluence to cold, fluctuating hydropower releases. [[McKinney et al. 1999]] found a relatively stable population at Lee's Ferry despite scarce young. The present authors suggest that young fish, though scarce, are sufficient to maintain the Davis Dam population.

**Conclusions**

- Altered habitat below Davis Dam supports natural flannelmouth sucker reproduction, which historically occurred mainly in upper tributaries.
- The successful colonization shows that one native species can tolerate altered conditions that other natives cannot.
- The authors suggest that similar opportunities may exist for other native fishes in altered rivers where those species were historically absent or uncommon.
