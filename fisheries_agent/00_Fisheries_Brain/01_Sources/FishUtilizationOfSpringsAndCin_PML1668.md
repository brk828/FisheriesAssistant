# Fish Utilization of Springs and Ciénegas in the Arid Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This paper argues that springs and [[Ciénegas]] (riparian marshes), although they cover only a small fraction of surface water in the arid American Southwest, are critical to the evolution, endemism and persistence of the region's native fishes. The author, [[Gary K. Meffe]], compiled distribution data for 81 native fish species in a defined core region, finding that 24 species (about one-third of the fauna) are restricted to springs or ciénegas and that 49 of 81 species use these wetlands in some way. At least 20 fish taxa have gone extinct in the Southwest in this century, and most extinctions are linked to the loss or alteration of wetland habitat. The paper concludes that the remaining wetland fauna is in immediate danger and that protecting these ecosystems is essential.

## Background & Description

**Aquatic setting.** The Southwest is extremely arid, and lakes are essentially absent as fish habitat. Its aquatic systems consist of streams, [[Desert Springs]] and ciénegas. Streams are unstable, subject to low flow, intermittent drying and destructive flash floods. Desert springs are comparatively constant in flow and physicochemistry, although some carry harsh conditions such as low pH or high free carbon dioxide that exclude fishes.

**Types of desert springs.** Springs range from tiny seeps with barely perceptible flow to large limnocrenes producing hundreds to thousands of liters per second. Thermal conditions range from near-ambient to lethally hot. Springs are typically located at discharge points of meteoric water along fracture zones, and many are heated by the geothermal gradient or by contact with volcanic rock. Common features are constant environmental conditions, geographic isolation and endemism.

**Ciénegas.** Ciénegas are saturated, organic marshes that act as climax communities and provide more stable habitat than streams or springs. They occur at low (<1000 m) to moderate (~2000 m) elevations, usually along stream headwaters or associated with spring outflows. They range from small patches at points of slack flow to marshes covering many hectares. Typical vegetation includes emergent macrophytes such as cattails, bulrushes and sedges, along with submerged macrophytes.

**Study scope.** The survey covered the [[Rio Grande Basin]] (upstream from and including the Devil's River), the [[Colorado River Basin]], [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]] drainages, California, all of Nevada and western Utah. It included only freshwater species of southwestern occurrence, excluding marine species and widespread species with peripheral Southwest populations. Local distributions and habitat use were drawn from [[Lee et al. Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes]], the literature, local experts and field observations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Isolation and endemism.** Surface waters are scarce, so springs and streams function as aquatic "islands" in an arid "ocean" of land that blocks interhabitat movement. Long isolation has produced in situ genetic and morphological differentiation and a high level of [[Endemism]], especially in springs. The fauna includes many monotypic genera and relict species from the wetter [[Pleistocene Epoch]]. Even very small waters may harbor unique biota.

**Physical and chemical limits.** Constancy is often paired with harsh chemistry, so only some fishes can persist in any given spring. Springs with benign chemistry support more diverse faunas.

**Comparison with the Southeast.** The author contrasted the Southwest with [[Peninsular Florida]] and southern Georgia. The Southeast has a rich and endemic fauna too, but it is also marked by abundant rivers, lakes and underground aquifer networks that connect habitats. In the Southeast, only 1 of 31 species (the Dixie chub) is restricted to springs or wetlands, and nine species use springs and marshes (Table 2). The author argues that fishes depend more heavily on wetlands in the arid Southwest than in the mesic Southeast.

**Cuatro Ciénegas, Mexico.** The Cuatro Ciénegas basin, an intermontane basin in the Chihuahuan Desert of Coahuila, has a notably high proportion of endemic biota, including plants, aquatic mollusks, snails and fishes. Springheads reach over 200 m in diameter and range from a few centimeters to over 10 m deep. Extensive subterranean flow connects many springs. Several native fish species occur there, most of which are endemic or recognized at the subspecific level (Table 3). The majority of this fauna depends directly or indirectly on spring wetlands. Mollusks show similar dependence, with high endemism often restricted to spring runs and outflows.

**Human impacts.** Dewatering, damming, diversion and the introduction of large predaceous fishes have drastically altered or eliminated many desert aquatic ecosystems. Some formerly wide-ranging species now survive mainly in ciénegas and springs, which act as their last refugia. Arroyo cutting in the late 19th century drained many wetlands and contributed to habitat loss.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish species dependence on wetlands.** Of 81 native species in the core Southwest group, 49 use springs and/or ciénegas. Approximately one-third of fish taxa are restricted to springs and ciénegas. The exact restricted count is partly illegible in the scan. Restricted species are mainly in the families [[Cyprinodontidae]] (killifishes), [[Poeciliidae]] (livebearers) and related groups. Table 1 lists about 90 spring-associated taxa, with extinct forms marked. Eight of the listed taxa are now extinct.

**2. Extinctions.** At least 20 fish taxa (species and subspecies) have gone extinct in the Southwest in the last century, many of them spring or spring-influenced endemics restricted to single or very few localities. The exact count for the most recent three decades is partly illegible in the scan. Extinctions were caused mainly by habitat alteration and by introduced fishes. Isolated spring endemics are especially vulnerable, because a single population's loss can eliminate a species.

**3. Sonoran topminnow case study.** The Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) was described in 1941 from the Colorado River system. It was later listed as endangered (1973 per the scan). Once widespread, it had been reduced to a few localities, mostly springs and ciénegas or ciénega-influenced streams, because of habitat destruction and introduced predators. The species now persists in only a small number of sites.

**4. Cuatro Ciénegas endemism.** The Cuatro Ciénegas basin is a natural laboratory for evolutionary biology. Most of its fishes are endemic to the basin or recognized as distinct at the subspecific level, and the majority of the fauna depends directly or indirectly on spring wetlands.

**5. Conservation conclusion.** Although wetlands make up only a small percentage of surface water in the Southwest, they support up to 15% of the region's ichthyofauna. Many of these fishes are endemic and depend largely or exclusively on wetlands. The author concludes that the remaining fauna is in danger from continued human population growth, groundwater pumping and other anthropogenic change, and that protecting spring and ciénega ecosystems is urgent. Further extinctions are considered likely without action.
