# Addendum to the Environmental Report of the Virgin River Water Resource Development Project, Clark County, Nevada (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 addendum, prepared by Woodward-Clyde Consultants, Dames & Moore and the [[Las Vegas Valley Water District]], updates the 1992 environmental report for a proposed diversion of [[Virgin River]] water at [[Halfway Wash]]. Design changes since the original report, including a new water right application filed in the name of the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]], altered the project's footprint, diversion rate and expected effects. The addendum also reports preliminary results of a 1993 fishery study funded by the District and conducted by BIO/WEST, Inc., on the river downstream from the [[Riverside Bridge]].

The main changes are a lower diversion dam (50 to 30 feet), a smaller diversion reservoir (355 to 105 acres), a larger storage reservoir (1,100 to 1,860 acres) and a higher maximum diversion rate (500 to 700 cubic feet per second, cfs). The fish findings show that the reach is used by several exotic species and by sensitive native fishes, including [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]], whose habitat would be lost or altered by the reservoir.

## Background & Description

The project began as a 1989 application by the District for Virgin River water. It was amended in May 1992 to move the point of diversion to Halfway Wash, consistent with the primary development option being studied by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR). In February 1993 the District became the operating entity for the SNWA, and the application was transferred and its diversion rate revised. In September 1993 the USBR advised that its salinity phase was not needed to meet Colorado River salinity goals, so the District proceeded without USBR assistance.

Key project parameters:

- **Diversion dam:** reduced from 50 to 30 feet high; reservoir reduced from 355 to about 105 acres, inundating about 1 mile of river rather than 2 miles.
- **Diversion rate:** 700 cfs (up from 500 cfs), to exploit brief high flows.
- **Average annual diversion:** 83,000 to 99,000 acre-feet (AF). The most recent application states 113,000 AF, which provides planning flexibility.
- **Storage reservoir:** 113,000 AF operating storage (previously 70,000 AF), located about 2.2 miles upstream of the Halfway Wash confluence, with a surface area of 1,860 acres at normal maximum pool.
- **Pumping:** three stations with 50,000, 17,250 and 13,950 horsepower lift water to the [[Las Vegas Valley]] via gravity flow through a 54-inch pipeline.
- **Land disturbance:** total permanent disturbance rises from 1,582 acres (original) to 2,030 acres (addendum), a net increase of 448 acres.

The facility design is preliminary and awaits State Engineer action, a final feasibility study and [[National Environmental Policy Act]] compliance. Groundwater diversions in the Virgin River Valley were placed on hold, so they are not evaluated here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water quality.** The hydrologic basin model estimates an average annual flow of about 141,000 AF at Halfway Wash. A linear regression estimated about 154,000 AF, and the two models agree within the accuracy of the historic discharge data. Simulated diversions reduce flow at [[Lake Mead]]. Net depletion from the [[Colorado River]] system is estimated at 45,000 to 54,000 AF per year. Total dissolved solids (TDS) in the Virgin River average about 1,800 mg/L during the October to May operating period, and diversions would remove an estimated 110,000 to 135,000 tons of salt per year. Phosphorus and nitrogen reductions to Lake Mead are estimated at about 60 and 180 tons, which the authors consider insignificant.

**Vegetation.** The diversion reservoir would inundate 105 acres of riparian vegetation: about 47% open water or bare sand, 51% dominated by [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) and 2% primarily native vegetation. Creosote bush scrub, which is not inundated under the new design, would bear most of the increased footprint, with a net increase of about 650 acres.

**Wildlife.** Large game, raptors and wild horses and burros are not expected to be affected. The 7-mile riparian corridor below the dam is mostly saltcedar, so small game (mourning doves and Gambel's quail) are the only species of special concern.

**Sensitive species.** The desert tortoise is federally threatened and would receive preconstruction surveys and USFWS consultation ([[Desert Tortoise]]). The loggerhead shrike, chuckwalla, gila monster and Arizona southwestern toad are federal candidates, and the phainopepla is state protected. The relict leopard frog, thought to be extinct, may still survive, and a cooperative study is underway. Cacti and yucca are protected under Nevada Revised Statute 527.060–120.

**Land use.** The project would permanently affect an additional 420 acres of [[Bureau of Land Management]] land, reducing grazing by 8 to 24 Animal Unit Months. Private land affected falls to 28 acres, down from 115 acres in the original design, and would be purchased by the District.

## Key Findings

**Fish community (1993 study, Holden, written communication).**

- Native species below the Riverside Bridge include [[Desert Sucker]], found only as drifting juveniles, and [[Speckled Dace]].
- Exotic species include common carp, channel catfish, black bullhead, green sunfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], red shiner and mosquito fish. Adults of all except largemouth bass were present, suggesting successful spawning in the river. Largemouth bass young likely originate in local farm ponds.
- Red shiner, mosquito fish and carp are found throughout the reach. Channel catfish, black bullhead, green sunfish and largemouth bass are restricted to rare, deep pools.

**Sensitive fishes.**

- [[Woundfin]] (federally endangered) were found in 1992 and 1993 but not in all sampling periods. No young of the year were found, suggesting downstream drift from upstream areas. They were the most abundant native species in September 1993.
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (federally endangered): two individuals (one adult, one young) were found in 1993. Only one other chub has been collected in this reach in the last 15 years.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (state-listed, federal candidate): juveniles were relatively common in 1993. The species is abundant in the upper Colorado River basin but rare in the mainstream below Lake Mead.
- [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (federal candidate) is found mainly upstream of the Riverside Bridge, where cool, clear tributary water is available.

**Habitat change at Halfway Wash.** Downstream of Halfway Wash the gradient decreases, the channel becomes wider, shallower and less rocky, and flow partly moves into the riparian corridors. About three miles downstream the main channel is dry. Only flannelmouth sucker and speckled dace were found below Halfway Wash, at lower abundance than upstream, while exotic numbers (especially largemouth bass and green sunfish) increase.

**Predicted impacts on fish.**

- The diversion reservoir would lose about 1 mile of stream habitat, including habitat for woundfin, Virgin River chub and flannelmouth sucker.
- The reservoir is expected to fill with sand and silt within one to two years, producing a wider, shallower stream like the reach below Halfway Wash. This is expected to reduce native fish use.
- Short term, the reservoir may favor black bullhead, channel catfish and green sunfish, increasing predation and competition on natives.
- Backwater effects may alter about an additional mile of stream upstream of the dam.
- Habitat below the Riverside Bridge is seasonally fragmented when the river dries. Whether isolated fish form viable populations is unknown. Two competing hypotheses are that lower-river fish are lost to the population, or that they survive in perennial pools until floods reconnect them.
- The Muddy River will not be affected by the new application.

**Mitigation and monitoring.** The District commits to monitoring water tables in downstream riparian communities, periodic vegetation mapping, monitoring of revegetated areas and wildlife populations, and an evapotranspiration study of saltcedar and native vegetation by researchers at [[University of Nevada-Las Vegas]] and [[University of Nevada-Reno]]. Proposed native revegetation includes cottonwoods and willows in low-salinity sandy soils and quailbush, four-wing saltbush and inkweed in saline areas. Bypass flows of 10, 25 and 45 cfs were modeled, but required flows will be set by regulatory agencies. The District is also funding a study of how woundfin and Virgin River chub use the lower river.

**Cultural resources and open items.** The additional 450 acres require pedestrian archaeological surveys and State Historic Preservation Officer approval. Baseline flow requirements and annual diversion volumes remain undetermined pending agency decisions.
