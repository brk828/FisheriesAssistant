# The Problems of Appeasement and of Sexual Roles in the Courtship Behavior of the Blackchin Mouthbreeder, *Tilapia melanotheron*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

Barlow and Green (University of California, Berkeley) tested courtship behavior in paired [[Blackchin Mouthbreeder]] (*Tilapia melanotheron*, family [[Cichlidae]]) in a laboratory arena. The study examined two problems: whether courtship follows the [[Appeasement Hypothesis]] or the [[Arousal Hypothesis]], and whether the sexes reverse their usual courtship roles. The appeasement prediction, that courtship rises as the mate becomes relatively larger, was "amply borne out." Sexual differences were real but small, "of degree and not of kind." Among the ten recorded action patterns, [[Nodding]], [[Tail Beating]] and [[Quivering]] formed the core of courtship, and they varied together. The authors also concluded that earlier reports of females courting more than males likely reflected an unequal distribution of relative size among the pairs previously studied.

## Background & Description

*Tilapia melanotheron* is a mouthbrooding cichlid. Earlier work on the group included [[Aronson 1949]], who reported that females courted more throughout courtship. This contrasted with studies of other cichlids, such as [[Orange Chromide]] (*[[Etroplus Maculatus]]*), in which males court more until spawning approaches. [[Heinrich 1967]] reached similar conclusions for the same species, which he called *T. heudelotii macrocephala*, but studied only the period just before spawning.

Two taxonomic notes apply. Van den Audenaerde considered *T. macrocephala* and *T. heudelotii* synonyms of *T. melanotheron*. The Addendum later reported that *T. melanotheron* is a senior synonym of *T. macrocephala*, a coastal species from the Ivory Coast to Cameroon, while *T. heudelotii* is a distinct Senegalese coastal species. Recent field work in the Ivory Coast, Liberia and Sierra Leone supports two allopatric, related species.

Sexes were identified by a large opercular spot: yellow in males and red in females, per the Discussion.

The authors chose this species partly because it is peaceable and tolerant of large size differences between mates. Screens were therefore unnecessary, so higher levels of behavior could be observed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Animals.** Parents came from an aquarium shop with unknown history. Male offspring were reared under two regimes, one crowded and underfed, one spacious and well fed. This produced a male size range of 12 to 108 g (9-fold) and a female range of 13 to 75 g (6-fold). Fish were about 1.5 years old during trials and were held by sex for over two months beforehand.

**Physical conditions.** Water was tap water with 5 g marine salt/l, pH 6.8 to 7.6, and temperature 28 ± 0.2 °C, filtered by sub-gravel filters. Lighting was warm-white fluorescent at 250 f.c. at the water surface, on at 06:00 and off at 20:00. Bottom substrate was No. 20 gravel, 5 cm deep. Fish were fed trout chow, Tetramin, wheat germ, oats, tubificid worms or duckweed.

**Arena.** A central compartment held about 20 guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*), 6 mollies (*Poecilia* spp.) and a heater. Four equal compartments each held one pair of subjects, with observation through a dark viewing port.

**Procedure.** A total of 35 trials were run. Each trial placed four pairs in the compartments and observed each pair for 20 minutes. Action patterns were tallied with keys linked to an Esterline-Angus recorder.

**Behavioral categories.** Ten action patterns were scored, using Aronson's names where available: yawning, foraging, digging (scooping, plunging and sucking), charging (nipping), chafing, nodding, tail beating (tail-slapping), quivering (body-quiver), nipping (nest-cleaning) and skimming (passing-nest).

## Key Findings

**1. Pair success.** Of 150 pairings attempted (including pilot tests), 110 succeeded. Seven pairs spawned on the observation day and were excluded from the size-effect analyses. Pair success did not differ significantly across ten relative-weight groups. When pairs were combined into male-larger and female-larger groups, success was significantly higher when the male was smaller (p < .005). Males were typically about 80% of female weight in the setups.

**2. Relative weight distribution.** The most frequent relative weights of males fell between 70% and 140% of the female's weight. Absolute and relative weights were positively related: small males tended to be absolutely small.

**3. Correlations within sexes.** Of 90 coefficients, 22 (24%) were significant, against about 5 expected by chance. Nodding, tail beating and quivering were involved in 48 coefficients and produced 20 of the 22 significant values, including all 13 at p < .01. Charging was mostly negatively correlated with the courtship patterns. Yawning, chafing and foraging were largely unrelated to them.

**4. Correlations between sexes.** Of 100 coefficients, 18 were significant. Twelve (67%) involved nodding, tail beating or quivering. Excluding same-pattern pairs such as male forage versus female forage, 10 of 12 (83%) still came from these three. A general pattern was that when one fish courted more, its mate courted less.

**5. Effect of absolute weight.** Of 80 correlations, 15 (19%) were significant, with 6 at p < .01. The pattern was inconsistent. Yawning and foraging were negatively associated with absolute weight, so smaller fish yawned and foraged more. Larger fish tended to nod and quiver more. The authors suggested that smaller, hungrier fish sought food rather than courting, which argues for a general reduction in courtship rather than competition with foraging.

**6. Effect of relative weight.** Of 60 correlations, 14 (23%) were significant. Nodding, tail beating and quivering showed large, frequently significant negative correlations with relative size. Charging was barely affected by relative size.

**7. Regression and sex differences.** Least-squares lines showed that males and females differed significantly for nodding, tail beating, quivering, and to a lesser degree yawning, foraging and digging. Chafing, charging, nipping and skimming could each be described by one common line. The differences were small: the female lines for nodding, tail beating and quivering lay above and were steeper than the male lines, but both sexes showed a strong negative relation between relative size and courtship.

**8. Appeasement versus arousal.** The data were in good agreement with the appeasement hypothesis. Both sexes showed negative slopes for most courtship patterns, which the arousal hypothesis would not predict for females. Because a clear species-typical size relationship could not be found, the arousal hypothesis could not be tested adequately. The authors noted that the apparent link between larger mates and less charging supported the premise that relative size signals potential threat.

**9. Interpretation of sexual roles.** Using the male-standard and female-reflected lines, the authors suggested that Aronson's finding of female-dominated courtship may reflect samples biased toward large males. When the male was large, the small female courted more. When sizes were similar, the sexes courted about equally.

**10. Comparison with earlier data.** This study recorded higher behavior rates than Aronson's. For example, tail beating in equal-size pairs averaged about 40 per 20 minutes on day one. Aronson's females reached at most about 2 per 15 minutes. Possible explanations include observer differences, husbandry, temperature (Aronson did not report his) and genetic changes in long-kept stocks.

**11. Courtship length.** Aronson reported a courtship of one to two weeks. Here, fish often spawned within a few days of pairing and sometimes on the first day. The authors placed the species between typical mouthbrooders and substrate breeders in pair-bond durability.

**12. Brood care.** Males carry eggs in the mouth for 10 to 14 days, eating little or nothing during that time. Aborted cycles follow if a new cycle begins too soon. This may explain the relatively long courtship phase, since both sexes must be physiologically prepared for spawning.

**13. Conclusions.** The authors concluded that which sex courts more depends on which sex is smaller. They doubted that the observed sex differences are biologically very meaningful. The core courtship signals are nodding, tail beating and quivering. Digging is part of courtship because it builds the spawning pit. Sexual roles are not reversed; the apparent reversal reflects size arrangement.
