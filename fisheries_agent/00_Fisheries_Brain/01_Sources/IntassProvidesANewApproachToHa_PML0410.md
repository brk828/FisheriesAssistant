# INHASS Habitat Evaluation Method and Clavey River Rainbow Trout Population Model

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two freshwater fisheries items. The first is a Department of the Interior bulletin describing the [[INHASS]] (Interaction Assessment) method, a statistical approach for evaluating how habitat and species interactions shape fish population growth. The method is illustrated with an example for [[American Eel]] in the [[West River]], Vermont, in summer 1983. The second is a 1990 journal article by E. F. Cheslak and A. S. Jacobson in *Rivers* (Volume 1, Number 4, October 1990). It uses a life-stage-based population model to predict how a proposed flow release schedule would affect [[Rainbow Trout]] in the [[Clavey River]]. The model predicts a net enhancement of the adult fishery, with a mean increase of 77% across the project area and a 107% average adult increase at [[Cottonwood Bridge]].

The first page is a largely illegible form (apparently a shipping or permit form with partial text on shipment, quantities and signatures). It contains no usable scientific data and is not summarized further.

## Background & Description

**INHASS.** The bulletin presents INHASS as a newly developed analytic method for evaluating habitat. Its stated purpose is to determine how the shape of density-dependent fitness responses, and the intensity of interactions among species, affect population density. It can estimate the impacts of habitat changes without requiring habitat-specific population densities. The approach uses data from fish collected across several habitat patches, and it does not require a complete census of specimens. It is based on the assumption of equal fitness across habitat patches: on average, individuals occupying patches of one habitat type are assumed to show the same fitness as individuals in patches of other types. Under this assumption, a statistical best fit to constant fitness over the representative habitat can be used to fit model parameters. The bulletin states that copies of the INHASS software are available on request. The technique is also said to apply to plants, with tests and theory presented in [[Emlen et al. 1989]].

**Clavey River model.** The Cheslak and Jacobson article describes a spatially explicit population model of rainbow trout in the Clavey River. The model divides the river into 43 reaches and tracks three life stages: fry, juveniles and adults. Carrying capacity is tied to physical habitat, measured as [[Weighted Usable Area]] (WUA). The model is used to compare pre-project and post-project conditions under a recommended flow release schedule.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**American eel and the West River (INHASS example).** The example model estimates eel fitness from density feedback and from the abundance of other fish. The predictor variables are:

- Distance to shore (D)
- Substrate coarseness (SC)
- Substrate heterogeneity (SH)
- Absolute stream velocity (V)
- Small smallmouth bass (<10 cm length)
- Large smallmouth bass
- Longnose dace (LND)
- Blacknose dace (BND)
- Eel density

All variables are in standardized (standard deviation) units. Density feedback is negative, which is consistent with density-dependent regulation. The model shows several interactions:

- Small [[Smallmouth Bass]] have a facilitative effect on eel fitness (negative beta).
- Large smallmouth bass have a negative effect.
- [[Blacknose Dace]] have a negative effect, probably competitive, that is nearly twice the magnitude of density feedback from eels themselves.
- Loss of substrate heterogeneity is expected to raise eel fitness and lead to decreased population density.

**Rainbow trout and the Clavey River.** Habitat and temperature control the population dynamics at the two model sites.

- *Upper Clavey (Cottonwood Bridge).* Fry appear in the model only during late spring and summer. Recruitment from the egg/alevin stage typically starts in May and produces far more fry than can survive. Juveniles recruit from fry starting four weeks after emergence. Adults recruit from juveniles each January. Limiting events are mainly periods of extremely high late-spring flow, when WUA drops. Low summer flows also reduce physical habitat, but the effect is smaller.
- *Lower Clavey (km 10).* High late-summer water temperature is a significant limiting factor under natural flow. Juveniles and adults suffer thermal mortality, and the population rarely approaches habitat-driven carrying capacity. Some years see nearly total juvenile loss as fry advance. The reach becomes unpopulated after several years of recurring high temperatures and can be reseeded by migration from upstream reaches, where temperatures are more suitable.

The model can run life-stage-specific analyses for each of the 43 reaches, which allows comparison of total population responses with reach-specific responses.

## Key Findings

**INHASS (habitat evaluation method)**

- INHASS estimates the shape of density-dependent fitness and the impacts of habitat changes using data from multiple habitat patches. It does not require habitat-specific population densities.
- The method assumes equal mean fitness across habitat patches. Model parameters are fitted by statistical best fit to constant fitness across representative habitat.
- The American eel example (West River, Vermont, summer 1983) shows that density feedback, several smallmouth bass and dace size classes, substrate, and velocity all affect eel fitness.
- Competition from blacknose dace has a larger negative effect on eel fitness than eels' own density feedback.
- Loss of substrate heterogeneity is predicted to increase eel fitness.

**Clavey River rainbow trout model (Cheslak and Jacobson, 1990)**

- The model covers 43 reaches and three life stages, with carrying capacity tied to WUA.
- At Cottonwood Bridge, high spring flows are the main limit on the population. Adult recruitment is strongest each January, when juveniles recruit into the adult stage.
- At the lower Clavey (km 10) reach, high summer water temperature is the main limit. Thermal mortality keeps the population well below habitat-based carrying capacity, and the reach can be reseeded only by upstream migration.
- The total relative adult abundance (a sum over all 43 reaches, standardized to the maximum pre-project total) shows a mean percent increase of 77%, averaged over all weeks, under the recommended flow release schedule. The authors attribute this to improved recruitment into the adult stage and higher adult survival.
- At Cottonwood Bridge, project operations produce an overall average adult enhancement of 107%. Project flows remove or reduce most natural high-flow events, which stabilizes physical habitat at a higher, less variable level after week 221 post-project. High flows still occur under post-project conditions, but they are shorter in duration.
- The authors conclude that the project will result in a net enhancement of the rainbow trout fishery resource.

**Overall.** The two items use different methods but share a theme: population response depends on interactions among life stages, habitat, and environmental stressors such as density feedback, flow, and temperature. INHASS offers a general statistical framework for quantifying these interactions, while the Clavey River model applies a life-stage simulation to evaluate a specific flow management decision.
