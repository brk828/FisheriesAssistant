# Longitudinal Variation in Fish Community Structure and Water Temperature in the Upper Colorado River: Implications for Colorado Pikeminnow Habitat Suitability

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (March 1999, Recovery Implementation Program Project No. 48, author Douglas B. Osmundson) examines how forage fish availability and water temperature shape the distribution and carrying capacity of adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in the upper [[Colorado River]]. The adult population (estimated at 500-600 fish in 1991-1994) is well below the roughly 5,500 adults that [[Lentsch et al. 1998]] estimated as a minimum for long-term viability. The study combined electrofishing surveys, body condition, diet analysis, and thermal-unit modeling to delimit suitable range and estimate carrying capacity.

Key conclusions: forage-size fish are about three times denser upstream of [[Westwater Canyon]] than downstream; adult pikeminnow density falls off sharply where growth temperatures drop below about 40 annual thermal units (ATU); passage at diversion dams and possible temperature augmentation on the [[Gunnison River]] could expand suitable range by 74-115%; and preliminary carrying capacity estimates are roughly 700 adults currently, rising to about 1,000 with passage and 1,200 with Gunnison temperature augmentation.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] is endangered and restricted to warm-water reaches of the Colorado, Green, and San Juan rivers and tributaries. Historical accounts indicate it was far more abundant in the upper Colorado River. The report attributes declines to reduced adult range (diversion barriers and cold-water releases), reduced prey quality and quantity, altered food webs from nonnative species, reduced spring flood pulses, and loss of preferred mesohabitats.

The study area covered the mainstem Colorado River from the [[Green River]] confluence upstream to Rifle, Colorado, plus a 19-km section of the Gunnison River. Of 15 initial strata, 12 were sampled (numbered 1-11 upstream from the Green River confluence, with the Gunnison River as Stratum 12); four were omitted, comprising 53 of 388 km (14%). Sampling used [[Electrofishing]] in spring and fall, with catch per effort used as a relative abundance index. Condition was measured as relative weight (Kn), and diets were examined through stomach sampling. Temperature was tracked with thermographs, and thermal units were calculated using the [[Kaeding and Osmundson Thermal Unit Method]].

Native suckers and chubs were the dominant species, and the report focuses on bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker, and roundtail chub as the main native forage species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage availability:** Catch rates of fish 100-300 mm TL (soft-rayed, forage-size) were roughly three times higher upstream of Westwater Canyon. Stratum 1 had the lowest catch rate (0.1 fish/min overall). Downstream forage was mostly young [[Bluehead Sucker]] confined to riffles, while upstream forage included several species in both runs and riffles.
- **Riffles vs. runs:** Riffles were "islands of productivity." Bluehead and flannelmouth sucker catch rates were significantly higher in riffles on a river-wide basis; downstream run habitats held little usable forage.
- **Body condition:** Kn of bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker, and roundtail chub generally increased upstream and was higher in spring than fall. The Gunnison stratum had the highest condition.
- **Diet:** Organic debris dominated diets (bluehead 83%, flannelmouth 55%, roundtail chub 43%). Invertebrates were the second major item for suckers (flannelmouth 41%, bluehead 16%), with [[Chironomidae]] dominant downstream and Ephemeroptera, Simulidae, and Trichoptera more common upstream. Roundtail chub also consumed fish (29%), mostly cyprinids. Adult pikeminnow 400-550 mm consumed mainly small nonnative minnows (e.g., [[Fathead Minnow]]).
- **Temperature:** ATU declined upstream. Stratum 1 values were about double those at Stratum 10 (Cameo), roughly 234 km upstream. Reported site means included Gunnison at rk 53 (estimated 40 ATU), Yampa at rk 150 (46.5 ATU), and Yampa at rk 188 (41.3 ATU).
- **Temperature threshold:** Adult pikeminnow in the [[Yampa River]] and Gunnison River declined sharply upstream of about 40 ATU, suggesting that year-round feeding ranges require at least this thermal input. Upstream use above that point is limited to late-summer feeding forays.
- **Nonnative species:** Introduced [[Red Shiner]], fathead minnow, and sand shiner made up 84-99% of backwater small-fish samples from the ISMP surveys.

## Key Findings

**Fish community and forage (Results)**
- Upstream strata mean total catch rates were about 2-5 fish/electrofishing minute, versus less than 1 fish/min downstream.
- Strata 7, 8, 9, 11, and 12 had significantly higher forage-size catch rates than strata 1-6.
- Stratum 11 had significantly higher white sucker and salmonid (mainly [[Mountain Whitefish]]) catch rates than any other stratum.
- The Gunnison stratum (Stratum 12) had forage-size catch rates as high as or higher than any Colorado River stratum, driven largely by bluehead suckers.
- Temporal variation was largest in forage-size fish; fall 1994 catch rates were significantly higher than other periods riverwide.

**Body condition and diet**
- Relative condition regression coefficients: bluehead sucker (n = 4,430), flannelmouth sucker (n = 3,949), roundtail chub (n = 562).
- Bluehead suckers in fall 1995 had higher condition than in fall 1994, and flannelmouth condition in spring 1995 was lower than in spring 1994.
- Diet of Colorado pikeminnow was hard to interpret: 39-48% by volume was unidentified fish or fish parts. Of stomachs with identifiable fish (n = 27), 63% held one fish.
- Pikeminnow feeding appeared to decline before spawning: 93% of 400-550 mm fish sampled 26 May-20 June had empty stomachs, versus 41% before 26 May.

**Carrying capacity and range**
- Preliminary carrying capacity: about 1-2 adults/km in downstream strata and 3-5 adults/km upstream (2-3 and 5-7 per mile).
- Current estimate: about 700 adults in the Colorado River up to Grand Valley Diversion Dam plus the lower 3.5 km of the Gunnison River.
- With passage at all diversions: about 1,000 adults. With Gunnison temperature augmentation: about 1,200.
- Predator-to-prey biomass ratio estimated at 6-9%, based on forage biomass of 14.1 kg/ha from [[Anderson 1997]].
- Range expansion: Redlands passage (1996) added about 50 km; Grand Valley passage (1998) added about 5 km; Price-Stubb and Government Highline passage would add about 17 km more. Aspinall Unit selective withdrawal could add about 40 km (Gunnison to Delta).

**Recommendations**
1. Provide passage at Price-Stubb and Government-Highline diversion dams on the Colorado River.
2. Start a feasibility study for warming Gunnison River temperatures near Delta by modifying Aspinall Unit outlet structures. Simulations suggested the Delta ATU could rise from 32 to 46 with June, September, and October warming of 1°C and July-August warming of 2°C.
3. Conduct bioenergetic studies to refine carrying capacity estimates.

**Broader conclusion:** Even with improved reproduction and recruitment, limited adult habitat capacity may keep the population below the viability target. Management that boosts riverine productivity (for example, reducing fine sediment and restoring spring flood pulses) is needed alongside range expansion.
