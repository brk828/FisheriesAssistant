# Life Cycle and Ecology of Gammarus fasciatus Say in Western Lake Erie

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 Ohio State University contribution (Franz Theodore Stone Institute of Hydrobiology, Contribution No. 12, by Howard P. Clemens) examines the life cycle, culture, toxicity, distribution and population dynamics of the amphipod [[Gammarus Fasciatus]] in [[Lake Erie]]. The work was framed as part of limnological programs assessing fish food, since the amphipod is a major macrobenthic food source for [[Lake Erie Fishes]] such as [[Yellow Perch]], [[Whitefish]], [[Sauger]] and [[Sturgeon]]. Field sampling used a newly developed net method, and laboratory culturing allowed observation of life-cycle stages unobtainable in nature. Study period: 1948 to 1949, with biweekly collections across a full year.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Gammaridae is the largest North American freshwater amphipod family. The genus *Gammarus* includes six freshwater species, of which *G. fasciatus* and *G. limnaeus* are the most widely known in the Nearctic. Identification of the study material was confirmed by C. R. Shoemaker of the U.S. National Museum.
- **Morphology:** Males are longer and more laterally compressed than females, have second antennae at least equal to the first, and possess a massive third uropod pair. Females carry four pairs of brood plates forming a [[Brood Pouch]] (marsupium); males have one pair of breeding papillae on the seventh peraeon segment.
- **Leg numbering:** The author adopted a segment-based numbering system (gnathopods are modified peraeopods 1 and 2), abandoning the older convention.
- **Locomotion:** Males swim quickly with the body extended; females move in a curved posture. Three pairs of pleopods propel the animal; the third uropods act as a rudder.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Reported from the Caribbean seacoast to the St. Lawrence River system and from the Atlantic coast west to the Mississippi River. Records were compiled in a table of about 20 authorities; the author excluded some questionable records (e.g., Kunkel 1918 from Burlington, Iowa) and noted that confusion with *G. limnaeus* complicated earlier reports.
- **Habitat:** Found in streams, springs, ponds and lakes. Generally most abundant in colder water; associated with algae (e.g., *Cladophora glomerata*) and rooted vegetation (*Valisneria*, *Potamogeton*, *Myriophyllum*). Earlier water-chemistry reports conflicted; the author concluded these differences do not clearly control distribution.
- **Behaviour:** Positively thigmotropic, positively rheotropic and negatively phototropic. Individuals burrow into fine sediments, which protects them and maintains respiratory water flow.
- **Feeding:** Omnivorous, consuming living and dead plant and animal matter, zooplankton (*Daphnia*, *Leptodora*, copepods), other gammarids, its own moults and faeces. Cannibalism was notable, especially in males. Individuals also gnawed untreated cotton and linen fishing net threads, and anecdotal reports suggested net damage in shallow water.
- **Fish predation:** Not studied directly; the author noted it may be most important in October and winter.

## Key Findings

**Culturing and toxicity**
- Filtered Lake Erie water with yeast (0.2 mg every second day) and dried elm leaf (*Ulmus americana*) gave the best survival: 179 of 200 young survived the first moult. Hackberry leaf proved toxic to young.
- Median toxicity thresholds of newly hatched *G. fasciatus* were compared with [[Daphnia Magna]] for nine compounds. *G. fasciatus* was more susceptible to five salts, less susceptible to two (CuCl2, ZnCl2), and about equal for FeCl3. The author judged it less convenient than *D. magna* as an assay organism because of cannibalism and sexual reproduction.

**Sexual dimorphism and maturation**
- Females matured at 8.8 mm in late March/early April (about 6°C), 7.6 mm in late April (12°C), 6.9 mm in May (17°C), and 5.4 mm in August (26°C). Males matured between 9.0 and 10.2 mm.
- Immatures reached maturity after seven moults (occasionally eight). Intervals between moults shortened with temperature: at 25°C the average first-to-seventh interval ranged from 3.1 to 5.7 days; at 21°C from 4.7 to 11.7 days.

**Reproduction**
- Females laid unfertilised eggs after moulting in isolation, but these disappeared from the brood pouch within one to three days; sexual reproduction is obligatory, with parthenogenesis unknown.
- Pairing: the male carries the female, and pairing began about four days before ovulation at 21°C. Copulation followed moulting and ovulation, with up to 33 matings recorded for one pair.
- Egg production from 830 egg-bearing females (1948, Fish Hatchery Bay): average eggs per brood fell from 26 in April to 11 in September; the seasonal average was 17. Smallest class (5.5 mm) averaged 7.2 eggs; largest (15.0 mm) averaged 45.0. The maximum was 75 eggs in one brood.
- Incubation at constant temperatures: 7 days (24°C), 8 (22°C), 9 (20°C), 14 (18°C), 22 (15°C). The first spring incubation in habitat water was about 41 days.
- Up to 17 successive broods per year were theoretically possible; the author estimated five to eleven broods per female in nature.
- Hatch per brood approximated eggs per brood, validating egg counts as a proxy for recruitment.

**Sampling method**
- A roll of cotton minnow seine netting (one square yard, 1/4-inch mesh, about 10 inches long) placed on the bottom collected 62 to 296 gammarids per net per 24 hours, far more than dredges (generally five or fewer) or the Helgoland trawl (366 in one haul, but labour-intensive and unsuitable for most bottoms).

**Distribution and abundance**
- Gammarids were more abundant in vegetated areas than non-vegetated areas, with ratios of 7:1 and later 12:1 and 16:1 at Terwilliger's Pond.
- Among habitats sampled in September 1948, coarse rubble averaged 107 animals per net day, sand 20, shelving rock 23, gravel bar 2, and open-lake deep water 2 (muddy) to 7 (rocky) per net day.

**Population dynamics**
- Biweekly sampling (February 1948 to February 1949) showed a low winter level, gradual rise to a maximum in September to early October, then a sharp decline to a winter minimum in late December.
- Daily sampling over 107 consecutive days (September to December 1948) showed fluctuation magnitude proportional to population size; water movement and wind (especially easterly winds) were proposed as influences.
- Recruitment and depletion were tracked through monthly size-frequency distributions. July was a month of replacement, with old animals dying and young maturing.
- Comparisons with [[Blegvad 1922]] (G. locusta) and [[Watkins 1941]] (Corophium volutator) supported similar seasonal patterns of recruitment and mortality.

**Summary conclusions**
- *G. fasciatus* is a major, year-round food source for western Lake Erie fishes and is best sampled quantitatively with the net method.
- Its productivity is driven by seasonal recruitment from brood production, temperature-dependent maturation, and mortality of overwintering adults.
