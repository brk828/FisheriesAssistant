# Habitat Use by Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Little Colorado River and Other Colorado River Tributaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) final report, prepared for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase II program and submitted to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (October 1994), quantifies microhabitat use by the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and evaluates Grand Canyon tributaries as possible sites for a second spawning aggregation. The study ran from 1991 to 1994 and used a passive sampling design built on [[Microhabitat Sampling]] of depth, current and substrate (DCS). The LCR is treated as the habitat model for the smaller tributaries. Havasu Creek was identified as the tributary most similar to the LCR and was recommended for an experimental humpback chub population. The report also sets out management recommendations and future research priorities linked to the [[USFWS Biological Opinion]] reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs).

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] was described in 1946 from a single specimen collected in the Grand Canyon. Historically it was abundant in the mainstem Colorado and its large tributaries, but its range is now restricted, and the species is federally listed as endangered. Adults were associated with fast currents or deep channels in earlier studies. Work by USFWS (1982–1986) reported preferred adult habitat as water under 9.1 m deep, over silt, sand, gravel or bedrock, at velocities below 30 cm/s. The LCR is the largest known reproducing population in the species' range. Most successful lower Colorado spawning was believed to occur there.

Study objectives (USFWS 1990) were to:
1. Identify and quantify preferred habitats of juvenile and adult humpback chub and other fishes in the LCR.
2. Identify seasonal habitat use patterns.
3. Identify spawning habitat in the LCR.
4. Identify potential habitats in other tributaries.
5. Develop discharge-frequency and flow-duration curves (dropped; permission for hydrological work was not secured).
6. Identify research needed to enhance environmental conditions.

Native species recorded include [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]]. Exotic species include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Plains Killifish]] and rainbow and brown trout. Table 1 lists 21 species, with codes and status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Little Colorado River.** The LCR is a bicarbonate, travertine-depositing, saline and relatively warm stream. Dissolved CO₂ levels appear to limit the distribution of all but small speckled dace above km 15. Chloride levels restrict riparian vegetation to salt-tolerant species (*Tamarix*, *Typha*, *Phragmites*). Base flow in the lower 21 km is maintained by about 250 cfs from the Blue Springs complex at km 21.

**Hydrology.** The LCR was flooded through much of 1992. The winter of 1992–93 was the wettest in 20 years, with three floods above 6,000 cfs (Jan 3 >7,000 cfs; Jan 13 >17,000 cfs; Feb 23 >15,000 cfs). These scoured fine sediment, deepened pools, slowed mid-channel currents (typically <0.1 m/s in summer 1993) and produced unusually clear water (<2 NTU). Turbidity as low as 1.7 NTU was recorded.

**Mainstem.** Glen Canyon Dam releases cold water from Lake Powell. The report states that cold, stable mainstem conditions have limited humpback chub recruitment, with young-of-year (YOY) growth arrested in cold water (Lupher and Clarkson 1993).

**Tributaries.** Kanab Creek had summer temperatures above 33 °C and dissolved O₂ near or below 4 ppm, and the report notes many dead or diseased fish. Tapeats Creek is high-gradient and cold (13.8–17.4 °C). Bright Angel, Shinumo and Tapeats support exotic trout populations.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort (LCR, 1991–1994)**
- 28 LCR field trips totaling 232 days; 2 Grand Canyon tributary trips totaling 29 days.
- Sampling included 981 ASU hoopnet habitat measures, 3,328 FWS minihoopnets, 1,917 FWS minnow traps, 549 seine samples, 1,097 habitat transects and 71 fish observation transects.
- The ASU hoopnet data were judged unrepresentative of available habitat and excluded from habitat-use analysis.

**Fish captures**
- 28,837 fish were captured in the LCR; 17,704 (61.4%) in 1993.
- Humpback chub comprised 52.7% of catches; 46.8% were YOY or juveniles.
- Speckled dace made up 38.7%; native fishes were 98.5% of all captures.
- Exotic catches (channel catfish, fathead minnow, plains killifish) rose after summer spates.

**Year-class dynamics**
- 1991: a large humpback chub year class indicated successful spring reproduction.
- 1992: prolonged flooding gave very low catch rates and poor reproduction.
- 1993: successful reproduction; YOY grew from a modal 35 mm to 55 mm TL between June and July, and reached about 95 mm by September.
- 1994: the 1993 year class dominated; successful spawning followed late March–early April floods.

**Habitat use (LCR)**
- Adult humpback chub used deeper water in 1993 (>2 m) than in 1992, which the authors link to high clarity and to a possible negative phototaxis.
- At night, adults moved to shallower, open areas.
- Adult humpback chub in 1993 showed 81.8% of captures at night; YOY showed 60.3% during daytime.
- YOY humpback chub up to 50 mm restricted themselves to shallow edge habitat; larger YOY moved into deep, center-channel water in mid-summer.
- Spring habitat use of humpback chub resembled summer patterns, with no distinct spawning habitat detected. The authors suggest spawning may be opportunistic and rare.

**Microhabitat methods**
- Sampling used a point-centered approach based on depth, current and substrate (DCS), following Gorman and Karr (1978), plus cover, vertical structure and travertine variables.
- Passive minihoopnets and minnow traps were set in grids, with seines for edges (0–10 cm depth was the only gap).
- Day and night sampling were both essential for detecting diel habitat-use patterns.

**Tributaries**
- Havasu Creek was most similar to the LCR, with more deep water than other tributaries and comparable substrate, current and travertine distributions. Analysis suggests it offers habitat for all life stages of humpback chub.
- Discriminant analysis correctly classified 89% of Havasu transects and 100% of LCR transects, showing the LCR channel was distinct.
- Speckled dace and bluehead sucker dominated Bright Angel, Kanab, Shinumo and Havasu creeks; Tapeats Creek contained essentially only rainbow trout.

**Management recommendations**
1. Establish an experimental humpback chub population in Havasu Creek (addresses Conservation Measure 7 and RPA 5).
2. Protect key tributary confluences, including the lower 500 m of the LCR and lower 200 m of other tributaries, from February to June, and control tourist traffic at other times.
3. Manage exotic trout, including unlimited fishing in Bright Angel and Shinumo creeks, and study their effects.
4. Pilot cryopreservation of humpback chub germplasm for gene banking.
5. Establish a fish disease monitoring baseline; the report documents heavy *Lernaea* and Asian tapeworm infections.

**Future research priorities**
- Long-term humpback chub ecology (the species is long-lived, >20 years).
- Year-to-year habitat variation in tributaries.
- Gene banking and genetic management.
- Fish health and epizootics.
- Mainstem habitat surveys and assessment of stabilized flows and warmer temperatures.
- Other native fishes, especially flannelmouth sucker, whose population structure suggests an aging population without recruitment.

**Objective achievement**
- Objectives 1–4 and 6 were addressed to varying degrees; objective 5 was dropped.
- Products include a baseline native fish database for the LCR, a water quality database, GIS-linked transects, underwater photographs and video, and four Master's theses.
