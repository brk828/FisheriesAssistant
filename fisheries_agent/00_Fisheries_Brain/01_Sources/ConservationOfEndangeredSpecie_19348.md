# Conservation of Endangered Species of Fish and Wildlife (H.R. 9424, Senate Report No. 1463)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-and-wildlife-conservation

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Senate Committee on Commerce report, ordered printed August 17, 1966, accompanies H.R. 9424, a bill to conserve, protect, restore, and propagate native fish and wildlife (including migratory birds) threatened with extinction, and to consolidate the Secretary of the Interior's authority over the [[National Wildlife Refuge System]]. The House passed the bill on October 18, 1965. The committee reported it favorably with amendments. The report is primarily legislative: it explains the bill's purpose, the case for federal action, a section-by-section analysis, cost estimates, agency comments, and the text of the amended bill and affected existing statutes. It contains no original field data on fish populations.

## Background & Description

**Legislative history.** The bill and its Senate companion, S. 2217, were based on an executive communication from Interior Secretary Udall dated June 5, 1965. Committee hearings were held on August 12, 1965. The Departments of the Interior, Agriculture, and State supported the bill, and the Department of Justice deferred to Interior. The [[Comptroller General of the United States]] raised procedural concerns about contracting and land-exchange cash disposition.

**Stated need.** The report argues that growing population, urbanization, highways, agriculture, river harnessing, and forest cutting will strain habitat. It warns that, without vigorous action, 30 to 40 bird types and nearly as many mammal types could follow the [[Heath Hen]] and [[Passenger Pigeon]] into extinction. It also cites commercial pressure, including alligators killed for leather. Several Western Hemisphere nations had declared endangered species to the Organization of American States, and the United States had ratified the [[1940 Convention on Nature Protection and Wildlife Preservation in the Western Hemisphere]].

**Core provisions (as amended):**
- **Section 1:** Congressional findings and purposes. Federal departments of Interior, Agriculture, and Defense must protect threatened species and, where practicable, preserve their habitats on their lands. The Secretary of the Interior designates threatened species after consulting States and scientific experts (including ichthyologists) and publishes the list in the [[Federal Register]].
- **Section 2:** Conservation program using existing authorities ([[Migratory Bird Conservation Act]], [[Fish and Wildlife Act of 1956]], [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]]), plus new land acquisition authority for threatened fish. Acquisition funds may come from the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]].
- **Section 3:** Cooperation with States, including consultation before land acquisition and cooperative management agreements.
- **Section 4:** Consolidation of refuge areas into the National Wildlife Refuge System, with new authorities for accommodations, donations, exchanges, permits, and enforcement.
- **Sections 5–10:** Definitions, repeal of obsolete provisions (e.g., [[Migratory Bird Hunting Stamp Act]] acreage limit), amendments to the Migratory Bird Conservation Act, and special provisions for the [[Key Deer National Wildlife Refuge]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat protection focus.** Protection depends on acquiring and managing habitat rather than on species-level measures alone. The bill covers all forms of threatened fish and wildlife, not only game species. The Migratory Bird Conservation Act amendment removes the word "game" so that threatened nongame migratory birds can be included.

**Key deer case study.** The report uses the [[Key Deer]] of the [[Florida Keys]] as a concrete example:
- Acreage owned: 762.6 acres in six townships of [[Monroe County]] (523.4 donated; 239.2 purchased with appropriated and donated funds).
- Habitat leased: about 6,664 acres historically, with about 5,337 acres under lease at the time of writing. Leases are year-to-year and terminable on 90 days' notice.
- Estimated need: at least 1,800 acres in fee, at about $1.8 million. The report states that even this minimum would assure perpetuation of the herd but would not maintain or increase its size.
- Constraints: a 1,000-acre acquisition cap and a $35,000 appropriated-funds limit, plus a ban on condemnation on islands crossed by [[U.S. Highway 1]]. The report proposes replacing the cap and limiting the condemnation ban to a 1,000-foot zone along the highway.

**Multiple-use and compatibility rules.** Public uses (hunting, fishing, recreation, roads, pipelines, powerlines) are allowed only if compatible with each area's primary purpose. The report stresses that "the Secretary should be most restrictive when considering requests for roads" through refuges. Up to 40 percent of an inviolate migratory bird sanctuary may be opened to migratory game bird hunting, and the amendment extends this limit to resident game birds.

**State jurisdiction.** The bill does not change State authority over resident fish and wildlife on non-federal lands. Within refuges, federal regulations must be "to the extent practicable, consistent with State fish and wildlife laws." This language preserves the status quo in a long-running dispute between States and Interior over management authority. The Department of the Interior's 1964 Solicitor's opinion and the States' position both remain in play.

## Key Findings

**Funding and cost**
- Interior estimated the program at about $4 million per year for the first five years, probably continuing at a similar level.
- The committee capped land acquisition for endangered species at $5 million per year and $15 million in total.
- The per-area limit is $200,000 unless Congress authorizes more.
- Estimated additional expenditures for the first five years: $3,244,000 (year 1), $3,950,000 (year 2), $4,252,000 (year 3), $4,345,000 (year 4), and $4,520,000 (year 5).
- Estimated additional civilian man-years: 25, 75, 102, 122, and 140 across the same five years. Substantive positions include biologists, engineers, negotiators and appraisers, and maintenance staff.
- Key Deer acquisition costs are estimated at about $1.8 million. A prior acquisition of 762.6 acres cost $54,851 in total, of which $34,500 was appropriated.

**Agency positions**
- Interior recommended enactment with amendments. Its main concerns were the land-exchange language, extending the sanctuary hunting limit to resident game birds, and consultation with other federal agencies.
- Agriculture had no objection. It administers habitat for the California Condor and Kirtland's Warbler on national forest lands.
- State Department had no objection, citing support for the 1940 Western Hemisphere Convention and an Inter-American conference planned for Mar del Plata, Argentina, in October 1965.
- The Comptroller General made no comment on the bill's merits. It raised issues about negotiated accommodation contracts bypassing formal advertising (citing [[41 U.S.C. 5]]) and about the disposition of cash received in land exchanges.

**House amendments accepted by the Senate committee**
- Deleted provisions on mining surface control, pending a [[Public Land Law Review Commission]] study.
- Added a disclaimer that the bill does not authorize federal control of hunting or fishing outside the System.
- Deleted authority to permit incompatible uses deemed in the public interest. The House report said such uses should be permitted only when "extreme caution" confirms they are compatible and secondary.
- Added a provision preserving the 1962 recreation policy.

**Enforcement**
- Violations carry fines of up to $500, up to six months in prison, or both.
- Authorized officers may make warrantless arrests for violations in their presence and seize illegally taken wildlife.
- Existing refuge regulations (50 C.F.R. Parts 32 and 33) remain in effect until modified. These cover hunting and sport fishing permissions, State license requirements, and Federal Migratory Bird Stamp requirements for hunters 16 and older.

**Bottom line:** The report establishes a federal framework for endangered fish and wildlife conservation. Its key constraints are the $15 million land acquisition ceiling, per-area spending limits, compatibility standards for refuge uses, and preservation of State authority over resident wildlife outside and inside refuges.
