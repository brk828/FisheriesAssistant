## Executive Summary

This 1936 geological paper by M. M. Knechtel describes the stratigraphy, age, and depositional history of the **[[Gila Conglomerate]]**, a Pliocene-age lacustrine and fanglomerate formation in the Gila and San Simon valleys of southeastern Arizona. Although the paper is primarily geological/paleontological rather than ichthyological, it documents a fossil freshwater aquatic ecosystem preserved in ancient lake beds, including **diatom floras**, **freshwater gastropods**, and associated terrestrial vertebrate fauna (horses, camels, mastodons) that establish the depositional environment as a warm, mildly saline, upper Pliocene lake system. No fish remains are reported, but the diatom and molluscan evidence provides a detailed paleolimnological reconstruction of the aquatic habitat that once occupied this structural trough. The data are relevant to understanding ancient continental aquatic ecosystems and their sedimentary/paleoclimatic context, which is foundational for reconstructing historical freshwater biogeography in the American Southwest.

## Background & Description

No fish or fish-related morphological data are present in this source. The aquatic biological remains described are limited to:

- **Freshwater Gastropods** (Red Knolls locality, from a limestone bed ~40 ft above vertebrate fossil horizon):
  - *Lymnaea?* sp. — identified only from a fragmentary spire; genus assignment tentative.
  - *Planorbis* sp. — species indeterminate due to poor preservation (specimens badly crushed).
- **Diatom Flora** (from two diatomite localities, 111 Ranch and opposite Fort Thomas):
  - A diverse assemblage of at least 17 identified diatom taxa, including *Melosira italica*, *Podosira* sp., *Stauroneis* cf. *S. phoenicenteron*, *Anomoeoneis sphaerophora*, *Navicula* spp., *Pinnularia major*, *Pinnularia microstauron*, *Gomphonema longiceps* var. *subclavata*, *Denticula elegans*, *Epithemia zebra* var. *porcellus*, *Rhopalodia gibberula*, *Nitzschia* sp., *Campylodiscus clypeus*, *Cyclotella meneghiniana*, *Mastogloia* cf. *M. smithii*, *Gomphonemia* cf. *G. lanceolatum*, and *Surirella striatula*.
  - Abundance classifications ranged from Abundant (A) to Rare (R), with diatoms constituting only ~30% of total sediment at one locality (mostly volcanic ash/clastic material) and forming an impure diatomite (~15% silt impurity) at the other.
- All diatom species identified are extant taxa, indicating no significant morphological divergence from modern forms; this limits their use as strict age markers but supports paleoenvironmental inference.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic fossils derive from a **Pliocene lacustrine basin** — the ancestral lake system that once occupied the [[Gila Valley]] and [[San Simon Valley]] trough in southeastern Arizona. Key ecological inferences:

- **Water chemistry**: The diatom assemblages at both diatomite localities suggest deposition in **warm, somewhat saline lake waters** — indicative of a closed or semi-closed basin with elevated evaporation, consistent with a mild, humid-to-warm climate during the depositional interval.
- **Habitat structure**: The lake bed facies (gray/red clays, gypsiferous in places, with tongues of sandstone, tuff, limestone, and diatomite) records a low-energy, quiet-water depositional setting suitable for diatom and mollusk accumulation, grading laterally into coarse marginal fanglomerates deposited contemporaneously along the basin margins (interfingering facies relationship, as illustrated in the paper's cross-section).
- **Co-occurring fauna**: The lacustrine molluscan fauna (Lymnaeidae, Planorbidae) is characteristic of shallow, vegetated freshwater margins — both families are extant pulmonate snail groups still common in modern ponds and lakes, implying a similar shallow-water littoral niche in the Pliocene lake.
- **Associated terrestrial vertebrate fauna**: Camel, peccary, sloth, horse (*Hipparion* [*Nannippus*], *Plesippus*), and mastodon-like proboscidean (*Rhynchotherium?*) remains found in or near the lake beds indicate the lake was fringed by terrestrial grassland/savanna habitat supporting large mammal communities that likely used the lake margin as a water source.
- **Volcanic influence**: Stratified tuff layers within the lake beds point to nearby contemporaneous volcanic activity, which likely episodically altered water chemistry and contributed ash to the diatomite deposits.
- **Basin evolution**: The lake was impounded following a phase of late Miocene–early Pliocene tectonic deformation, then drained near the end of the Pliocene or in the early Pleistocene via headward erosion at the [[Coolidge Dam]] outlet, transitioning the aquatic habitat to a fluvial/erosional (pediment-forming) regime under increasingly arid Pleistocene conditions.

## Key Findings

- The **[[Gila Conglomerate]]** comprises two contemporaneous, interfingering facies — central lacustrine (fine clay, diatomite, limestone) and marginal fanglomerate (coarse, angular detritus) — deposited simultaneously in a single structural trough, contradicting earlier interpretations (Schwennesen) of sequential deposition.
- Diatom floras from two localities (111 Ranch and opposite Fort Thomas) indicate a **warm, somewhat saline lacustrine environment**; since all identified diatom species are still living today, they provide **no independent age constraint** beyond confirming a Pliocene-or-younger depositional window.
- **Freshwater molluscan fossils** (*Lymnaea?*, *Planorbis*) from the Red Knolls locality confirm fresh-water depositional conditions but are too poorly preserved for precise age determination; qualitatively, they "do not look very old" per W. C. Mansfield's assessment.
- Vertebrate fossils (horses, camels, mastodon-like proboscidean) firmly establish an **upper Pliocene age** for at least part of the Gila Conglomerate, correlating it with similar-aged deposits in the [[San Pedro Valley]] (Arizona) previously described by Kirk Bryan and J. W. Gidley.
- No fish fossils were recovered or reported from any locality in this study — the aquatic paleoecological record here is defined solely by diatoms and freshwater gastropods.
- The lake system was climatically **mild and humid** during deposition, contrasting with the subsequent **arid, erosional Pleistocene** regime that produced extensive mountain-front pediments after the lake drained.
- This record serves as an important reference point for **paleolimnological and paleoclimatic reconstruction** in the North American Basin and Range province, and indirectly informs the broader biogeographic history of freshwater aquatic taxa (though not fish specifically) in the arid Southwest.
