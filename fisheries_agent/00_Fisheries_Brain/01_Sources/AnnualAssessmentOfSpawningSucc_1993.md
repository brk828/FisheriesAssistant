# Annual Assessment of Spawning Success, Larval Distribution, and Habitat Selection of Mainstem Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This FY93 annual report from the Colorado River Recovery Program documents field investigations into the spawning ecology and early life history of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the mainstem [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]]. The study, part of the Flaming Gorge Flow Five Year Research Plan, aimed to (1) confirm egg deposition on suspected spawning bars and assess the influence of temperature/discharge on spawning and larval emergence, and (2) characterize larval habitat use within 20 miles downstream of spawning sites. Key methods included electrofishing, trammel netting, larval drift nets, light traps, and fine-mesh seining. Results confirmed razorback sucker congregation and spawning readiness at a known spawning bar (river mile 311), and larvae tentatively identified as razorback sucker were collected in multiple downstream backwater and bottomland habitats, though high spring discharge significantly constrained sampling effort and effectiveness.

## Background & Description

- Adult razorback suckers captured during spawning aggregation showed secondary sexual characteristics indicative of reproductive readiness: males exhibited **expressed sperm**, while the single female captured was described as **"hard" and near spawning condition**.
- Co-occurring [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] individuals showed **tubercles and expressed sperm** in one specimen, consistent with concurrent spawning activity in the same habitat.
- No tubercles or expressed sex products were observed in captured [[Bluehead Sucker]] specimens, suggesting a different reproductive timing.
- Larval razorback suckers were reared in aquaria to approximately **17-20 mm total length (TL)** to reach a size sufficient for positive taxonomic identification at the Larval Fish Laboratory (CSU).
- Light-trap-caught larvae exhibited notably higher survivorship in captivity than seine-caught larvae, suggesting seining induces greater physiological stress or physical damage during capture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat**: Suspected spawning bar at river mile 311 ("razorback bar") on the Green River served as a documented adult congregation and spawning site; a comparable site exists on the [[Yampa River]].
- **Spawning conditions**: Ripe adults were first documented on 29 April 1992 at a water temperature of **10°C**, indicating a lower thermal threshold for spawning onset.
- **Larval drift and dispersal**: Larval razorback suckers drift downstream from spawning bars; sampling was conducted via passive drift nets set at dusk approximately 0.8 mi downstream of the Green River spawning bar due to high flows preventing on-site netting.
- **Nursery habitat**: Larvae utilized a range of off-channel and low-velocity habitats within 20 miles of spawning sites, including:
  - In-channel backwaters (e.g., between rmi 311 and rmi 298, near Jensen bridge)
  - Flooded bottomlands (Wyasket, Indian, Leota bottomlands)
  - Tributary mouths (e.g., [[Cliff Creek]], rmi 302)
  - Side channels/wetlands (Old Charlie Wash, Stewart Lake, Island Park)
  - Canyonlands reach of the Green River
- **Interspecific habitat overlap**: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] co-occurred with razorback suckers at the spawning bar, suggesting some overlap in spawning habitat requirements among catostomid species.
- **Environmental hazards**: Flooded bottomland habitats sampled in early July showed hypoxic conditions (zooplankton-clogged light trap trays leading to anoxia and warm water), resulting in larval mortality — indicating these habitats may become ecological traps under certain hydrologic/seasonal conditions.
- **Hydrologic influence**: High Green River discharge in spring 1993 delayed and constrained both drift-net and seining efforts, restricting drift-net deployment to shoreline sets and delaying seinable backwater habitat availability until 11 June.

## Key Findings

1. **Adult spawning congregation confirmed**: On 29 April 1992, electrofishing at the Green River spawning bar (rmi 311) captured 10 male and 1 female razorback sucker in spawning condition, alongside 5 flannelmouth and 3 bluehead suckers, confirming multi-species use of the site.

2. **Larval production documented at both spawning bars**:
   - **Green River**: 16 days of drift-net sampling (13 May–24 June 1993) using three nets fished 1-hour post-dusk; no larvae captured until **26 May 1993**, after which catches became more consistent. High water forced relocation of the sampling site 0.8 mi downstream.
   - **Yampa River**: 36 drift-net samples (20 May–9 June 1993) yielded **2 suspected razorback sucker larvae** (26–30 May window).

3. **Successful larval rearing for identification**: Six representative larval samples were successfully reared in aquaria, yielding approximately **200 larval suckers** grown to ~20 mm TL for confirmatory identification.

4. **Extensive habitat sampling effort**: Light trap deployment totaled 178 sets across seven distinct habitat types (Green River mainstem backwaters: 120 sets; Stewart Lake: 9; Island Park: 10; Old Charlie Wash: 14; Wyasket bottomland: 15; Indian bottomland: 7; Leota bottomland: 3), supplemented by 47 seine hauls.

5. **Notable larval concentrations**:
   - Old Charlie Wash: **>100 fish** tentatively identified as razorback sucker larvae (mid-June).
   - Canyonlands reach of the Green River: **~140 razorback-like larvae** collected 17–19 June 1993.
   - Cliff Creek mouth (rmi 302): first light-trap captures of suspected razorback larvae on 18 May.
   - Yampa River: light trapping (22 sets) and seining (23 hauls) between 26 May–9 June 1993 yielded **3 suspected razorback sucker larvae**.

6. **Methodological comparison**: Light traps outperformed seines both in larval capture effectiveness and in post-capture survival for aquarium rearing. Equipment failure rate was low (10 of 178 light trap sets, ~5.6%), primarily due to battery/switch operator error rather than mechanical failure.

7. **No major shortcomings reported**; the study is recommended to continue comparing light traps, drift nets, and seines to determine an optimal long-term monitoring protocol, potentially consolidating to a single method or defined combination.

8. **Budgetary note**: FY92 funds were fully expended as allocated ($20,000 Vernal CRFP; $5,000 Larval Fish Lab), with no discrepancy reported.
