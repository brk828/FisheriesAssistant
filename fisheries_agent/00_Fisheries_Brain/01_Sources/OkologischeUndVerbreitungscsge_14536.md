# Troglochaetus beranecki: Ecology, Distribution and Origin of a Stygobiont Archiannelid

#marine-biology #stygobiology #nerillidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[Siegfried Husmann]] at the [[Max Planck Society]] hydrobiological station in Schlitz (Hesse) examines the groundwater archiannelid [[Troglochaetus beranecki]]. The author compiles new records from the [[Danube River]], [[Rhine River]], [[Weser River]] and associated tributaries and analyses them alongside earlier Harz and Main data. The worm is found to be strongly bound to groundwater in sandy-gravelly deposits, with its main ecological centre in interstitial groundwater away from river banks. The author proposes that the species is a freshwater descendant of a marine mesopsammal lineage that colonised continental groundwater during Tertiary marine incursions.

## Background & Description

*Troglochaetus beranecki* [[Delachaux]] 1921 is an archiannelid of the family [[Nerillidae]]. It is a [[Stygobiont]] of marine origin, inhabiting water-filled subterranean cavities of very different sizes. The author lists the habitats in which it had been recorded:

- Seepage water in large mountain caves (for example, the [[Barbarossa Cave]] and the [[Reyersdorf Cave]]).
- Groundwater in scree slopes (Ankel 1943).
- Water-filled microcaverns in gravels, gravel-sands and sands beneath floodplains and terraces.
- The interstitial pore systems of the [[Hyporheic Zone]] of streams.

The species was first described from the Grotte de Verte in the canton of Neuchâtel, Switzerland. By 1962 its distribution spanned the Danube basin and Central European river systems, and it had also been found in Czechoslovakia ([[Kulhavý]] 1959), Hungary ([[Andrassy]] 1955) and Romania ([[Plesa]] 1957).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Substrate specificity.** Earlier work and the new data both show that the species is closely associated with groundwater in sandy-gravelly sediments. Its ecological centre lies in the [[Interstitial Groundwater]] of floodplains, away from river banks ("uferfernes Grundwasser"). The hyporheic occurrences are sporadic and mainly restricted to gravels that contain coarse sand.

**Microcavern habitat.** The author argues that coarse-sand-filled microcaverns offer protection from predatory insect larvae, other enemies and food competitors that are less restricted in the more spacious cavities of coarse gravel. Sediment sorting in river deposits produces adjacent zones of open gravel and sand-filled pores, and the worm's preference for sand-filled pores is seen as a key ecological factor.

**Associated fauna.** The author groups the co-occurring interstitial fauna by size and life-form ([[Mesopsammal]] concept after [[Remane]]):

- Typical mesopsammal species living in coarse-sand pores: [[Parastenocaris]] spp., [[Chappuisius inopinus]], [[Chappuisius singeri]], [[Graeteriella unisetigera]] and [[Bathynella natans]].
- Fine-gravel groundwater forms: [[Niphargellus nolli]], [[Bogidiella albertimagni]], [[Microniphargus leruthi]] and [[Crangonyx subterraneus]].
- Open-cavity gravel forms: [[Niphargus]] species such as *N. inopinatus*, *N. fontanus* and *N. aquilex*.

Other taxa in the samples include nematodes, oligochaetes, copepods, water mites and insect larvae (Plecoptera, Coleoptera, Diptera, including Chironomidae).

**Sampling bias.** The author stresses that the standard shovel-and-sieve method ([[Chappuis Method]], 1942) mixes substrate and blurs distinct cavern systems. Well water and [[Norton Tubes]] (Nortonröhren) draw in water from mixed pore systems, so the resulting faunal lists reflect composite samples. Filtrates from 150 to 200 litres of pumped water were used for well and Norton-tube samples.

## Key Findings

**1. Harz-Weser-Main data.** Table 1 and Table 2 summarise 17 sites in the Weser, Leine, Innerste and Oker systems that yielded at least 4 specimens each, totalling 287 individuals. One well in the Main valley (Noll and Stammer 1953), with 39 specimens, was added. Most records came from interstitial groundwater away from the banks of the rivers Weser, Leine and Main. One hyporheic site in the Innerste valley (Harz) was exceptional.

**2. Exceptional Innerste population.** At one Innerste bank excavation, three circular gravel layers of 25 cm diameter were sampled at depths of 0–10, 10–25 and 25–40 cm below the water table. The upper layer alone contained 29 specimens. The author considers this probably the highest population density recorded for the species. Across more than 80 bank excavations in gravels of mountain streams, the species was found at no more than 4 sites. The author interprets the Innerste site as a rare exception, probably caused by a sandy-gravel microcavern structure and the former presence of a soil cover.

**3. Distribution records.** Section IV lists new sites by river system:

- *Danube system*: a well in the Danube floodplain at Melk (24 specimens, 20 September 1958); gravel of the [[Ybbs River]] near Weißenburg (30 August 1958); the [[Ötz River]] at Brunnau (11 August 1959, abundant with associated insect larvae); a Norton tube in the Isar floodplain at Mittenwald (22 March 1957).
- *Rhine system*: a well in the [[Lahn River]] floodplain at Biskirchen (30 specimens, 18 September 1960); a Norton tube near the Lahn (18 September 1960); the [[Niederrhein]] floodplain at Krefeld (4 specimens, 17 September 1959; 37 specimens, 1 November 1960); two Norton tubes in the [[Ruhr River]] floodplain at Wandhofen (April 1955) within a groundwater recharge plant.
- *Weser system*: a Norton tube at Großenwieden (25 October 1961); a Norton tube at Liebenau (6 specimens, 20 July 1955).

**4. Ecological conclusion.** The new records confirm that the ecological centre of the species is interstitial groundwater of river floodplains, away from banks. Hyporheic occurrences are secondary and patchy.

**5. Secondary habitats.** The author suggests that the species can persist for long periods in caves that are no longer fed by sandy-gravel groundwater, provided conditions remain stable and the habitat is isolated from predators and competitors. This is offered as an explanation for populations such as the Barbarossa Cave population.

**6. Origin hypotheses.** Three earlier proposals are reviewed:

- [[Chappuis]] (1927) suggested a relict from a mid-Neogene sea covering the Swiss Plateau.
- [[Remane]] (1928) proposed immigration from the Mediterranean into western Swiss freshwater via the Rhône valley.
- [[Thienemann]] (1950) objected that the Remane route could not explain populations in Silesia.

Husmann notes that the new distribution follows the Tertiary epicontinental sea belt that extended from the Alpine foreland through the Pannonian, Euxinian and Paracaspian basins. Remnant lakes such as Balaton, Pontus, Caspian and Aral were connected via the [[Rhône Corridor]], Bosporus and Mesopotamia in the mid-Miocene. Desalination began in the Sarmatian and continued into the Pliocene.

**7. Weser-Main bridge.** The author argues that the Upper Lahn area functioned as a Pliocene Weser-Main contact zone and a faunal bridge between the Weser system and south-western river systems. Brackish-adapted marine animals could have spread northward across this bridge.

**8. Northeastern extension.** A branch of the mid-Miocene Alpine foredeep reached the source areas of the Oder and Vistula, linking the Silesian caves (Stammer 1937) to the same marine network.

**9. Nearest relatives.** Related nerillids such as [[Thalassochaetus palpifoliaceus]] (Ax 1954) live in marine sand interstices along modern coasts. The author suggests that several nerillid species once lived in Tertiary European seas and that most became extinct regionally as those seas dried up. *Troglochaetus* survived by making a permanent transition to freshwater, perhaps thanks to a broad physiological tolerance (possibly euryhaline).

**10. Glacial dispersal.** The distribution in both northern German and Alpine sites suggests that the species spread into formerly glaciated areas. Retreating glaciers deposited the sandy-gravel sediments the species prefers over wide areas.

**11. Romanian addendum.** Plesa (1957) reported the species from the Pestera cave in the Bihar Mountains, Romania, in interstitial groundwater of a cave-stream sandbank within the Danube basin. This record was added during proof correction and supports the author's conclusions.
