# Studies on a Kentucky Knobs Lake I: Some Environmental Factors

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1954 limnological paper by Gerald A. Cole (University of Louisville) establishes a physical and chemical baseline for [[Tom Wallace Lake]], a small artificial impoundment about ten miles south of Louisville in [[Jefferson County]], in the [[Kentucky Knobs]] physiographic province. The study covers morphometry, benthic zonation, sediments, thermal structure, transparency, dissolved oxygen, pH, free carbon dioxide and alkalinity, based on observations from September 1950 to 1954. The lake is a holomictic, eutrophic, semi-drainage impoundment with an anoxic lower hypolimnion for much of the year. The author concludes that the lake is nonetheless productive, that morphometry should weigh more heavily than surface area in planning fishing lakes, and that the lake's suitability as a [[Bass]] habitat is questionable. Fish are discussed only in relation to stocking and habitat; no fish survey data are presented.

## Background & Description

**Location and climate.** The lake lies at 38° 5' N, 85° 46' W, at 600 ft elevation, about 1.5 miles south-southwest of Fairdale. Climate data come from the U.S. Weather Bureau station at Standiford Field, seven miles north. Key values:
- Mean winter temperature: 36.3°F; 70 winter days at or below 32°F
- Mean spring and fall temperatures: 56.1°F and 58.7°F; mean summer temperature: 76.8°F
- Mean total rainfall: 42.59 in., mostly in winter; average snowfall 13.5 in.
- Average annual possible solar radiation: 58%, peaking in July; prevailing winds from the south

**Drainage basin and geology.** The basin consists of wooded hills rising 150 to 250 ft above the lake, underlain by siliceous, low-calcium Mississippian rocks (the [[Brodhead Formation]], [[Floyds Knob Formation]] and [[Muldraugh Formation]]) that weather to infertile soil. Oak species dominate, with [[Virginia Pine]] (*Pinus virginiana*) on south-facing slopes. The drainage area is 36.26 ha (89 acres), about 15 times the lake area. An intermittent stream enters from the west; the only outlet is a concrete spillway at the northeast corner. Inlet and outlet are both dry in summer and fall. Following [[Pennak]] (1945), the lake is classed as a semi-drainage lake.

**Origin and morphometry.** The earthen and stone dam was built in 1946 and the basin filled in the winter of 1946-47. The lake was stocked with bass and bluegills in early summer 1947 (Distler, 1952). Morphometric values (Table I, calculated after Welch, 1948):

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Maximum length | 328 m |
| Maximum width | 168 m |
| Mean width | 71 m |
| Surface area | 2.34 ha |
| Maximum depth | 9 m |
| Mean depth | 4.1 m |

The basin is steep-sided, with about 25% slope from shore to the 5 m contour and a mean basin slope of 18.9%. The shore development index is 1.65. The author notes that the precipitous margins mean there is less shallow water than this index suggests. The basin is compared with [[Sodon Lake]], Michigan, which has the same surface area but is nearly twice as deep and is [[Meromixis|meromictic]]; Tom Wallace Lake is [[Holomixis|holomictic]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Benthic zonation and sediments.** Zones were defined by summer temperature profiles and the distribution of rooted plants. The littoral zone extends to 2 m, where scattered *Potamogeton diversifolius* clumps and seasonal mats of *Spirogyra* occur. It makes up 26.1% of the bottom area (the "littoral index"). The sublittoral (2-5 m) covers 31.5% and the [[Profundal Zone]] (below 5 m, to 9 m) covers 42.4%.
- Littoral sediments are soft, clayey and light brown, with midge larvae and oligochaete tubes abundant. Dead *Spirogyra* mats leave a black sapropelic layer.
- Lower sublittoral and profundal sediments are fine-grained, black, sapropelic, clay-rich and form hard bricks when dried.
- Profundal deposits are [[Varved Sediments]]: alternating clay (winter) and organic (summer) bands. Summer bands are about 0.5 cm thick and winter clay bands about 0.25 cm. By October 1952, 3.75 cm of sediment had accumulated over the original basin. Loss on ignition of a profundal sample was 10.8%.

**Thermal regime.** Approximately 50 temperature assays were made, with a Taylor maximum-minimum thermometer and later a Whitney electrical thermometer. The lake is usually isothermal near 4° C from December into February. Key observations:
- Lake isothermal at 4.2° C on Feb. 1, 1954, and 4.4° C on Feb. 1, 1952.
- Under ice on Dec. 25, 1953 (ice present about a month), the lake was [[Inverse Stratification|inversely stratified]], with 4° C water from 5 m to the bottom.
- A [[Thermocline]] is established by mid-March by [[Birge's Rule]] (drop of 1° C or more per meter).
- Surface thermocline persists late into spring, reaching 5 m on May 23, 1952, and 1-6 m on June 9, 1953.
- Stratification persists to early November; on Oct. 25, 1952 the thermocline reached the bottom, with its upper limit at 7 m.
- Bottom waters peak at 10° C in early November after the overturn. The lake is close to 5° C by the end of November.
- Steep thermal gradients occurred within the thermocline, including a 10° C drop between 2 and 3 m on June 26, 1954.
- Estimated annual heat budget: 5,500 to 6,000 gram calories per cm² of surface.

**Transparency.** A 20 cm white [[Secchi Disk]] was used. The maximum was 3.6 m (Oct. 16, 1952) during the early fall overturn. The minimum was 97 cm on Feb. 1 (1952 and 1953), associated with a large zooplankton population. Seasonal averages: summer 1.7 m; October overturn 2.2 m; winter circulation 1.6 m; early spring 1.25 m.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Oxygen was measured first by the unmodified [[Winkler Method]] and from early 1953 by the [[Alsterberg Modification]]. The results were similar.
- The 8 m water becomes anoxic from mid-May to the end of October.
- Maximum winter value at 8 m: 11.2 ppm on Feb. 1, 1954 (about 85% saturation).
- Surface supersaturation was common May-September. Highest concentration: 12.5 ppm in June 1953 (about 125%). Highest percentage: about 130% at the surface on Sept. 2, 1951.
- Anoxic water in the thermocline was recorded at 5 and 6 m in 1951 and 6 m in 1952, with traces as high as 4 m in 1951. Oxygen maxima usually occurred at 3 m in the thermocline; no thermoclinal minima were observed.
- Lowest surface values occurred during the October-November overturn.

**pH.** Surface pH ranged from 8.6 on bright summer days to 6.8 on overcast winter days. At 8 m, pH was normally 6.8 and fell to 6.4 by the end of stagnation. It rose to 7.2 on Dec. 6, 1952. Water from 5 m to the bottom was usually slightly acid, with neutral or alkaline conditions limited to the November-December circulation period.

**Free carbon dioxide.** Values were calculated from pH and alkalinity (APHA, 1946).
- Summer epilimnion (upper 3 m) usually had less than 1 ppm; free CO₂ was always present at 4 m and below.
- Surface values peaked at 16.3-17.2 ppm in November 1951, uniform from top to bottom on Nov. 11 at 17.2 ppm.
- At 8 m, values rose from about 10.5-13.8 ppm in April to 21-22 ppm in May and 29.6 ppm on July 25, 1952. Early September values were 70.6-74.9 ppm, peaking at 112.4 ppm on Sept. 18, 1953 and 109.9 ppm on Sept. 29, 1951.
- After overturn, values fell to 63.9 ppm (Oct. 15, 1951), 35.6 ppm (Oct. 25, 1952) and 17.2 ppm (Nov. 11, 1951). The annual low (under 4 ppm at 8 m) occurred on Feb. 1, 1954.

**Alkalinity.** Total alkalinity (brom-cresol green) was almost entirely bicarbonate. Carbonates appeared only in epilimnetic summer waters, never below 4 m and never October to April. The highest carbonate value was 6.5 ppm (Sept. 2, 1951), when total alkalinity was 24 ppm and a large population of the green flagellate *Hudorina* was present.
- Surface alkalinity peaks near 50 ppm in November (maximum 56 ppm). It declines over winter through flushing over the spillway: a loss of 27 ppm in 1951-52 and 13 ppm in 1952-53. In 1953-54, with no overflow, April surface alkalinity was 47 ppm.
- Total precipitation for January-March: 16.94 in. (1951), 14.0 in. (1952), 9.26 in. (1953), 7.48 in. (1954). The author links the winter alkalinity loss directly to precipitation.
- At 8 m, alkalinity peaked at 142 ppm (1951), 163 ppm (1952) and 146 ppm (Sept. 18, 1953, when observations stopped because of forest fires).
- The nearby [[South Park Pond]] had alkalinity of 9 to 25 ppm. Using [[Moyle]] (1946) categories, the pond is very soft to soft, while Tom Wallace Lake is changing from soft to medium hard.

**Iron hypothesis.** The author proposes that the hypolimnion acts as a trap for allochthonous iron. Anaerobic samples darkened and formed rusty flocculent masses after exposure to air, consistent with ferrous bicarbonate oxidizing to ferric compounds. This could explain the apparent rise in deep-water alkalinity, though the author notes other explanations, including differences in winter flushing, longer stagnation and missed sampling dates.

## Key Findings

- **Productivity is higher than the basin's form suggests.** Although the small, steep, sheltered basin suggests low potential, the summer oxygen curve is eutrophic and preliminary studies show high benthic productivity. The author suggests that any small Kentucky impoundment with an adequate drainage basin may be productive because of the favorable climate.
- **Stocking and fertilization advice.** The author recommends that morphometry and topography be emphasized in planning future fishing lakes, that lakes not be stocked routinely on surface area alone, and that fertilizer be applied with caution.
- **Anoxia limits fish habitat.** During summer stagnation, more than 40% of the bottom area may be unavailable to fish because of anaerobic water. The author attributes this ultimately to the excavation of a small, steep-sided basin in a sheltered site.
- **Bass habitat is questionable.** The lake's fitness as an ideal bass habitat is doubtful despite its eutrophy. The paper does not present any fish population data.
- **Strong thermal insulation, but not total.** Sheltering by surrounding terrain produces a high, persistent thermocline, cold hypolimnia and extreme thermal gradients. Wind is not blocked enough to prevent circulation throughout the year, so the lake is holomictic.
- **Sediment record.** Varved profundal sediments provide an annual record since the lake formed in 1946-47, though the 1947 summer band is missing from many cores.
- **Alkalinity trend.** Deep-water alkalinity appears to have increased each year (142, 163 and 146 ppm at 8 m), and the lake appears to be shifting from soft to medium hard water. The author cautions that this trend could be an artifact of sampling dates or flushing differences.
- **Preliminary status.** The paper establishes baseline data only. The author plans further reports on flora, fauna and additional physico-chemical phenomena.
