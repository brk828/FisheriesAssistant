# Vertical Raceways (Silos) Versus Horizontal Raceways for Rainbow Trout Production

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish bulletin (No. 17, 1978) by Thomas M. Moody and Richard N. McCleskey compares rainbow trout rearing in [[Vertical Raceways]] (silos) with conventional [[Concrete Raceways]] at the [[Seven Springs Hatchery]]. Silos grew trout faster and were more cost-efficient, largely because of labor savings, even though they required more feed per pound gained. Water reuse through five silos, with a [[Clarifier]] between Silo 4 and Silo 5, kept water quality higher than in raceways reusing the same water. The authors conclude that silos are a valuable supplement to trout culture where water or land is limited, best suited to trout longer than five inches.

## Background & Description

Vertical raceways, or silos, are cylindrical tanks with a depth-to-diameter ratio of 1.3 to 1.5. Water enters through a drop tube, rebounds off the floor and rises, and spills over an overflow screen at 9 to 10 gallons per minute per 100 gallons of capacity. Vertical velocity is about one foot per minute, and the full water volume is exchanged in 10 to 11 minutes (Allgood et al., 1973). Earlier work by Buss et al. (1970) described self-cleaning silos with low thermal exchange and high fish production per cubic foot.

The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish tested an experimental silo at its Red River Hatchery in 1969 and 1970, reporting densities of 5.0 to 6.0 pounds of fish per cubic foot versus about 0.8 pounds per cubic foot in ponds and raceways (Allgood et al., 1970). A prototype system at Seven Springs (1971 to 1973) produced the recommendation for an expanded five-silo study with a clarifier.

The Seven Springs system comprised:
- **Silos:** five steel tanks, 7 ft 3 in diameter and 11 ft high, each with 3,400 gallons (454 cubic feet) of rearing capacity, buried one-half to two-thirds of their height.
- **Clarifier:** a circular unit with 54,000-gallon capacity providing three-hour retention of a 300 gpm flow, later fitted with an opaque cover.
- **Raceways:** fifteen pre-cast concrete raceways, 100 ft long, 6 ft wide and 3 ft deep, each holding 1,500 cubic feet (11,219 gallons), arranged in three batteries of five.

Costs: each silo cost $4,300.00 installed; the total silo system with covered clarifier cost $63,530.00 ($12,706.00 per silo). Each raceway cost $6,065.00 installed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The hatchery sits at 8,000 feet elevation on a mountain creek that drains to the Rio Grande. Its water came from springs supplying about 700 gallons per minute, so limited supply made efficient reuse a priority.

**Water quality parameters measured** at seven permanent stations:
- **Dissolved oxygen:** stayed at acceptable levels except briefly after feeding; recovery began within 20 minutes and reached suitable levels within one hour. Maximum station-to-station loss was 15.91 percent, with none of the differences statistically significant.
- **Ammonia (NH3-N):** rose from 0.012 mg/l in supply water to 0.665 mg/l in fifth-use water, averaging 0.084 mg/l added per silo. Accumulation was lower than predicted, likely because of agitation-driven stripping (Loehr et al., 1973). Gill examinations found no hyperplasia (all graded 0).
- **Unionized ammonia:** peaked at 0.008 mg/l (summer 1976, pH 7.7, 55°F), well below the 0.02 mg/l continuous-exposure limit cited from Wedemeyer and Wood (1974).
- **Suspended solids:** ranged from 0.5 to 81.2 mg/l; about 41.5 to 42 percent were removed by sedimentation after the fourth water use.
- **Settleable solids:** ranged from 0.0 to 0.5 ml/l; the clarifier removed 83.3 percent after fourth use.
- **pH:** after six months, 6.7 to 7.7 (median 7.2, mode 7.0).
- **Temperature:** silo water varied less than 1°F year round, while raceway water ranged from 32°F to 63°F. Air temperatures ranged from -33°F to 85°F.

Algae growth in the clarifier, caused by sunlight and air exposure, was remedied by an opaque cover installed in 1977.

## Key Findings

**Trout production (rainbow trout, Salmo gairdneri):**
- Ten tests in silos (A to J) and nine concurrent tests in raceways were run, plus two raceway tests using first-use (fresh) water.
- Fresh-water and reused-water raceways did not differ significantly, so silos and raceways could be compared directly.
- For production loads (33 samples per unit type), silos gained weight 39.13 percent faster (0.96 vs 0.69 percent per day; significant at α = 0.05) and grew 84.62 percent faster (0.024 vs 0.013 inch per day; significant at α = 0.01).
- Feed conversion was better in raceways (2.56 vs 3.01), a 17.58 percent difference that was not statistically significant.
- Mortality did not differ significantly between systems.
- Trout under 5 inches (about 20 per pound) performed poorly in silos; best production came from trout averaging 15 per pound (5.5 inches) or larger.
- Raceways lost 67 percent of their rate of gain after the third water use (1.04 to 0.34 percent per day), whereas silos showed no significant decline.

**Density and capacity:**
- Silos supported a density index about three times higher than raceways. The density index at maximum capacity was 0.745 for silos and 0.271 for raceways (36 percent of the silo value).
- Estimated maximum silo loads were 2,029 lb (6-inch fish) to 3,044 lb (9-inch fish). Raceways held a comparable maximum weight with 3.3 times the water volume.

**Water-quality ranking:**
- Silo 1 ranked first overall (19 points), followed by Silo 2 (14), Silo 5 (9), Silo 3 (7) and Silo 4 (5), based on dissolved oxygen, ammonia, and solids.
- Silo-rank water quality and trout production showed a correlation of r = 0.80. In raceways the correlation was higher (r = 0.90), meaning declining water quality hits raceway production harder.

**Labor and cost:**
- Silo culture required 59.7 percent of the personnel time of raceways (pond cleaning down 48.4 percent, mortality removal down 42.5 percent, feeding down 45.7 percent; moving and grading 1.7 percent higher).
- Operational cost was $1.07 per pound in silos versus $1.35 per pound in raceways (20.7 percent less), although silo feed cost was higher ($0.54 vs $0.46 per pound).
- Projected annual output per unit: 2,067 lb net gain per silo ($2,212.00 cost) versus 2,456 lb per raceway ($3,316.00 cost). The $1,104.00 annual saving per unit would repay the $6,641.00 capital difference in just over six years.

**Recommendations:**
- Silos are a valuable supplement to trout culture where water supply or land area is limited, or where daily or seasonal air temperatures fluctuate significantly.
- Silos suit catchable or market-size trout from stock longer than five inches; smaller silos or typical raceways are preferable for trout shorter than five inches.
