# Recommendations on Bonytail Retrieval Plan

#ichthyology #Cyprinidae #Bonytail #ConservationManagement #ColoradoRiver

## Executive Summary
This 1987 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum from the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] addresses a draft field protocol for the handling, transport, and retrieval of wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a critically rare, endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River basin. Rather than presenting primary biological data, the document establishes operational and ethical guidelines for field researchers who may incidentally encounter this species during other fieldwork, emphasizing the priority of securing any wild-caught bonytail for captive conservation purposes despite inherent handling risks. The memo reflects the extreme rarity of the species at the time, noting that only "a few" individuals were expected to be encountered over "the next few years."

## Background & Description
- The document references **minimum identification criteria** for distinguishing [[Bonytail]] from similar co-occurring [[Gila (genus)|Gila]] species (e.g., [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]]) in the wild, though the specific morphometric thresholds are contained in the referenced (unattached) draft protocol dated 24 June 1987.
- Field identification was to be supported by standardized tools: **calipers** (for morphometric measurement) and a **fish key**, indicating identification relied on quantifiable body-shape/meristic criteria rather than field judgment alone.
- The memo explicitly recommends that these **minimum identification criteria** "should be strictly adhered to," underscoring the taxonomic difficulty of separating bonytail from morphologically similar congeners in the Colorado River system — a well-documented challenge in [[Gila Chub|*Gila* species complex]] identification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Wild [[Bonytail]] individuals were encountered incidentally during general fisheries fieldwork in the [[Colorado River]] system (upper basin), implying a riverine, mainstem-associated habitat consistent with historic bonytail distribution.
- **Conservation status context**: By 1987, bonytail were already so rare in the wild that the capture of "whatever few" individuals were found was treated as a high-priority conservation event, justifying accepted risk to individual fish during transport in order to establish or supplement a captive broodstock/refuge population.
- **Human interaction**: The species' interaction with humans in this context was defined entirely by scientific collection under permit, transport in oxygenated coolers, and potential multi-day holding by field researchers before transfer to FWS personnel — reflecting active human intervention as a core component of the species' contemporary ecological/management context.

## Key Findings
- **Field discretion authority**: Researchers who encounter a probable bonytail should have complete discretion to judge whether safe transport to a river-accessible retrieval point is feasible; if risk is judged too high, the fish should be released back into the river rather than risked in transport.
- **Permit modification needed**: Existing collecting permits required amendment to legally authorize removal of bonytail from the wild, since the species' endangered status otherwise restricted such handling.
- **Liability provision**: Researchers acting without negligence should not be held responsible for the death of a fish during transport, formally acknowledging that risk is unavoidable when wild, endangered fish are captured and held.
- **Equipment standardization**: The FWS committed to supplying standardized transport/holding equipment — appropriately sized coolers, oxygen cylinders, calipers, and a fish identification key — to ensure consistent handling protocol across cooperators.
- **Extended holding capability**: Researchers needed to be prepared to hold a captured bonytail under proper care conditions for **two or more days** while awaiting FWS personnel to take possession, implying logistical readiness (aeration, temperature control) was a key protocol requirement.
- **Rarity-driven risk tolerance**: The memo explicitly frames the conservation value of capturing any wild bonytail as outweighing the operational and welfare risks of transport and holding — a management judgment reflecting the species' critically endangered status in the upper [[Colorado River]] basin.
- **No universal protocol claim**: The authors acknowledge no single protocol can guarantee fully safe and cost-effective transport/handling of wild bonytail, framing the guidelines as best-available practice rather than a definitive solution.
