# Endangered Colorado River Fishes: Sandstone and Muddy Creek Biological Opinions and Recovery Program Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document set, circulated in August 1989 ahead of the [[Implementation Committee]] meeting of August 30, 1989, concerns the consultation process under [[Section 7 Consultation]] of the [[Endangered Species Act]] for water development projects in the Upper Colorado River Basin. It contains draft [[Biological Opinion]]s for the [[Sandstone Project]] (Wyoming and Colorado) and the [[Muddy Creek Project]] (Colorado), a State of Colorado memo on the "sufficient progress" issue, and a status update on the [[Propagation Genetics Management Plan]]. Each opinion concludes that the project is not likely to jeopardize the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]], provided that agreed conservation measures are carried out. The central dispute is whether progress under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] is sufficient to offset depletion impacts, and whether that progress is judged on instream flows alone or on all five program elements.

## Background & Description

The cover memorandum from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) Colorado River Recovery Program Director transmitted four review items: the draft Sandstone Biological Opinion conservation measures, a discussion paper by [[Bill McDonald]] on "sufficient progress," a revised Muddy Creek conservation measures document, and a paper on the Rare Fishes Propagation and Genetics Management Plan by [[J. Holt Williamson]].

The Recovery Program was signed on January 21-22, 1988, by the Secretary of the Interior, the governors of Wyoming, Colorado, and Utah, and the Administrator of the [[Western Area Power Administration]]. It aims to identify reasonable and prudent alternatives that allow new water development while ensuring the survival and recovery of the listed fishes. Its five elements are instream flows, habitat development, stocking, reduction of competition with non-native species, and research and monitoring.

The fishes covered are:
- **Colorado squawfish**, whose larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) survival depends on backwater nursery habitat.
- **Humpback chub**, which uses point bars for spawning and resting.
- **Bonytail chub**, included in the no-take determinations.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, the focus of the propagation and contaminant work in the plan update.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and flow.** The [[Little Snake River]] supplies most of the sediment load to the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]]. The baseline depletes its sandload by almost one-third of historic yield. The Sandstone Project would reduce peak flows in the Little Snake, Yampa, and Green rivers and reduce their sandload transport capacity. Yampa flow reductions are 2.3-2.6 percent from baseline, and Green River reductions are 1.1-1.3 percent. The Service judges these small reductions to be cumulatively important because they reduce peak spring flows that trigger spawning, maintain channel geomorphology, provide access to off-channel habitat, and preserve spawning substrate.

**Channel morphology.** Reduced sandload is expected to degrade Yampa point bars, which are important humpback chub habitat. Over several decades, the Green River is expected to narrow and deepen, losing backwaters, embayments, and side channels. That loss would reduce successful rearing of larval and YOY squawfish. Squawfish spawning on cobble bars is not expected to be affected.

**Muddy Creek effects.** In the 15-mile reach of the [[Colorado River 15-Mile Reach]], cumulative depletions from Muddy Creek and other development would reduce monthly and instantaneous flows by 10 to 13 percent at Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon (BLM 1989). Peak spring flows help flush fine sediment from cobble spawning substrate, and the Service expects the Muddy Creek changes will not appreciably affect this. Simons et al. (1983) found the upper Colorado transports sediment far below capacity and has an armored channel, so the Service concludes channel morphology will not change significantly. Temperature and turbidity are also not expected to change significantly. Late-summer flows could be reduced in some years, and under State water law the small accretions have no guaranteed protection from diversion without a legal instream flow mechanism.

**Propagation.** The [[Propagation Genetics Management Plan]] aims for self-sustaining wild populations of rare fishes by protecting habitat and using artificial propagation grounded in genetic conservation. The [[Service Field Stations]] at Grand Junction, Colorado, and Vernal, Utah, are critical to implementation.

## Key Findings

### 1. Sandstone Project (draft Biological Opinion)
- Average annual depletion is approximately **30,600 acre-feet per year**.
- The applicant agreed to a one-time payment of **$10 per acre-foot**, totaling **$306,000**. Ten percent ($30,600) is due at Section 404 permit issuance, and the balance ($275,400) is due at construction start or depletion, whichever comes first. The amount is indexed to inflation from October 1, 1989.
- The Commission will not commit **20,000 acre-feet** of firm annual yield to consumptive use until the Service finds sufficient progress. Until then, that water is released downstream to benefit Yampa River flows in July through September.
- Under a worst-case scenario, depletion would be **12,200 acre-feet**. The analysis indicates the 20,000 acre-feet could offset this.
- Each acre-foot of Recovery Program instream flow legally protected in the Yampa frees one acre-foot of the 20,000 from the opinion's conditions. Water supplied to offset other projects (Muddy Creek, Ruedi) cannot count.
- If sufficient progress is not shown six months before storage begins, the Corps and the Service must reinitiate consultation. This consultation determines the use of the $306,000 and whether the 20,000 acre-feet should protect Yampa flows.
- The Corps' special permit condition requires notice before any irrevocable commitment of the 20,000 acre-feet.
- **No incidental take** of Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, or bonytail chub is authorized.

### 2. Muddy Creek Project (draft Biological Opinion)
- Average annual depletion is **7,716 acre-feet per year**.
- The applicant will pay **$77,160** ($10 per acre-foot). Ten percent ($7,716) is due at federal approval and the balance ($69,444) at construction start. The fee on October 1, 1989, is **$10.41 per acre-foot**. Half of the funds go to acquiring water rights for instream flows, and the rest supports other recovery activities.
- The Service found progress sufficient, based primarily on a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) under which the Colorado River Water Conservation District will release **3,000 acre-feet** from Muddy Creek Reservoir from July 15 to October 15, roughly **3 out of 5 years** on average. The releases are intended to help meet the Service's **700-1,200 cfs** flow recommendation for the 15-mile reach.
- Progress items cited include completion of the Service's July-September flow recommendation and the Reclamation alternatives study, plus pending agreements on protecting Ruedi and Green Mountain releases.
- The State of Colorado must agree not to subordinate Muddy Creek releases, and the Service retains authority to protect them if the State defaults.
- The Service found no incidental take is anticipated, given the MOU and the monetary contribution.

### 3. Sufficient Progress Dispute (McDonald memo, July 5, 1989)
- The Recovery Program document says the program depends on all five elements, and that the $10 per acre-foot contribution funds any element. Water users therefore read "sufficient progress" as basin-wide progress across all elements.
- The Service's position is narrower. Only the instream flow element counts toward offsetting depletions, and progress alone is not enough without "reasonable assurance" that instream flows will be protected in a timely manner. The Service found that assurance lacking.
- The Regional Solicitor (May 12, 1989) held that the extra conservation measure is legally required and that opinions cannot rest on measures not yet implemented.
- Under the Service's position, instream flows on the Little Snake would not count toward Muddy Creek because the project is not on that river. McDonald argues this conflicts with the basin-wide understanding.
- The proposed Muddy Creek MOU would end the obligation to release water once an equivalent amount is acquired for the 15-mile reach, if the Service finds the flows unnecessary, or after consultation if neither applies.

### 4. Propagation/Genetics Management Plan Update (August 17, 1989)
- The second draft is under review by the Management and Technical Groups, Regions 2 and 6, the ad hoc Propagation Work Group, and the hatchery advisory team.
- The plan has **seven objectives**, including identifying short- and long-term fish needs (**15 years in 5-year increments**), developing facilities, a genetics management plan, and rare fish refugia as gene banks.
- FY 1990 priorities include Gila spp. and razorback sucker genetics, Colorado squawfish and razorback broodstock, larval feeding trials, and marking and tagging studies.

### 5. Procedural Implications
- Reinitiated consultation is required if any project change alters depletion beyond the 30,600 or 7,716 acre-feet analyzed, if new species are listed, or if any conservation measure fails.
- The Service asks the Corps and the Bureau of Land Management to incorporate the MOU and retain jurisdiction in their approval documents.
