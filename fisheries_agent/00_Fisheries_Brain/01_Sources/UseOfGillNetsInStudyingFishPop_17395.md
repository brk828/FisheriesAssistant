# Gill Net Catch Patterns and Relative Abundance of Fishes in Clear Lake, Iowa, 1947–1952

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report uses [[Gill Nets]] catch records from [[Clear Lake Iowa]], collected between 1947 and 1952, to examine changes in the abundance of the lake's fishes. The nets were originally set to provide samples for [[Age and Growth Studies]]. The author notes that catch per net is shaped both by fish abundance and by fish activity, so the data were corrected for [[Diel Activity Patterns]] before being summarized as [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] values. The resulting [[Relative Abundance Index]] values are presented as probable indicators of population trends, not as firm measures of abundance. The author also cautions that gill nets are not suitable for comparing abundance between species.

## Background & Description

The standard experimental gill net was 25-foot lengths of stretch mesh in sizes of 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3 and 4 inches, and was five feet deep. Netting in Clear Lake began in the summer of 1947. A 6-inch mesh was included during the first three years but was discontinued because it caught so few fish. The nets sampled [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Yellow Bass]], [[White Bass]] and [[Black Bullhead]] with little size selection, from about 4.5 to 23 inches depending on species.

Nets were lifted at roughly two-hour intervals, so the catch could be compared across times of day. Gill-net catches from 1947 through 1952 were made with linen. The author expected nylon nets, which other studies reported to fish more efficiently, to be used in future work. This made 1947–1952 a suitable period for summarizing the existing record. The work was carried out as Project 39 of the [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]], supported by the Iowa State Conservation Commission and the Industrial Science Research Institute of Iowa State College, with cooperation from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Field collections were made by a group of named student collectors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diel activity.** Fish movement strongly affects gill-net catch, and each species shows a characteristic activity cycle.
- Walleye, yellow bass, white bass and black bullhead were more active at night, with activity peaking soon after dusk. [[Moore 1944]] found that walleye eye structure did not permit adaptation to bright light, which may explain their low daytime movement.
- Yellow perch, [[Northern Pike]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]] were most active during the day and relatively inactive at night.
- [[Crappies]], [[White Sucker]] and [[Carp]] showed no clearly marked hourly effect.

**Depth migration.** Earlier studies in [[Muskellunge Lake]], Wisconsin, and [[Lake of the Woods]], Minnesota, found suckers in deeper water mostly by day and in shallow water at night. Clear Lake showed no such migration. Suckers caught in water less than 6 feet deep at different hours matched catches from deeper water.

**Consistency.** Activity patterns for each species with sufficient data were essentially the same each year, suggesting the patterns are stable at least through the summer months.

**Netting location.** Only nets set June through August near the State Fish Hatchery on the north shore were included in the abundance index. The author considered this location fairly typical of much of the lake.

## Key Findings

**Correction for hourly bias.** Because the nets were not fished for a full 24 hours, the mean catch per net for each hour was calculated and these hourly means were summed to give a 24-hour catch, as if equal effort had been applied in every hour. The 24-hour means by year appear in Table 1 (summarized below).

**Table 1 summary (mean catch per 24-hour period, 125-foot experimental gill nets, 1947–1952).**
- Walleye: 4.4 (1947), 4.4 (1948), 13.9 (1949), 18.0 (1950), 5.1 (1951), 14.8 (1952).
- Yellow bass: 9.3, 14.0, 17.6, 51.0, 26.7, 19.9. This was the highest index in the series, peaking in 1950.
- Yellow perch: 23.8 (1947), then 6.2, 0.1, 3.7, 9.1 and 17.4. The 1949 value was the lowest.
- Black bullhead: 4.8, 1.4, 0.4, 13.4, 9.5 and 16.1.
- Other species (carp, white bass, northern pike, white sucker, crappies, bluegill, pumpkinseed, channel catfish) had indices mostly below 3.0.
- Hours netted per year ranged from 240.5 (1947) to 1,084.5 (1948).

**Interpretation of trends.** The author offered several explanations for the year-to-year changes:
- Yellow bass: the 1948 and 1949 year classes were particularly abundant, while few of the 1950 year class survived their first year, according to [[Carlander Et Al 1953]]. The author suggests these differences may have produced a larger population in 1951 than in the next two years.
- Walleye: the low gill-net catch in 1951 followed an unusually successful angling period in May and early June. Larger walleyes may have been less abundant that summer until younger fish reached catchable size.
- Yellow perch: the extremely low 1949 catch coincided with a sharp drop in angling success. The angler's catch of perch per hour in 1949 was one-tenth of the 1948 rate ([[Parsons 1950]]).
- Increases in bluegill, pumpkinseed, crappie and channel catfish in the gill-net catch may indicate growing abundance of these species, although these fish are not usually taken in gill nets.

**Validity.** The author concludes that the indices probably reflect real population trends, since the nets were fished in much the same way each year, comparisons were restricted to the same part of the lake, and hourly corrections were applied. The author also states that the validity of the indices can only be confirmed with additional data.

**Caution on cross-species comparison.** Gill nets probably cannot be used to compare relative abundance across species, because susceptibility to capture varies. [[Largemouth Bass]], crappies and sunfishes avoid gill nets and may be much more abundant than their catch rates suggest.

**Future methods.** Nylon nets were expected to replace linen, which may change catch efficiency and complicate comparisons with the 1947–1952 record.
