# Some Limnological Features of Clear Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #freshwater-ecology #chironomidae #amphipoda #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study by William G. Pearcy (Iowa State College) presents a comprehensive limnological survey of Clear Lake, Iowa, undertaken to support fisheries management by characterizing the physical, chemical, and biological conditions underlying fish productivity. The investigation, conducted from June to October 1951, documents thermal regime, water chemistry, plankton dynamics, benthic invertebrate communities, and aquatic macrophyte succession in this shallow eutrophic lake. While the paper is not fish-species-focused, it explicitly links limnological patterns—especially benthic invertebrate abundance (notably *Hyalella* and chironomid larvae)—to food availability for [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]] (yellow pikeperch), [[Yellow Bass]], [[Carp]], and crappie populations previously studied by the Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit.

## Background & Description

**Study System:** [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]] is a shallow, eutrophic, glacially-formed lake (Wisconsin drift) in Cerro Gordo County, north-central Iowa. Key morphometric features:
- Length: 4.8 miles; maximum width: 2.1 miles
- Surface area: 3,642 acres (5.69 sq. miles)
- Maximum depth: 20 feet; average depth: ~12 feet
- 25% of basin < 7 feet deep
- Shore development index: 1.58
- No surface inflow streams; fed largely by springs/groundwater; single outlet to Cedar River
- Watershed: only 8,400 acres of farmland/woodland

**Bottom substrates:** Fine sand dominates shallow areas (<7 ft), grading to soft organic muck (39% organic matter by ignition) beyond the 7-foot contour. Boulder/rubble/gravel patches occur locally; no exposed bedrock despite underlying limestone.

**Study Period:** June 21–September 10 and October 19–21, 1951.

**Research Context:** Part of ongoing work by the Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit (active since 1941), building on prior species-specific studies of [[Black Bullhead]] (Forney 1952), [[Yellow Perch]] (Parsons 1950), [[Walleye]] (Cleary 1948), [[Carp]] (English 1952), crappies (Erickson 1952), and [[Yellow Bass]] (Lewis & Carlander 1948).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Physical Features
- **Thermal regime:** No summer thermocline developed due to shallow depth and wind mixing; surface-bottom temperature differences were minimal.
- Maximum summer water temperature: 86°F (early August)
- Mean daily temperature range: 5.3°F
- **Light penetration (Secchi disc):** Averaged 4 ft 10 in; declined progressively through the season (June 7.3 ft → September 3.0 ft) as phytoplankton density increased, driven partly by turbidity from suspended soil particles after heavy rains.

### Chemical Features
- Dissolved oxygen: 5–12.5 ppm, with supersaturation common near the surface (up to 146% saturation over dense *Elodea canadensis* beds)
- Marked vertical DO stratification without thermal stratification: deep water mean 67% saturation vs. 96% (non-vegetated shallow) and 116% (vegetated shallow) — attributed to reduced circulation and lower phytoplankton photosynthesis at depth
- Methyl orange alkalinity: mean 156 ppm
- Free CO₂: averaged 51 ppm

### Habitat Zonation (Aquatic Vegetation Communities)
Four major plant communities were identified, reflecting successional stage and substrate:
1. **Shallow water vegetation** (0–7 ft, sand-dominated): most diverse and abundant; dominated by *Potamogeton* spp. (submerged) and [[Scirpus validus]] (softstem bulrush, emergent)
2. **Deep water vegetation** (>7 ft, muck): only *Potamogeton pectinatus* and *Najas flexilis* present, the latter reaching 15 ft depth
3. **Reed-swamp vegetation** (SW west end, South Bay marsh): fibrous peat/detritus substrate; dominated by *Sagittaria* spp., *Phragmites communis*, *Sparganium eurycarpum*
4. **Marginal vegetation**: sandy shore/wet soil species, concentrated near Ventura Marsh

Vegetation showed marked interannual change: dense 1945–47, sparse 1948–50, resurgent in 1951; species composition also shifted (e.g., loss of *Ceratophyllum demersum* dominance, gain of *Potamogeton nodosus*).

## Key Findings

### Plankton Community
- Mean standing crop: 40,328 organisms/liter
- **Diatoms** dominant overall (61.3% of phytoplankton), peaking in September–October (up to 177,930/liter); *Asterionella* spp. and *Melosira* spp. were key genera
- **Blue-green algae** averaged 34.6% of phytoplankton; abundance tracked water temperature, with bloom-forming genera (*Gloeotrichia*, *Anabaena*) reaching up to 743,900 units/liter, while *Chroococcus*, *Aphanocapsa*, *Coelosphaerium*, and *Microcystis* were consistently present at lower densities
- **Green algae** contributed 13 species but only 4.1% of phytoplankton volume, peaking in cooler early summer (12,691/liter)
- **Protozoa/Rotifers** comprised 51.37% of total plankton numerically, driven almost entirely by *Dinobryon* spp. (up to 9,237,700/liter in some samples)
- **Crustacean zooplankton** (*Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, *Diaptomus*, *Cyclops*) were minor (0.09% of total plankton, ~100 units/liter)
- Statistical field-count requirements were derived for plankton enumeration precision (e.g., 134 fields needed for ±10% deviation)

### Benthic Invertebrate Fauna — Food Base for Fish
Sampled at 3 shallow stations (2–4 ft, sand) and 1 deep station (>7 ft, muck):

- **[[Hyalella knickerbockeri]]** (scud/amphipod): dominant shallow-water organism — 53.7% of volume, 52.9% of numbers; strongly associated with algae/vegetation; important food source for young fish
- **Chironomid (midge) larvae**: second most abundant in shallow water (32.1% volume, 33.2% numbers); dominant taxon at the non-vegetated station
- No statistically significant difference in bottom fauna abundance between vegetated and non-vegetated shallow stations (volume F=0.524; numbers F=0.785), though species richness was higher in vegetated areas
- Significant seasonal variation in shallow-water fauna (volume F=5.20, numbers F=6.62): low of 0.35 mL/ft² in September, peak of 2.70 mL/ft² in October
- **Deep water station**: mean volume 2.90 mL/ft²; dominated by *Tendipes (Tendipes) tentans* ("blood worm"), comprising 94.5% of fauna by volume and 55.5% by numbers; *Planaria* spp. comprised 20.6% of numbers; no significant month-to-month variation (F=1.517–1.748)

**Direct fish-food linkage:** *Tendipes tentans* was identified as the predominant food item (~80% of diet) of the [[Black Bullhead]] (citing Forney 1952), directly connecting benthic community structure to a key Clear Lake fisheries species.

Other invertebrate taxa documented (with habitat associations) include Turbellaria (*Planaria*), Oligochaeta (Naididae, Tubificidae), Hirudinea (leeches), Gastropoda (*Physa*, *Helisoma*, *Lymnaea*), Pelecypoda (Sphaeriidae), Isopoda (*Asellus communis*), Odonata (*Anax junius*, *Enallagma* spp., *Ischnura* sp.), Ephemeroptera (*Caenis* sp.), Hemiptera, Trichoptera (multiple *Oecetis* and *Mystacides* species), Coleoptera, Megaloptera, and Lepidoptera — most closely tied to vegetated microhabitats.

### Management Relevance
- The absence of thermal stratification and generally favorable oxygen conditions suggest limited habitat restriction for fish across depth in Clear Lake, except in deep muck areas with reduced DO saturation.
- Seasonal pulses in benthic fauna (October peak) and plankton (diatom/blue-green cycles) likely influence seasonal fish feeding and growth patterns previously documented for [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Bass]], and [[Black Bullhead]] in companion studies.
- The dramatic interannual fluctuation in aquatic macrophyte abundance (dense 1945–47, sparse 1948–50, resurgent 1951) represents a major habitat variable warranting continued monitoring for fisheries management, given macrophytes' role in structuring invertebrate prey communities (e.g., higher richness in vegetated substrates) and moderating dissolved oxygen dynamics.
