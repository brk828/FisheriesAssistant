# Preliminary Forage Food Survey of the Colorado River Below Davis Dam (1963–1964)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

A four-mile reach of the [[Colorado River]] immediately below [[Davis Dam]] had its silt-sand substrate stripped by scour, leaving a gravel-cobble-boulder bottom. This exploratory study, conducted by the California Department of Fish and Game (Region 5, Inland Fisheries) under the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]], aimed to identify which fish forage organisms had colonized the exposed gravel and whether introducing additional forage species would be advisable.

Three surveys (October 1963, December 1963, February 1964) tested sampling tools. Most benthic methods failed. The newly designed [[Fast Dredge]] was the only mechanical device that captured benthic organisms, yielding one amphipod, 18 [[Blackfly Larvae]], and one blackfly adult. Stomach analysis of fish added information, but the diet data were dominated by artificial foods from angler-caught hatchery trout. The authors concluded that further study is needed before any introductions are made.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and context.** Sand and silt normally form the upper, mobile layer of the river bed, while gravel and boulders remain stable beneath it. Sandy bottoms are unstable and poor for fish food organisms, whereas gravel bottoms are stable and support them. Because [[Davis Dam]] impounds the river to form [[Lake Mohave]], silt and sand are deposited in the reservoir, and water released downstream is nearly sediment-free. Sediment was therefore not replenished below the dam, exposing the gravel bed. A change in the benthic fauna was expected.

**Study status.** The authors stated that this report does not summarize a completed project. Sampling techniques and tools were still being developed.

**Study area.**
- The first four miles below the dam follow an almost straight course, with a hard-packed gravel-cobble-boulder bottom to an S-shaped bend.
- The first mile is relatively narrow, with an average depth of about 15–20 feet at low flow.
- The next four miles widen, with an average depth of about five feet at low water.
- Banks are steep, and a 10-foot rise in water level inundates little area.
- Releases follow hydroelectric demand. Water level fluctuates 6–10 feet, usually in two cycles daily.

**Methods overview.** Surveys were conducted October 23–24, 1963; December 11–13, 1963; and February 8–9, 1964. Methods included:
- Shoreline sieving with a No. 30 mesh brass sieve (October).
- [[Ekman Dredge]] sampling from stationary and drifting boats (October).
- [[Fast Dredge]] tows, two 100-foot tows on February 8, 1964, at Sites A and B, combined for analysis.
- [[Bottom Drift Net]] sets (three in December, two in February).
- [[Gill Net]] overnight sets (125-foot experimental nets, five graded stretched-mesh panels, roughly 1.5–4 inches) at Sites A and C.
- Angler-caught fish sampled near the dam on February 8, 1964.
- Two baited trotlines at Site C (February).
- Seining was not attempted because no suitable areas exist.

Stomachs from all gill-net and angler fish were preserved in 10 percent formalin, and each stomach was labelled to its fish. Contents were analyzed under the methods of [[Stomach Content Analysis]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical constraints on sampling.** The authors described benthic sampling in this reach as extremely difficult because of swift current, deep water, and daily water-level fluctuation. Shoreline areas that were accessible appeared largely barren of [[Benthic Fauna]], likely because of alternating inundation and desiccation.

**Substrate and habitat differences.** Gill-net catches were most productive at a backwater location with sand and mud bottom and relatively calm water. The authors inferred that this habitat did not hold the same benthic species as the swift, gravel-bottomed main channel. Hatchery trout in the vicinity of the dam were probably foraging in the immediate area.

**Periphyton and midge larvae.** Several [[Chironomidae]] pupae (*Tendipes* spp., probably) were found in trout stomachs. The authors suggested these were ingested near the dam. A dense growth of [[Periphyton]] covered the concrete spillway face, and the authors proposed that this periphyton harbors the midges.

**Hatchery influence on feeding.** The authors proposed that the failure of rainbow trout to consume blackfly larvae may reflect hatchery rearing on artificial feed. Hatchery fish may not yet have had enough time in the river to acclimatize and exploit natural food.

**Angler bias.** Angler-caught fish came from the same area as gill-net fish, but the authors considered their stomachs unrepresentative of the whole study area because of the high proportion of artificial baits in them.

## Key Findings

**Sampling outcomes**
- **Sieve (October 1963):** No benthic organisms recovered. Method abandoned after this survey.
- **Ekman dredge (October 1963):** Few samples obtained. The dredge was carried downstream from anchored boats, tumbled from drifting boats, and could not penetrate compact gravel. None of the samples contained forage organisms. The authors judged it inadequate for this area.
- **Peterson dredge:** Not used on these survey dates. Later trials required three men per sample and were difficult. Results were not discussed.
- **Fast dredge (February 1964):** Captured one amphipod (Gammaridae), 18 blackfly larvae (*Simulium* spp.), and one blackfly adult from the two combined tows. This was the most successful mechanical sampler. It broke during its first December trial, was modified, and was then used successfully in February.
- **Bottom drift nets:** Two of three December nets were swept away, and the third captured nothing. February nets (two nets, 48 total net-hours) captured only one blackfly larva.
- **Gill nets:** Current-set nets in December were destroyed by the current; the remaining net caught three rainbow trout without stomach samples. A February overnight set caught seven rainbow trout and one carp.
- **Trotlines:** Caught no fish. They became tangled on rocks and debris.

**Fish stomach data**
- **Gill-net fish (n = 8):** Seven rainbow trout (mean fork length 9.5 in) and one carp (8.9 in). Contents were organic detritus, small pebbles, and empty stomachs. One trout had one anisopteran naiad and one dipteran pupa. Table 1 lists detailed contents.
- **Angler-caught rainbow trout (n = 36):** Mean fork length 8.4 in. Contents were largely artificial: 47 percent of stomachs contained at least one artificial food item (corn, salmon eggs, or cheese), and 37 percent were empty. Some stomachs contained dipteran pupae, blackfly-like insect material was not identified, and a few contained algal or plant detritus. Table 2 lists detailed contents.
- **Overall:** Several dipteran pupae (probably *Tendipes* spp.) and one anisopteran naiad were identified across fish stomachs.

**Conclusions**
- Only the February survey yielded substantial information on forage organisms.
- The Fast dredge is a valuable qualitative sampler for this reach. It can sample a relatively large area quickly, is nearly indestructible, and can be operated by one or two people from a motorboat.
- Drift nets were unsatisfactory here, though they may be useful if the benthic community changes through immigration or introduction.
- Of the methods tested, the Fast dredge and fish stomach analysis hold the most promise.
- The authors recommended further study to identify which forage organisms are present. That knowledge will determine whether any species should be introduced.
- Analysis of carp stomachs, which could not be captured, was suggested as a future source of information on natural food use.
