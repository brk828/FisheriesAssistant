# Effects of Net Length and Set Time on Fish Catches in Gill Nets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #gill-net-sampling

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Charles K. Minns]] and [[Donald A. Hurley]] tests two core assumptions behind gill-net catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) indices: that CPUE is independent of net length, and that CPUE is independent of set time. Experiments were run in the [[Bay of Quinte]], [[Lake Ontario]], at Hay Bay (1983) and Big Bay (1984) using a [[Latin Square Design]]. Net length had little effect on total CPUE, but set time did. Responses differed by species, which the authors attribute to differing activity patterns and [[Gill Net Saturation]]. The authors conclude that gill-net CPUE must be validated species by species before it is used as an index of abundance.

## Background & Description

Catch-effort models in fisheries science have traditionally assumed that CPUE is proportional to population size, an assumption now questioned (Skalski et al. 1983; Richards and Schnute 1986). Much freshwater fish assemblage knowledge comes from gill-net catches, yet few experiments had validated the assumptions behind them, apart from [[Mesh Size Selectivity]] studies.

The gear was a standard 2-in stretched-mesh gill net built in 25-yd panels (40 meshes deep, 210/2 twine, 14 meshes per tie, 82 ties, 8 corks and 8 leads per panel). Experimental treatments were:
- **Net length:** 25, 50, 100 and 200 yd, each set for 12 h (about 1800 to 0600 h).
- **Set time:** 50-yd nets set for 1.5, 3, 6 and 12 h, starting at about 1800 h.

Hay Bay sites were about 400 yd apart at depths of about 25 ft, with water temperatures of 9.5-20.0°C from June to late July. Big Bay sites were about 400 yd apart at about 15 ft, with temperatures of 22-24°C from June through August.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame gill-net performance around [[Gill Net Saturation]], meaning diminishing returns as effort increases. Proposed mechanisms include:
- **Space limitation:** a captured fish and adjacent meshes cannot catch others (Meth 1970; Koike and Takeuchi 1982).
- **Behavioral deterrence:** struggling or dead fish make nets conspicuous or trigger alarm reactions (Kennedy 1951).
- **Local depletion:** reduced fish availability lowers efficiency over multiple day-night cycles.
- **Diel activity:** [[Yellow Perch]] are mainly crepuscular, [[Walleye]] mainly nocturnal, [[Alewife]] mainly diurnal (but feeds at night in Lake Michigan), and [[White Perch]] are active both day and night (Leach 1962).

Earlier work by Hurley (1986) on the Bay of Quinte, which used trawls and gill nets, was consistent with the species occurrence and abundance differences found between the upper and lower bay.

## Key Findings

**Study design and data**
- A Latin square design (4 treatments × 4 locations × 4 dates) was used. In 1984, four of seven squares were incomplete.
- Catches were analysed with a multiplicative regression model, log(catch + 1) against log(net length), log(set time), and dummy variables for station and date.

**Catch patterns**
- Catches at Hay Bay (1983) were lower than at Big Bay (1984). Only 37.5% of Hay Bay catches exceeded 0.5 fish/yd, versus 62.6% at Big Bay. Catches above 2 fish/yd occurred in 4.7% of Hay Bay sets versus 19.8% at Big Bay.
- Across both years, 24 fish species were captured (12 in 1983, 19 in 1984). Seven species were represented by single specimens.
- Hay Bay catches were dominated by yellow perch, alewives and rainbow smelt. Big Bay catches were dominated by white perch (6,018 fish in 84 nets), alewives (2,655) and yellow perch (1,038).

**Regression results**
- The models explained 83.1% (1983) and 85.4% (1984) of total variance (both P < 0.001).
- Net-length coefficients did not differ significantly from 1, indicating constant CPUE with net length for total catch.
- In 1983, the set-time coefficient was significantly less than 1 (CPUE declined with set time). In 1984 it was significantly greater than 1 (CPUE rose with set time), driven mainly by white perch.
- Hay Bay showed no significant station differences. In Big Bay, station A had a significantly lower catch rate than the other three stations.

**Species-specific responses**
- Yellow perch: net-length coefficient near 1 and set-time coefficient below 1 in both years, so CPUE did not change with net length but fell with set time.
- White perch (1984): net-length coefficient below 1 and set-time coefficient above 1 (1.321, SE 0.178).
- Alewives and walleyes (1984): coefficients for both net length and set time were below 1. Walleyes were caught in 54 of 91 nets.

**Species richness**
- Species richness increased significantly with both log(net length) and log(set time) (P = 0.05), with year as a significant intercept shift.

**Management conclusions**
- Gill-net catches can be misleading as abundance indices. Short sets suggested yellow perch matched or exceeded white perch, while overnight sets suggested white perch were far more abundant.
- Abundance may be overestimated at low densities and underestimated at high densities if saturation and behavior are not controlled.
- The authors recommend species-specific sampling designs, using the gear least biased for each species, and further study of saturation phenomena.
