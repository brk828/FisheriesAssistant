# Glen Canyon Dam and the Ecology of the Colorado River Through Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 opinion piece by environmental writer Ronald Harris, published in *The Arizona Republic*, argues that [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has damaged the Colorado River ecosystem through Glen and [[Grand Canyon]] since its operation was intensified for [[Hydropower Peaking]]. The author describes how fluctuating and flood flows disrupted fish habitat, spawning sites and the food chain, and how the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) were delayed, underfunded and constrained. The piece traces the policy path from the 1982 studies through the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] (signed October 30, 1992) to the impact statement due in 1994. It concludes that adaptive management and continued monitoring are essential, and that the dam should be modified or, in the author's closing words, removed.

## Background & Description

The document is a newspaper commentary, not a primary research report. It covers the following:

- **Dam history:** Glen Canyon Dam was authorized as a flow-regulation, water-storage and irrigation facility, with hydropower as a secondary purpose. The author argues that operations reversed these priorities. The dam was built about six years before the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) of 1969 was passed. Turbines and generators were later upgraded, and the filling of [[Lake Powell]] in 1980 enabled [[Peaking Power]] generation.
- **Species of concern:** The text names the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], along with the [[Lee's Ferry Blue Ribbon Trout Fishery]]. It does not give population counts or other quantitative biological data for these species.
- **Scope of content:** The article contains no sample sizes, survey results or fish statistics. Its quantitative content is limited to flow rates, water-level changes, funding figures and dates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrological disturbance.** According to the author, operations for peaking power caused large daily and seasonal swings in flow:

- Winter releases were as low as 1,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), while summer releases reached 31,500 cfs.
- Water levels could rise or fall as much as 13 feet at a time.
- Rapid falls stranded fish and desiccated [[Spawning Redds]]. Rapid rises washed away eggs, carried off fry, and rearranged sand, beaches and [[Riparian Habitat]].
- The author states that these fluctuations scoured aquatic vegetation and the food chain it supported.

**Flood conditions.** During high releases the Bureau of Reclamation bypassed the turbines and released clear, sediment-free water. The article reports this as "over 256,100 cfs" (figure reproduced as printed, and it may be an OCR error). The author describes the resulting damage as the flushing away of fisheries, the loss of beaches and the disappearance of gravel bars.

**Other concerns.** The author links the dam's operations to the wider Colorado River system and to the cumulative effects of the dam. He describes the [[Lake Powell]] reservoir and Glen Canyon Dam as among the "most monstrous monoliths" that impound a river, and he notes that [[Hoover Dam]] is in the same system.

## Key Findings

**1. Policy and study history**

- The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] began operating the dam for peaking power, and the author states that this was contrary to its authorized purposes.
- The Reagan administration ordered the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies, which began in 1982 under Interior Secretary James Watt. The author describes the studies as delaying tactics.
- A [[National Research Council]] review found that the first phase failed its scientific objectives, partly because of serious study flaws and weather variability.
- The Phase 1 summary, published in 1988, stated: "This study was not intended nor designed to lead directly to changes in dam operations."
- In August 1989, after lawsuits from the [[National Wildlife Federation]] and the [[Grand Canyon Trust]], Interior Secretary Manuel Lujan ordered an environmental impact statement. Lujan ordered interim flows in August 1991.
- Rep. George Miller introduced the Grand Canyon Protection Act in April 1990. President George Bush signed it on October 30, 1992.

**2. Constraints on the science**

- The act required the studies to be completed by October 30, 1994, which the author describes as "a scant two years to do a decade's work."
- More than 150 research trips collected data for the impact statement.
- The impact-statement team developed nine alternatives for future dam operations.
- The preferred option was a Modified Low Fluctuating Flow, which the author describes as a consensus that may not be the best long-term choice.

**3. Alternative proposals**

- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] issued a biological opinion recommending a seasonally adjusted steady flow, which would mimic the pre-dam natural hydrograph and benefit endangered fish habitat and spawning and nursery sites.
- The author notes that further studies and experimental flows will be needed, and that protecting these species will require years of cooperation among users, the public and government.
- The article proposes retrofitting the dam with outlets for [[Multiselective Withdrawal]] at a cost of about $60 million. This would allow managers to control outflow temperature for endangered fish and the trout fishery.

**4. Costs and conclusions**

- The author states that the impact statement has already cost about half as much as the original construction cost of the dam.
- He argues that monitoring must be ongoing and that [[Adaptive Management]] is the key to success for the Grand Canyon and other multiple-use resources.
- His final position is that, until the river eventually erodes the dam, the operation should be made "as natural as the dam allows," or "we could remove the dam."
