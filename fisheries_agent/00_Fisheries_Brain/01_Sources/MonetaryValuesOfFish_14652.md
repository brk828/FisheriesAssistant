# Monetary Values of Fish: State and Provincial Fish Kill Valuation Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #percidae #ictaluridae #fish-kill-valuation

## Executive Summary

This publication, compiled by John M. Stubbs in Nashville, Tennessee (March 1966), summarizes a questionnaire sent to all U.S. states and nine Canadian provinces on how fish killed by pollution are valued in monetary terms, whether legal or out-of-court damages were collected, and what each jurisdiction considers its sport fishery to be worth. The study is explicitly framed as an aid providing standards of comparison rather than definite guidelines. It reports that monetary values placed on fishes, and success in recovering losses, vary considerably from state to state. The compilation includes per-fish and per-pound price schedules for more than 35 taxa, case-by-case accounts of fish kill settlements, and state-level estimates of sport fishing value.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The introduction states that placing monetary values on fishes is "a complex and difficult task" faced by Federal and State wildlife agencies, particularly for pollution-caused fish kills. The study aims to provide standards of comparison, and it notes that recovery of monetary values for fish lost to pollution was relatively new at the time.

**Ecological framing.** The text argues that every fish species does not carry a monetary value, that fishes serve specific roles, and that aquatic life is interdependent. It notes that a natural decimation of a population may ultimately benefit ecological structure, whereas removal of fishes and/or supporting organisms can upset the optimum balance of an aquatic community with far-reaching ill effects.

**Methodology.** A questionnaire was sent to all states and nine Canadian provinces. It asked whether legal action had been taken concerning a fish kill (including out-of-court settlements), whether damages were collected and by what type (lawsuit, administrative action, other), and, for each case, the claimed and received damages, fish numbers and weights, species-level values and cost per pound. A final question asked for the respondent's valuation of sport fishing in the state.

**Document structure.** The compilation contains: an introduction and questionnaire; an index of species price schedules (Rainbow Trout through Crayfish); a summary table of legal action types; a table of sport fishing values by state and province; and detailed state and provincial responses, including Illinois' standard price list and Pennsylvania's approved price list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish kill causes reported across jurisdictions:**

- **Industrial and chemical discharges:** cyanide (Kalamazoo River, Michigan; Connecticut Willimantic and Hockanum Rivers), acid and chemical wastes (San Francisco Bay, California), phosphate wastes (Alafia River, Florida), gasoline and oil (Tennessee hatchery supply), and coal wastes and mine drainage (Pennsylvania, Missouri, West Virginia).
- **Pesticides:** agricultural and aerial spray (Alabama, Connecticut Rimmon Brook, Tennessee Chapman Chemical case, and forest insecticide spraying in New Brunswick, Canada).
- **Sewage and organic wastes:** low dissolved oxygen from domestic sewage (Mississippi, Pearl River) and sugar beet processing (Utah, Malad and Bear Rivers).
- **Siltation and habitat loss:** logging-related siltation in California and Utah, where the report notes that bottom fauna and habitat losses cause more long-term damage but are less likely to produce immediate cash settlements (Colorado).
- **Low flow and dam operation:** stream drying by a dam (Washington, Bremerton) and shutdown of tunnel flow (California, Owens River).
- **Spills and accidents:** settling-basin failures (West Virginia, Tennessee), cement and dye spills, and a dairy cleaning-solution release (Rhode Island).

**Sampling and evidence guidelines** (Michigan Department of Conservation, Supplement #1, 1960) describe how investigators should estimate kill extent by sampling counts across measured sections, drifting counts in rivers, and seining in turbid water, and how to preserve evidence and document conditions for court use.

## Key Findings

**1. Legal action and damage recovery (Table of Legal Action).**
- Most states reported that no damages were collected, or that action was taken through administrative or out-of-court routes.
- Lawsuits with damages collected were reported in several states, including California, Michigan, Missouri, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Wisconsin and Wyoming.
- Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Delaware, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, New Mexico, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Canadian provinces Alberta, Manitoba, New Brunswick and Newfoundland reported no legal action or no information.

**2. Notable settlements.**
- **Pennsylvania, Glen Alden Corporation (October 1962):** damages asked $58,504.50; received $45,000.00; estimated 116,280 fish killed over a 67.5-mile kill. The report describes this as the largest amount ever received as compensation for a fish kill in Pennsylvania.
- **Tennessee, Victor Chemical Company (July 20, 1963):** damages asked $6,933.49; received $2,500.00; approximately 12½ stream miles affected, with about 5,000 lbs of fish estimated.
- **Connecticut, Roncari Construction Company (cement spill, January 7, 1958):** damages $7,817.76 received; 82,900 fish killed.
- **Illinois, Mid-South Chemical Corporation (August 1, 1961):** damages asked $77,368.87; received $7,736.87; 212,232 fish killed.
- **California, Stauffer Chemical Company (May 1957):** damages $13,369.38 received; 2,009 striped bass (minimum count). Replacement was costed by importing live striped bass from Coos Bay, Oregon, at $0.40 per pound, plus air freight and oxygen.
- **Colorado, New Jersey Zinc Company:** damages $13,604.00 received for approximately 60,000 fish killed (13,604 lbs), at $1.00 per pound.
- **Michigan, Union Steel Company (1955):** estimated roughly 200,000 fish killed; damages claimed $15,000, with an in-court settlement of $3,000.
- **New York:** statutory penalty of $10.00 per fish killed, though the report notes this penalty was not usually collected.

**3. Sport fishing value by jurisdiction (Table: Values Placed by States and Canadian Provinces on Sport Fishing).**
- Highest reported values: Washington $693,000,000 (1955, University of Washington commercial fishing study); Texas $285,000,000 (1960, U.S. Bureau of Census survey); New York $186,000,000 (1955, Crossley Survey, plus 10% increment); Ohio $85,000,000 (1960 National Survey).
- Other stated values include Illinois $92,250,000 (survey 1960), Kentucky $83,000,000, Arkansas $77,000,000, Colorado $60,000,000, Indiana $59,000,000, Pennsylvania $57,000,000, Georgia and Oklahoma $50,000,000 each, and Alberta $15,000,000 (Canada).
- Many states reported "None Given," including California, Florida, Michigan, Minnesota, Wisconsin and most Canadian provinces.
- Tennessee's 1962 survey: 362,000 fishermen caught 49,000,000 fish and spent $30,000,000 (the text also lists $80,000,000 in the surveyed text, but the table value is $30,000,000).

**4. Standard price schedules.** Prices vary by species, size class and jurisdiction. Examples:
- **Rainbow trout:** average cost per fish reported as the basis for many states; size-class schedule ranging from $.15 (1 to 3 inches) to $5 per pound (18 inches and over, as printed).
- **Largemouth bass:** size-class schedule from $.28 (1 to 2 inches) to $4.75 per fish (14 inches and over).
- **Smallmouth bass:** average cost per fish $1.60; average cost per pound $5.62.
- **Channel catfish:** size schedule from $.51 to $.84 per fish across size classes (as printed).
- **Muskellunge:** highest cost per fish $25.00 (Ohio special list); average $15.00.
- **Illinois Standard Price List (1962-1963):** game fish such as bass ($0.40 for 1 to 6 inches; $2.69 for 11 inches plus); carp at $0.05 per pound; buffalo $0.10 per pound; catfish $0.25 per pound; sturgeon $0.21 per pound; eel $0.18 per pound; mooneye $0.03 per pound.
- **Pennsylvania Fish Commission Approved Price List (1961):** e.g., brook, brown and rainbow trout $0.15 (1 to 3 inches) to $3.75 (16 to 18 inches), with trout over 18 inches at $1.75 per pound; walleye $0.50 (under 6 inches) to $2.00 (15 inches and up); black bass $0.16 (1 to 2 inches) to $4.75 (over 14 inches); minnows $0.20 per 100 for 1 to 2 inches and $0.30 per 100 for over 2 inches (printed as $20.40 and $30.00 per M).
- **Crayfish (Ohio):** $1.00 per dozen.
- **Bait minnows (various states):** per pound $0.50 to $5.00; per fish $0.02 to $0.03.

**5. Replacement cost methods.** Several jurisdictions valued losses by the cost of replacing fish through hatchery stocking or commercial purchase:
- Florida (Virginia-Carolina Chemical Company, Alafia River) estimated replacement at $92,400 for all fish killed, $104,800 for fingerling stocking, $286,000 for average fingerling restocking and $52,800 for breeder-size restocking (with 20% added for mortality).
- Indiana calculated replacement through commercial sources and added up to two years of lost stream use; a 1961 example valued a 7/10-mile stream portion at $4,500.00.
- Virginia restocked waters in settlements (e.g., 20,000 young-of-the-year smallmouth bass to satisfy a $14,000 settlement with American Cyanamid Company).

**6. Commercial and anadromous fishery valuation.** Washington's Bremerton case (1958) valued losses using fish-by-fish returns to commercial fishermen, spawner value, and replacement cost of yearlings and fry; the claim was $10,840.00, settled for $3,250 ($2,000 from insurer plus $1,250 Game Department). Oregon reported a commercial fishery value of $7,622,000 (five-year average) and sport fishing expenditure of approximately $16,815,000.

**7. Legal framework highlights.**
- **Wisconsin Statute 29.65** set minimum civil damages per fish: $10.00 (muskellunge, rock or lake sturgeon); $5.00 (large or small-mouthed black bass); $3.00 (brook, rainbow, brown, steelhead, Loch Leven or grayling trout); $2.00 (walleye and other game fish).
- **North Carolina (Chapter 1087, Laws 1963, Senate Bill No. 409)** authorized the State Stream Sanitation Committee to recover damages equal to replacement cost plus investigation costs, with proceeds used to replace fish or improve habitat.
- **New Jersey** pollution statutes set penalties of $500 for a first offense and $1,000 for subsequent offenses (dyestuffs, tar, and similar substances).
- **Illinois** Sanitary Water Board may impose a penalty of $100 per day for failure to abate pollution.
- **Missouri Section 252.210 (RSMo 1949)** made it unlawful to place deleterious substances in waters in quantities sufficient to injure or kill fish.
- **British Columbia (Fisheries Act, 1932, Section 33)** prohibited harmful substances and sawdust in fish-bearing waters; fines were noted as "distressingly low" (e.g., $20 maximum in one 1959 case).
- **Pennsylvania** prosecutions in 1959 to 1961 (184 cases with voluntary contributions, 55 prosecutions, 105 cases with insufficient evidence) reported an average of 72% of cases successfully concluded over the three-year period; 95.6% of prosecutions resulted in convictions.

**8. Key procedural and policy observations.**
- Colorado noted that its success is limited to "flash disasters" where fish are killed outright and the cause is immediately pinpointed; lingering pollution that destroys bottom fauna causes more damage but rarely yields cash settlements.
- Several states (Arkansas, Nevada, Missouri) emphasized correction of pollution sources over monetary recovery.
- Illinois' 1963 directive stated that a biologist's primary responsibility in a pollution kill is to prepare a monetary estimate of fish destroyed, and that biologists should not investigate pollution sources or take water samples.
- Tennessee's case files and forms (Fish Kill Investigation Form #1 and Water Sample Data Form #2) document extent of kill, species counts, pH, dissolved oxygen, alkalinity, and possible pollution sources.
- Mississippi noted that most major kills were caused by low oxygen from untreated domestic sewage, and that the state had no authority over municipalities.

**9. Survey reliability notes.** The compiler flagged several figures as rough or uncertain: the Missouri sport fishing value was described as "the roughest kind of estimate"; Michigan declined to produce a value; Virginia said its licence figure (about 400,000 licences per year) was not a value estimate; and the Tennessee sport fishing value differs between the narrative ($80,000,000) and the summary table ($30,000,000).
