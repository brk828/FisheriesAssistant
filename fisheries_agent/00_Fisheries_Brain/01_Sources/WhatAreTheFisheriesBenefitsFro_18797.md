# Fisheries Benefits from Current Stocking Practices in Wyoming Rivers, Streams and Tailwaters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This policy paper by Area Fishery Supervisor Mark Fowden of the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]] (WGFD) asks what fisheries benefits current [[Trout Stocking]] practices in streams and tailwaters actually deliver, and at what cost. The author argues that the sole benefit of stocking is good angling, and that the WGFD has rarely applied [[Benefit-to-Cost Analysis]] to justify it. The paper promotes [[Wild Trout Management]] as the default for streams, limits stocking to waters where wild populations cannot sustain fishing, and describes how stocking is evaluated and restructured in the [[Fontenelle Tailwater]] and the [[Green River Management Area]].

## Background & Description

A sport fishery is described as fish plus the anglers who pursue them, so the benefit of stocking is framed as angler opportunity. The WGFD has used hatchery trout to replace wild stocks lost to disaster, to restock waters after [[Chemical Rehabilitation]], and to establish populations where none existed. The paper states the program objective as "good fishing" but notes that stocking investments have rarely been tested against defined return criteria.

A review of Wyoming trout standing stocks (Wiley, 1992), cited alongside [[Platts and McHenry]] (1988), concluded that natural production cannot be artificially raised by stocking. Trout occupy habitat up to its carrying capacity, at least without human disturbance such as harvest. The corollary is that stocking may be a viable option where natural recruitment is limited, for example by lack of spawning habitat, or where angling pressure is extreme.

The paper also states that 15% of all trout planted in Wyoming are stocked in streams and tailwaters, even though WGFD Fish Division staff voiced a preference for wild management in November 1992.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream management premise.** The author argues that wild trout fisheries should be the default. Wild trout are valued for the diversity of habitats and population adaptations they represent, and for their lower management cost, since stocked fisheries often need tackle restrictions and reduced creel limits. Endemic populations with high genetic purity should also be managed as wild. Only the native [[Cutthroat Trout]] excepted, most wild fisheries were originally stocked, but their persistence shows local adaptation.

**Policy shift.** A December 1992 WGFD guideline states that all Wyoming streams able to support wild trout will be managed as wild fisheries. Only three percent of suitable streams are stocked. Of 4,694 flowing waters suitable for trout, only seven are tailwaters.

**Tailwater limits.** Tailwaters are artificial habitats that offer little spawning habitat, lack spawning tributaries, and have impoundment-driven spring and early summer flows that seldom support mainstem spawning. Limited natural recruitment, unnaturally high productivity, and high angling pressure make these waters suitable for stocking. [[Fall-Spawning Brown Trout]] have sustained wild populations in some tailwaters, while rainbow and cutthroat trout are stocked for catch rate and diversity.

**Habitat as a constraint.** Siltation has reduced survival and recruitment in the [[Big Horn River]] near Thermopolis and in the [[North Platte River]] Grey Reef tailwater. Stocking failures are frequently attributed to habitat constraints rather than to poor hatchery stock.

## Key Findings

**1. Stocking volumes and angling value**
- Tailwater fisheries generate 148,500 angling days a year.
- In 1994, about 330,000 fingerling or subcatchable and 13,000 catchable trout were planned for the seven tailwaters.

**2. Fontenelle Tailwater case study**
- From 1970 to 1977, the creel return ranged from 2 to 8 fish per 100 stocked. Larger stocked fish returned at higher rates. The average return by weight was 208%.
- In 1985, [[Fontenelle Dam]] nearly failed, requiring high discharges that threatened the fishery and habitat. The fishery was re-evaluated after repairs.
- In 1992, 208,936 trout (Eagle Lake rainbow, Snake River cutthroat, brown and Bear River cutthroat) averaging 5 inches were stocked. The creel survey covered 77 miles over seven months from mid-April.
- 38,817 trout were caught, giving returns of 18.6% by number and 237% by weight. Anglers harvested 9,129 trout (4.4% by number). Harvest fell within the 1970–1977 range.
- Stocking cost $66,664.58. Production cost was $5.29 per pound (inflated by capital expenses), or about $1.90 per fish. The 29,889 pounds caught, valued at $5.29 per pound, equal $158,113.
- The program supported 32,433 angling days, and without stocking the tailwater would provide under 10% of those days. Catch rate was 0.69 fish per hour.

**3. Winter mortality and restructuring**
- Across eight paired fall–spring population estimates since 1980, about 90% of stocked and wild trout under 14 inches die during winter.
- Spring stocking rates exceed winter carrying capacity by an order of magnitude. Anglers now return 75% of their catch, so a summer–fall surplus is no longer justified.
- Proposed 1994 stocking (835 trout/mile under 14 inches, 19 lb/mile, 0.5 lb/acre) is far lower than past rates (3,200 trout/mile, 155 lb/mile, 3.9 lb/acre). Estimated carrying capacity is 87 trout/mile, 16 lb/mile, and 0.4 lb/acre.
- Evaluation objectives are to maintain catch rates of 0.7 trout/hour, maintain spring population and biomass estimates, and increase the proportion of wild brown trout in the upper third of the river.
- Other recommended actions include negotiating winter flows, developing a rainbow strain adapted to the tailwater at [[Flume Creek Hatchery]], continuing side-channel restoration, and maintaining trophy regulations in the upper third.

**4. Strain development and accountability**
- Two new broodstocks, [[River Strain Rainbow Trout]] and [[Snake River Cutthroat Trout]], were developed from wild sources in the last four years. Evaluation has only just begun.
- The paper notes that managers lack definitive criteria for success across economic, recreational, and biological perspectives.

**5. Put-and-take stocking and the Green River Management Area**
- Catchable put-and-take stocking (rainbow, Snake River, and Yellowstone cutthroat) must return 50% of stocked trout (9 inches) and is restricted to waters near urban, tourist, or public-use areas where other management is impossible.
- Just over 750 stream miles (753.1) are suitable for trout in the Green River area. About 10.4% (78.1 miles) were stocked in 1993.
- In 1994, 123,950 catchable trout were planned. Reducing the creel limit from 6 to 3 fish per day is presented as preferable to eliminating opportunity.
- Catchable stocking was removed from 32 miles of the [[Greys River]] in 1990. It is now wild Snake River cutthroat water with an 11–16 inch slot limit and a two-fish creel limit. The lower 14 miles, with degraded habitat, still receive 10,000 catchables.
- Brook trout stocked in beaver ponds (13 miles) cause little conflict. Natural reproduction in two ponds led to a 500-fish reduction. Catch rates exceeded 1.0 fish per hour with average creel size of 10 inches or greater.
- Natural recruitment on 8 miles of the Hams Fork was sufficient, so stocking will end in 1995. Brown trout (3,500 at 3.5 inches) stocked into privately controlled [[Blacks Fork]] water provide 10 miles of public access. The benefit is unquantified, but landowners report better fishing and anglers now ask permission.

**6. Unique species and special waters**
- Colorado River cutthroat trout were stocked to increase genetic purity in four populations. Overwinter survival reached up to 40% after habitat structures raised carrying capacity. These streams are catch-and-release only.
- A five-mile wild stream was restocked with cutthroat for access. Cutthroat now make up nearly 50% of the trout population, because the landowner permits only rainbow harvest.

**7. Conclusions**
- The WGFD must balance short-term angler recreation against long-term habitat protection. Angler support from stocked fisheries sustains funding for habitat work.
- Angler expectations can be inflated by stocking programs with poorly defined objectives, and support erodes when fishing is poor.
