# Tilapia aurea, A Rapidly Spreading Exotic in South Central Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document contains three components: a reference list of roughly 33 freshwater fisheries and biology publications, a full research paper on the rapid spread of [[Tilapia Aurea]] in south central Florida, and an abstract for a study of the 1966 year class of [[Striped Bass]] in three Virginia rivers. The central work reports that tilapia, introduced by the [[Florida Game And Fresh Water Fish Commission]] in 1961, had spread to several rivers, creeks and lakes by 1968. Its densest population was in [[Lake Parker]], a 2,291-acre eutrophic lake, where the species congregated over muck substrata and warm power-plant discharge, competed with [[Centrarchids]] for spawning space, and proved to have low catchability.

## Background & Description

**Tilapia aurea** was obtained from [[Auburn University]] in August 1961 (3,000 individuals) and stocked in experimental phosphate pits at the [[Pleasant Grove Research Station]]. Publicity presented the species as highly desirable, and private citizens and anglers subsequently stocked private ponds and public lakes. After the experiments, the Commission judged the species undesirable because of low catchability and strong resistance to chemical control.

**Striped bass** (*Morone saxatilis*) age composition was estimated from scale impressions. The study compared stocks in the [[James River]], [[York River]] and [[Rappahannock River]] between June 1967 and March 1968.

The Florida paper's sampling toolkit included:
- [[Electrofishing]] from a boat with a 230-volt portable generator and copper cable electrodes
- [[Trammel Net]] sets (3-inch inner and 12-inch outer stretch mesh, 125 yards long)
- A 20-foot minnow seine, with a 20 x 4-foot seine described in the methods
- A [[Creel Survey]] on Lake Parker
- Bottom cores using a 6-foot clear plastic tube
- [[Length-Weight Relationship]] comparisons with Alabama pond-raised fish

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference.** All waters where tilapia were collected, except [[Lake Morton]], were eutrophic. Tilapia preferred enriched waters and muck bottoms. Muck deposits ranged from 4 inches to more than 6 feet deep. Sandy substrata were only sparsely populated during winter.

**Thermal aggregation.** In January and February, tilapia heavily congregated in the outflow of an electric power plant on Lake Parker. Water temperatures there were 4° to 9° F higher than background lake temperatures, and this was considered the main congregating factor. Table 1 shows tilapia made up 19.88% to 63.19% of total weight at winter muck stations, with [[Centrarchids]] at 2.09% to 26.22%.

**Competition for spawning ground.** As water warmed, males separated from schools and established territories, while females dispersed and returned to shore to spawn. In one and a half hours of shocking, 185 tilapia were taken, 180 of them males. Tilapia, [[Bluegill]] and [[Redear Sunfish]] were collected from the same spawning area, suggesting competition for spawning space.

**Feeding ecology.** Earlier work by McBay (1961) indicated that 3- to 5-inch tilapia consumed insects and 6- to 9-inch fish consumed algae. Young tilapia competed with young centrarchids for invertebrate food. Sightings of large specimens by sport anglers were rare, consistent with a herbivorous adult diet.

**Dispersal pathways.** Tilapia were recorded in the Alafia, Peace and other rivers, several creeks, and at least six lakes, with private ponds numbering 10 or more. Lakes connected by boat canals were judged likely to be invaded over time. The region had unusually heavy rains in May, June and July, which may have aided spread.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Invasion Status
- Tilapia spread from the Pleasant Grove Research Station to 2 rivers plus 1 additional river, 4 creeks, several lakes, and private ponds (10+).
- Lake Parker was the most intensively sampled site, and the Peace River was sampled in its northern (more enriched) and southern parts. Tilapia were found only in the northern section.
- Tilapia congregated around cattle watering sites where organic debris from feces had accumulated.

### Lake Morton Eradication Attempt
- Lake Morton, within the City of Lakeland, was heavily fed with cracked corn for waterfowl, which the authors considered a likely food source for tilapia.
- The lake was renovated in October 1966. 146 pounds of tilapia were collected, comprising 20% of the weight of an acre block-net sample.
- No tilapia were collected after renovation, but reinvasion was judged certain because adjacent lakes held the species.
- The authors concluded that [[Largemouth Bass]] predation, suggested earlier by Crittenden (1962), did not prevent tilapia from becoming a nuisance.

### Catch Data and Angler Attitudes (Lake Parker, May–July 1968)
- The creel covered 14 randomly selected days over 2 months: 10 weekdays, 2 Saturdays and 2 Sundays, surveyed 4:00–6:00 p.m.
- Daily averages were 0.74 tilapia per hour for "tilapia fishermen" versus 2.49 for other species per hour.
- Tilapia comprised a daily average of 16.95% of the total catch. Early-May values were high (51.00% on 5/19) and later values were low (0.40–1.96% on several days).
- Of 366 anglers interviewed, 159 (43%) had tasted tilapia, and about 64% considered it a desirable food fish. Most dissatisfied anglers complained of soft flesh.

### Growth and Size
- Florida tilapia averaged higher empirical weights than Alabama pond-raised fish in most length groups (Table 5). Examples: 14-inch fish weighed 2.27 lb in Florida versus 1.90 lb in Alabama, and 4-inch fish weighed 0.07 lb versus 0.04 lb.
- A sharp weight reduction in the 12- and 13-inch groups was tentatively linked to spawning activity.
- The largest individual recorded was 7 pounds at 19 inches.
- Favorable habitat, climate (no winter kills expected) and eutrophic conditions were cited for larger Florida sizes.

### Trammel Net Harvest
- A single 125-yard trammel net caught 266 pounds of tilapia, comprising 84% of the sample by number and weight.

### Management Conclusions
- Tilapia had become the fastest-spreading exotic in Florida since introduction seven years earlier.
- Low catchability meant the species could not easily be controlled by harvest, and it competed with centrarchids for space, spawning areas and food.
- The authors warned that control of both experimental introductions and news-media promotion of exotics was needed. They judged that, if tilapia caused problems like those of the introduced carp (Hubbs, 1968), control with known methods would already be too late.

### Striped Bass Year Class (Virginia)
- Age composition from scale impressions for June 1967–March 1968 showed a relative deficiency of the 1966 year class in the James River.
- The deficiency appeared in samples from non-selective gear (pound nets, fyke nets), selective gear (gill nets, haul seines, hook-and-line), and routine 30-foot semi-balloon trawl surveys.
- The full paper was to be presented to the Southern Division of the American Fisheries Society (Baltimore, October 21–23, 1968). The project was partly funded under the Anadromous Fish Act (P.L. 89-304).
