# Hybrid Nocomis micropogon × Rhinichthys cataractae: Status, Distribution and Hybridization as a Mode of Speciation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study re-examines a cyprinid hybrid, *Nocomis micropogon* × *Rhinichthys cataractae*, first described as a species (*Rhinichthys bowersi*) in 1908 and later reinterpreted as a hybrid in 1940. The authors confirm its hybrid origin, map its distribution in the upper [[Monongahela River System]], and compare its morphometric and meristic traits with those of its parents using a [[Hybrid Index]]. Although the hybrid is unquestionably of hybrid origin, the authors argue it meets the criteria of a morphological species: a definite range, consistent characters and success in nature. They conclude that hybridization may be a viable, though unproven, mode of [[Sympatric Speciation]] in fishes and recommend protecting the population until its taxonomic status is resolved.

## Background & Description

The hybrid was originally described by Goldsborough and Clark (1908) as *Rhinichthys bowersi* from Dry Fork and Shavers Fork of the [[Cheat River]], West Virginia. Raney (1940) reidentified four specimens collected below Cheat Bridge in 1935 as a hybrid of [[Nocomis Micropogon]] and [[Rhinichthys Cataractae]]. Fifteen additional specimens were collected from Shavers Fork in 1975–1976 by the West Virginia Department of Natural Resources, prompting the present study.

The study had four objectives:
- Verify the hybrid's systematic status.
- Determine its range and relative abundance in the upper Monongahela River system.
- Provide a morphometric, meristic and descriptive analysis.
- Evaluate hybridization as a possible mode of sympatric speciation.

**Methods.** Fishes were collected by seine and electroshocker, preserved in 10% formalin, and stored in 40% isopropanol at the Appalachian Environmental Laboratory Fish Museum. Fifty specimens each of the two parent species were compared with 42 hybrids collected before 1977. Measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm with dial calipers. The hybrid index followed Hubbs, Hubbs and Johnson (1943), where a value of 50 indicates exact intermediacy, values above 50 indicate closer affinity to *N. micropogon*, and values below 50 indicate closer affinity to *R. cataractae*. Dentition was examined in six hybrid specimens.

**Morphology.** The hybrid is described as an intermediate mixture of parental traits. Key features include:
- Large scales; body greenish-brown dorsally, white ventrally.
- Almost black dorsal head, with microtubules between the eyes and nostrils.
- Barbels at the rictus and a yellow upper lip.
- Melanophores on the pectoral fin leading edge and anterior rays.
- A faint caudal peduncle spot.
- Some smaller individuals showed fluorescent irridophores at the occiput-opercle junction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Before 1975, 14 hybrid specimens were known from nine collections. Apart from two Lake Erie drainage collections (1939 and an Ohio record), all came from the Monongahela River system. A single hybrid from the Greenbrier River was interpreted as a [[Nocomis Platyrhynchus]] × *R. cataractae* cross, because *N. platyrhynchus* is endemic to the New River drainage and *N. micropogon* is absent there. Hybrids were also recorded from the Tygart Valley River and the Youghiogheny River, and three from Snowy Creek in 1977.

**Abundance.** In 1976 the authors collected 22 hybrid specimens from upper Shavers Fork. The hybrid occurred at all stations above a 3-m falls except one. Hybrids represented 5.1% of all *R. cataractae* and 6.8% of all *N. micropogon* collected above the falls.

**Sympatry.** Both parents co-occur across the Appalachian region, including the James, York, Rappahannock, Potomac, Kanawha, Big Sandy and Tennessee rivers, as well as the Susquehanna, Delaware and western Chesapeake Bay drainages.

**Possible triggers.** The authors tested several hypotheses for hybridization:
- *Crowding of spawning fishes* does not appear to apply on Shavers Fork.
- *Rare-abundant cohabitation or exotic introduction* is unlikely, since both parents are native and equally abundant.
- *Environmental stress* is considered plausible, because timbering and mining degraded Shavers Fork water quality in the late 1800s, though water quality is now generally good.

**Size and age.** Hybrids from upper Cheat River ranged from 40 to 125 mm standard length and represented four age classes.

## Key Findings

- **Hybrid origin confirmed.** Morphometric and meristic data clearly place the specimens between the two parental species. The overall hybrid index of 50 supports this.
- **Character profile.** Of 16 characters (12 morphometric and four meristic), the hybrid index was intermediate (35–65) for six, closer to *R. cataractae* for three, and closer to *N. micropogon* for two. Five characters were "extreme," falling outside the parental means.
- **Dentition.** Five hybrids showed 1, 4-4, 1 dentition and one showed 0, 4-4, 1. The parents are typically 0, 4-4, 0 (*N. micropogon*) and 2, 4-4, 2 (*R. cataractae*).
- **Scale counts.** Scale size is intermediate, with little or no overlap between the hybrid and parental ranges for lateral line, above-lateral-line and below-lateral-line scales.
- **Frenum.** When present, the hybrid's frenum is typically narrower than in *R. cataractae*, another intermediate trait.
- **Consistency.** Hybrid characters are as constant as those of the putative parents, meeting a key criterion for a morphological species.
- **Reproduction.** Dissected females had small gonads with eggs about the size of those in co-collected *R. cataractae* females, and males had testes. The authors could not demonstrate genetically consistent reproduction.
- **Historical duration.** The hybrid has been recorded continuously from 1899 to the present, and several generations appear to exist.
- **Speciation argument.** The authors suggest that if hybrids are fertile and ecologically segregated from parents, they could become reproductively isolated, so hybridization may be a viable mode of speciation in isolated circumstances. They stress this does not replace [[Natural Selection]] as the dominant mode.
- **Taxonomic status unresolved.** The authors state that the population's exact status is uncertain and cannot be determined without further evidence.
- **Recommendation.** Protect the population in the upper Cheat Basin until its taxonomic status is confirmed through [[Karyotyping]], electrophoresis and artificial cross-fertilization of the parental species.
