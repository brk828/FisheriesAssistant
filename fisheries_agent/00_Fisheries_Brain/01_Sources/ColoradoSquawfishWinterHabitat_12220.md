# Colorado Squawfish Winter Habitat Study, Yampa River, Colorado, 1986-1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by Edmund J. Wick and John A. Hawkins (Colorado State University, Larval Fish Laboratory, February 1989) documents two winters of radiotelemetry on adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Yampa River]], Colorado. The work was commissioned under Section 7 consultation of the [[Endangered Species Act]] for the proposed [[Stagecoach Reservoir]] and aimed to supply winter habitat data for flow recommendations. Fish showed strong fidelity to small overwintering areas, moved little under ice cover, and selected low-velocity off-channel habitats. Ice cover itself shaped usable habitat, so the authors recommend steady mid-winter flows and protection of flows during initial freeze-up and [[Ice-Out]].

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]], a large minnow (Cyprinidae) and the top predatory fish of the native upper Colorado River fauna. It was once widespread in the basin but is now restricted to free-flowing reaches above [[Lake Powell]], including the Yampa, Green, Gunnison, White, San Juan and lower mainstem Colorado.
- **Endemism:** Of 13 native fish species above Lake Powell, six are endemic, including the squawfish, three chubs, and the razorback and flannelmouth suckers (Behnke and Benson 1983).
- **Life history context:** Adults migrate hundreds of miles to spawning areas, with spawning in [[Yampa Canyon]] at an average of 22°C (range 15-27.5°C). Declines are attributed to dams blocking migration and cold tailwater releases.
- **Objectives:** (1) determine whether adults remain in specific reaches from fall to early spring; (2) define habitat and microhabitat use (depth, velocity, substrate, temperature, cover); (3) develop habitat utilization curves; (4) evaluate [[PHABSIM]] for winter flow requirements; (5) track spring 1988 movements and spawning migration timing.
- **Co-study:** Northern pike ([[Northern Pike]], *Esox lucius*) were radiotagged in cooperation with the Colorado Division of Wildlife to compare habitat overlap.

**Methods overview**
- 10 squawfish were radiotagged in Winter 1 (1986-1987) and 10 more in Winter 2 (1987-1988), plus a fall 1987 cohort; a total of 20 fish were implanted during the fall-based study.
- Tags were implanted surgically after anesthesia with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222). Tag frequencies were 40.6-40.7 MHz.
- Bi-weekly tracking trips ran from fall through spring (15 winter trips; 21 trips in the full 1987-1988 season).
- Habitat was measured at fish locations, through ice holes where necessary, including effective depth (ice-free water), total depth, velocity at 0.6 depth, and substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas (Yampa River, RMI 51-105)**
- **[[Government Bridge Study Area]] (RMI 95-100):** Dominated by man-made rock jetties and a large 50 x 100 m backwater at RMI 95.7, formed by 1984 floods. Long slow runs and a deep eddy-pool at RMI 99.
- **[[Maybell Study Area]] (RMI 70-82):** Less altered, with a vegetated embayment at RMI 81.1, a 13-14 ft pool at RMI 80.8, and several large pools; a diverse mix of habitat.
- **[[Lily Park Study Area]] (RMI 51-54):** The richest pool and eddy habitat per mile on the upper Yampa, with 8-15 ft pools; no backwater and little embayment in winter. Added in Winter 2.

**Hydrology and ice**
- Flows are naturally stable in mid-winter, with the river largely ice-covered from mid-December to early March. Winter 1 flows and air temperatures were above normal; Winter 2 was a relatively low-water year.
- Ice formation begins on low-velocity habitats (November 11, 1986 and November 17, 1987). Frazil ice can pack beneath solid ice and reduce effective depth in pools and runs.
- Dissolved oxygen was 17 ppm in the RMI 81.1 embayment against 12 ppm saturation, suggesting primary productivity in embayments.

**Habitat classes** (Appendix A): main channel, side channel, backwater, embayment, run, eddy, riffle, pool, and shoreline. Backwaters and embayments freeze first and provide the lowest velocities.

## Key Findings

**Movement and fidelity**
- Of 20 fall-implanted fish, 3 moved downstream more than 4 miles or out of the study area after implantation; 14 remained within their tagging study areas.
- The farthest-moving fish (A09) went 15 miles downstream within about 3.5 weeks after tagging. Disorientation from surgery or anesthesia was considered a possible cause.
- Under ice, the mean movement was 0.3 mile per year (0.0-1.1 mile in Winter 1; 0.0-0.5 mile in Winter 2).
- Fall 1987 movement averaged 0.9 mile (0.0-4.4 miles) for 11 fish.
- Spring 1988 movement averaged 6.5 miles (0.0-43.2 miles); excluding fish A00 (43.2 miles, 24.6 miles in April 19-21), the mean for the other 10 fish was 2.9 miles.
- Downstream movements of 17 fish averaged 0.8 mile through the two ice-off periods.
- Fish moved an average of 65 miles one-way to [[Yampa Canyon]] spawning areas (range 34.5-84.5 miles).
- Fish C87 was recaptured and relocated 0.1 mile from its original capture site, 7 years after tagging, showing exceptional homing.

**Habitat use**
- Winter 1: backwater, embayment and run habitats were most used; backwater use was almost exclusively at RMI 95.7 (three fish).
- Winter 2: pool and run habitats dominated, largely because of the added Lily Park reach.
- Fall 1987: pool was predominant, followed by run, shoreline and eddy.
- Spring 1988: eddy predominated, followed by backwater and shoreline; embayments were flooded out.
- Fish in off-channel habitats are believed to gain low-velocity energy conservation, concentrated food, easier home-range cues, and protection from frazil ice.

**Depth and velocity (winter means)**
- Backwater and embayment (BA EM): mean effective depth 2.0 ft (range 0.5-3.3), total depth 3.2 ft; velocity 0.1 ft/s.
- Run and shoreline (RU SH): mean effective depth 2.4 ft, total depth 3.9 ft; velocity 0.5 ft/s (range 0.1-1.0).
- Eddy and pool (ED PO): mean effective depth 3.3 ft, total depth 5.7 ft; velocity 0.2 ft/s.
- Preferred run velocities were about 0.2-1.0 ft/s.

**Substrate**
- Of 36 possible combinations of clay, silt, sand, gravel, cobble, boulder and bedrock, 21 were observed. Sand was the most common substrate in both winters.

**Northern pike**
- Seven pike were radiotagged; four were encountered in squawfish areas. Pike and squawfish used similar habitat but were not observed within 5 m of each other; squawfish favored shallower gravel inside areas, pike deeper silt-sand areas.

**Stage-discharge and ice effects (Winter 2, RMI 81.1)**
- Discharge rose from 142 cfs (Dec 15, 1987) to 340 cfs (Mar 2, 1988). Ice thickness rose from 0.85 ft to 2.12 ft.
- Rising water surface kept effective depth in the embayment nearly constant, varying only 0.1 ft from Dec 15 to Feb 17.
- Regression predicts about 0.2 ft stage loss per 20 cfs reduction in discharge; reducing flow from 142 to 75 cfs would lower stage about 0.7 ft.
- Ice-out can reduce stage at a fixed flow: a 10 cfs decrease produced a 0.4 ft drop in March.

**PHABSIM applicability**
- The authors conclude PHABSIM was not appropriate. Backwaters and embayments were not captured by standard transects, and ice invalidates standard assumptions. WUA may be maximized at very low flows if off-channel habitat is not weighted.

**USGS winter flow records**
- Ice-affected gage readings were adjusted by USGS, and the record was considered less reliable for winter stage-discharge work. Gage checks at least twice monthly are recommended for ice-affected periods.

**Recommendations**
- Avoid reductions below natural baseflow at initial ice formation (late November to early December), so that ice covers the maximum usable habitat.
- Maintain steady mid-winter discharge with little fluctuation; flows normally vary no more than 140 cfs from the annual mean from mid-December through February.
- Maintain 200-300 cfs in mid-winter in Winter 2 conditions to preserve embayments; lower flows reduce embayment depth and higher flows flood them.
- Avoid any flow reduction until ice-out is complete, since March ice-out is the most critical period.
- Evaluate winter flow alterations by ice stage, air temperature and discharge, rather than by a single rule.
