# The Fishes of the Upper Guadalupe River, Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 survey by [[Clark Hubbs]], [[Robert A. Kuehne]] and [[Jack C. Ball]] (University of Texas and Texas Game and Fish Commission) is the first in a proposed series on Texas stream systems. It documents the fish fauna of the upper [[Guadalupe River]] and its tributaries, west of the Cotton Mill Dam at New Braunfels, Comal County. The authors list about 50 taxa, record their distributions and abundance, and recognize four fish associations shaped by spring inputs, water clarity and downstream dispersal. A notable finding is a hybrid swarm between [[Notropis venustus]] and [[Notropis lutrensis]] linked to a 1930s gravel-company discharge that turned clear water turbid.

## Background & Description

Few earlier collection records existed for the survey area, most dating before 1900 and mainly from New Braunfels collections (including [[Jordan and Gilbert]] 1886 and [[Evermann]] 1893). The new survey drew on several sources:

- Twelve gill net and 63 seining stations in Kerr County; six gill net and 13 seining stations in Kendall County; twelve seining and nine rotenone stations in Comal County (the rotenone work was in 1951).
- A 1939 Kerr County survey by Kelshaw Bonham, most of whose specimens are no longer extant.
- A 1938 collection at Hunt, Kerr County, by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and party.
- A reconnaissance survey by Frank T. Knapp (personal communication, 1950).

Most collections were made in summer and fall 1951. Day seining dominated, so easily seined species may appear more abundant than they are.

The river flows through Cretaceous limestones of the [[Edwards Plateau]], crosses the [[Balcones Fault Zone]] near New Braunfels, and then enters Upper Cretaceous marls at the edge of the coastal prairie. Its bottom is mostly bedrock in the upper reaches and gravel-silt in the lower reaches. Shoreline vegetation ranges from grasses and poplars to cypress, and submerged plants include Potamogeton, Myriophyllum, Sagittaria and Chara.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water clarity:** The upper river and most spring-fed tributaries are clear, with bottom visible at two feet or more. A gravel company, starting in the 1930s, discharged wash water about one mile west of Kerrville, creating an abrupt transition to murky water. Overgrazing in the watershed also increased turbidity.
- **Temperature:** Spring sources stay near 69 °F year-round. The Guadalupe reached about 95–97 °F in summer 1951. The Comal River starts at 74 °F but is warmed to at least 80 °F by the [[Comal Power Plant]] condenser units.
- **Spring-dependent fauna:** Some species survive only where springs keep temperatures within their tolerance, such as [[Ambloplites rupestris]] and the introduced [[Rio Grande Cichlid]], which increases after mild winters.
- **Human impacts:** Flood scouring, siltation, pond-making at spring areas, and stocking are all cited as drivers of changes in abundance and possible extirpation.

## Key Findings

**Fish associations.** The survey recognizes three main associations plus a fourth approached at the eastern boundary:

1. **Spring Association:** Restricted to spring sources and Comal Springs. Includes [[Dionda episcopa]], [[Gambusia sp.]], [[Etheostoma fonticola]] and [[Ictalurus punctatus lupus]] (the latter two only at Comal Springs).
2. **Clearwater Association:** Extends from the springs to the gravel-company site. Centrarchids dominate. Typical species include [[Notropis deliciosus]], [[Lepomis auritus]] and [[Lepomis microlophus]].
3. **Murky Water Association:** From the gravel-company site to Cotton Mill Dam. Cyprinids are more abundant here. Species nearly restricted to it include [[Extrarius aestivalis]], [[Notropis volucellus]], [[Pilodictis olivaris]] and [[Percina caprodes]].
4. **Downstream Association:** Upstream limit near New Braunfels. Includes [[Notropis buchanani]], [[Fundulus notatus]] and [[Mollienesia latipinna]].

**Species of note:**
- The survey recorded 22 species at one station four miles below Kerrville on 6 August 1951, the highest single-station richness.
- Only two young spotted gar were obtained, so [[Lepisosteus productus]] is considered rare. [[Lepisosteus osseus leptorhynchus]] was in seven collections but is likely more abundant than recorded.
- [[Dorosoma cepedianum]] occurred in 16 of 17 gill-net stations and four of 90 seining stations.
- [[Notropis amabilis]] (Texas shiner) appeared in 47 collections, and clear-water samples held the most individuals.
- [[Micropterus salmoides salmoides]] was more common in headwaters: 85% of 239 largemouth bass came from headwaters, versus 9% of 78 spotted bass.
- [[Lepomis megalotis aquilensis]] was the most widely collected species.
- Several taxa are interpreted as stocked or introduced: [[Notemigonus crysoleucas]], [[Pomoxis annularis]], [[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]], [[Ambloplites rupestris]] and [[Herichthys cyanoguttatus]].

**Hybridization.** Hybrid frequency between [[Notropis venustus]] and [[Notropis lutrensis]] ranged from 23% to 62% in collections exceeding ten specimens, peaking between Kerrville and the Kerr–Kendall county line. The authors attribute the swarm to the sudden 1930s turbidity from the gravel company's discharge, which persists to the present.

**Relict and possibly declining populations.** A two-specimen [[Erimyzon oblongus claviformis]] record near the South Fork is interpreted as a relict population. Post-1880s changes are thought to have reduced [[Ictalurus punctatus lupus]] and a [[Gambusia]] population at Comal Springs, now both restricted to a few spring localities.

**Taxonomic notes.** Several taxa are described as undescribed subspecies or in need of revision, including [[Hadropterus scierus]], [[Etheostoma spectabile]] and [[Lepomis microlophus]]. [[Etheostoma lepidum]] names have been confused with [[Etheostoma spectabile]] in the literature.

**Future work.** The authors plan to track the hybrid swarms through field and aquarium studies at the University of Texas, and the Texas Game and Fish Commission was expected to subdivide the downstream association.
