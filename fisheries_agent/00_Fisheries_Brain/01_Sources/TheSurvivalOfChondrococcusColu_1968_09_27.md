# The Survival of Chondrococcus columnaris in Waters of Different Quality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-pathology #Cytophagaceae #Myxobacteria #ColumnarisDisease

## Executive Summary

This 1968 experimental study by N. Fijan investigates the environmental persistence of **[[Chondrococcus Columnaris]]**, the causative myxobacterium of **[[Columnaris Disease]]** in fish, across waters of varying chemical quality. Columnaris disease was identified as one of the most economically damaging bacterial fish diseases in the United States during the study period, responsible for 27.5% of bacteriologically examined mortality cases at Auburn University in 1967, and implicated in an ~$8 million loss to sockeye salmon fisheries on the Horsefly River, British Columbia. The study systematically tested bacterial survival across reconstituted deionized waters (varying hardness, pH, organic carbon) and natural pond/river waters, finding that survival was significantly reduced in **soft, acidic water with low organic matter**, while higher hardness and organic carbon content prolonged bacterial viability. The findings offer a plausible ecological explanation for geographic variability in disease occurrence and severity.

## Background & Description

*Note: This paper focuses on bacterial physiology/survival rather than fish morphology. Relevant organism-level details:*

- **Causative agent**: *Chondrococcus columnaris* (Davis), Ordal and Rucker, 1944 — a **myxobacterium** (gliding bacterium), now more commonly referenced in later literature as *Flavobacterium columnare*.
- **Culture source**: Isolated from necrotic skin lesions of a **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*).
- **Identification method**: Confirmed via slide agglutination (serotyping per Anacker & Ordal, 1959).
- **Culture conditions**: Grown in liquid cytophaga medium, incubated 24 hrs at 25°C on a mechanical shaker; harvested via centrifugation and washed in sterile tap water.
- **Cell density for testing**: ~5 × 10⁵ viable cells/mL, quantified via turbidimetry (Coleman Nepho-Colorimeter, green filter).
- **Detection threshold**: Assay sensitivity allowed detection down to ~50 viable cells/mL from an original 500,000 cells/mL inoculum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames *C. columnaris* survival as a function of abiotic water chemistry rather than host biology, situating the pathogen within a **freshwater aquaculture and riverine ecosystem context**.

**Water types tested:**
1. **Reconstituted deionized waters** — three hardness levels (soft, normal, hard) per Lennon & Walker (1964) methodology, with pH buffered to 7.4 in half of samples, and four organic carbon levels (0, 10, 50, 100 p.p.m.) — yielding 24 distinct water conditions.
2. **Natural waters** — six sources:
   - Charcoal-filtered municipal supply water (Auburn, AL)
   - Gravel-filtered fish farm supply water
   - Pond S-1 & S-7: heavily stocked (~2,000 kg/ha), fed catfish ponds
   - Pond S-3 & S-15: lightly stocked (~100 kg/ha), unfed, mixed species (catfish, bluegill, bass, carp, grass carp)
   - Natural water parameters: hardness 7.5–80.0 p.p.m., alkalinity 11–103 p.p.m., pH 7.0–8.2, organic carbon 8.8–21 p.p.m.
3. **pH-gradient reconstituted waters** — normal hardness, no organic matter, pH range 6.0–10.0 in 0.5 increments (phosphate buffer for pH 6–8; borate buffer for pH 8–10).

**Host species associated with columnaris disease (per literature review):**
- **[[Sockeye Salmon]]** (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) — pre-spawning mortality, Horsefly River, British Columbia
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — source of study culture
- **[[Loach]]** (*Misgurnus anguillicaudatus*) — Japan
- **Coarse fish** — Trent area, United Kingdom
- **[[Fundulus Heteroclitus]]** — Northeastern US
- Cold-water fishes of the Pacific Northwest; warm-water fishes of the Southeastern US

**Geographic distribution noted**: United States (Mississippi Valley, West, Northwest, Northeast, Southeast), United Kingdom, Japan, and inferred presence in Yugoslavia based on author's experience.

## Key Findings

- **General persistence**: More than 50 viable cells/mL survived in **all** reconstituted and natural waters for up to **8 days** at 25°C.
- **Soft water vulnerability**: By day 16, viability was negative *only* in **soft water with no organic matter added** — all other reconstituted and natural waters retained viable cells.
- **pH 6.0 is critical**: This pH level had a **profound negative effect** on survival — viable cell counts dropped below 50/mL after just **17 hours** (versus >9 hours of survival at this pH).
- **pH tolerance range**: At all other pH levels, viability remained positive for up to 8 days. By day 16, viability dropped below threshold only at pH 6.5, 9.5, and 10.0 — moderate deviations from neutral were less damaging than extreme acidity.
- **Organic matter effect**: Organic carbon content **prolonged survival**, consistent with a preliminary unpublished experiment showing survival up to 32 days in only 3 of 24 test waters — specifically those with **normal-to-high hardness and 50–100 p.p.m. organic carbon**. In soft water, even high organic matter did not rescue survival.
- **Hardness effect**: Higher water hardness combined with organic matter supported the longest survival times, suggesting mineral content and nutrient load act synergistically to sustain the pathogen environmentally.
- **Ecological/epizootiological implication**: Soft, acidic, nutrient-poor water appears **unfavorable** for *C. columnaris* persistence, potentially explaining regional variation in disease incidence. This parallels findings by **Kimbrell (1963)** linking low water hardness to increased **[[Bacterial Kidney Disease]]** severity in salmonids, though **Fijan notes an inverse relationship direction** compared to columnaris disease (soft water reduces columnaris survival but appears to worsen kidney disease outcomes — an interesting contrast in pathogen-specific water chemistry sensitivity).
- **Contrasting disease reference**: Šulmanović (personal communication) found that soft, acidic water *slowed* progression of chronic **[[Infectious Dropsy]]** in artificially infected carp compared to hard, neutral/alkaline water — reinforcing that water chemistry differentially modulates fish bacterial diseases depending on pathogen identity.
- **Practical implication**: The ability of *C. columnaris* to persist for extended periods in suitable (hard, organically enriched) water likely contributes to the **widespread occurrence of columnaris disease** in U.S. aquaculture and natural systems, whereas soft acidic waters may serve as a natural environmental limiting factor.
