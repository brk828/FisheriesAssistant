# Pelagic Shark Euprotomicrus bispinatus off California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #dalatiidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports a small pelagic squaloid shark, [[Euprotomicrus Bispinatus]], captured at the surface by dip-net under a surface light about 500 nautical miles off California on the night of November 19, 1948. The specimen (California Academy of Sciences No. 20431, a 233 mm female) is only the second known specimen of its genus from the Pacific Ocean. The authors conclude that [[Euprotomicrus Hyalinus]] is a synonym of *E. bispinatus*, that [[Squaliolus Sarmenti]] is a synonym of [[Squaliolus Laticaudus]], and that both genera are monotypic. They also argue that the family [[Dalatiidae]] is unnatural and propose fusing it with [[Squalidae]], with Dalatiinae and Echinorhininae retained provisionally as subfamilies.

## Background & Description

**Identification.** The specimen was identified with *Euprotomicrus* Gill, a genus assigned to the Dalatiidae (Scymnorhinidae). It is conspecific with the unique type of *E. hyalinus* Eigenmann (1893), collected between Hawaii and California and destroyed in the San Francisco fire. The authors regard the new specimen as the only extant Pacific example and probably the only one in an American museum.

**Distribution of the species.** Besides the two northeastern Pacific localities, recorded occurrences of *E. bispinatus* include Mauritius and Bourbon (Réunion) in the Indian Ocean and Campbell Island, south of New Zealand. A New Zealand record was considered erroneous.

**Corrections to earlier descriptions.** The authors found that [[Fowler]]'s (1941) redescription of *E. bispinatus* was based largely on the crude original figure of [[Quoy And Gaimard]] (1824) rather than on the original text. Errors included the pectoral fin extent (less than one-third, not one-half, of the distance to the first dorsal) and the first dorsal origin.

**Morphology of the specimen (selected):**
- Body essentially cylindrical; very slimy, almost uniformly blackish; outer fin edges clear.
- No spines in any fin, either externally or on X-rays. The name *bispinatus* refers to a spiny process on each pelvic fin of the male holotype.
- First dorsal origin midway between caudal tip and middle of eye; base very small (about one-thirtieth of total length).
- Second dorsal base about three times the first dorsal base.
- Caudal fin nearly symmetrical, both lobes greatly expanded, little trace of a subterminal notch.
- Mouth an almost transverse slit with very long lateral grooves.
- Upper teeth in about 4 quincunx rows; lower teeth 11-1+10, strongly bent outward, with one functional series.
- Denticles quadrate with a central pit and lateral roots; occasional larger denticles with additional pits.
- Vertebrae elongate and spindle-shaped, with 48 between head and upper caudal lobe origin and about 22 muscle bands between paired fins.
- Spiracles very large; gill slits very short, with the fifth hardly more than a pore.

**Proportions.** Measurements are given in thousandths of total length (233 mm), with holotype comparisons (192 mm total length). Examples: predorsal length 527 (521 in type); pectoral insertion to dorsal origin 300 (312 in type); greatest body depth 116; second dorsal base 86 (88 in type).

**Coloration and behavior.** The shark appeared brown in water and blackish on deck, a change the authors compare to some myctophids. It swam slowly at the surface with an undulatory movement, showed little activity when handled, and exuded much mucus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe *E. bispinatus* as obviously pelagic. The wide temperature difference among its known occurrence areas suggests a widely ranging species. The specimen was taken at the surface over very deep water far from land. The paper gives no direct measurements of water chemistry, plankton, or other ecosystem variables.

The authors suggest that the slimy skin may possibly be luminous, though no luminous organs were found. The analytical key classes dalatiine genera as either boreal and probably demersal (large, strongly upturned caudal axis) or chiefly warm-water and probably pelagic (small, transverse mouth, large spiracles), placing *Euprotomicrus* in the latter group.

## Key Findings

1. **Specimen record:** A 233 mm female *Euprotomicrus* was caught at the surface nearly 500 nautical miles off California, the second known Pacific specimen of the genus.
2. **Species synonymy:** *E. hyalinus* Eigenmann is synonymized with *E. bispinatus* (Quoy and Gaimard); the holotype comparison by Dr. Léon Bertin supports this view.
3. **Squaliolus synonymy:** *S. sarmenti* de Noronha (Madeira) is synonymized with *S. laticaudus* Smith and Radcliffe (Philippines).
4. **Monotypic genera:** Both *Euprotomicrus* and *Squaliolus* are treated as monotypic. The authors argue that *Squaliolus* is generically distinct, citing eight or more differences, including the presence of a first dorsal spine in *Squaliolus* and its absence in *Euprotomicrus*.
5. **Family revision:** The Dalatiidae is considered unnatural because it is separated from the Squalidae almost solely by dorsal spine presence, which was lost in several lineages. The authors propose synonymizing Dalatiidae and Echinorhinidae with Squalidae, provisionally retaining Dalatiinae and Echinorhininae as subfamilies.
6. **Specialized status:** *Euprotomicrus* is a highly modified terminal product of specialization, shown by its transverse mouth, dimorphic teeth, pitted denticles, expanded upper caudal lobe, and reduced vertebral count.
7. **Phylogeny:** Similar features arose through convergent evolution, so no simple evolutionary tree can be reliably built from current evidence.
8. **Corrected descriptions:** Fowler's (1941) figures and measurements for *E. bispinatus* were in error for pectoral and dorsal positions, and the new specimen and Bertin's holotype measurements were used to correct them.
