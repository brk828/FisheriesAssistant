# Hybridization vs. Intergradation, Lateral-Line Literature, and a New Flatfish from Puerto Rico (Copeia 1961, No. 2)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This OCR-degraded issue of *Copeia* (1961, No. 2) contains three items. The first is a reference list and abbreviation key for a study of cephalic [[Lateral Line System]] canals in Ostariophysi. The second, and main, item is a taxonomic paper on whether [[Notropis Cornutus]] and [[Notropis Chrysocephalus Chrysocephalus]] (Cyprinidae) should be treated as subspecies or full species. It uses [[Hybridization Versus Intergradation]] criteria, [[Scale Count]] distributions, color pattern, and distribution data. The author concludes that the two forms have differentiated to the species level. The third item describes a new bothid flatfish, *Monolene megalepis*, from Puerto Rico, based on [[Oregon Expedition]] trawl collections.

## Background & Description

**Lateral-line reference list (pp. 1, 4–5 of the scan).** The opening pages list about 20 references on cephalic lateral-line canals and related bones in fishes, from authors including Coombs, Kapec, Lekander, Pehrson, and Sanderson. A figure key defines abbreviations for infraorbital, supraorbital, mandibular, preopercular, and temporal canals and their pores.

**Species-subspecies framework.** The paper opens by noting that the definitions of species and subspecies, which Mayr, Linsley, and Usinger (1953) gave as "groups of actually (or potentially) interbreeding natural populations" and "geographically defined aggregates of local populations," do not hold up well in North American freshwater fishes, especially Cyprinidae.

**Study genus.** *Notropis* is young, large, and contains many incipient species. Its cornutus group is the main case study.

**Hybrid vs. intergrade criteria.** Table 1 contrasts hybrids and intergrades on appearance of offspring, ratio of intermediates, frequency among mixed populations, fertility, sex ratio, heterosis, phylogenetic relationship, ecology, and breeding behavior. Hybrids are usually intermediate, with low frequency and partial or complete sterility. Intergrades form graded series with high frequency, full fertility, and usually limited geographic ranges.

**Study material.** Scale-count tables summarize specimens from Illinois, Indiana, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Wisconsin, and Ontario, with catalogue numbers (INHS, IU, OSU, UMMZ) and localities. Figures 1–2 show adult *N. cornutus* and *N. chrysocephalus* and their dorsal pigment patterns. Figure 3 shows ventral head pigmentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sunfish hybrids (Centrarchidae).** Hybrids in the genus *Lepomis* arise in natural crosses that usually result from heterogametic mating in areas with a shortage of spawning habitat (Bailey and Lingen, 1938; Hubbs, 1955). Their morphological and physiological traits agree with the criteria above.

**Cyprinid hybrids.** *N. cornutus* × *N. rubellus* hybrids are among the most common cyprinid crosses in the eastern United States. They form when the two parents spawn together over the same nest sites, whether over the nests of *Hybopsis* or other nest-builders, or over a rubble bottom in different parts of a riffle. An examination of 18 subadult to adult specimens of this cross, taken in May and June in western New York, showed an equal sex ratio (Raney 1940). Hubbs and Brown (1929) reported that their numerous specimens "completely bridge the wide gap in characters" between the parent species.

**Guadalupe River, Texas.** *N. lutrensis* and *N. venustus* occur together across much of the basin and are both abundant in the upper reaches. Hybridization is absent in headwater tributaries, where conditions remain relatively unchanged. Hybrid swarms appear where turbidity has sharply increased. Hybrids first appeared in the 1930s, when a gravel company began dumping wash into the river, and the author treats this as evidence that human disturbance drove the hybridization.

**Glacial history.** The author considers that the two *Notropis* taxa may have been separated during the Pleistocene and reunited after deglaciation. Post-Wisconsinan recolonization of the Great Lakes region could explain present intergradation. Historical changes in the Northern Common Shiner's range after 1800, linked to agricultural clearing and warming of headwater streams, are also discussed.

**Monolene megalepis collection.** The new species was collected in the Gulf of Mexico, West Indies, and western Caribbean on cruises of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service vessel *Oregon*. The holotype came from 43 fathoms west of Arecibo, Puerto Rico, taken with a 40-foot semi-balloon trawl on 7 October 1959. Paratypes came from comparable depths and gear.

## Key Findings

- **Species status of *N. cornutus* and *N. chrysocephalus*:** The author concludes these forms have differentiated to species level and are not subspecies. Five lines of evidence are given:
  1. Intergrading populations should show a smooth "bell-shaped" blending of characters. Some mixed samples do, but many populations show two distinct forms with few intermediates.
  2. The two forms differ in temperature tolerance, and their northern limits reflect this more than expected for subspecies. *N. cornutus* prefers cooler headwaters and *N. chrysocephalus* the warmer lower reaches.
  3. A collection of 349 specimens from Mill Creek, Michigan (June 1959) contained no *N. chrysocephalus*, even though it had been taken there before. Subspecies should keep stable phenotypes across a population.
  4. Competition appears to exclude the two forms from each other's ranges. Their allopatric distributions in Missouri and western Illinois, with isolated relict populations of *N. cornutus* far south, support this.
  5. The distinct distributions and incomplete merging of the two forms suggest a long separation, possibly back to the Late Pliocene.
- **Scale counts:** Of 2,546 *N. cornutus* specimens, 90% had anterior dorso-lateral scale counts of about 48–59 (extremes 42–67). Of 1,023 *N. chrysocephalus*, 90% had counts of 40–44 (extremes 38–48). Mixed series ranged from almost entirely intermediate to almost entirely one parent type.
- **Pigmentation:** *N. cornutus* has a wide, light olive stripe along the back. *N. chrysocephalus* has three zig-zag stripes and usually dark pigment on the chin and anterior gular region, which *N. cornutus* typically lacks. This pigment pattern separates many collections without using scale counts.
- **Hybrid fertility:** Hybrids of *N. cornutus* × *N. rubellus* are often partly or fully fertile despite distant relationship. Hybrids of *N. lutrensis* × *N. venustus* in the laboratory are vigorous and fully fertile, and backcross successfully. The author notes that fertility alone does not prove subspecific status.
- **Spawning and taxonomy:** Similar spawning habits, which earlier workers cited as evidence for subspecies, are not conclusive. *N. spilopterus* and *N. whipplei* share spawning behavior and are clearly separate species.
- **Summary:** The author concludes that the *N. cornutus* and *N. chrysocephalus* complex is "extremely complicated," with evidence favoring a specific relationship. Interactions among other North American cyprinids, *Chrosomus*, and *Carassius*/*Cyprinus* are noted as less clearly resolved.
- **Monolene megalepis:** A new bothid flatfish described from the holotype (CNHM 65746, 43 fathoms) and paratypes (USNM 195859; CNHM 65749, 3 specimens, 68–81.4 mm, 25 September 1959). It is distinguished from three known western Atlantic congeners, and the paper also summarizes geographic and bathymetric ranges of those four species using the Chicago Natural History Museum collection.
