# The 1944 Year Class of Lake Trout in South Bay, Lake Huron

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus-namaycush

## Executive Summary

This study by the [[Ontario Department of Lands and Forests]] and the University of Toronto follows the [[Year Class]] of [[Lake Trout]] hatched in 1944 in [[South Bay]], a 30-square-mile arm of [[Lake Huron]] on the south-east corner of [[Manitoulin Island]]. This cohort supported a significant sport fishery from 1948 to 1950, when lake trout were scarce in the rest of Lake Huron. Tagging showed the South Bay population to be resident. Mark-recapture estimates fell from about 21,000 fish in 1948 to about 600 in 1951, with the steepest losses after 1949. The authors attribute most of the mortality to [[Sea Lamprey]] attack, supported by a correlation between lamprey scarring and population decline.

## Background & Description

Lake trout (*Salvelinus namaycush*) were present in South Bay in numbers when the Ontario Department of Lands and Forests began an experimental fishery there in 1947. About 1,000 specimens, averaging 14.7 inches, were examined that year, mostly taken in gill nets of 4½-inch mesh or larger. The population was mainly one [[Year Class]], that of 1944. Fish reached about 20 inches and 4 pounds at age-group VI.

The Department kept a [[Field Station]] on South Bay from 1947, using [[Pound Net]]s and traps for experimental fishing and a nearly complete [[Creel Census]]. Fish were marked by [[Fin Clipping]] and by celluloid tags (1¼ × ⅜ inch) attached with monofilament nylon behind the first dorsal fin ray. Fish were anaesthetized in about 5% urethane solution, measured to the nearest half-inch, and scale-sampled. Scales were read as impressions on cellulose acetate.

Marking methods changed through the study:
- 1947 and 1948: fin clipping only.
- 1949: clipping combined with tagging.
- 1950 and 1951: tagging only.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population isolation and residency.** South Bay connects to Lake Huron through a 700-foot-wide narrows and is divided by a second constriction into an unequal outer basin (about 7 fathoms, shallow) and an inner basin with deep water to 30 fathoms. Of about 3,700 lake trout tagged in 1949–1951, only 3 were recovered in outside waters. By contrast, about 10% of tagged [[Whitefish]] were taken by outside fishermen, mainly in [[Georgian Bay]] and off the west coast of the Saugeen Peninsula.

**Summer distribution.** In summer, trout were concentrated below the [[Thermocline]] in the inner basin. Trolling took fish in 8 fathoms or deeper, mostly west and south of Net G on the 10-fathom contour. Recaptures of tagged fish were roughly proportional to release numbers from each net, so fish released near the mouth returned to the deep water as readily as those released inside the bay.

**Spring and winter movement.** Trout moved actively in spring. In 1949 only 44 of 369 pound-net recaptures came from the same net of release, close to the 1:8 ratio expected under complete mixing, though this resemblance was judged largely coincidental. Most recaptures (315 of 369) came from nets adjacent to the inner basin. Winter catches were confined to the inner basin and narrows, with only 12 trout in 1950 and 10 in 1951 from extensive [[Gill Net]] effort (for [[Burbot]], *Lota lota*).

**Spawning.** Trout returned to shallow water in October and spawned on broken rock along the inner-basin shores. In 1950, 143 spawners were sampled, 47 of them tagged, indicating local spawning with little dilution from outside fish. Sex ratios among spawners were 129 males to 7 females, with 8 undetermined.

**Sea lamprey.** The authors judged the sea lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*) the major cause of natural mortality, with no other pathological condition found. Scarred fish increased sharply in 1949 and 1950, across all sizes, suggesting a rising lamprey population as well as larger fish being more vulnerable. The authors note that fishing alone cannot explain the decline where lamprey are resident, citing [[Hile]] and related work on Lakes Huron and Michigan.

## Key Findings

**Population decline (Table 5 and text)**
- [[Schnabel Estimate]] from the 1948 netting: 19,500; [[Petersen Estimate]] from angling: 23,000. The text gives the 1948 population as about 21,000.
- 1949 estimates: 14,000 vulnerable to pound nets and 20,000 to the sport fishery. The authors judged there was no unusual mortality between 1948 and 1949.
- 1950 estimates: 4,160 (net fishery) and 7,000 (sport fishery).
- 1951: a Schnabel estimate of 558 from a very small sample. The population is estimated at about 600 for 1951.
- Natural mortality: about 25% over 1948–49, about 70% over 1949–50 (about 1,800 of 2,700 fish with 1949 tags), and about 60% for 1948 marks in 1950.
- Only 3,124 lake trout were recorded as removed by angling from 1948 to 1951, and the creel census was judged at least 90% complete. Of about 21,000 fishable fish in 1948, anglers harvested no more than 3,500.

**Year-class yield (Table 1)**
- The 1944 class yielded about 5 times as many fish as the 1943 class and 10 times as many as the 1945 class over the period studied. Other year classes contributed little.
- Of 97 post-1944 trout taken in 1952 experimental nets, only 33 lacked the adipose clip from the 1949 and 1951 yearling plantings.

**Growth (Table 4 and Figure 2)**
- Mean fork lengths were 12.7 in (age II), 16.5 in (III), 18.8 in (IV), 20.6 in (V), and 22.7 in (VI) in the table's mean row, with weights given in ounces in the table.
- Trout grew steadily through summer, adding about 1 inch by mid-September. The total 1949 increment for the 1944 class up to May 1950 was estimated at 1.8 inches.

**Spawning and recruitment**
- The 1950 spawning was estimated at roughly 50,000 to 120,000 eggs, based on about 2,400 spawning males and 130 to 290 spawning females. The authors judged it the largest spawning since 1947.
- Survival of about 25% to age IV would probably have been needed for the next year class to match 1944, which the authors considered phenomenal and unlikely.

**Collapse**
- About 40 lake trout were taken in 1952, mostly last survivors of the 1944 class, and none by angling. The population first observed in 1947 was considered virtually extinct by 1952.

**Management recommendations**
- The best prospect for a South Bay fishery while lamprey persist is stocking yearling lake trout and harvesting them at about 15 inches. Yearlings with clipped adipose fins were planted in 1949 and 1951, with a third planting planned for 1952. In 1952, 63 fish from the 1949 plantings were taken, against 23 native fish of the same age, indicating appreciable survival.
- Summer trolling would need to reach at least 15 fathoms because the thermocline lies deep, which exceeds the capacity of ordinary trolling reels with metal line.
