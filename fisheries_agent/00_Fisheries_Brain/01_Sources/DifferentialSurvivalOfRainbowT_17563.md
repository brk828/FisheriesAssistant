# Differential Survival of Rainbow Trout Fed Living Organisms and Hatchery Diets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study in *The Progressive Fish-Culturist* tested whether the diet hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) received affects how well they survive after planting in a stream. Full-sibling fry were reared for 149 days on hatchery diets or natural foods, then stocked at about 3 inches into a small, overstocked stream section. Fish fed only [[Liver And Pellet Diet|liver and pellets]] survived best, and fish fed only [[Natural Food]] survived worst. Groups switched to the other diet for the final 41 days had intermediate survival. The authors state that the cause of the difference is not clear. They suggest that the results are preliminary and should be replicated at different levels of competition.

## Background & Description

Hatchery-reared trout often survive less well after stocking than wild-spawned trout. The authors list three possible causes: reduced wariness from [[Domestication]], less selection for survival before testing, and poorer competition for food and space with resident fish. Because genetic background and environmental experience are hard to separate, this study used domestic fish from one pair of parents. It was designed to isolate survival differences acquired through diet, whether behavioral or physiological. Related behavioral variables (aggregation, swimming activity, and acceptance of unfamiliar food) were also studied, but the data were not analyzed in this paper.

Eggs came from the [[Pennsylvania Fish Commission]]'s experimental station at [[Benner Spring Fish Culture Station]] and were hatched at the University. The work was supported by the [[Pennsylvania State University]] College of Education.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing conditions:**
- Water temperature was 52°F at the start and warmed to about 60°F by the end of May.
- Eggs hatched on or about December 8, 1960. First feeding was January 3, 1961.
- Four tanks each began with 125 fry.
- Intermittent problems included filter failures, possible chlorine contamination, a suspected toxic material in natural food, and unexplained poor feeding periods. These produced 6- to 8-day feeding cessations. Feeding levels were adjusted to keep growth similar among groups.

**Test stream:**
- A screened bypass section of stream, 100 feet long and 6 feet wide, with flow regulated by a weir to about 250 gallons per minute.
- The stream was infertile and soft-water, in a mountain run, and had been cleared of other fish.
- Temperature stayed in the 50s°F until June 30, when it reached 60°F.

**Recovery method:** Fish were counted by repeated [[Electrofishing]] runs (400-cycle, 230-volt AC generator) through the section. Sampling stopped when only four to six fish were caught in a run. The authors judged that most fish present had been recovered.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Four groups, each from a single mating of one male and one female trout:
  - **L-1:** liver, later pellets and liver.
  - **D-2:** daphnids, later freshwater shrimp, midges, aquatic sowbugs, and tubificids.
  - **L-3:** same as L-1, switched to D-type diet on April 20 (renamed LD-3).
  - **D-4:** same as D-2, switched to L-type diet on April 20 (renamed DL-4).
- The first 108 days (January 3 to April 20) used the original diets. The last 41 days (to June 1) used the reversed diets. The total feeding period was 149 days.
- On June 2, 1961, 319 trout were planted (80 per group after size-based selection), averaging about 3 inches long.

**Survival results**

| Group | Planted | Survivors at 4 weeks (June 30) |
|---|---|---|
| L-1 (liver and pellets) | 80 | 14 (about 18%) |
| DL-4 | 79 | 13 (about 16%) |
| LD-3 | 80 | 9 (about 11%) |
| D-2 (natural food) | 80 | 7 (about 9%) |
| **Total** | **319** | **43 (about 14%)** |

- Overall, 21 fish died during handling.
- Week-by-week recovery: 247 (77%) on June 9, 207 (65%) on June 16, 121 (38%) on June 23, and 43 (about 14%) on June 30.
- An adjusted chi-square analysis showed significant differences among groups through week 3. By week 4, few fish remained and the difference was no longer significant (chi-square 1.27, reported as p = .75).
- Mortality during the 5-month feeding period was under 25% in each tank.

**Size and growth**
- L-1 fish were slightly larger than the other groups at stocking, but size did not predict survival within groups.
- Mean lengths for all groups stayed close to 3 inches throughout the test.

**Interpretation and limits**
- The authors could not clearly explain why the liver-and-pellet group survived best.
- Earlier work by [[Miller And Miller (1962)]] linked higher mortality in hatchery trout to elevated blood [[Lactate Level]] levels in a stream with resident [[Cutthroat Trout]].
- [[Phillips Et Al. (1956)]] reported that wild trout flesh contains more protein and ash and less fat and water than hatchery trout, and that natural foods produce trout that are chemically superior. The authors did not analyze their fish flesh or foods, so this could not be tested.
- Near-lethal water episodes during feeding did not appear to explain survival differences. The LD-3 group had no such episodes but still survived worse than two other groups.
- The overstocked test area was intentionally competitive. The 43 survivors still represented a density well above that of normal streams, so the results may not extrapolate to natural carrying capacities.
- Testing differences at normal densities would require a large, fish-free stream section and much greater recovery effort.
- The authors describe the findings as tentative and recommend replicate tests at several competition levels.

**Conclusion:** In this preliminary, overstocked stream test, rainbow trout reared entirely on hatchery diets survived better than those reared entirely on natural foods. Groups switched between diets during the last 41 days showed intermediate survival. The mechanism is unresolved.
