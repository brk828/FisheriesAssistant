# Pathological Effects of Sub-Acute and Acute Phenol Poisoning in Rainbow Trout in Hard Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 laboratory study by Mitrovic, Brown, Shurben and Berryman, published in *Water Research*, describes the pathology of [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) exposed to [[Phenol]] at concentrations near the 48 hr median lethal concentration (LC50). Fish that died within hours showed gill and pharyngeal necrosis, internal haemorrhage and splenic swelling. Fish that survived 7 days showed wider damage to the skin, liver, kidney, spleen, small intestine and ovary. The authors conclude that sub-lethal phenol exposure can impair survival and reproduction within one week.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** [[Rainbow Trout]], 1+ years old, 13–15 cm fork length.
- **Toxicant:** [[Phenol]] (C6H5OH), analytical grade (assay not less than 98.5%).
- **Context:** Industrial phenolic wastes are common contaminants of natural waters. Reports conflict on tolerance. [[Kalabina (1935)]] proposed that fish would be absent above 0.2 mg/l, while Krombach and Barthel (1964) recorded salmonids in a stream at about 1.0 mg/l.
- **Prior work:** The authors review pathological effects reported by Havelka and Effenberger (1957), Lammering and Burbank (1960), Vishnevetskii (1962), Halsband and Halsband (1963), Skrapek (1963) and Reichenbach-Klinke (1965), the last of which described gill, skin, intestinal, liver and muscle lesions at 0.02–0.07 mg/l in wild Rhine and Elbe fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water quality:** Borehole water with total hardness of 290 mg/l as CaCO3 and total alkalinity of 230 mg/l as CaCO3, at 12.6 °C and pH 7.8, flowing at 1 l/min.
- **Dosing:** Phenol was delivered by a continuous dosing apparatus ([[Stark Dosing Apparatus]], 1967) from a stock of 4.0 g/l. Concentrations were verified by [[Ochynski Absorptiometric Method]] (1960).
- **Test conditions:** The 48 hr LC50 was 7.5 mg/l (95% CI 6.6–8.5 mg/l). Exposure concentrations were 6.5, 6.9, 7.3 and 9.3 mg/l. Fish were fed during tests.
- **Ecological implication:** The authors argue that fish can be harmed well below lethal levels, which affects fisheries and population persistence.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Eight fish per concentration: three held in a plastic mesh cage and five outside. Fish were sampled at set intervals, with three fish per tank taken after 7 days or at death.
- Gills, skin, small intestine, liver, spleen and gonads were fixed in 5% formalin, sectioned at 8 µm and stained with Delafield haematoxylin and eosin.

**Gross pathology**
- Fish dying within hours had internal haemorrhage, blood in the body cavity, splenic swelling, and damage to the pharynx and gills. Fin-base haemorrhage was also seen, especially at the pectoral and anal fins.
- Seven-day survivors had slimy skin from excess mucus, inflamed gills with lost filament sections, an intestine filled with yellow-green mucus, a distended gall bladder, a blotched or spotted liver, an enlarged spleen and a swollen, partly white (possibly necrotic) kidney.

**Gill histopathology (time course)**
- **2–4 hr:** Secondary lamellae separated from pillar cells, with haematomas and fused lamellae. At 9.3 mg/l, epithelium was acutely inflamed and partly destroyed, and the pharynx was inflamed.
- **4–12 hr:** Epithelium was thinned or destroyed, and filament tips were eroded to the cartilage at 7.3 mg/l. In a fish killed at 12 hr in 6.5 mg/l, only pillar cells remained.
- **7 days:** Lamellae were fused, thickened or destroyed. Distal filaments were lost at 6.5 and 6.9 mg/l. Only one fish survived at 7.3 mg/l, and none survived 7 days at 9.3 mg/l.

**Non-gill organs**
- Short exposures (2–8 hr) caused skin changes, with swollen, full mucous cells, while internal organs showed only swollen outer membranes.
- Seven-day survivors had thickened skin with chronic inflammation, damaged ovarian oocytes (shrunken, yolk-depleted), swollen Malpighian bodies in the kidney, dilated hepatic capillaries and loss of intestinal goblet cells.

**Conclusions**
- Phenol rapidly damages gill epithelium, with lethal effects concentrated at 6–8 hr in higher concentrations.
- Even below the 48 hr LC50 (the lowest concentration killed only 20% of fish within 48 hr), damage within one week could impair survival and prevent egg development, affecting reproduction.
