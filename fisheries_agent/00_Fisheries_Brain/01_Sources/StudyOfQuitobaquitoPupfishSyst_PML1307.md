# Quitobaquito Pupfish: Water Quality, Population, Pesticide and Systematic Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This report, the second of three studies on the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*[[Cyprinodon Macularius]]*), covers June 1976 to September 1977 and was prepared at the University of Arizona. It addresses five topics: water quality at [[Quitobaquito Pool]] and [[Williams Spring]], population size and age structure, [[Pesticide Contamination]] in the pupfish and their habitat, the attempted establishment of a refugium at [[Gachado Tank]], and [[Morphometric Analysis|morphological]] and [[Courtship Behavior|courtship]] comparisons with other pupfish populations from Mexico.

The population fell sharply after a [[Winter Mortality]] event, with a 35% reduction in abundance. Very few adults spawned in spring 1976. Adult fish carried detectable but non-lethal levels of an organophosphate pesticide, probably linked to cotton cultivation just south of the border. The authors conclude that the Quitobaquito population is a distinct subspecies.

## Background & Description

The Desert Pupfish is a small cyprinodontid fish of desert springs and pools. The Quitobaquito population lives in a single small habitat within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], which is the reason for the study's emphasis on protecting it.

Earlier work had described the species from the upper [[Gila River]] drainage in Arizona. A morphological analysis had concluded that Quitobaquito fish were probably a subspecies of *C. macularius*. [[Miller]] found several differences between Quitobaquito fish and those from Rio Sonoyta, Sonora, and from California, but did not analyze them statistically. Earlier courtship observations also suggested that Quitobaquito males differed in behaviour from other populations.

The present study had three aims: to clarify morphological and behavioural differences among allopatric populations, to provide information for defining the biological status of the Quitobaquito population, and to document baseline pesticide conditions.

Three Mexican populations were sampled for comparison:
- **Rio Sonoyta** population, which coexists with [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia*) and [[Sailfin Molly]]-type poeciliids, and which probably became isolated from the Quitobaquito population around 1900.
- **Pozo Caliente**, a pool of about 10 m diameter fed by spring seeps on the eastern Colorado River delta plain. These fish coexist with larger mollies and mosquitofish.
- **Pozo Calienté** (Pozo 1 and 2) populations, which also coexist with exotic species.

[[Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] were noted as introduced predators at some sites, and their presence is considered a threat to native pupfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water quality at Quitobaquito and Williams Spring was monitored from July 1976 to September 1977. It was essentially unchanged across the period and was excellent for pupfish. Sampling and analysis were done with the help of the Soils, Water and Engineering College at the University of Arizona.

**Habitat size and threats.** The Quitobaquito pool is small, so the species is extremely vulnerable to chemical contamination and habitat change. The Gachado site is an artificial tank that depends on regular water deliveries. Water was not trucked to it often enough, and the tank dried up within less than six months, killing all pupfish and making the refugium attempt a failure.

**Agricultural pesticides.** The land south of Quitobaquito in Mexico was cleared and planted in cotton for the first time in the fall and spring of 1975. Wind from the south and west can carry spray drift into the monument, and drift has been observed several times. The earliest fish samples tested for pesticides were collected in December 1976, after the first Mexican cotton harvest. The authors consider these the best available baseline data.

**Regional status.** The status and condition of Desert Pupfish populations were reported to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The authors consider the Rio Sonoyta and other Mexican populations at risk because of increasing agriculture, groundwater pumping and surface water diversion.

## Key Findings

### Water Quality
- Water quality was essentially unchanged from 1976 to 1977 and was excellent for pupfish.
- Algal growth was recorded at the sites, and the study lists values for chlorophyll and organic matter, but the exact values are garbled in the scan and are not reproduced here.

### Population Size and Age Structure
- Population estimates were made by [[Mark-Recapture]] in November 1976 and April 1977. Fish were marked by clipping the left pectoral fin, released where captured, and then recaptured about 24 hours later.
- The **November 1976** estimate was **2,325** markable-size fish (95% confidence limits 1,667–3,333). The abstract gives a different figure for this estimate, which is garbled in the scan.
- The **spring 1977** estimate was **1,628** fish. Subadult fish under 25 mm made up only **1.3%** of recaptures.
- A **35%** reduction in abundance occurred over the winter because of mortality. The authors describe this as severe, and note that the population density in winter was lower than that found in 1975.
- Length-frequency data show an **absence of large fish** (over 29 mm, mostly age 2 and older) in fall 1975 and a higher percentage of young-of-the-year fish in 1976 and 1977.
- Very few territorial breeding males were seen in April and May 1977, and very few adults spawned in spring 1976.

### Pesticide Contamination
- No pre-1976 pesticide data exist for Quitobaquito pupfish, so the authors could not confirm whether pesticides caused the lack of adults in spring 1976.
- Fish collected in fall 1977 had **detectable but non-lethal** levels of [[Parathion]] in their body tissues.
- Chlorinated hydrocarbons and organophosphate peaks were found in some samples. The authors say the sulfur and phosphate peaks were not associated with any parathion or malathion standard. Chlorinated hydrocarbons were not detected in water, plants or floc.
- Fish from Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta contained detectable parathion. One fish population contained approximately **0.03 ppm** of n-parathion (Kynard, 1979).
- Some Quitobaquito fish also carried p,p'-DDE, p,p'-DDD and p,p'-DDT, as did Sonoyta fish, showing that the species is exposed to legacy and current pesticide sources.

### Refugium Attempt at Gachado Tank
- In October 1976, Gachado Tank, which had held a population of desert fish since the 1960s, was drained and all mosquitofish and pupfish preserved. The tank was then restocked with Quitobaquito pupfish (110 adults; no subadults).
- The refugium failed within six months because water was not delivered regularly and the tank dried up. The authors use this to argue that populations dependent on artificial water supplies are very fragile.
- A second option, a spring-fed pool at a site on [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] land, was considered but its water supply was judged insufficient.

### Morphological Comparisons
- Discriminant analysis, using 15 characters, separated Quitobaquito fish from the Sonoyta and Pozo Caliente populations.
- Quitobaquito fish differed significantly from Sonoyta fish in body depth, fin lengths, fin-ray counts (dorsal, anal, pectoral, pelvic) and gill rakers. Pectoral and pelvic ray counts and gill raker numbers were higher in Quitobaquito.
- Quitobaquito fish had less deep bodies than Pozo Caliente fish, and more pelvic and gill rakers.
- Overall morphological differences were small (the authors call them minor), though statistically significant in several characters.

### Courtship Behaviour
- Courtship was observed in five allopatric populations held in plastic swimming pools. Behaviour was recorded by verbal descriptions and video and still photography.
- Quitobaquito males courted in a distinct pattern from the Mexican populations. Both studies found that behaviour patterns were largely genetically determined, though the Quitobaquito pattern was "almost identical" to that of Sonoyta males in some respects.
- Major behavioural differences in courtship were found between Quitobaquito and the other populations, which the authors regard as more important than the morphological differences.
- Table 4 lists 11 motor action patterns, with frequency of occurrence and an associated behavioural sequence diagram.

### Conclusions and Management Recommendations
- The Quitobaquito pupfish is distinct from other populations of *C. macularius*. The authors classify it as a subspecies.
- The population is at risk because of its restricted habitat, pesticide exposure, and the threat of exotic predators.
- Recommendations include: establishing a reliable refugium, continuing pesticide monitoring, maintaining regular population monitoring, and protecting the habitat at Quitobaquito from water diversion and groundwater pumping.
