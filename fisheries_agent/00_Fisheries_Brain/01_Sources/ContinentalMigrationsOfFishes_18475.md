# Continental Migrations of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #serranidae #clupeidae #pomatomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Robert Victor Miller]], a fishery biologist at the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]] Tropical Atlantic Biological Laboratory, summarizes the migratory movements of three commercially and recreationally important coastal fishes along the North American coasts: [[Striped Bass]], [[American Shad]], and [[Bluefish]]. The paper focuses mainly on striped bass, drawing on large tagging programs, and compares the patterns with those of shad and bluefish. Its central conclusions are that striped bass form a coastal migratory system linked to [[Chesapeake Bay]], that shad show homing and an extensive Gulf of Maine feeding range, and that bluefish move mostly inshore and offshore within discrete regional races rather than making long seasonal migrations.

## Background & Description

Migration has long shaped angling and commercial fishing, since fishers have learned when and where particular species appear. State agencies and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] began large-scale studies of migratory fishes in about the 1930s, largely through tagging. The paper cites a body of tagging methods literature, including work on [[Tagging Methods]] and tag types.

- **Striped bass** (*Morone saxatilis*), locally called "rockfish" or "striper," belongs to the sea bass family [[Serranidae]]. It is anadromous, moving from ocean or brackish water into rivers to spawn.
- **American shad** (*Alosa sapidissima*), a member of the herring family [[Clupeidae]], also ascends rivers to spawn. It was once prized as food and remains an important sport fish, although its numbers have declined over recent decades.
- **Bluefish** (*Pomatomus saltatrix*) is the only species in the family [[Pomatomidae]]. It is a cosmopolitan game and food fish found along the Americas from Argentina to Massachusetts (occasionally Nova Scotia), throughout the Gulf of Mexico, and in the Mediterranean, around Africa, across the Indian Ocean, and around Australia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Striped bass distribution.** Native populations occur on the Atlantic coast from the St. Lawrence River to the St. Johns River, Florida, in Gulf of Mexico tributaries from western Florida to Louisiana, and on the Pacific coast from central California to northern Oregon. Introductions include numerous freshwater lakes and the Colorado River in Arizona. The major populations are in two large estuaries: [[Chesapeake Bay]] and the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]].

**Shad migration timing.** Spawning runs begin earliest at the southern end of the range (about November in the St. Johns River, Florida) and become progressively later northward, reaching as late as June in the St. John River, New Brunswick. Shad in rivers south of North Carolina die after spawning, while Middle Atlantic and northern stocks survive and return to sea.

**Temperature and bluefish movement.** Bluefish move inshore and offshore to stay in waters above 50°F. Water temperatures off east and west Florida remain moderate year-round, which probably explains the minor seasonal movement of the southernmost group.

**Pacific introductions.** A stock of 435 striped bass was transported by train from New Jersey waters to San Francisco Bay in 1879 and 1881. The species spread from San Francisco Bay south to southern California and north to the Columbia River, with moderate populations as far north as Coos Bay, Oregon. The center of abundance is the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta.

## Key Findings

### Striped bass (Atlantic)

- Early tagging studies (1938 to 1952) were based on relatively few fish, mostly small, sexually immature females and young males. Most were recaptured near the tagging site, though some moved long distances within the Bay.
- Later programs (1961 onward) tagged over 10,000 fish, including several hundred weighing more than 6 pounds or exceeding 20 inches in fork length. These showed that a large population winters in the deep areas of Chesapeake Bay.
- Males spawn at about 2 years of age; females generally at 4 years or older. Larger fish (over 16 inches) move greater average distances than smaller fish.
- In the [[Potomac River]], spawning fish returned to the same spawning area in successive years, indicating a homing tendency. It is unclear whether fish return to their natal stream, because some recaptured fish may never have left the lower river.
- A small but significant proportion of winter-tagged fish later recovered had left the river and bay and migrated up the Atlantic coast (Nichols and Miller, 1967).
- The proposed emigration route runs through the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal, down Delaware Bay, and north along New Jersey and New York (Long Island) to Nova Scotia. One fish was recaptured outside Chesapeake Bay 8 days after release, 275 miles from the release point.
- Migrating fish feed during summer and early fall off the New England coast, then return south to Chesapeake Bay by late fall or early winter.
- Post-spawning fish from the York, Rappahannock, and James Rivers, and from the Roanoke River and Albemarle Sound, appear to join this coastal cycle in similar proportions.
- Long Island analyses (Alperin, 1966; Schaefer, 1968; Clark, 1968) indicate that the composition of surf-caught schools varies with stock abundance. Hudson River fish dominate when Long Island stocks are low, while Delaware and Chesapeake fish dominate when stocks are abundant. Immature and post-spawning stripers from Chesapeake Bay are largely responsible for the migrating schools fished along the northeast coast.
- Striped bass stocks south of Albemarle Sound apparently do not migrate north. The St. Johns River population appears entirely local. The Santee-Cooper system in South Carolina may contain a landlocked upstream form and a downstream estuarine form, neither mixing with other stocks.
- The Roanoke River-Albemarle Sound population divides in winter: smaller fish winter in Albemarle Sound, while larger fish (6 to 75 pounds) winter on the ocean side of the Outer Banks.

### Striped bass (Pacific)

- Pacific fish retain many pre-transplant migratory traits. Adults move in fall from highly saline San Francisco Bay upstream to brackish-to-fresh waters from San Pablo Bay to the Delta, where they remain through winter.
- In spring they disperse across the Delta and up tributaries to spawn, some moving more than 60 miles upriver. Afterward they retreat downstream to feed in San Francisco Bay and adjacent waters.
- Juveniles are carried downstream, with distribution determined by river outflow, and feed over shallow flats of the upper bays.
- Tag returns show little likelihood of massive coastal migration, though a few returns from outside the Bay suggest more fish may reach the open ocean than previously believed.
- The fall movement into the Delta has no counterpart on the East Coast, where eastern striped bass move from shallow areas into deeper, more saline estuarine waters.

### American shad

- Post-spawning fish from Middle Atlantic and northern stocks return to sea in late spring or early summer, migrate north or south along the coast, and spend summer and autumn in the Gulf of Maine.
- Stocks scatter across the Middle Atlantic in winter, then in late winter and early spring migrate to their native streams. Shad show homing, though it is not known whether this is natal-stream homing as in Pacific salmon or repeated spawning in the first spawning stream.
- Juveniles leave the streams in fall, winter in the Middle Atlantic, and follow the adults to the Gulf of Maine the next summer.
- Earlier theories, that the whole population moved north and peeled off segments, and that young shad wintered near their home river mouths, have been revised by tagging studies (Talbot and Sykes, 1958).

### Bluefish

- Commercial landings show no bluefish in northern states in winter and a spring rise to a summer peak. This may reflect an offshore-to-onshore movement rather than long migration.
- Deep-water catches off Martha's Vineyard and the Hudson Gorge during winter suggest offshore wintering.
- [[Lund]] (1961) proposed up to six racial groups along the Atlantic coast: a northern group (New York to Massachusetts), groups off New Jersey and off Delaware-Maryland-Virginia, a Middle Atlantic group (Virginia to North Carolina), a South Atlantic group (North Carolina to Georgia), and a probable single Florida group.
- Movements are mainly within each race, and current patterns isolate the groups.
- A 1964 tagging program (Beaumariage and Wittich, 1966) recaptured 8 of 98 fish, at liberty 12 to 433 days (mean 71.1 days). The 433-day fish moved 195 miles within Florida waters; the others moved 1 to 22 miles, supporting the view that long-distance migration is limited.
- An editor's note reports a larger Sandy Hook Marine Laboratory program: 1,181 bluefish tagged in New York-New Jersey in summer 1966, with 38 returns, and one fish recovered in Gulf of Mexico waters near St. Marks, Florida, from Virginia tagging. This suggests a more significant north-south movement than earlier assumed.

### Synthesis

- Otherwise unrelated fishes can share habits. Anadromous striped bass and shad spawn in rivers much like Pacific salmon, and all three show some homing tendency, best documented in salmon.
- Shad and segments of the striped bass population feed in the Gulf of Maine during summer and fall.
- Bluefish, once thought to migrate seasonally north to south, probably move onshore and offshore within discrete races.
- The author argues that modern tagging research has produced information essential for managing the many state fisheries that share these migratory species.
