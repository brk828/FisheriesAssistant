# Final Report on the Little Colorado River Population of the Humpback Chub, May 1987–1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted by C.O. Minckley of the [[Northern Arizona University]] Biology Department to the Nongame Branch of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (dated 20 December 1988), documents two months of field research (27 days in 1987 and 30 days in 1988) on the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Little Colorado River]] near its confluence with the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Objectives were to document reproduction, conduct mark-and-recapture studies for a population estimate, and conduct thermal tolerance studies on larval fishes. Population estimates were 5,783 ± 679 humpback chubs for May 1987 and 7,060 ± 574 for May 1988. Native fishes dominated catches in both years, and the report recommends continued monitoring, tagging, upstream investigations, and June–July collections.

## Background & Description

- **Species of focus:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), described by Miller (1946) from specimens taken at Bright Angel Creek. Federally listed as endangered.
- **Study site:** [[Little Colorado River]] drains 141,155 km² of eastern and northern Arizona, flows 412 km from the White Mountains to its confluence with the Colorado River at Km 98. The river drops 1,922 m overall, with about 1,066 m in the first 87 km; average gradient is about 12 m/km, dropping to about 3 m/km below that point.
- **Hydrology:** The largest spring, [[Blue Springs]], enters from the Redwall limestone about 21 km from the mouth, with discharge of 217–232 cfs (Johnson and Sanderson 1968). Combined springs provide 212–247 cfs and are the only water source when the river is not in flood.
- **Geology:** Lower 21 km cross Kaibab, Toroweap, Coconino, Supai and Redwall strata (all Paleozoic). [[Travertine]] deposits form scalloped waterfalls and cascades; a barrier waterfall about 5 m high lies 15 km upstream.
- **Water chemistry:** The Little Colorado is the only sodium chloride type tributary of the Colorado mainstream. Specific conductance ranged 4,140–6,100 micromhos/cm (not measured in this study); pH 7.8–7.9.
- **Study methods overview:** Hoop, fyke, D-hoop, trammel and Swedish gill nets; seining; trotlines; hook and line; Floy fingerling tagging; population estimate using N = MC/R (Ricker 1971).
- **Riparian vegetation:** [[Salt Cedar]] dominates lower reaches; [[Coyote Willow]], [[Common Reed]], cattail, bulrush and [[Phragmites]] occur in patches; *Zannichellia palustris* appears in shallow pools during extended low flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Tidal effect:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] releases cause the Little Colorado to be ponded upstream during high mainstream flows, with the confluence becoming a swift chute as flows drop. Flow regime varies daily or hourly.
- **Temperature gradient:** Little Colorado water was 15–24 °C during May 1988 (Table 1), versus about 10 °C in the Colorado mainstream. The report proposes this gradient as a main factor concentrating humpback chubs at the confluence.
- **Turbidity and flood:** Flood flows of 400 cfs (1987) and 665 cfs (1988) at study start produced high turbidity; catch rates dropped when flooding stopped, especially in upstream trammel nets in 1988.
- **Anchorworm parasite:** *Lernaea cyprinacea* was present in less than 1% of humpback chubs in both years (compared with 53.8% reported in 1978), and is considered currently not a problem.
- **Predators and competitors:** Channel catfish and carp are the most common exotic fishes; fathead minnow, golden shiner, Rio Grande killifish, black bullhead and redside shiner are incidental. The report notes these species can rapidly increase and threaten humpback chub.
- **Colorado River trout:** [[Rainbow Trout]] use the confluence zone only when cold Colorado waters are present.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents of non-native fishes were mainly *Cladophora glomerata*, scuds (amphipods) and chironomid larvae.

## Key Findings

### Catch and composition
- **1987:** 1,140 fishes of 9 species; humpback chub 681 (59.7%); native fishes 97.4%, exotics 2.6%.
- **1988:** 1,904 fishes of 9 species; humpback chub 1,153 (60.5%); native fishes 97.6% (N=1,858), exotics 2.4% (N=46).
- Flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) 275 (1987), 258 (1988); bluehead sucker (*Pantosteus discobolus*) 94 (1987), 173 (1988); speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) 66 (1987), 274 (1988).

### Population estimates
- **May 1987:** 5,783 ± 679 humpback chubs (fish from confluence to 1,225 m downstream).
- **May 1988:** 7,060 ± 574 humpback chubs (same area).
- The report states several thousand humpback chubs continue to use the Little Colorado during May.

### Sex ratio and size
- **1987:** Sexed chubs ranged 44.5–91.6%; females outnumbered males each week (about twice as abundant overall). Mean female total length 145.7–295 mm (week-dependent); smallest female 136 mm, largest 474 mm.
- **1988:** Sexed chubs ranged 41.2–81.7%; sex ratio essentially equal. Female mean 327–347 mm; smallest female 166 mm, largest 555 mm. Male range 127–488 mm.
- Overall chub total length ranged 80–555 mm in 1988.
- The report notes sexing was unreliable until late 1988; a method using urogenital papilla eversion was found effective.

### Tagging and movement
- **1987:** 562 chubs tagged (82.5% of 681); 67 recaptured (11.9% of tagged). Days at large 0–14 (mean 3.4). Largest upstream movement 621 m.
- **1988:** 698 chubs tagged (60.5% of 1,153); 149 recaptured (21.3%). Days at large 0–23 (mean 4.4). Maximum upstream movement 9.2–9.8 km (text varies) over 13 days; maximum downstream movement 2.9 km over 10 days.
- **Long-term recaptures (1988):** 20 fish tagged in 1987 were recaptured (3.3%); 31 Arizona Game and Fish fish tagged 1985–1986 were recaptured (mean 748 days at large); six other fish tagged 1980–1985 were recaptured. One fish (Y306897) was tagged in the Colorado River at Km 98, 2.4 km above the confluence.
- The report suggests the species may home to the Little Colorado, and that the confluence is a staging area.

### Growth
- Growth of 1985 tags 6–13 mm (1988 recaptures); 1986 tags 1–56 mm; a 1980 tag grew 69 mm; a 1981 tag 48 mm; a 1984 tag 62 mm; a 1985 tag 15 mm.
- Overall, growth is described as slow in a long-lived species.

### Age structure (inferred, arbitrary)
- **1987:** Age 0: 8%; age I: 10.7%; age II: 16.7%; age III: 19.9%; older: 44.7%.
- **1988:** Age 0: 3.6%; age I: 16.2%; age II: 13.3%; age III: 8.2%; older: 58.7%.
- Otoliths from two skeletonized fish suggested ages of 27 and 34 years.

### Reproduction
- Spawning confirmed both years by secondary sexual characters (tubercles), gamete expression, breeding colors and larval fishes. Direct observation of spawning not achieved for humpback chub.
- Larval fishes first collected 18 May 1988, 550 m upstream; none were found in 1987 until the third week (six larvae preserved).
- Roughly 30% of larvae taken in 1988 were cyprinids, many thought to be humpback chubs.
- A thermal tolerance experiment was run twice in 1988 with about 400 larvae; results to be presented elsewhere.

### Gear and catch rates
- **Hoop and fyke nets (1987):** day 0–0.56 f/hr (mean 0.21); night 0–0.69 f/hr (mean 0.27); 24 hr 0–0.63 f/hr (mean 0.24); 72.5 net days.
- **Trammel nets (1987):** day 0.08–0.23 f/hr (mean 0.13); night 0–0.29 f/hr (mean 0.19); 35.5 net days.
- **1987 total:** 106–108 net days, 22 net sets.
- **Hoop and fyke nets (1988):** day 0–0.34 f/hr (mean 0.09); night 0–0.66 f/hr (mean 0.20); 24 hr 0–0.42 f/hr (mean 0.15); 208.5 net days.
- **Trammel nets (1988):** day 0.04–0.26 f/hr (mean 0.14); night 0.08–0.36 f/hr (mean 0.19); 24 hr 0.11–0.31 f/hr (mean 0.17); 51 net days.
- **1988 total:** 259.5 net days, 28 net sets.
- Hoop nets were described as far more effective than trammels and seldom lethal, a benefit for an endangered species.
- Trotlines (31 humpbacks, 5/20–5/26) and angling (17 humpbacks, 5/20–5/30) contributed to the 1988 catch.

### Thermal and water conditions
- Water temperature at 621 m upstream (Table 1): 15–18 °C early May 1988, 18–24 °C by month end.
- Colorado River flows: 1,961–26,529 cfs (1987); 1,270–20,408 cfs (1988).
- Little Colorado flood flows: 0–400 cfs (1987, excluding Blue Springs); 0–645 cfs (1988). Including Blue Springs, approximately 650 and 895 cfs.

### Management recommendations
1. Continue monitoring to document spawning requirements.
2. Continue tagging to document movement in the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers.
3. Conduct investigations at least 10 km upstream to document movement and reproduction.
4. Collect data in June or July to assess spawning success and recruitment.
5. Continue investigations to inform management of the humpback chub to ensure its continued existence.

### Caveats
- Several figures contain internal inconsistencies from the scanned source (e.g., maximum upstream movement given as 9.8 km and 9.2 km; 1987 recapture total 67 vs. 11.9%). Values are reported as given in the source.
- Age-class percentages are described by the author as "educated guesses" except for age class 0.
