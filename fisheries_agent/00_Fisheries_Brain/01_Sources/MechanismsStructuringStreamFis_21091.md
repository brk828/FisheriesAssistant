# Mechanisms Structuring Stream Fish Assemblages: Lessons from Introduced Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This invited essay asks whether the introduction of non-native (exotic) and transplanted fishes into streams can reveal the processes that structure native stream fish assemblages. The author reviewed 31 studies in 26 systems. Most documented declines in native fishes after introductions (77%), and biotic interactions were implicated in 62% of the studies. Competition and predation were proposed in roughly equal measure. The review concludes that post-disturbance responses give only circumstantial evidence for biotic control, and that the lack of pre-disturbance data prevents firm conclusions about the processes that structured these assemblages beforehand.

## Background & Description

- **Scope of introductions:** At least 42 exotic fish species have become established in North America within about 300 years. Introductions rose sharply after the 1960s. Native species are also frequently transplanted into watersheds where they do not naturally occur.
- **Establishment success:** Reported establishment of introduced fishes ranges from 38% to 77% across regions. Success is generally higher in areas altered by humans or initially low in fish species richness.
- **Conceptual framework:** The author treats introductions as natural experiments. The review focuses on [[Schoener (1987) Axis 3]], which contrasts physical (abiotic) and biological (biotic) control of communities. Real communities are viewed as dynamic positions along a biotic-to-abiotic gradient, and the level of physical harshness is considered a key determinant of how much biotic control occurs.
- **Taxonomic and regional pattern:** Eastern and southeastern US streams are more species-rich than northern or western streams. Depauperate faunas appear more vulnerable to successful establishment. The [[Cyprinus carpio]] and [[Ctenopharyngodon idella]] are noted as the main exceptions among widely successful introduced species in diverse eastern drainages.
- **Impact pathways:** Following [[Taylor et al. (1984) Impact Classification]], introduced fishes may affect native ecosystems through habitat alteration, disease or parasites, hybridization, trophic alteration, and spatial alteration. This review emphasizes trophic effects (competition and predation).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mechanisms of control.** Predators may maintain diversity by altering interspecific competition, or they may have shaped community composition historically while playing a smaller role today. Introduced predators often drastically change species composition. Different life-history stages of the same fish can act as both predator and competitor.

**Establishment and failure.** If an introduction fails, the three main proposed causes are:
1. Physiological intolerance of the new environment (probably the most common).
2. Elimination through biotic interactions with the native assemblage (little information available).
3. Too small an initial population for successful reproduction (probably common, but poorly documented).

Establishment itself reveals little about the community. It may indicate that biotic processes are important but resources are non-limiting, that the invader is a superior competitor or predator, or that abiotic forces dominate.

**Regional comparison.** The author compared five high-diversity states (≥200 native species: Alabama, Kentucky, Tennessee, Illinois, Mississippi) with five low-diversity western states (California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico), using data from [[Courtenay et al. (1986) Exotic Fish Distribution]]. The low-diversity states had 36 documented introductions versus 9 in high-diversity states. Establishment was 44% in low-diversity states and 33% in high-diversity states. The author notes that sampling effort may bias these figures.

- [[Brook Trout]] declines with [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] (multiple studies in Michigan, Tennessee, North Carolina, and Minnesota).
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) is linked to declines of southwestern native fishes in Arizona, including the [[Sonoran Topminnow]].
- [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanella*) is associated with reduced native populations in North Carolina first-order streams.
- Brown trout predation is linked to declines of nongame fishes in Virginia.
- Native galaxiids in New Zealand and Australia declined following salmonid introductions.

## Key Findings

- **Declines dominate:** Of 31 studies, 77% documented a decline in native fishes following exotic or transplanted introductions. Only 10 studies examined resource-use shifts, and 5 of those documented or suggested habitat shifts. No studies examined trophic dimensions of niche shifts.
- **Biotic interactions are common:** Biotic interactions were implicated in 62% of studies. In Table 2, competition or predation was indicated in 16 of 26 systems.
- **Mechanisms are poorly resolved:** Only five studies tested mechanisms experimentally, and three of these focused on populations rather than assemblages. Proposed mechanisms split equally between competition and predation. Predation has better documentation (3 studies) than competition (2 studies).
- **Growth effects are rare:** Only one study ([[Meffe (1985) Predation Study]]) examined growth. Under laboratory but not field conditions, native *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* showed lower somatic weight and reduced reproductive output when reared with *Gambusia affinis*.
- **Geographic and taxonomic patterns:** 69% of systems are in North America, and 69% were affected by a single introduced species, more than half of them salmonids.
- **Regional contrast in threat:** Biotic interactions were implicated in 54% of threatened western US fishes versus 9% of threatened eastern fishes. The author attributes eastern stability to stochastic, non-equilibrial assemblages, but notes recent evidence of more equilibrial control in some southeastern assemblages.
- **Contrasting prior views:** [[Simberloff (1981) General Review]] reported no effect in 79% of introductions, whereas [[Herbold and Moyle (1986) Vacant Niches]] found that introductions into undisturbed habitats usually displace natives. The author argues that many natives are displaced in disturbed habitats too, depending on context.
- **Major limitations:**
  - Few studies looked for subtle changes in assemblage structure or niche shape.
  - Pre-disturbance data are generally lacking, so the processes structuring assemblages before introduction cannot be determined.
  - Unrecorded introductions may bias establishment rates upward.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Conduct careful descriptive and manipulative studies of native assemblages before and in the absence of introduced species.
  - Use ongoing introductions, which are expected to continue, as opportunities to study how natural communities function.
