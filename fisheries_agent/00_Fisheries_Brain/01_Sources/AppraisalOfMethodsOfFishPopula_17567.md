# Appraisal of Methods of Fish Population Study: The Measurement of Fish Population Size

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 paper by Gerald P. Cooper and Karl F. Lagler reviews methods for estimating the number of fish in a population, focusing on inland waters. It separates direct enumeration (actual counts) from indirect estimation, and tests two indirect approaches, the [[Delury Regression Method]] and [[Mark-and-Recapture Estimation]], using laboratory models with dyed navy beans as "fish." The authors also show that combining size groups in [[Stream Electrofishing]] surveys biases population estimates low, and they quantify that bias using trout data from the Pigeon River, Michigan. The central message is that estimates are only as reliable as their assumptions, and that workers should test those assumptions with models and field data.

## Background & Description

The authors argue that knowing fish population size is basic to fishery management, especially for relating standing crop to yield and for reaching [[Optimal Sustained Yield]]. A key question for managers is how many fish of harvestable size must be maintained for satisfactory fishing. Population counts also allow managers to measure the effects of regulation changes, stocking, fish removal, and shelter installation.

The paper classifies enumeration methods as:

- **Direct methods:** draining water bodies and recovering fish (the most accurate), weir counts on migratory species, and mass-mortality counts after [[Rotenone Treatment]]. Rotenone recoveries ranged from about one-fifth to nearly all fish present. Other tried approaches include aerial and streambank counts, cameras, photoelectric eyes, telescopes, diving transects, and seining or trapping to exhaustion, which had not been tested extensively for absoluteness.
- **Indirect methods:** these estimate absolute abundance either from declining catch per unit of effort or from the recapture of marked fish. The Petersen formula, and its modifications by Schnabel, Schumacher and Eschmeyer, and Chapman, are the main mark-and-recapture tools.

The authors did not cover catch-effort indices of relative abundance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper emphasizes that indirect estimators rest on assumptions that are rarely fully met in nature, including closed populations, constant catchability, random mixing of marked fish, and equal mortality of marked and unmarked fish. Laboratory models remove many sources of bias (sampling error, animal behavior, mixing), so they set a lower limit on field estimate error.

Field catchability is not uniform. Trout catchability by electrofishing varies strongly with body size: a shocker crew might capture only 5 to 10% of very small trout but 40 to 50% of large trout, because large fish are more completely electro-narcotized and easier to see. The authors also note that differential catchability can arise from habitat. Electrofishing may work better over shallow riffles than deep pools, and trap nets may outperform in open water compared with weed-choked areas. They extend this concern to bluegills and bass taken by trap nets, seines, and other gear in lakes.

## Key Findings

**1. Laboratory models of the DeLury regression method**
- Models used known populations of 2,000, 5,000, and 10,000 "fish" (dyed or white navy beans) in a two-gallon minnow pail. Catches per dip were fixed at 10, 40, or 100, except for one initial run with a chance catch averaging 36.9.
- For a known population of 5,000 fished at 100 per sample, the fitted regression was C = 102.54 − 0.020631P (70 trials). The extrapolated x-intercept gave a population estimate of 4,970, very close to the known 5,000.
- The regression was well established after as few as ten samples.
- The method requires that fishing significantly depletes the population, a closed population, constant effort, and constant catchability.

**2. Timing of reliable Schnabel estimates**
- A "good" estimate was defined as within about ±5% of true population size, and it had to level off over successive days.
- For P = 5,000 with sample size 10 (sampling rate 0.2%), a good estimate took about 57 days. For sample size 40 (0.8%), about 42 days. For sample size 100 (2.0%), about 24 days.
- Across the experiments, a daily catch of about 2% of P gave a good estimate in about 20 days, while 0.1% of P daily required about 80 days.

**3. Assumptions of mark-and-recapture estimators**
- The five assumptions are: marks are retained and recognized; marked fish mix randomly or effort is proportional to density; marked and unmarked fish are equally catchable; the population is closed; and mortality or emigration is proportionate for marked and unmarked fish.
- Fin-clipping was judged preferable to tagging because it is less likely to change recapture susceptibility.
- Spring or fall sampling in temperate waters, with intensive netting over about 30 to 50 days, helps meet these assumptions.

**4. Bias from pooling size classes in electrofishing surveys**
- A hypothetical population of 16,200 fish, with shocker efficiency of 5 to 20% by size group, produced a pooled Petersen estimate of 13,423, which underestimates the true population by 17%.
- Using the Pigeon River trout data (about 10,000 brook and brown trout over 2 inches in 4.8 miles, or 24.1 acres; Cooper, 1952), the authors modeled size-specific efficiency with a regression, Y = −5 + 4.22X, and built two hypothesized size-frequency distributions.
- For the simple unimodal distribution (Type A), estimates with four size groups were 9.5% low (9,055 fish); two groups gave 17.8% low; and one group covering 2 to 12.9 inches gave 30.2% low (6,984 fish). The one-inch-class estimates reproduced the true population exactly.
- For the polymodal distribution (Type B), errors were smaller where groupings matched modes. The authors estimate that field size-grouped estimates typically underestimate by about 10 to 20%.

**5. Recommendations**
- Where mark-recapture data allow it, estimate separately within each size class (or habitat stratum) and sum the results.
- Where data are too sparse, compute and apply a theoretical catchability correction.
- Test estimation methods with laboratory models and field data before relying on them.
- Accept that no estimate is free of bias, but that almost any carefully derived correction improves on none.
