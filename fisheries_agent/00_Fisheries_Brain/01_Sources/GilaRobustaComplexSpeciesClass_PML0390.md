# Gila Robusta Complex Species Classification: Critique of Carter et al. (2016) and Reexamination of Diagnostic Keys

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The source is a packet of three related items. Its core is a 2016 peer-style study, [[Carter et al. 2016]], that tested whether the [[Minckley and DeMarais Key]] can reliably separate three chubs of the [[Gila Robusta Complex]]: [[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Nigra]] and [[Gila Intermedia]]. The packet also includes a critique of that study by a second group of authors (the critique is undated, but it responds to Carter et al. and to the [[AFS/ASIH Names Committee]] decision), and a 2016 email on ongoing genomic work by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

Carter et al. found that the key does not reliably separate the three taxa. Observers using the key identified 54.1% of specimens correctly, and [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] (DFA) assigned 73.3% correctly. The authors concluded that the three forms should be reexamined, possibly as a single polymorphic species. The critique argues that the study design was flawed, that its key was misapplied, and that the study's own results support keeping [[Gila Intermedia]] as a valid taxon whatever happens to [[Gila Nigra]]. It also argues that the complex must remain listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic history:** The complex has been treated as two subspecies of *G. robusta*, as two species with one subspecies, as two species with one intergrade, and, most recently, as three species. [[Gila Intermedia]] is listed as Endangered with a draft recovery plan. [[Gila Nigra]] and the lower Colorado River Distinct Population Segment of [[Gila Robusta]] were proposed for Threatened status in 2015.
- **Morphological basis:** The key uses two morphometric ratios (head length/least caudal peduncle depth, HL/LDCP; caudal peduncle length/least depth, CPL/LDCP) and three meristic counts (dorsal fin rays, DFR; anal fin rays, AFR; lateral line scales, LLS). The source describes the three forms as phenotypically similar and not genetically distinguishable at the species level.
- **Study area:** [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] drainages in Arizona. Fish were collected October 7 to December 3, 2014, by backpack electrofishing, collapsible hoop nets and hook-and-line. Specimens were at least about 100 mm total length, to reduce allometric bias.
- **Earlier work:** [[Rinne 1976]] and [[Rinne 1969 Thesis]] supplied the reference morphometric data used for comparison. [[Dowling et al. 2015]] found that microsatellite data could not separate the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range loss:** [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listing documents report that 85–90% of the historical range of each formerly separate species is now unoccupied. The critique argues that lumping the three taxa roughly triples the range of a single species but still leaves the complex with an 85–90% loss.
- **Sympatry:** Observers assigned at least two of the three assumed species at every locality, and all three at 86.0% of localities. The authors state that this either reflects real sympatry or misidentification of a single species at each site.
- **Local examples:** Two sites were examined in detail. [[Aravaipa Creek]] was assumed to contain *G. robusta*, but HL/LDCP means pointed to *G. nigra*. The [[East Verde River]] was assumed to contain *G. nigra*, but HL/LDCP means pointed to *G. intermedia*.
- **Comparable taxa:** The critique cites other difficult-to-separate pairs in the southwest, including *Cyprinella lutrensis*/*C. formosa*, *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*/*P. sonoriensis*, and *Rhinichthys osculus*/*Tiaroga cobitis*. The last pair co-occurs, and *T. cobitis* is federally listed as endangered.

## Key Findings

### Carter et al. (2016) study results

- **Sample:** 89 specimens were identified by observers (G. robusta n=30, G. nigra n=29, G. intermedia n=30; 5 capture locations each). Each specimen was identified an average of 5 times (range 2–7), giving 451 identifications by 10 observers. Mean total length was 146.5 mm (range 111–220 mm).
- **Observer accuracy:** Overall correct identification was 54.1% (244/451). Per species: *G. intermedia* 67.9%, *G. robusta* 62.7%, *G. nigra* 32.7%. *G. nigra* was most often called *G. intermedia* (45.1%) and less often *G. robusta* (22.2%). No species was identified perfectly.
- **Observer bias:** ANOVA found significant differences in ratios and counts among observers and among species (p<0.0001). The authors nonetheless reported no clear observer bias, with each observer performing at a level that did not differ from random chance.
- **DFA sample:** 162 specimens (G. robusta n=59 from 6 locations; G. nigra n=45 from 5 locations; G. intermedia n=58 from 7 locations), mean total length 137.1 mm (range 103–205 mm), 18 capture locations overall.
- **DFA accuracy:** Three-species DFA correctly assigned 73.3% of fish (60.0% better than chance). Per species: *G. robusta* 83.9%, *G. intermedia* 76.5%, *G. nigra* 55.8%.
- **Pairwise DFA:** *G. robusta* vs *G. intermedia* 94.5% (highest separation); *G. robusta* vs *G. nigra* 85.0%; *G. intermedia* vs *G. nigra* 77.0%. The authors describe the last two as low-confidence assignments.
- **Univariate tests:** Meristic characters and most ratios differed significantly among species, except that the two ratios did not separate *G. intermedia* from *G. nigra*. Among the meristic characters, DFR, AFR and LLS were the best separators of canonical axis I.
- **Fin-ray evidence from Aravaipa Creek:** Rinne's data (n=79) gave 9 DFR in 85.0% and 8 in 15.0% of fish. The observer analysis had 9 DFR in 91.2% and 8 in 5.9%. Observer classifications were 56.0% *G. robusta*, 23.0% *G. nigra* and 21.0% *G. intermedia*.
- **Fin-ray evidence from East Verde River:** Rinne's data (n=107) gave 9 DFR in 94.0% and 8 in 5.6%. HL/LDCP means from this study (2.81–2.85) placed the population in *G. intermedia*. Observer classifications were 43.3% *G. intermedia*, 30.0% *G. nigra* and 27.0% *G. robusta*.

### Critique of Carter et al. (2016)

1. **Misapplied key:** The key calls for population sample means of HL/LDCP, but observers classified individual specimens. The critique argues that the poor observer results therefore reflect misapplication, not failure of the key. It also acknowledges that using population means is non-traditional.
2. **Unequal measurement:** Each specimen was measured an average of 5 times (range 2–7) by different observers, so the design is unbalanced.
3. **Measurement repeatability:** The ANOVA results suggest observers did not agree on basic measurements. The critique notes that measurements were made with a ruler rather than calipers, and suggests that repeated freezing and thawing and lab conditions may also have affected results.
4. **Meristic variation:** The critique argues that individual fish have fixed fin-ray and scale counts, so variation between counts reflects observers, not fish. It says this variation was not analysed to resolve the question.
5. **Rejection of the null:** The critique says that the statistical tests rejected the hypothesis that the three forms are one species, and that Carter et al. did not acknowledge this.
6. **Validity of *G. intermedia*:** Even if *G. nigra* is not supported, Carter et al. did not test the validity of *G. robusta* or *G. intermedia*, and their own results do not show that these taxa are invalid.
7. **Process:** The critique argues that the [[AFS/ASIH Names Committee]] should have waited for peer-reviewed publication before issuing its decision, and that the process should serve as an example of what to avoid.

### Discussion and management conclusions

- The authors argue that the key does not reliably differentiate the species. Both observer and DFA results show that misclassification is high and that ratios and counts overlap, which they attribute to [[Phenotypic Plasticity]] and to limits of the characters themselves.
- Using population means in the DFA can classify a population as a species other than the one assumed, for example *Aravaipa Creek* and the *East Verde River* above.
- Carter et al. state that the keyed characters cannot be used with confidence to separate three distinct species, and that either the three species co-occur at most localities or the key misassigns individuals.
- Carter et al. conclude that the single-polymorphic-species option should be reexamined. They reject the option that *G. nigra* is an intergrade or subspecies of *G. robusta* and recommend urgent resolution because of the listing status of the taxa.

### Genetic follow-up (2016 email)

- The Arizona Game and Fish Department contracted the University of Hawaii for an ezRAD genomic scan. That work was completed in July 2016 and identified markers for future targeted sequencing and SNP analysis. The department was also collecting fin clips from all extant Arizona populations, with plans to publish the genomic and morphological results in 2016 or 2017.
- The email's author expressed doubt that the planned approach would resolve the taxonomy and wondered about funding.
