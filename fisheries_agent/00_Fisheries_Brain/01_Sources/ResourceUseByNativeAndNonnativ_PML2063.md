# Colorado River Fish Predation, Nonnative Species and Bibliographic Appendices

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of appendices from a study on the impact of non-native fishes on native fishes of the Lower Colorado River. It contains a species search-term list (Appendix I), a bibliography of reports documenting non-native fish predation on native Colorado River fishes (Appendix III), a set of database bibliographic records (Appendix IV, Table 4, and Appendix M, Table 6), and a meta-analysis literature list (Appendix 5). The central theme is that nonnative fishes, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]], are widely cited as a primary cause of [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment failure and of declines in native Colorado River fishes. OCR artifacts are present throughout and have been silently corrected where obvious.

## Background & Description

**Appendix I (search terms)** lists 6 native and 40 non-native fish families/species of the Lower Colorado River:
- **Native (13 species, 8 families):** [[Pacific Tenpounder]] (Elopis affinis), [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha), [[Bonytail]] (Gila elegans), [[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta), [[Woundfin]] (Plagopterus argentissimus), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis), [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus), [[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius), [[Striped Mullet]] (Mugil cephalus).
- **Non-native (approximately 30 species across about 10 families):** including salmonids, cyprinids, ictalurids, poeciliids, centrarchids and cichlids, such as [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Blue Tilapia]].

**Appendix III (predation literature)** is organized into three categories:
1. *Published reports, direct evidence* (9 entries) - mostly studies of razorback sucker larvae and predation by ictalurid catfishes, cyprinids and centrarchids.
2. *Published reports, indirect evidence* (6 entries) - including laboratory predation by odonate nymphs and predator-avoidance studies.
3. *General references* (about 14 entries) - reviews such as Minckley (1991), Miller (1961) and Schoenherr (1981), and the draft [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery plan (Tyus 1997).

**Appendix IV / M (database records)** contain bibliographic abstracts from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and dissertation databases on topics such as [[Lake Mead]] and [[Deer Island Lake]] fish ecology, [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] fish studies, [[Humpback Chub]] biology, and [[Razorback Sucker]] nutrition.

**Appendix 5 (meta-analysis)** is a reference list of about 60 methodological meta-analysis sources (e.g., Hunter & Schmidt 1990; Rosenthal 1991; Gurevitch & Hedges-type syntheses), plus a small number of ecological meta-analysis examples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Documented decline and replacement:** Multiple sources state that native Colorado River fish abundance declined as nonnative fishes increased. [[Dill]] (1944) noted that post-1930 native declines in the Lower Colorado River coincided with large increases of [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Schoenherr (1981) considered evidence "overwhelming" that native fishes were replaced by aggressive introduced species. Miller (1961) associated the changing fish fauna of the American Southwest with replacement by introduced fishes.
- **Predation on larvae:** Marsh and Brooks (1989) identified predation by ictalurid catfishes as a deterrent to re-establishment of introduced [[Razorback Sucker]]. Johnson and Hines (1998) examined how [[Suspended Sediment]] affects larval razorback sucker vulnerability to [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] predation. Karp and Tyus (1990) reported that [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] may attack razorback sucker larvae.
- **Little Colorado River:** Marsh and Douglas (1997) documented predation by introduced fishes on endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native species.
- **Habitat and flow:** Studies in the Green River and Yampa River link [[Colorado Squawfish]] recruitment to flow regimes, nursery habitat availability and biotic interactions (Bestgen 1997; Rakowski 1997). Vaill (1996) linked water development to a 29% decrease in peak flows upstream and 38% downstream, with 12-29% loss of instream and backwater habitat.
- **Water quality and toxicology:** Beyers (1993) estimated median lethal concentrations of technical [[Carbaryl]] and [[Malathion]] for Colorado Squawfish (1.31 and 9.14 mg/L, respectively) and [[Bonytail]] (2.02 and 15.3 mg/L).
- **Limnology:** A study of the lower Colorado River (Broadway and Hergesell 1978) sampled over one year for physicochemical and phytoplankton variables.

## Key Findings

- **Primary conclusion of the cited literature:** Multiple reviews (Mueller 1995; Minckley et al. 1991; Tyus and Saunders 1996; Tyus 1997) attribute [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment failure to predation by nonnative fishes, while Johnson et al. (1993) showed that native larvae recognized Mississippi River predators poorly, indicating limited defensive behavior.
- **Nutrition as a contributing factor:** Horn (1996) found substantial seasonal variability in larval razorback sucker condition at [[Lake Mohave]] and suggested that nutrition acts in concert with predation rather than alone, with 23-78% of larvae showing slowed growth (values as reported in the abstract).
- **Colorado Squawfish recruitment:** Bestgen (1997) found that size-selective predation by large [[Red Shiner]] reduced survival of early-hatched cohorts; the author concluded that non-native fishes may substantially affect age-0 recruitment.
- **Humpback Chub identification:** Muth (1990) developed artificial keys separating larvae of [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] using meristic and morphometric characters.
- **Habitat preference:** Barrett (1992) found velocity and depth preferences of smallmouth bass were transferable among sites, but substrate preferences were not; roundtail chub preferences differed between two streams.
- **Desert pupfish and mosquitofish:** Jennings (1986) showed both species can self-regulate population numbers via emigration relative to available resources; McMahon (1984) found emigration regulated pupfish populations in open pools.
- **Mullet ecology:** Cardona (1994) identified three mullet assemblages in Mediterranean estuaries, linked to salinity, oxygen and nitrite levels, and found *Liza saliens* most common in high-quality waters.
- **Overall statement:** The document is largely a bibliographic and methodological compilation; it does not present new primary field data beyond the cited abstracts. Several records contain OCR errors and incomplete entries, so the individual numeric results should be checked against the original sources before use.
