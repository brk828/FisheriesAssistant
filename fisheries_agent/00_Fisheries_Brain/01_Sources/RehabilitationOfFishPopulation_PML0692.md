# Rehabilitation of Fish Populations with Toxicants: A Symposium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-toxicants #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled symposium, edited by Paul H. Eschmeyer and published by the [[North Central Division]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]] (Special Publication No. 4, May 1975), presents papers from a 1973 session at the 35th Midwest Fish and Wildlife Conference in St. Louis. It reviews the use of [[Fish Toxicants]] (piscicides) for [[Fish Population Rehabilitation]], covering regional usage statistics, historical development, marketing and registration barriers, toxicological testing protocols, counteraction (detoxification) methods, and case studies from Wisconsin, Michigan, and Indiana. The volume is largely pro-management, but one contribution (Becker) is a sharp critique of large-scale treatments in Wisconsin.

## Background & Description

The symposium was developed at the request of fishery managers to highlight the role of toxicants, document their use and development, and identify associated problems. Common fish names follow the American Fisheries Society's third (1970) edition of Special Publication No. 6. Chapters are:

- Summary on the Use of Toxicants in the Midwest (A. C. Lopinot)
- History of Fish Toxicants in the United States (Kenneth B. Cumming)
- Problems in Marketing a Fish Toxicant (Gilbert C. Radonski)
- Toxicological Protocol for the Development of Piscicides (Leif L. Marking)
- Counteracting Chemicals Used in Fishery Operations (Verdel K. Dawson)
- Fish Toxification: Biological Sanity or Insanity? (George Becker)
- Use of Fish Toxicants in Wisconsin, 1941-73 (John H. Klingbiel)
- Evaluation of Angler-Use Benefits in Michigan (E. John Trimberger)
- Renovation of Cagles Mill Reservoir and Its Tributaries (Gary E. Hudson)
- Effect of Renovation on Fish Harvest in J. C. Murphy Lake (Robert Robertson)

The main toxicants discussed are [[Rotenone]], [[Antimycin]], [[Toxaphene]], [[TFM]], [[Copper Sulfate]], [[Endrin]], [[Sodium Cyanide]], [[Squoxin]], [[Bayluscide]], and [[Thanite]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional scale of use (Midwest, 1963-72):** Treatments covered 6,107 waters totaling 121,087 surface acres, plus nearly 4,200 miles of streams. Complete treatments numbered 5,597 waters (89,588 acres); partial treatments 377 waters (22,462 acres); selective treatments 133 waters (9,037 acres). About 82% of treatments were considered successful. The Midwest contains more than 6 million acres of lakes, reservoirs, and ponds, excluding the Great Lakes and Mississippi backwaters.

**Drivers of rehabilitation:** Cumming attributes the need for control to transplanted and introduced species (e.g., carp, brown trout, goldfish), impoundment and reservoir construction, pond building, rising fishing pressure, and water pollution that favored rough fish. Reservoir storage rose from under 10 million acre-feet in 1900 to more than 359 million acre-feet.

**Environmental concerns:** Several chapters address nontarget effects, persistence of residues (notably toxaphene), and cumulative impacts on biodiversity. Becker's chapter documents declines in species richness and abundance after treatments and reports the death of thousands of ducks in 1973 from botulism following Rock River treatment.

**Regulatory context:** The [[Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act]] (FIFRA), the [[Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act]] (FFDCA), the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA), and the [[Federal Environmental Pesticide Control Act]] of 1972 (FEPCA) progressively tightened registration and use. Only four piscicides were registered for nonfood use by 1973 (antimycin, rotenone, TFM, Bayluscide).

## Key Findings

**Toxicant use and status**
- In 1972, more than 22,000 gallons of 2-1/2% rotenone were used across Midwest states and provinces, the most popular toxicant (used by 12 states or provinces). Antimycin was used by 12 states or provinces.
- Restrictions were imposed in seven states; no variance is required in any reporting state, though Ontario and Manitoba require one.
- Antimycin was first used in Wisconsin in 1963 and is now available in several Fintrol formulations from Ayerst Laboratories.

**Historical development**
- Copper sulfate was probably the first deliberately applied fish toxicant in the US (1914, Vermont, 65-acre lake). Rotenone was first used in Michigan in 1934.
- Chlorinated hydrocarbons (toxaphene, endrin) were used in the 1950s, then abandoned because of persistence and toxicity. The US Fish and Wildlife Service and several states voluntarily discontinued toxaphene in 1963.
- The Fish Control Laboratory at La Crosse, Wisconsin was established in 1959.

**Regulation and marketing**
- Of about 11 chemicals registered for fishery use, only four piscicides were registered for nonfood use. Clearance costs are high; one example cited a loss of $2.1 million that would have grown to $5.5 million in lost revenue.
- Radonski argues that Fintrol's single-use market could not justify registration costs, making federal data-gathering support essential.

**Toxicological protocol (Marking)**
- Standard acute tests use rainbow trout, channel catfish, and bluegill, with eggs of rainbow trout; LC50 values are the reference metric.
- Chronic testing favors time-independent LC50s over 30-day exposures; residual toxicity is expressed as half-life of biological activity.

**Counteraction**
- Techniques reviewed include microorganisms, coagulants, ultraviolet light, chemical deactivators (notably potassium permanganate and chlorine), and adsorbents (activated carbon is the most efficient general adsorbent).
- Antimycin's biological half-life ranged from 3 to 10 hours at pH 6.0 to 1.5 hours at pH 10.0; its photodegradation half-life is only a few minutes in sunlight.

**Case studies**
- *Wisconsin (Klingbiel):* More than 300 lakes (over 30,000 acres) and 1,600 miles of streams were treated during 1941-73. Wisconsin managers judged about 80% of pre-1970 projects successful; 13% were re-treated, on average 8.1 years after initial application.
- *Wisconsin (Becker):* Opposition led to a 1971 lawsuit against the DNR over the Rock River basin program (2,902 miles of streams, 100,400 acres of marshes, 39,940 acres of lakes and ponds; estimated cost near $5 million). Becker reports reduced species counts in the Tomorrow River and Sidie Hollow Creek, the loss of a rare population of longear sunfish, and mussel mortality.
- *Michigan (Trimberger):* Since 1960, 23,135 acres of lakes and 840 miles of streams were treated. Chemically treated marginal trout streams in Region III drew 59.1 angler days per acre in 1970, versus 53.6 on unstocked trout streams. Partial warm-water lake treatments produced 32.4 angler days per acre versus 13.6 on unmanaged warm-water lakes.
- *Indiana, Cagles Mill Reservoir (Hudson):* The reservoir was drained and treated in October 1971 at a total cost of $21,000; about 1,100 adult bass, 120 flathead catfish and 142 channel catfish were salvaged, and game fish dominated by September 1972.
- *Indiana, J. C. Murphy Lake (Robertson):* Before renovation, the catch averaged 1.2 fish per hour (67% bullheads). After the 1963 renovation, it averaged 0.23 fish per hour (82% bluegills), with more than 728,000 fish caught from December 1964 to 1973; more than 50% were taken through the ice.

**Becker's policy conclusions (Wisconsin)**
- Toxicants should be used only as a last resort, in confined areas, and after comprehensive planning, including pre- and post-treatment surveys, protection of unique and rare species, and environmental impact assessment.
- Recommended research includes long-term effects on nontarget organisms and the toxicity of degradation products such as blastmycic acid.
