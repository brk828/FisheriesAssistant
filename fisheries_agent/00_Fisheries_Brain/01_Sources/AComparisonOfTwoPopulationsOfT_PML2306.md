# Comparison of Two Populations of the Endangered Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*): First Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This first annual report by [[Allan A. Schoenherr]] compares two populations of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in Riverside County, California. The study contrasts a constant-temperature population at [[Oasis Spring]], introduced in 1977, with a natural population in [[Salt Creek]], which experiences daily and seasonal temperature fluctuation. The work was conducted under contract to the [[California Department of Fish and Game]]. The central question was whether the Oasis Spring fish have lost the remarkable thermal tolerance recorded by [[Lowe and Heath 1969]].

Key outcomes include a trend toward reduced cold tolerance in the constant-temperature population, year-round breeding at Oasis Spring, and a [[Flash Flood]] on 9 June 1990 that removed most large pupfish and submerged vegetation from Salt Creek. The flood was followed by an influx of [[Non-Native Species]], including [[Tilapia Zillii]], a potentially serious threat to one of only two natural populations in California.

## Background & Description

*Cyprinodon macularius* is a small desert fish. Its populations in this study were compared on the basis of life history, population structure, water chemistry and thermal tolerance.

- **Oasis Spring:** The population has been held at a constant temperature of 24–29 °C since its introduction in 1977. Earlier fieldwork by the author (1988–1989) suggested that the upper spring and middle pool were subsets of one large population, so the 1990 length data were drawn entirely from the middle pool.
- **Salt Creek:** One of the two known "natural" populations, located about 2 km west of Oasis Spring. Its water temperature fluctuates daily and seasonally.
- **Thousand Palms Oasis:** Fish introduced there in 1987 from Salt Creek. They were used for Salt Creek thermal experiments after a June flood sharply reduced the Salt Creek population. The author argued that their short time in a fluctuating environment means they likely retained tolerances similar to the parental stock.

### Methods

- **Trapping:** Quarterly visits during the 1990 fiscal year. Fish were captured in wire minnow traps baited with canned cat food (12 traps for 24 hours at Salt Creek, per Table 2).
- **Physical parameters:** Temperature (quick-recording thermometers), pH (electronic meter), and dissolved oxygen (a continuously recording instrument, 24-hour record on one occasion). Water samples were analyzed for salinity and basic mineral ions at Fullerton College.
- **Length-frequency analysis:** Total length was measured to the nearest millimetre. Where a sample had fewer than 100 fish, data were converted to percentages.
- **Thermal tolerance:** 30 fish from each population were tested at the [[Claremont College]] laboratory. Four experiments were run after acclimation to 29 °C:
  1. **[[Critical Thermal Maximum]] (CTMax):** Water warmed at 0.5 °C per minute until loss of equilibrium.
  2. **[[Critical Thermal Minimum]] (CTMin):** Cooled at 10 °C for the first hour from 30 °C, then 5 °C per hour until fish no longer responded to prodding.
  3. **TLEW:** Time to loss of equilibrium when moved directly to 42 °C.
  4. **TLEC:** Time to loss of equilibrium when moved directly to 10 °C.

Fish were not killed in these experiments.

Oasis Spring data are described as essentially constant, drawn from numerous samples in 1989 and 1990.

### Oasis Spring Decline

The lower spring at Oasis Spring suffered a serious decline in 1990. Dissolved oxygen was 0.01 ppm on 9 June 1990, and remaining fish were seen gulping at the surface. Caretakers resolved the problem by 25 November 1990 by clearing aquatic roots from the outlet and cutting a trench through a downstream marsh. Improved flow then allowed the standpipe to be raised about 5 cm, which restored adequate oxygen. The author stressed that this problem needs careful future attention.

### Salt Creek Flood and Habitat

- **Flood:** Provisional U.S. Geological Survey data showed flow at the mouth of Salt Creek rising from a base of 0.9 cfs at 1700 hrs on 9 June 1990 to 1,080 cfs a few hours later. Flow did not return to normal until 13 June.
- **Aftermath:** On 16 June only one pupfish was collected, and submerged vegetation had been stripped from the area.
- **Vegetation:** Before the flood, the best trapping sites were associated with mats of [[Zannichellia Pondweed]] (*Zannichellia palustris*). After the flood, a cyanobacterium, [[Lyngbya Cyanobacteria]] (*Lyngbya* sp.), became the most abundant aquatic vegetation. It forms thick mats in muddy sediment that provide refuge, but juvenile cichlids also use this cover.
- **Riparian plants** (listed by estimated abundance): salt cedar (*Tamarix ramosissima*), cattail (*Typha domingensis*), iodine bush (*Allenrolfea occidentalis*), saltgrass (*Distichlis spicata*), giant reed (*Arundo donax*), inkweed (*Suaeda torreyana*), and honey mesquite (*Prosopis glandulosa*).

### Animals in Salt Creek (Table 2)

Six species plus invertebrates were recorded. Pupfish counts for Salt Creek were 119 (30 Mar), 110 (9 Jun), 1 (16 Jun), 57 (8 Sep) and 35 (24 Nov). Non-native fishes include [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), and [[Tilapia Zillii]] (*Tilapia zillii*). The study also recorded [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus natalis*), crayfish (*Procambarus* sp.) and prawns (*Macrobrachium* sp.).

## Key Findings

### 1. Length-Frequency and Growth

- At Oasis Spring, histograms from October 1988 through 1990 were essentially the same, indicating **year-round breeding** in the constant-temperature environment. The middle-pool sample for 31 March 1990 had N = 176.
- At Salt Creek, fish grew about **6–10 mm between March and June 1990**. The June sample also showed recruitment of small fish, probably from the onset of spring reproduction.
- Post-flood Salt Creek samples showed a similar size distribution, with no clear sign of growth. The author attributed this possibly to a small number of breeding adults, and to sampling bias.
- The author concluded that reproduction at Salt Creek **appeared to cease during the cold winter months**, with at least one more year of study needed.

Note: the source table is partly garbled. Values above are read against the Conclusions text, which gives Oasis Spring as 5.3–42.7 °C and Thousand Palms as 4.4–42.0 °C. The trial-2 thermal scope and TLEW/TLEC assignments are approximate.

- The Thousand Palms fish had a **CTMin of 4.4 °C, the lowest ever reported for the species**.
- Thousand Palms fish lasted longer when moved directly to 10 °C than Oasis Spring fish did.
- The thermal scope difference between populations was judged **probably not significant**.
- Compared with [[Quitobaquito Spring]] fish in Arizona (Lowe and Heath 1969), the California populations had a similar thermal scope but the Arizona fish had higher CTMax and CTMin.
- All differences were within 2 °C, but the author identified a **trend toward loss of cold tolerance** at constant-temperature Oasis Spring after about 13 generations of isolation (since 1987).

### 3. Effects of the Flash Flood

- The flood **challenged two long-held assumptions**: that floods wash out small fish, and that they favor native over non-native species. Here large pupfish were removed and small fish later repopulated the site, while non-native fish became more common.
- Non-native fishes appear to have washed down from upstream, near the Nature Conservancy facility at Dos Palmas.
- The author noted that no natural pupfish population is known to have been eliminated by flooding, though the possibility cannot be ignored.
- The flood-related loss of pondweed removed cover and spawning substrate, a loss the author considered profound for the pupfish.

### 4. Threats and Non-Native Species

- The appearance of [[Tilapia Zillii]] in pupfish habitat, where it had not been recorded before, was described as a **serious threat**. Elsewhere in the desert, this species has been strongly implicated in Desert Pupfish declines.
- Low dissolved oxygen during hot weather, or cold winter nights, might eliminate the non-native fishes. On 25 November, when water temperature dropped to 10 °C, large adult cichlids were nearly moribund.
- A proposed [[Trash Train Spill Risk]] passing within a few hundred metres upstream adds to concern for the Salt Creek population.

### 5. Conclusions and Recommendations

- Differences between populations **reflect the contrast between constant and fluctuating temperature regimes**.
- The Salt Creek population "makes constant monitoring worthwhile," given that it is one of only two natural California populations.
- Habitat modifications at Oasis Spring should **include options for variation in flow and temperature**. Elongating stream channels and building additional downstream pools could help avoid a [[Genetic Bottleneck]].
