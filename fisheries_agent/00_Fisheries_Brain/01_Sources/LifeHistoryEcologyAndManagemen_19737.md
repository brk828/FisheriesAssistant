# Life History, Ecology, and Management of the Smallmouth Buffalo (*Ictiobus bubalus*) with Reference to Elephant Butte Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Agricultural Experiment Station monograph (Research Report 261, Las Cruces, September 1973, by [[Douglas B. Jester]]) synthesizes eight years (August 1963 to March 1972) of field and commercial investigations on [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] in [[Elephant Butte Lake]], combined with a review of the published literature. The study covers taxonomy, range, age and growth, survival, length-weight relationships, condition, reproduction, food, population dynamics, movement, disease, commercial value, and harvest management.

The central conclusion is that buffalo can be managed as a commercial resource on a sustained-yield basis. Commercial harvest reduced buffalo density and biomass, while [[River Carpsucker]] and [[Common Carp]] partly replaced them on shallow shoals. The author recommends a sustained harvest of 12,000 to 15,000 fish per year, aiming for an equilibrium population of about 600,000 fish, to meet the goal of enhancing sport fishing while utilizing "rough fish" resources.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and classification.** The species was first described as *Catostomus (Ictiobus) bubalus* by [[Constantine Rafinesque]] in 1820 and became *Ictiobus bubalus* in 1929 after taxonomic revisions. Confusion with the [[Black Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus niger*) was resolved only about 110 years after Rafinesque's descriptions. The fish belongs to Family Catostomidae, Subfamily Ictiobinae, Order Cypriniformes. No subspecies are recognized. A key to *Ictiobus* species is included in the original report.

**Species complex.** Three buffalo fishes are currently recognized: the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), the Black Buffalo, and the Smallmouth Buffalo.

**Range.** The species is native to the Rio Grande and Pecos River in New Mexico and was introduced into central Arizona in 1918. The authors extend the western boundary to Montana, New Mexico, and central Arizona. In the Rio Grande, the species is the westernmost indigenous drainage for the fauna.

**Elephant Butte Lake checklist.** Table 1 lists about 40 fish species known from the lake, including gizzard shad, northern pike, carp, river carpsucker, channel and flathead catfish, largemouth bass, walleye, and white bass. Species marked as rare include rainbow and brown trout, white sucker, goldfish, and yellow perch.

**Study samples.** Samples came from three main sources:
- [[Patterson]] (1968): 363 fish, April 1966 to March 1967.
- [[Moody]] (1970): 695 fish, April 1968 to March 1970.
- Jester's own program: 115,870 buffalo caught from August 1963 to March 1972.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Age and growth.** Scale-based ageing was validated against Lagler's criteria. Growth rates in Elephant Butte Lake rose after commercial harvest reduced density. Mean annual growth increments increased, and the age at which year classes became 100 percent vulnerable to six-inch stretch-mesh gill nets fell from eight to five years. Neither [[Lee's Phenomenon]] nor sexual dimorphism in length growth was detected.

**Growth comparison.** Growth rates and maximum sizes were compared across 24 waters in nine states. Buffalo in Elephant Butte Lake were among the slowest-growing in 1966-67 and reached an intermediate position by 1969-70. The largest fish recorded from the lake, 688 and 687 mm, is the fourth-largest reported in age-growth studies. The largest reported overall was from the [[Neosho River]], Oklahoma.

**Survival.** Survival declines exponentially with age. Mean life span is 1.02 years (372 days), and about 2 of 1,000,000 fish hatched reach Age Group XV. Estimated hypothetical maximum longevity extends to Age Group XXI, but the oldest observed fish was in Age Group XV.

**Length-weight relationship.** The relationship was essentially constant between 1966-67 and 1969-70, showing that weight-at-length was not affected by reduced density. Females were heavier than males of similar length in Age Groups IX to XIII. Two to 11 years were needed to reach 1,000 g across nine waters. In Elephant Butte Lake, this time fell from nine to six years after three years of commercial fishing.

**Condition.** The mean coefficient of condition (K_TL) was 1.44 for 354 fish in 1966-67 and 1.39 in 1970-71. Values were about average for the species, somewhat below the maximum potential. Condition varied with spawning cycle, with the lowest values in late summer.

**Reproduction.** Spawning in Elephant Butte Lake occurs from early May into September, at temperatures of 19.1 to 27.5 °C, with a peak in July at 22 to 26.5 °C. Fecundity ranged from 18,200 ova per fish in Age Group II to 525,500 in Age Group XV. Age Groups V and VI produced 55 percent of the population's ova. Spawning occurs over all bottom types at 1.2 to 6.1 m depth. Males released milt in every month, suggesting spawning is controlled by the presence of ripe females.

**Food and feeding.** The species is an indiscriminate omnivore, feeding on bottom material, detritus, and invertebrates, with diet controlled by availability. Sand and organic detritus were prominent in gut contents. [[Minckley]] (1969) reported diet data from Apache Lake, Arizona.

**Interspecific interactions.** Buffalo, carpsucker, and carp compete for benthos on shoals less than 11 m deep, which make up about 35 percent of the lake bottom. Buffalo declined as carpsucker increased. This fits a model in which carpsucker replace buffalo as an "opportunist."

**Movement.** Movement was continuous, random, and mostly upstream, which replaced fish removed by harvest. The author found no bona fide migration. Seasonal concentrations occurred in the upper, middle, and lower thirds of the lake.

**Disease.** The only agent observed in Elephant Butte Lake was light infestation by the fungus *Saprolegnia parasitica*, which caused no recorded mortality. Hoffman (1967) lists 29 parasite species from *Ictiobus bubalus*.

**Density comparisons.** Mean catch per net-unit (C_n) in Elephant Butte Lake was about 10 times that of [[Lake Sharpe]] (South Dakota). Biomass-based estimates in Missouri lakes reached 700 lb/acre (780 kg/ha), but this figure was not independently verified by the authors.

## Key Findings

- **Population reduction:** The Schnabel estimator indicated a net reduction of about 222,865 fish (26.4 percent), weighing 245,654 kg, from 843,613 (1964-65) to 620,748 (1971-72). The reduction exceeded the commercial catch (112,627 fish; 203,124 kg, 1963 to 1972).
- **Harvest effects:** Commercial harvest reduced buffalo density, and [[Carpsucker Replacement]] on shoals partly filled the niche. Carpsucker density rose by about 86,000 fish (594 percent) from 1965-66 to 1971-72.
- **Recommended management:** A sustained harvest of 12,000 to 15,000 fish per year, targeting an equilibrium population of about 600,000 fish 250 mm or longer and older than two years, was judged most feasible for combining profitable commercial harvest with rough-fish control and sport fishing enhancement.
- **Gear selectivity:** Five- and six-inch stretch-mesh gill nets gave the best results. Brown nets were superior when the commercial-fish group was the target, while white nets suited buffalo alone. Baiting with five materials did not increase catch.
- **Market value:** Buffalo sold at $0.25 to $0.35 per pound FOB Las Cruces. Taste-panel ratings for flavor averaged 3.3 out of 5 (aroma 2.8). Market expansion depends on a better distribution system for smaller towns.
- **Equilibrium model:** The population-recursion model P(t+1) = 280.538 + 0.598 P(t) − 2.751 C(t) was fitted to 1966-71 data. Unexploited equilibrium is about 697,900 fish, reduced from earlier estimates.
- **Conclusion on Lee's Phenomenon:** Evidence against the phenomenon in buffalo was stronger than evidence for it, though the authors note limited data in some age groups.
- **Commercial product:** Fish are sold mainly to Los Angeles wholesalers. Refrigerated weekly pickups keep fish fresh without preservatives.
