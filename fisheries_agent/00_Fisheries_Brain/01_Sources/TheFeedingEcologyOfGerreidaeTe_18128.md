# Feeding Ecology of Gerreidae in the Kosi System, Northern Natal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gerreidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by D.P. Cyrus and S.J.M. Blaber (University of Natal, Pietermaritzburg; published in *The Lammergeyer* 32, January 1984) describes the food, feeding periodicity and seasonal diet of five [[Gerres]] species of the family [[Gerreidae]] in the [[Kosi System]]. The authors characterise the family as poorly studied and note its local economic importance and its role in the estuarine food web. Sampling at five sites from 1978 to 1980 combined seine netting, benthic grab sampling and stomach-content analysis. The authors report that seasonal variation in diet was limited, that the number of species present declined in winter and spring, and that resource segregation probably arises from differences in diet, feeding periodicity and food superabundance during summer and autumn.

## Background & Description

Gerreidae were described as poorly known in southern Africa, with most information coming from incidental records and general surveys. Six Gerres species occur in southern African coastal waters, according to [[Smith 1965]]. Their range extends as far south as the Zwartkops River in the eastern Cape, though they are uncommon south of the Umtamvuna River in southern Natal. Five species occur in the Kosi system, where three are abundant over shallow sandy areas.

The family is economically important in India and southeast Asia, notably at [[Pulicat Lake]] (Prabhakara Rao, 1968), and locally at Kosi. [[Wallace et al. 1971]] classed two Kosi species as "non-angling species of significance as human food". Long-term management plans for the local fishery provided further impetus for the work.

The study builds on earlier research published separately on Natal estuaries (Cyrus & Blaber, 1983a) and on the diet of fry under 40 mm S.L. (Cyrus & Blaber, 1983b). Related work by the same authors covered reproduction, predation and species identification. The paper notes that the study period is given as July 1978 to July 1980 in the Methods section but as 1978 to 1981 in the Summary; the Methods dates are reported here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** The Kosi system comprises four connected lakes draining to the sea through a usually permanently open estuary. The catchment covers 500 km², the feeder river is 30 km long, the estuarine area is about 3500 ha, and the shoreline is 53.5 km long. Environmental condition was classed as good by [[Begg 1978]].

**Salinity gradients.** Salinity varies among the lakes according to distance from the mouth and freshwater inflow:
- [[Lake Amanzimnyama]]: always fresh.
- [[Lake Nhlange]]: fresh to 5‰ generally, between 0.5 and 1‰ during the study period.
- [[Lake Mpungwini]]: 3 to 6‰.
- [[Lake Makhawulani]]: 7 to 14‰.

At high tide a salt wedge extends from the mouth about 6 km to the entrance of Lake Makhawulani.

**Physical conditions.** Temperatures of 17 to 28 °C were recorded. Average annual rainfall at the Natal Parks Board station (1965 to 1980) was 1035 mm (n = 16, S.D. = 249). The study years received 836 and 912 mm, slightly below average. Kosi is an unusually clear system: turbidity in 1980 ranged from 0.8 to 4.7 Nephelometric Turbidity Units, with little variation across the system.

**Published background.** Mangrove mortality after 1965 flooding was documented by [[Breen & Hill 1969]]. The benthic fauna of Lake Nhlange was surveyed by [[Boltt & Allanson 1975]], and the distribution of the burrowing sandprawn *Callianassa kraussi* by [[Forbes 1979]]. Earlier fish surveys were largely non-quantitative. The authors plan to complete a food web for the fish community once studies of [[Monodactylus]] species are finished.

**Sampling sites and methods.**
- Five sites were sampled quarterly from July 1978 to July 1980 using a 70 m × 2 m × 12 mm bar-mesh seine. The target was a minimum of 30 Gerres per species per site per trip, which was not always achieved.
- Twenty-four-hour seining at the [[Water Level Recorder]] site, with hauls every three hours, was carried out in summer and autumn 1979.
- Benthic samples used an Eckman-type grab (0.023 m²). Two samples of five grabs were taken per seining site in each of the four seasons of 1979, at 10 to 50 m from shore.
- Samples were fixed in 10% formalin with phloxine dye and sorted in the laboratory.

**Diet analysis.** Four methods were applied to stomach contents: frequency of occurrence, numerical occurrence, percentage calorific contribution, and the [[Ricker Points Method]] (1968), which approximates volumetric analysis. Benthic densities were converted to joules per square metre to calculate energy contributions. Feeding selectivity was assessed with [[Ivlev's Electivity Index]] (1961).

## Key Findings

**Species distribution and abundance (Table 1; 1979 seine catch per unit effort)**
- Of the five species, [[Gerres Oblongus]] and [[Gerres Oyena]] were uncommon and occurred almost exclusively at the estuary.
- [[Gerres Acinaces]], [[Gerres Filamentosus]] and [[Gerres Rappi]] occurred throughout the system and were most common in summer and autumn.
- In winter and spring only G. acinaces remained in significant numbers. The other species either left the system or moved off the lake shelves.
- Dominance varied by site: G. rappi in Lake Nhlange, G. filamentosus at the estuary mouth, and G. acinaces in Lakes Makhawulani and Mpungwini.

**Feeding periodicity**
- In summer, G. acinaces fed throughout the day, peaking at 09h00 and lowest at 24h00. G. filamentosus and G. rappi fed mostly in the early evening, and G. rappi also fed at 03h00.
- In autumn, all three species fed continuously, with the lowest fullness between 21h00 and 01h00.
- Fish moved into the estuary basin with the rising tide, and fewer fish were present at low tide. The authors suggest tides may influence feeding periodicity in the estuary.

**Diet of adults and juveniles (> 40 mm S.L.)**
- Overall, [[Polychaeta]] were important at the estuary, [[Hiatula Lunulata]] siphon tips (distal 5 mm) dominated at the Water Level Recorder, Makhawulani and Mpungwini sites, and [[Chironomidae]] larvae at Lake Nhlange.
- Ivlev's test showed that Gerres positively selected bivalve siphon tips.
- Dominant items made up 92% (G. acinaces), 84% (G. oblongus), 88% (G. oyena), 94% (G. filamentosus) and 89% (G. rappi) of energy intake (Table 2).

**Site-specific seasonal diet (Table 3)**
- **Estuary:** Polychaetes dominated for most species through the year. In winter, G. acinaces and G. oyena fed mainly on H. lunulata siphon tips, G. oblongus on calanoid copepods, and G. rappi on *Musculus virgiliae*. G. filamentosus had the most varied diet.
- **Water Level Recorder:** H. lunulata siphon tips were the main item. In spring *[[Hymenosoma Orbiculare]]* rose to 181 m⁻² in the benthos.
- **Makhawulani:** H. lunulata siphon tips dominated throughout the year. In spring the isopod [[Cirolana Fluviatilis]] contributed 24% of diet, coinciding with a rise in its density.
- **Mpungwini:** In summer, G. acinaces and G. rappi relied on H. orbiculare, the only site where this prey was significant. Outside summer, G. acinaces fed mainly on H. lunulata.
- **Lake Nhlange:** Chironomid larvae were the most important prey at all seasons (over 90% in most cases). Chironomid densities peaked at 5995 m⁻² in summer and fell to 137 m⁻² in spring, when G. rappi switched to the copepod *Pseudodiaptomus stuhlmanni*.

**Benthic composition (Table 4; percentage energy contribution of dominant groups)**
- Estuary: polychaetes 98%.
- Water Level Recorder: polychaetes 45%, *C. fluviatilis* 24%, *H. orbiculare* 15%.
- Makhawulani: *C. fluviatilis* 90%.
- Mpungwini: polychaetes 41%, *C. fluviatilis* 16%, *H. orbiculare* 26%.
- Lake Nhlange: chironomid larvae 59%.

**Discussion and interpretation**
- The authors found little seasonal variation in diet. The number of species at most sites declined in winter and spring, except at the estuary and Lake Nhlange.
- Co-occurrence of at least three species during summer and autumn may reflect peak benthic food availability. Staggered feeding peaks may reduce interspecific competition, and "loose association" shoaling may reduce intraspecific competition.
- Estuary benthos is subject to tidal movement and mobile coarse sands, which the authors consider typical of estuaries with such substrates. Stomach contents also contained prey (oligochaetes, juvenile *Solen corneus*, terebellid tentacles) absent from estuary grabs, indicating feeding in upstream areas at low tide.
- Polychaetes appear to be the single most important food item for the family in comparable studies, though bivalve siphon tips had not previously been reported in Gerres diets.
- Five species coexist sympatrically, with resource segregation probably arising from diet differences, feeding periodicity and food superabundance. In winter, when food may be limiting, most species leave the shelf areas and only G. acinaces remains.

**Related species context.** The authors note that the penetration of estuaries by the freshwater cichlid [[Sarotherodon Mossambicus]] was discussed by [[Whitfield & Blaber 1979]], and that other Kosi fish studies covered mullet, Carangidae, Sphyraenidae and Ambassidae.
