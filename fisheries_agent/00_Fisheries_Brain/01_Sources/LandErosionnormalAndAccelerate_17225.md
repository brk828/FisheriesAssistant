# Normal and Accelerated Erosion: 1941 Symposium Papers and Discussion

#geomorphology #hydrology #soil-erosion

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1941 *Transactions, American Geophysical Union* symposium section titled "Dynamics of Erosion in a New Land." It contains two lead papers, by [[C. F. Stewart Sharpe]] (not reproduced in full here) and [[Reed W. Bailey]], followed by a discussion by several specialists. The central question is how to separate **normal (geologic) erosion** from **accelerated erosion** caused by human disturbance. Bailey argues that plant cover governs soil stability and infiltration, and that flood disasters in the semiarid West often trace to depleted vegetation rather than to climate alone. Field data from the [[Wasatch Mountains]] and the [[Colorado Plateau]] support this, and the discussants debate definitions and related processes. Discussion contributions also cover volcanic erosion, mud-flows, [[Density Currents]] in reservoirs, and proposed new terminology.

## Background & Description

The document has three main parts:

- **Bailey's "Land-Erosion: Normal and Accelerated, in the Semiarid West."** Bailey is with the Climatic and Physiographic Division, Office of Research, [[Soil Conservation Service]]. He frames flood and erosion problems as a result of three factor groups:
  - (A) precipitation amount and distribution;
  - (B) basin and stream-channel characteristics;
  - (C) edaphic conditions, including soil mantle and plant cover.
- **Discussion of the papers.** Contributors include [[Jay A. Bonsteel]], [[E. G. Zies]], [[C. R. Hursh]], [[George B. Cressey]], [[Peter E. Wolfe]], [[Robert T. Knapp]] (with [[Hugh Stevens Bell]] on density currents), and [[Maurice Donnelly]].
- **Reference lists.** Each paper cites roughly 10–12 works, including [[Kirk Bryan]] (1925, arroyo cutting), [[W. C. Lowdermilk]] (1930, 1935, acceleration of erosion above geologic norms), [[Carl Sauer]] (1929), and [[Reed W. Bailey]] et al. (1934, floods in northern Utah).

Bailey's stated purpose is an accurate problem analysis before control programs are chosen. He notes that structures can confine floods but will not reduce flood frequency or stop silt entering reservoirs, and that watershed rehabilitation would be futile where floods are naturally driven by storms and steep gradients.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Slope stability and vegetation**

- Bailey measured maximum stable slope angles in the semiarid region, including Utah and Idaho.
- Non-vegetated talus stood at 68–80 per cent gradient (about 36°).
- Vegetated slopes on fine-textured soils, derived from the same parent material, stood at up to about 60° (173 per cent).
- Bailey concludes that the soil mantle persists on steep slopes only through interdependent development of plant cover and soil profile. Plant cover binds soil and maintains high infiltration.

**Gradational record in deposits**

- Streams draining stable, vegetated watersheds carry finer, more organic, and smaller quantities of sediment than streams from high-erosion watersheds.
- Fans, cones, terraces, and valley fills therefore record past changes in erosion rates.
- Lake Bonneville shorelines (deltas, terraces, bars) provide a datum for judging whether recent erosion exceeds the post-Bonneville norm.

**Infiltration and runoff**

- Eighty infiltration tests with a rainfall simulator gave 3.43 inches per hour on undisturbed slopes and 1.03 inches per hour on deteriorated slopes.
- Plant cover was 2.9 per cent on disturbed plots and 14.3 per cent on undisturbed plots.
- Runoff after four summer storms was 0.24 per cent of rainfall on undisturbed areas and 37.0 per cent on disturbed areas.

**Regional comparisons**

- Bryce Canyon (Utah) and the Chinle Bad Lands (Arizona) lack vegetative control, and show rapid runoff and deposition without acceleration.
- Kanab Creek and Johnson Valley (southern Utah) are stabilized watersheds that are now being deeply channeled. One arroyo on Kanab Creek is reported at 90 feet deep and 300 feet wide.
- Bailey ties the widespread channeling to the widespread removal of plant cover.

## Key Findings

### 1. Plant cover controls normal-versus-accelerated erosion (Bailey)

- Undisturbed vegetated areas absorb water faster, erode less, and produce less surface runoff than cleared, burned, or grazed areas.
- Accelerated erosion is "a sudden increase in the rate of erosion" caused by faster runoff. Indicators include new gully systems, dead or invading plant species, erosion pavements, and remnants of older soils.
- Watersheds are classified into three categories:
  - (A) fully stabilized by vegetation;
  - (B) never covered by a mantle soil;
  - (C) soil mantle disturbed so erosion has been much accelerated.

### 2. The Parrish Creek study (Utah)

- Study area: a 176-acre watershed at 7,800–9,000 feet, with 80 acres contour-trenched and reseeded, 81 acres well vegetated, and about 15 acres badly depleted.
- Flood-source areas made up only 5–10 per cent of the drainage. Gullies and runoff were confined to these depleted "sore spots," while adjacent vegetated ground showed none.
- Preventive measures (contour trenches, check dams, revegetation) prevented disastrous floods in 1936. Measured storms of similar intensity caused mud-rock flows on untreated adjacent watersheds.

**The July 10, 1936 storm**

- Maximum intensities: 5.04 in/hr (5 min), 4.32 in/hr (10 min), 3.76 in/hr (15 min).
- Duration about one hour; total rainfall 1.14 inches.
- Well-vegetated and terraced areas produced no surface runoff.
- The 15 impaired acres yielded 0.52 inch, or 28,234 cubic feet, of runoff.
- By the Horton method, average infiltration was about 1.5 in/hr on barren or poorly vegetated areas and in excess of 6.04 in/hr on well-vegetated areas.

### 3. Post-Bonneville floods and sediment record (Utah)

- Recent floods, some mud-rock flows, struck the Bonneville Basin between Salt Lake City and Willard. They destroyed homes, deposited boulders reported as up to 300 tons, and cut new channels in previously undisturbed deltas and terraces.
- Bailey judges current floods unprecedented in post-Bonneville history. Parrish Creek discharged boulders for the first time since the lake receded thousands of years ago.
- Since settlement in 1850, gullies have streaked the mountain front, following fire and excessive grazing.

### 4. Lost Creek (Wasatch Mountains)

- A 1938 flood destroyed a power flume, piled boulders on the railroad and state highway, and dammed the Provo River.
- Cone deposits show similar floods recurred many times in post-Bonneville time, so active erosion and periodic flooding are normal for this watershed.
- About 85 per cent of the drainage consists of steep, barren rocky slopes. Small islands of vegetated soil were not gullied.
- Bailey treats Parrish Creek and Lost Creek as the extremes of vegetation's role in flood prevention, with intermediate cases between.

### 5. Colorado Plateau channeling (Bailey)

- Widespread stream rejuvenation and accelerated runoff are removing watershed soils and cutting valley alluvium. Bailey regards this as the start of a new epicycle of erosion within historic times, and a threat to human use of the Colorado River Basin.
- Upper Kanab Valley, channeled for the first time since the aggrading cycle began, is attributed to modification of plant cover: road and trail cutting, overgrazing, trampling by livestock, and clearing for farming.

### 6. Discussion themes

- **Definitions (Bonsteel):** The boundary between normal and accelerated erosion is hard to draw. Natural changes (orogeny, base-level shifts, climate, ecological succession) also alter erosion, and "normal" is relative and difficult to reconstruct. Mass erosion should be included in the definition of erosion.
- **Soil creep and saprolite (Donnelly):** Soil creep and mass movement are important on cultivated contour slopes. Donnelly suggests two terms: "normerosion" (processes undisturbed by man) and "accelerosion" (processes induced or accelerated by man). He proposes using them as adjectives to rate watershed "normerosion hazard" and "accelerosion hazard" for forest and agricultural management.
- **Mud-flows (Hursh):** Mud-flows in the Appalachians occur after prolonged rain saturates deep colluvial soil on steep slopes. Rocks of 50 to 200 tons may be floated into valleys, and flows may cover several acres. Hursh states that mud-flows have strongly influenced valley geomorphology.
- **Volcanic erosion (Zies):** On Santa Maria, Guatemala, a 1902 explosion created a scar about 6,000 feet deep. Dry ash layers on a slope of about 50° allow fragmental rock and lava blocks to move rapidly, cutting V-shaped grooves. Rockfalls stop during rain and resume once ash dries.
- **Subsequent topography (Wolfe):** In the Piedmont Triassic lowlands of New Jersey and Pennsylvania, differential erosion works mainly through soil. A dense, clayey "B"-horizon on dip slopes retards gullying, producing shallow gullies. Scarp slopes lacking such a hardpan develop deep gullies.
- **Density currents (Knapp and Bell):** Sediment-laden river water entering a reservoir can form an underflow that carries clear water downstream. The two evidences are a surface demarcation line and a band of floating debris, shown on Lake Mead. Density currents can be stopped only by settling out sediment or by removing the effective slope. Laboratory tests show controlled withdrawal from layered reservoirs can release relatively clean water.
- **Erosion cycle (Knapp):** Erosion comprises entrainment, transportation, and deposition, governed by the balance between forces that move particles and forces that resist motion. Preventive work can strengthen resisting forces or weaken entraining forces.
- **Regional contrasts (Cressey):** In New York, forest was often replaced by pasture or grassland rather than cultivated crops, so accelerated erosion there is "locally important but still a minor phenomenon."

### 7. Concluding conclusion (Bailey)

- Some semiarid watersheds have naturally high degradation rates.
- Others have low erosion and runoff because a vegetative and soil mantle with binding power and infiltration equal to or greater than the degrading forces has developed.
- Accelerated erosion and runoff occur where plant-cover deterioration has reduced infiltration and soil stability.
