# The Pike: A Prized and Spurned Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Michigan Department of Conservation article profiles the pike family (Esocidae) and its three species commonly found in Michigan: the [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), the [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*), and the [[Mud Pickerel]] (*Esox vermiculatus*). It addresses the confusing common names applied to these fish, their identification by cheek and gill-cover scalation, their life history and growth, feeding and ecological roles, angling methods, and artificial propagation. The article argues that pike can be valuable to lake balance, since predation can prevent stunting of other fish, and that introductions should be made with great caution because pike are difficult to eradicate.

## Background & Description

- **Author and publication:** W. F. Carbine and W. I. Minckley, Institute for Fisheries Research, [[Michigan Department of Conservation]]. Reprinted from *Michigan Conservation*, August 1938, Vol. VII, No. 12, pp. 6-8 (revised April 1947).
- **Family:** [[Esocidae]], consisting of five species in the genus [[Esox]] in North America. All except the northern pike are restricted to North American freshwaters.
- **Michigan species:** Three pikes are commonly found in Michigan. The [[Chain Pickerel]] may occur in the extreme southern part of the state, but no record had been documented.
- **Identification:** The easiest way to separate the species is by scalation of the cheek and gill cover (operculum):
  - Mud pickerel: cheek and gill cover fully scaled.
  - Northern pike: cheek fully scaled, gill cover scaled on the upper half only.
  - Muskellunge: only the upper half of the cheek and gill cover scaled; lower half unscaled.
- **Body form:** Elongate, compressed body; long head with a duck-bill-like snout; large mouth with many teeth, some long and pointed and others shorter and hinged; dorsal fin set well back toward the tail.
- **Naming confusion:**
  - The [[Walleye]], a perch-family fish, has 80 legitimate names, and one name has been applied to seven different fish.
  - The muskellunge has more than 50 names and at least 40 legitimate spellings, derived from either the Indian "Mas-kinonge" or the French "masque-al-longé."
  - The northern pike has more than 40 names, including Wasserwolf, Jack, Snake, Grass pike, Duck-billed pike, and Canada pike.
- **Historical folklore:** Izaak Walton believed pike were offspring of the common pickerel weed. Stories of a 260-year-old pike and a 19-foot, 350-pound pike were also circulated; the article notes that a fish of that length should weigh several tons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feeding:** Small northern pike (several inches long) eat mainly small crustacea and insects. Larger pike feed almost entirely on fish. Recorded stomach contents include northern pike, walleyes, perch, bluegills, black bass, sunfish, suckers, minnows, mice, young ducks and other waterfowl, reptiles, frogs, crayfish, other crustacea, insects, snails, leeches, and carrion.
- **Ambush predation:** Pike wait motionless in dense grass or rush beds and strike prey crosswise, holding it until it is dead or exhausted, then turning it headfirst.
- **Seasonal habitat (northern pike):**
  - Summer: shallow plant beds near shore, in water averaging about four feet deep.
  - Autumn: migration to deeper waters, preferring steep, stony shores.
  - Winter: most return toward summer habitats, but larger fish usually remain in deeper water.
  - Clear water is preferred. In rivers, favored haunts are pools below rapids or falls and weedy bayous.
- **Mortality and predators:** The principal enemy of the northern pike is man. Water birds, fish, and turtles also take some pike. In summer 1937 a snapping turtle weighing 20 to 25 pounds was found to contain a 15-inch northern pike. Overall, predation on pike is small compared with most other fishes. Common parasites such as the yellow grub are present but pike are relatively free of them.
- **Food demand:** Between 10 and 15 pounds of food are required to add one pound of flesh to any member of the pike family.
- **Summer feeding:** Failure to bite in summer was disproved as a result of shed teeth; stomach examination showed pike do not go off their feed in summer. Reduced catches are attributed to abundance of young fish of other species, retreat to deeper water, or sluggishness from high water temperatures.
- **Stunting and balance:** In many lakes, excessive survival of young fish may cause stunting. Pike may check overabundant panfish and help restore balance, but introductions require extreme caution because pike are easy to introduce and very difficult or impossible to eradicate.
- **Creel evidence:** Houghton Lake creel census records show that as pike catch declines, panfish and bass fishing improve, and vice versa.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids among pikes are uncommon. In the East, chain pickerel and little pickerel have hybridized occasionally. Hybrids between northern pike, muskellunge, and mud pickerel are possible because their spawning periods and habitats overlap.

## Key Findings

### Taxonomy and Identification
- The pike family (Esocidae) includes five North American species in the genus *Esox*; only the northern pike occurs outside the United States.
- Scalation of the cheek and gill cover is the most practical field key for the three Michigan species.
- Common-name confusion is extensive: 80 names for the walleye, more than 50 names and at least 40 spellings for the muskellunge, and more than 40 names for the northern pike.

### Northern Pike (*Esox lucius*)
- **Range:** Freshwaters of northern Europe, Asia, and North America, north to Alaska and Siberia. In eastern U.S., found south to New York and the Ohio River. In Europe, occurs south to Italy and Greece.
- **Spawning:** Breeds in early spring as soon as ice leaves, when water approaches 46°F (March to May in Michigan). Spawns in shallow flooded marshes or meadows, and in sluggish or swampy stream sections or overflow areas. Breeding season lasts several weeks; younger fish spawn first.
- **Eggs and development:** Eggs are about one-eighth inch in diameter. A single female may deposit as many as 100,000 eggs. Hatching takes 14 to 30 days depending on temperature. Females are said to be larger than males. Minimum age at maturity is three years.
- **Growth (average Michigan waters):**
  - 1st year: 8-12 inches
  - 2nd year: 14-18 inches
  - 3rd year: 22-26 inches
  - 6th year: 39 inches
  - 12th year: 53 inches
  - Most rapid growth occurs in the first four years. Fish grow faster in warmer waters when other biological conditions are favorable.
- **Size and age records:** Maximum weight in Michigan approximately 35 pounds; greatest length around 54 inches; greatest recorded age 14 years.
- **Creel census averages:** 21.4 inches (1935) and 19.7 inches (1936) Michigan general creel census.
- **Spawning migration tagging (April 1932):** 84 mature northern pike were tagged in the [[Pine River]], Gratiot County, by C. W. Creaser of Wayne University. Twenty-three were recovered later that year. Two had traveled 25 miles upstream 38 days after tagging, showing that pike move rapidly and widely during spawning migration.
- **Hatchery release (October 14, 1937):** 10 northern pike reared at the [[Drayton Plains Hatchery]] were measured, tagged, and released in [[Walled Lake]], Oakland County, to track growth rates through returns.
- **Angling:** Trolling with large spoons and lures, or with live suckers, silversides, or frogs, is productive in shallow marginal waters. A large lively minnow is probably the best still-fishing bait. Surface or near-surface casting with spinner, plug, or frog is sometimes effective. Through-ice spearing uses a light-tight shanty with tackle spoons, live minnows, or wooden decoys. With natural bait, slacken the line so the fish can turn and swallow the bait before the hook is set.
- **Management history:** As late as 1931 (printed as "1981" in the source, an obvious scan error), spearing was allowed in non-trout streams only during May while pike were spawning.
- **Value:** Flesh is firm and palatable. Anglers prize heads and skins as trophies. Pike are valued for size and photographic appeal, and may be desirable in waters rich in minnows, suckers, and other coarse fish where bass or trout do not thrive. In trout or other desirable game-fish waters, pike may be detrimental.
- **Artificial propagation:** Pike culture experiments began in Michigan in 1937. A relatively high percentage of eggs hatch in an ordinary hatching jar, but cannibalism makes rearing difficult even with abundant live food.

### Muskellunge (*Esox masquinongy*)
- **Subspecies:** Three subspecies are recognized: one in the Ohio River and tributaries, one in the upper Mississippi drainage, and one in the Great Lakes-St. Lawrence region. The Great Lakes-St. Lawrence form is most abundant in Michigan. The second occurs in Lac Vieux Desert, Gogebic County, on the Wisconsin-Michigan boundary.
- **Identification:** Lacks scales on the lower half of the cheek and opercle. Dark gray coloration with round or square black spots on silver-gray; spots sometimes form bands; belly usually white; fins black-spotted.
- **Breeding:** Poorly known, but observations suggest breeding is similar to the northern pike.
- **Age and size:** Maximum recorded age 20 years. Top weight 60 to 75 pounds. Largest fish in the *World Almanac* weighed 75 pounds. Greatest recorded length for 1936 was 60.5 inches.
- **Growth (selected values from the Wisconsin table by Edward Schneberger):**
  - Year 1: 8 inches, 1/2 lb (approximate weight as printed)
  - Year 2: 16 inches, 1 lb
  - Year 3: 23 inches, 3 lb
  - Year 4: 28 inches, 6 lb
  - Year 5: 30 inches, 8 lb
  - Year 6: 32 inches, 9 lb
  - Year 7: 35 inches, 12 lb
  - Year 8: 38 inches, 14 lb
  - Year 9: 40 inches, 16 lb
  - Year 10: 43 inches, 19 lb
  - Year 11: 45 inches, 23 lb
  - Year 12: 46 inches, 25 lb
  - Year 20: 59 inches, 33 lb
  - Growth is most rapid in the first few years, similar to the northern pike.

### Mud Pickerel (*Esox vermiculatus*)
- **Size:** One of the smallest members of the family, rarely reaching 14 inches and averaging about eight inches. Not of importance as a game species.
- **Identification:** Grassy to grayish-green coloration with darker streaks and bars forming a network, lighter inner spaces worm-track-like, and a prominent dark streak below the eye. Cheek and gill cover fully scaled. Color varies with habitat.
- **Distribution:** Mississippi Valley, tributaries of Lake Erie and Lake Michigan south to the Mississippi and Arkansas. Not known east of the Alleghenies or in Texas. Abundant in creeks, ponds, and smaller rivers of the southern lower peninsula of Michigan. Prefers quiet, muddy waters.
- **Biology:** Feeds mainly on frogs, fishes, large insects, and crustacea. Spawns early; ripe specimens have been taken in March. Breeding habits probably resemble the northern pike.
- **Management status:** Not listed as a game fish in Michigan and not protected by fishing regulations. Anglers often mistake it for young northern pike or muskellunge.
- **Specimen record:** Fig. 4 shows a 10-inch mud pickerel taken from the [[Clinton River]] at Drayton Plains, October 15, 1937.

### Research Attribution
- In 1935, Dr. C. L. Hubbs and Milton Trautman of the University of Michigan disproved the belief that summer failure to bite resulted from shed teeth. Pike teeth are broken off or shed and replaced throughout the year, and stomach examinations showed pike continue feeding in summer.
