# Avoidance Conditioning of Electric Organ Discharge in Mormyrid Fish; The Effect of Visual Experience upon the Rat's Reaction to Near and Far Surfaces

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mormyridae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two 1968 *Animal Behaviour* papers. The first, the main study, shows that [[Avoidance Conditioning]] can control the rate of [[Electric Organ Discharge]] in [[Mormyridae|Mormyrid]] fish (*Gnathonemus*). The second, a truncated paper, asks whether early [[Visual Experience]] affects rats' preference for near versus far surfaces, using a rotated [[Visual Cliff]]. Only the methods of the rat study are present; no results or conclusions are included.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Mormyrid electric organ (Mandriota, Thompson & Bennett)**

- Mormyrids emit weak electrical pulses at a few per second at rest. Stimulation, including swimming, usually accelerates discharge. The pulses are too weak for defence or predation.
- The pulses likely serve an [[Electrosensory System]] that detects impedance changes in the environment. The putative receptors are modified [[Lateral Line]] organs. The electric organ acts as the energy source for active "probing."
- The electric organ behaves as a single motor unit. Electroplaques are driven by a medullary relay nucleus of about 25 cells that are electrically coupled, so amplitude cannot be controlled independently of rate.
- The study extends an earlier report of [[Classical Conditioning]] of discharge acceleration using electric shock as the unconditioned stimulus (Mandriota et al., 1965). That study used 14 specimens with resting rates of 2 to 8 impulses per second.

**Paper 2: Rat near and far surfaces (McGuigan)**

- Earlier [[Visual Cliff]] studies (Walk & Gibson, 1961) showed that many animals prefer to descend to an optically shallow surface. Dark-reared rats needed [[Motion Parallax]] to show this preference.
- More recent work (Kaess & Wilson, 1964; Carr & McGuigan, 1965) found that early light-rearing over a deep or shallow surface alters later preference.
- The question addressed is whether the same visual experience and cues affect horizontal near/far discrimination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish testing environment:** Subjects were held in perforated Lucite boxes inside shock-mounted, aerated aquaria, dimly illuminated and kept at 22 to 24 °C. Recording used silver-wire electrodes at the head and tail ends, and shock electrodes were placed bilaterally.
- **Rat testing environment:** Experiment 1 used a Y-maze built on a visual-cliff model rotated 90 degrees. A checkered near surface was 12 in. away and a far surface was 24 in. away, shown through glass plates. An outer wall removed extra-maze cues. The apparatus was illuminated from the front.
- Both studies were conducted in laboratories in New York (Columbia, City College, Hunter College, Albert Einstein College of Medicine) and New Jersey (Rider College). Funding came from US Public Health Service and US Air Force Office of Scientific Research grants.

## Key Findings

**Mormyrid avoidance conditioning**

- **Subjects and stimuli:** Eight *Gnathonemus* (8 to 15 cm), in four same-species pairs. Pulses were about 0.4 V with durations of 0.3 to 0.5 ms. The conditioned stimulus (CS) was light, lasting 5.0 s. Shock was about 7.5 mA rectangular pulses, delivered in the final 0.1 s if no response occurred.
- **Design:** A yoked control procedure with reversed roles across successive series of 220 to 240 trials (six blocks per series, intertrial intervals of 40, 60 or 80 s, mean 60 s). Each pair ran six to twelve series.
- **Response criterion:** An interpulse interval of 0.36 times the mean, corresponding to instantaneous rates of about 12 to 18 pulses per second.
- **First series:** Avoidance subjects' final-block response mean was 86% (individual values 95%, 88%, 86%, 71%). Yoked controls averaged 42% (72%, 60%, 22%, 12%). In the second half of the series the difference was significant (P<0.02, two-tailed t-test). The first-half difference was not (P>0.10).
- **Reversals:** When controls switched to the avoidance contingency, responding rose in all four fish. Three reached over 90%. Fish C2 rose to 39% and later to 69%.
- **Within-subject comparison:** Three of four pairs (A, B, D) showed higher responding in the avoidance series than in control series (P<0.02, based on the last four series). Pair C was ambiguous, because Cl responded more often regardless of role, and operant conditioning could only be confirmed for Cl within-subject.
- **Comparison with classical conditioning:** Avoidance and classical conditioning at 100% reinforcement reached similar learning rates and final response probabilities, measured by trials. Yoked classical conditioning gave lower response probability. Avoidance required fewer shocks, and shock appeared to reduce survival, so the authors favour operant conditioning on practical grounds.
- **Limitations:** Sample size was small. Extinction data were unavailable. Deceleration conditioning failed, probably due to rapid habituation and conflict with the shock-evoked acceleration. Body movement as an alternative explanation was not fully excluded.

**Rat near/far surfaces**

- The paper states two aims: to test whether light- or dark-rearing, or light-rearing over a near or far surface, affects preference for near versus far surfaces, and whether that preference depends on [[Motion Parallax]] or proximal size cues.
- Experiment 1 tested preference in rats reared in light or darkness. The text ends before its results, so no findings are reported.
