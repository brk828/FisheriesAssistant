# Outstanding Graduate Mentors: Peter Buseck and W. L. Minckley

#graduate-education #doctoral-mentoring #academic-award

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University booklet honors two scientists, [[Peter Buseck]] (Regents' Professor of Geology and Chemistry) and [[W. L. Minckley]] (Professor of Zoology), as recipients of the annual [[Outstanding Graduate Teaching Award]]. It contains a Dean's statement, a sponsor message from the [[ASU Foundation]], nominating statements from former students, and each mentor's own essay on doctoral training. The document is not a research study. It describes mentoring philosophy and practice in the sciences, and it contains no species, habitat, or field data.

## Background & Description

The booklet is organized around two honorees and is published through the [[Graduate College]] and the ASU Foundation.

- **Dean's statement (Brian L. Foster, Graduate College):** Describes both mentors as highly successful scientists and doctoral mentors whose statements converge on core issues of quality doctoral education. The Dean credits them with helping build [[Arizona State University]] into a major center for research and graduate education.
- **ASU Foundation statement (Lonnie L. Ostrom, Director):** Notes that the Foundation's first priority is education quality and that it co-sponsors the award, framing graduate teaching excellence as "an investment in the future."
- **Nominating statements:** Former students describe Buseck as a teacher who builds independent thinkers, and Minckley as a demanding, field-oriented mentor who teaches by example.
- **Awardee statements:** Each mentor sets out a personal philosophy. Buseck's is titled "How to Develop Scholars: Mentor or Tormentor?" and Minckley's is "Mentoring in an Evolving Research World."

## Key Findings

**Peter Buseck: mentoring philosophy and practice**

- **Goal of training:** Students should become recognized experts in their field, moving from a hierarchical student relationship to one of professional colleague and peer.
- **Admission test:** Buseck asks prospective students "Why do you want a Ph.D.?" and requires a strong desire for knowledge and enjoyment of academic work.
- **Three stages of a Ph.D.:** (a) conceiving and defining a worthwhile problem, (b) doing the research, and (c) writing results for publication. Oral and written communication runs through all three.
- **Problem definition:** Buseck does not hand students a problem. He offers research areas and open questions and insists that students ask "So what?" at every stage.
- **Research process:** He visits each student briefly every day or two, involves students in writing proposals and reports, and treats errors as part of learning.
- **Postdocs:** Postdoctoral researchers (typically one to three years of full-time research) are part of the training environment, and Buseck selects them for diverse backgrounds.
- **Writing:** Students write papers as soon as they have results worth reporting. Buseck distributes a sixteen-page guide, "Preparing manuscripts, theses, dissertations, and talks." He suggests adding six months to a student's estimate of time to finish writing unless the student has an M.S. thesis or published papers.
- **Group meetings:** Weekly meetings let students present their work and practice talks before professional meetings.
- **Courses:** Buseck considers courses largely incidental after the first two or three semesters. He prefers self-instruction through reading and seminars.
- **Role of the mentor:** Largely in the background, offering "an unnoticed prod" and periodic questions about whether results mean what they seem.

**W. L. Minckley: mentoring philosophy and practice**

- **Natural history training:** Minckley encourages a multidisciplinary approach and a broad, field-based grounding in natural history, conservation, and biology. Nominators describe him as one of a dwindling group of broadly knowledgeable natural historians.
- **Field-based mentoring:** Most of his mentoring takes place outside the classroom, especially in the field, where students' curiosity is encouraged.
- **Demanding standards:** He sets high expectations and pushes students to publish and present at scientific meetings.
- **Time commitment:** He says mentoring takes half of his time.
- **Changing science:** He identifies three shifts: human-driven degradation of natural systems, the information explosion that forces specialization (ecologist, physiologist, geneticist, systematist), and new technology such as remote sensing, electrophoresis, mitochondrial DNA analysis, and advanced statistics.
- **Team research:** Co-chaired committees and multidisciplinary dissertations are more common than before. Minckley argues that mentoring changes in rate and complexity, not in content, and requires continued learning.
- **Legacy:** He reports that many former students now work as professional biologists, university professors, and administrators, some in government agencies.

**Shared themes across both mentors**

- Graduate education and research are inseparable. Students train through their own research, and advisors' research is part of that training.
- Mentoring is personal and collaborative. Both mentors describe it as an intimate intellectual relationship.
- Students should experience the full research cycle, from problem definition to dissemination of results.
- Mentors' own commitment to keeping up with their fields inspires students.
