# A River in Common: The Columbia River, the Salmon Ecosystem, and Water Policy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1997 report, prepared by John M. Volkman for the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]], examines how water development in the [[Columbia River Basin]] has contributed to the decline of [[Columbia River Salmon]] and how federal and state water programs are responding. The author, a former lawyer for the [[Northwest Power Planning Council]], argues that water policy has moved from traditional allocation for irrigation, hydropower, municipal and industrial uses toward ecological functions needed by salmon. The report frames the basin's problem as fragmented jurisdiction, an uncertain science base, and a financial system tied to hydropower revenues. It concludes that recovery will be slow and difficult and recommends adaptive management, improved collaboration among agencies, a new financing balance and clearer governance.

## Background & Description

The Columbia is the fourth largest river in the United States, with a basin of about 259,000 square miles across the United States and Canada. Roughly 44 percent of its runoff is Canadian. The basin contains a mixture of arid interior, snow-fed mountains and wet western valleys. Its approximately five million residents (about 53 percent of the four-state Northwest population) live mostly in urban areas occupying less than four percent of the basin's U.S. land.

Pre-development conditions included extreme flow variability: peak flows at The Dalles reached 1,240,000 cubic feet per second (1894) and low flows fell to 35,000 cubic feet per second (1937). Development began with tributary irrigation and settlement in the 1800s, followed by mainstem dams from the 1930s. Key institutions discussed include the [[Bonneville Power Administration]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Army Corps of Engineers]], the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] and the [[Columbia River Treaty]] with Canada.

Historically, salmon were central to tribal cultures, and Lewis and Clark described enormous runs. Estimates place historic returns at roughly 10 to 16 million adult salmon per year, reduced to about one million later. Salmon connect the basin to the Pacific Ocean, transporting marine nutrients to inland headwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss.** Beaver trapping, logging, mining, overgrazing and irrigation diversions degraded tributary habitat from the 1800s onward. The report notes that only three percent of interior basin lands retain landscape patterns similar to historical ones. The Grande Ronde's State Ditch, for example, cut off 33 miles of river channel and floodplain. Mainstem dams blocked an estimated 18,700 miles of historically accessible streams in the U.S. portion of the basin, about 38 percent of the historic 49,300-mile range.

**Mainstem effects.** Reservoirs slowed juvenile migration. A juvenile starting in the Sawtooth headwaters now passes eight dams and takes more than 50 days to reach the river mouth, compared with 22 days before the dams. The spring freshet moved from June to May, and reservoir water is warmer in late summer and fall. The river has become a better habitat for predators such as squawfish than for salmon.

**Water quality.** The [[Environmental Protection Agency]] has identified persistent high temperatures on the mainstem Snake and Columbia. More than ten percent of eastern Oregon and Washington river miles are water quality-limited. Roughly half of the Columbia estuary's tidal swamp and marsh has been lost since the 1880s.

**Cumulative and population effects.** The report describes [[Metapopulation Theory]] as a reason connected habitat matters: lost connections reduce a basin's resilience. Hatchery production became the dominant mitigation tool, although it has been associated with reduced genetic diversity. The report notes that only a handful of populations, such as the Hanford Reach fall chinook and the Wenatchee sockeye, remain healthy.

## Key Findings

- **Fragmented governance:** Mainstem dams are federal, tributary water rights are mainly state and private, and the Columbia is governed partly by a treaty that treats ecosystem values as economic trades.
- **Science uncertainty:** Ecosystem science is described as "more like economics or weather prediction than chemistry or physics." Adaptive management is endorsed as the approach, but implementation has been weak.
- **Financial linkage:** Mitigation has been financed largely by hydropower revenues. Competitive electricity markets and ESA listings have strained this balance.
- **Water budget and flow:** The 1982 Water Budget supplied significantly less water than optimum or minimum flows. The Snake fell well short of the prescribed volume.
- **Water acquisition:** The Bureau of Reclamation's Snake River program sought about 427,000 acre-feet, acquired through rentals, reservoir space and power head space under willing-seller terms.
- **Tributary development:** Most Basin streams were fully or over appropriated. A 1993 estimate found 80 percent of 153 Oregon tributaries had low-flow problems.
- **Policy shifts:** Idaho, Oregon and Washington adopted new-diversion moratoria in the early 1990s, and tribal water rights and the [[Endangered Species Act]] increasingly shape river management.
- **Salmon decline:** Wild runs declined sharply from the late 1980s, and hatchery fish formed a growing share of returns.
- **Recommendations:** The report urges coherent ecological objectives, federal data and expertise support, one-voice federal messaging to watershed groups, realistic expectations for watershed efforts, stable funding, and incremental governance reform toward a more ecologically aligned model.
- **Outlook:** Recovery is described as "slow, difficult and risky," and no quick resolution is expected.
