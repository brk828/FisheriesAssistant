# Propagation and Distribution of Food Fishes, Fiscal Year 1930

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fish-culture

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Fisheries report (Appendix XVI to the Commissioner's 1930 report, by Glen C. Leach, Chief of the Division of Fish Culture) documents the artificial propagation, egg collection, rescue, and distribution of food and game fishes by the [[United States Bureau of Fisheries]] during fiscal year 1930. Total output of fish and eggs reached about 7.57 billion, a record that exceeded the previous year by roughly 500 million. Marine species (cod, haddock, pollock, winter flounder, mackerel) dominated the total, while game species made up about 2.7 percent. The report also covers cooperation with state agencies and federal conservation bodies, egg transfers, shipments abroad, distribution-car operations, and the conditions that hampered production, chiefly drought and storms.

## Background & Description

The division's stated purpose extends beyond sport angling. Its work is meant to support commercial fisheries and augment the national food supply, not only game fishing. The report notes that the three marine stations operate at negligible cost, comparable to the price of trout food at game-fish hatcheries.

The bureau propagated 44 commercial and game species. Major groups include:

- **Salmonids:** [[Pacific Salmon]] (chinook, chum, pink, coho, sockeye), steelhead, [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Loch Leven Trout]], and [[Black-Spotted Trout]].
- **Centrarchids:** [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Smallmouth Black Bass]], crappies, bluegill, rock bass, and warmouth.
- **Percids:** [[Walleye]] (pike perch) and yellow perch.
- **Marine gadids and flatfish:** [[Atlantic Cod]], [[Haddock]], [[Pollock]], and [[Winter Flounder]].
- **Other:** [[Lake Whitefish]], [[American Shad]], cisco, carp, buffalofish, mackerel, and freshwater drum.

Operations were run through 37 stations and 48 substations and auxiliaries. Some substations were temporary, opened for short egg-collection seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several environmental factors shaped results:

- **Drought:** Extremely low water in Pacific Northwest and Oregon streams disrupted salmon migrations. Chinook and sockeye declined sharply. Drought also caused a virtual failure of all [[American Shad]] operations and reduced eggs at eastern trout stations.
- **Storms and weather:** Gales on [[Lake Superior]] ended the lake trout collecting season at Duluth. Storms and cold snaps curtailed whitefish and cisco collections on Lake Ontario and Lake Erie.
- **Flooding:** Upper Mississippi high water created a salvage operation that rescued over 161 million stranded fish.
- **Water quality and temperature:** Chlorine in municipal supply at Alpena damaged pike perch incubation. Pond temperature swings caused losses at Tupelo, and warm springs at Spearfish were noted to suit healthy trout.
- **Pollution and disease:** Pollution was reducing salmon numbers on the Penobscot River. Gyrodactylus infected brood trout at several stations.
- **Upper Mississippi Wild Life and Fish Refuge:** Biological studies of five sloughs found oxygen exhausted by late January under ice. Pickerel were identified as the main predator of game fish in the sloughs.

## Key Findings

**Overall output**
- Total fish and eggs distributed: about 7,570,482,300 (fish and eggs), an all-time record.
- Game species: about 205 million (≈2.7%). Marine species: 85.9%. Interior waters: 6.9%. Anadromous Atlantic forms: 4%.
- Fingerlings exceeded 250 million.

**Stations and substations**
- 37 stations and 48 substations and auxiliaries operated.
- Egg collections rose by close to 1 billion over 1929. The increase was driven by cod, pollock, winter flounder, pike perch, whitefish, and trout.

**Marine stations**
- Boothbay Harbor (Maine) produced 2,434,538,000 winter flounder fry and a record 1,068,142,000 cod eggs.
- Gloucester (Mass.) produced 616,713,000 pollock fry and 229,769,000 cod fry.
- Woods Hole (Mass.) collected about 837,000,000 winter flounder eggs from 2,380 females.

**Great Lakes**
- Michigan stations produced large whitefish and lake trout output. Charlevoix handled 40,218,000 lake trout eggs.
- Put in Bay (Ohio) collected 316,275,000 pike perch eggs, nearly three times the prior year.
- Duluth was curtailed by gales, yielding 6,603,500 lake trout fry.

**Salvage**
- Over 161 million fish rescued from Upper Mississippi overflow areas at a cost of about $0.11 per thousand. Only about 0.3% were distributed to applicants or transferred to non-native waters.

**Cooperation**
- Cooperative nurseries: 125 operated, with about 4 million fish supplied (a decline of nearly 1 million from 1929).
- State hatcheries: 369 hatcheries and 1,096 employees reported, with 3,624,046.55 dollars spent on propagation. Licence fees were about double that amount.
- Congress passed a five-year expansion program (over 30 items) in May 1930. The division's activities were expected to nearly double in number and magnitude.

**Shipments and transfers**
- About 63.96 million fish eggs were assigned to state commissions.
- Foreign shipments of fewer than 2.5 million trout eggs went to four countries, including Chile, where acclimatization of U.S. species was attempted.

**Other notes**
- A dried-salmon-egg trout feed showed promise for growth and coloration.
- A refrigerated plant at Little White Salmon was built to store spawned-out salmon for fish food.
- Pyramid Lake (Nevada) was taken over as a black-spotted trout egg station after tribal permission delays.
- Distribution used four cars for over 7 billion fish, down from six cars when output was 3 billion.
- The U.S. Forest Service summary reported declining fish numbers in national forest streams, citing inadequate planting, transplant losses, and weak coordination among agencies.
