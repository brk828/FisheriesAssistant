# Glen Canyon Dam Draft Biological Opinion: Flow Operations and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This package is a 1993–1994 consultation record for the operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the Colorado River. It includes a Reclamation transmittal memo for a technical review meeting (March 2, 1994, Las Vegas), a June 11, 1993 summary of a flow-scenario review, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Draft Biological Opinion (2-21-93-F-167, dated October 13, 1993). The Draft Opinion concludes that operating under the [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative]] (MLFF), as described in the Preliminary Draft EIS, is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It finds no jeopardy for the [[Bald Eagle]], [[Kanab Ambersnail]], and [[Peregrine Falcon]]. The opinion sets out a [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative]] built on experimental flows, habitat maintenance flows, a research program, and possible selective withdrawal.

## Background & Description

**Consultation context.** Reclamation requested formal [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] on February 5, 1993. The 90-day consultation period began February 8, 1993. A draft opinion was provided on May 20, 1993, and the consultation period was extended to coincide with the final EIS.

**Operational history.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963 as part of the Colorado River Storage Project. A 1978 jeopardy opinion found that dam operations jeopardized the humpback chub. Key milestones include:
- 1963–1980: operation under filling criteria.
- 1980 onward: an objective of maximizing firm capacity and energy.
- June 1990: research flows began.
- August 1991: [[Interim Flows]] were established and remain in operation.
- 1992: the Grand Canyon Protection Act directed the Secretary to protect and improve Grand Canyon values.

**Water years.** Annual releases are classed as low (e.g., 8.23 maf in 1989), moderate (13.6 maf in 1987), or high (19.3 maf in 1985). Low water years are considered normal and likely to occur more than 50% of the time. Flood flows above 45,000 cfs are estimated to occur at a rate of 1 in 40 years under the No Action Alternative.

**Operating limits of the MLFF (per the draft table):**
- Minimum releases of 1,000 cfs (Labor Day to Easter) and 5,000 cfs (Easter to Labor Day), with some alternatives differing.
- Maximum releases of 31,500 cfs, which may be exceeded during high water years.
- Daily fluctuation limits of 5,000, 6,000, or 8,000 cfs, depending on monthly release volume.
- Habitat maintenance flows of up to 33,200 cfs for 7 to 14 days in March or April.
- Beach and habitat building flows of 40,000 to 45,000 cfs, occurring about 1 in 5 years when Lake Powell is low.
- Flood protection limiting floods above 45,000 cfs to no more than 1 in 100 years.

**Governing studies.** The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program provided most of the supporting data, including endangered fish annual reports, the Preliminary Draft EIS (January 8, 1993), and the Advanced Draft EIS (July 1993).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish community.** The Colorado River basin has 32 fish species, of which 75% are endemic. Four "big river" endangered fishes (bonytail chub, Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, and razorback sucker) historically occurred in Grand Canyon. Of the eight native Grand Canyon fishes, three are extirpated (bonytail chub, Colorado squawfish, roundtail chub) and only four have reproducing populations.

**Humpback chub.**
- Grand Canyon population centered on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) confluence area. Estimates include 7,000–8,000 chub over 200 mm in the LCR (Kaeding and Zimmerman, 1982) and 2,407 chub over 175 mm (Valdez et al., 1992a; 95% CI 1,102–6,564).
- Aged to 22 years by otoliths. Growth to about 100 mm in year one.
- Range has contracted since dam closure. The last collection above Lee's Ferry was in 1967. Other records were at RM 17.8 in 1980 and RM 29.9 in 1993.
- Larvae and young-of-year use [[Backwaters]] and nearshore habitats. Adults use eddies, shoreline habitats, and runs.
- Mainstem spawning is prevented by cold releases. Hatching success was 84% at 19–20 °C and fell to 12% at 12–13 °C.
- Pre-dam mainstem temperatures reached 25–29 °C in July–August. Lee's Ferry temperatures now range 7–12 °C.

**Razorback sucker.**
- Now occupies about 25% of its former range. Only the bonytail chub is considered rarer among the big-river fishes.
- Successful recruitment is virtually nonexistent in natural riverine environments. Larvae do not develop into young-of-year in the wild. Predation on early life stages is considered the most probable cause.
- Adults can live 24 to 44 years. Thirty-six individuals were tagged in western [[Lake Mead]] in 1992.
- Rarely collected in Grand Canyon.

**Bald eagle.**
- Winter concentration in Grand Canyon, first documented in 1985–1986. A peak of 26 birds was counted at [[Nankoweap Creek]] in February 1990.
- Spawning rainbow trout comprise more than 99% of eagle foraging attempts. Eagle numbers track trout abundance, and daily discharge affects trout access to the creek.

**Peregrine falcon.**
- 71 breeding areas were identified in Grand Canyon National Park from 1988 to 1990. Extrapolation suggests 96 pairs in the project area.
- Recovery is greatest on the Colorado Plateau. The opinion judged effects on prey unlikely to be significant.

**Kanab ambersnail.**
- Recorded in three locations. The Grand Canyon population is the only one in a natural setting.
- Restricted to a spring-fed wetted area of about 50 m by 30 m, dominated by vegetation such as monkey flower. Individuals occur mostly above the 40,000 cfs zone, with some between 30,000 and 40,000 cfs.

**Non-native fishes.** Introductions of non-native species are identified as a principal cause of native fish decline. Brown trout remain in sizeable numbers, mainly in Bright Angel Creek. Rainbow trout are stocked in Glen Canyon National Recreation Area, and the Lee's Ferry tailwater supports a large population. Of nine tributaries sampled by Angradi et al. (1992), seven were dominated by natives. Maddux et al. (1987) found native species made up 79% of tributary catches.

**Other threats.** A catastrophic spill at the LCR bridge at Cameron, or chronic pollutant releases (oils, heavy metals, pesticides, uranium compounds), could seriously affect the [[Humpback Chub]] population.

## Key Findings

**Technical review (June 11, 1993).**
- Experts agreed the Service's concept has merit for helping endangered and native fish survive.
- The flow numbers are not supported by technical data. They were derived to match a generalized pattern and water delivery requirements.
- It is difficult to determine the biological difference between the Service flow scenario and the Preferred Alternative.
- Reclamation cannot replicate historic high flows, sediment, or temperature, but can replicate the general flow pattern.
- Reducing daily fluctuations is important, but seasonal variability is equally important.
- Backwaters may not limit the humpback chub population, since large numbers occur in backwaters under turbid conditions.

**Biological opinion conclusions.**
- Jeopardy: [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] under the MLFF as described in the Preliminary Draft EIS.
- No jeopardy: [[Bald Eagle]], [[Kanab Ambersnail]], and [[Peregrine Falcon]].
- The opinion states that the 1978 jeopardy finding remained in effect.
- Flood effects: no obvious long-term adversity was found after the high flow years 1983 to 1986. All age classes present before remained.

**Sediment and habitat.**
- Probability of a net gain in riverbed sand between Lee's Ferry and the LCR is 64% for MLFF versus 50% for No Action after 20 years.
- After 50 years, the probabilities are 73% (MLFF) and 41% (No Action).
- Reduced daily fluctuations are expected to increase food base and nearshore stability.

**Reasonable and Prudent Alternative.**
- Element 1: experimental flows with high steady spring flows and low steady summer and fall flows during low water years (about 8.23 maf). Preliminary estimate of five years of study. The 9,000 cfs low flow was selected as the highest level that still allows backwater formation.
- Element 2: a management plan for the LCR, with a draft due within two years.
- Element 3: a research program covering temperature effects, tributary hydrology, native and non-native interactions, life history, origins of food resources, and parasites and disease.
- Element 4: a razorback sucker workshop within one year and a memorandum of understanding.
- Element 5: establish a second humpback chub spawning aggregation below the dam.
- Element 6: feasibility of [[Selective Withdrawal]] (multi-level intake structure).
- Element 7: an [[Adaptive Management Program]] with flexibility for impact review.

**Incidental take.**
- Humpback chub: young may be transported downstream into unfavorable habitat during habitat maintenance or building flows. Take is expected to be difficult to detect.
- Kanab ambersnail: some individuals and habitat may be lost due to flood releases.
- No take is authorized for bald eagle, peregrine falcon, or razorback sucker.

**Conservation recommendations.** Operate under the [[Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow Alternative]] (SASF), monitor peregrine falcon and bald eagle populations, and investigate Kanab ambersnail life history and habitat requirements.

**Research planning (September 17, 1993 meeting).**
- Arizona Game and Fish planned to finish research by June 1994.
- Arizona State University proposed field work through 1995.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service requested an additional 1994 field season to follow the 1993 humpback chub cohort.
- Participants raised concerns about data sharing, overlap between agencies, and the need for a synthesized monograph on Grand Canyon chub. A threatened lawsuit by the Havasupai Tribe concerning research in Havasu Creek was also noted.
