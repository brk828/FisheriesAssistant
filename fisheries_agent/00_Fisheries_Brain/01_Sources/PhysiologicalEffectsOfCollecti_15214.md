# Physiological Effects of Collecting and Transporting Emigrating Juvenile Chinook Salmon past Dams on the Columbia River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study by [[Maule Et Al]] (Oregon State University) examined whether the [[Mcnary Dam Collection System]] and the barge and truck transport operations of the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] stress emigrating juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] on the [[Columbia River]]. Fall and spring Chinook were assessed with plasma cortisol and glucose, white blood cell (WBC) counts, and challenge tests (saltwater challenge, secondary stress, swimming endurance) during the 1982–1984 seaward migrations. The authors concluded that the collection system stresses fish cumulatively, that loading into transport vehicles is the most stressful step, that barge transport allows more recovery than truck transport, and that spring Chinook appear more sensitive to collection stress than fall Chinook.

## Background & Description

Hydroelectric dams on the Columbia and Snake rivers have altered the riverine ecosystem and adversely affected anadromous salmonids. Mortality through turbines, increased migration time (two- to threefold after completion of the last four dams), predation, gas supersaturation, pathogens, and high water temperatures all contributed to losses; the authors cite an estimated 95% mortality for migrants between the upper river and The Dalles Dam in low-flow years. More than 70% of migrants were of hatchery origin.

Juveniles are diverted from turbines at McNary Dam (the fourth dam, 471 km upstream of the ocean), held in a collection facility, and trucked or barged below [[Bonneville Dam]], the last downstream barrier. Prior work on handling and transport stress in salmonids (e.g., [[Cortisol Stress Response]] studies) had not examined the collection system itself. The study aimed to identify which physically separable elements were most stressful, the optimal recovery period, transport density effects, and the value of a recovery period after transport.

Fish sampled were juvenile fall Chinook (8–25 g; June–August 1982–1984) and juvenile spring Chinook (12–45 g; May 1983).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Migration corridor:** The Columbia and Snake rivers, with multiple impoundments that lengthen migration time and expose smolts to predators such as gulls and [[Walleye]].
- **Water conditions:** Temperatures were 15–20 °C for fall Chinook emigrating in warm water versus 11–12 °C for spring Chinook; water temperatures rose from under 8 °C to above 20 °C during the later part of the fall run.
- **Competing fish:** Adult [[American Shad]] aggregating at the end of the collection channel appeared to inhibit smolt movement into the vertical pipe; juvenile [[Steelhead Trout]] presence was suggested as a possible stressor during the spring run.
- **Hatchery context:** The majority of migrants were hatchery-origin fish, which the authors note may have had disrupted feeding habits after release, affecting glucose responses.

## Key Findings

**Collection system stress**
- Plasma cortisol increased sequentially at the gatewell, fish sorter, and raceway, indicating cumulative stress from system elements. Plasma glucose showed similar but less consistent patterns.
- Interrenal nuclear diameter (6.3 ± 0.1 µm, N = 44) showed no hypertrophy, indicating acute rather than chronic stress.
- Fish held 12–48 h in raceways recovered cortisol to near gatewell levels; WBC counts were depressed at 24 h and generally rose by 48 h.
- Swimming endurance was poorest in fish that had just entered the raceway; fish held 48 h took up to eight times longer to fatigue than fish about to enter the raceway (Table 1, fall Chinook, 1984).
- Saltwater challenge results showed low plasma Na⁺ in fresh water (120–135 meq·L⁻¹) for fish reaching the raceway, versus 140–145 meq·L⁻¹ in gatewell fish or those held 24–48 h.
- Holding beyond 3–4 days coincided with increased disease mortality in 1982, consistent with depressed immune function under elevated cortisol.

**System modifications**
- Increased water flow to the upwell box after 1982 (pinch valve pressure 0.6 to 0.8 kg·cm⁻²) appeared to reduce collection stress; 1982 plasma cortisol was higher than in 1983 or 1984.

**Spring versus fall Chinook**
- Spring Chinook reached peak cortisol of 350–400 ng·mL⁻¹ and did not respond further to a secondary stress, suggesting maximal stimulation by the system.
- Fall Chinook peaked at 125–135 ng·mL⁻¹ and did respond to a secondary stress.
- Spring Chinook emigrating at cooler temperatures showed greater glucose increases, supporting greater sensitivity to stress.

**Truck and barge transport**
- Loading into a truck or barge was the most stressful step: cortisol rose significantly immediately after loading.
- Cortisol fell by 3–4 h in transit and did not differ from raceway levels on arrival at Bonneville Dam, indicating the transport vehicles themselves were not further stressful.
- Truck loading densities of 0.02–0.36 kg·L⁻¹ did not differentially affect cortisol dynamics; interrenal nuclear diameter (7.0 ± 0.1 µm, N = 30) indicated no chronic stress.
- Barges (four holds, maximum capacity about 21,818 kg, roughly 1.4 million fall Chinook) were never loaded above 40% capacity during sampling, so loading-density effects in barges were not tested.
- Barge fish recovered faster in transit, with cortisol reduced to raceway levels within 3 h and glucose returning to preloading levels before arrival.

**Recovery and sensitization**
- Fish transported by barge (15–16 h) were more sensitive to a secondary stress than truck-transported fish (3–4 h); fish held 18 h after barge arrival returned to prestress cortisol within 6 h, compared with 12–24 h for fish stressed on arrival.
- The authors argue barge transport is preferable because fish are protected during this period of sensitivity and released at night, when most salmonids emigrate.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Reducing stress in any element of the collection system should reduce total stress.
- Spring Chinook may be seriously affected by the system; the authors call for investigation of release strategies to minimize postrelease stress.
