# Factor Analysis of Species Associations among Fishes of the Kiamichi River, Oklahoma

## Executive Summary
This study applies factor analysis to abundance data for 48 fish species collected at 131 localities in the [[Kiamichi River]], a major tributary of the [[Red River (Oklahoma)]] in southeastern Oklahoma. Six major factors (species association groups) were identified, each corresponding to distinct habitat zones along the river's longitudinal gradient — from clear, high-gradient upland streams in the upper drainage to sluggish, turbid, soft-bottomed reaches in the lower drainage. The analysis reveals that species distribution patterns are strongly correlated with a river's position along an upstream-downstream gradient, with lower-river species tending to also range further west into prairie/lentic environments of Oklahoma. The paper also discusses the likely ecological impacts of the newly constructed [[Hugo Reservoir]] and two additional proposed impoundments on native stream fish communities, predicting increased dominance of lentic-adapted species (Factors I & IV) and depletion of lotic specialists (Factors II & III).

## Background & Description
- **Study system**: The [[Kiamichi River]] Basin is ~185 km long, draining from the steep, forested Kiamichi and Ouachita Mountains (upper basin) through a narrow valley (near Antlers) into a lower alluvial floodplain (near Hugo).
- **Gradient and substrate**: Upper river — gradient ~1–2 m/km, gravel/sandstone substrate, long clear pools and riffles. Middle river (near Antlers) — gradient decreases to ~0.5 m/km. Lower river — gentle, alluvial, sluggish, soft sediments, higher turbidity, extensive agriculture.
- **Climate**: Average annual temperature ~17°C (range 6.3°C in January to 28.2°C in July/August); average annual precipitation 122 cm.
- **Data**: Based on collections by [[Pigg and Hill 1974]], covering 131 localities sampled May–August 1973 via electroshocking, gill netting, and seining. Abundance coded on a 0 (absent) to 4 (abundant) ordinal scale.
- **Analytical approach**: Pearson correlations among 48 species (with ≥7 occurrences) were factored into 30 principal axes (70.0% of variance), with 6 principal axes retained (43.4% of total variance, 61.9% of common variance) and obliquely rotated. Factor scores were computed per locality using the "complete" method (Guertin and Bailey 1970), following methodology similar to [[Stevenson et al. 1974]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Six species-association groups ("Factors") were identified, each linked to a habitat gradient across the drainage:

- **Factor I — [[White Bass Group]]**: 8 species associated with lentic/lake-pond conditions; concentrated in the lower mainstream Kiamichi and large tributaries (sluggish, soft-sediment, base-level habitat). Additional rare large-river species (e.g., *Lepisosteus* spp., *Dorosoma petenense*, *Carpiodes carpio*, *Ictiobus* spp., *Ictalurus furcatus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Menidia audens*) likely belong here but were too scarce for inclusion.

- **Factor II — [[Steelcolor Shiner Group]]**: 10 species tied to larger, permanent, clear upland stream channels; concentrated in the main channel of the upper Kiamichi and large clear tributaries. Bottom-oriented species dominate (e.g., *Percina copelandi*, *P. sciera*, *Etheostoma radiosum*, *Campostoma anomalum*, *Noturus nocturnus*).

- **Factor III — [[Brook Silverside Group]]**: 9 species preferring sluggish sections of clear small-to-large streams; concentrated in upper two-thirds of drainage. Predominantly surface-oriented species (e.g., *Labidesthes sicculus*, *Notropis boops*, *N. emiliae*, *Fundulus olivaceus*, *F. notatus*). Notable complementary distribution between *Fundulus notatus* and *F. olivaceus* (significant negative correlation, r = -0.63, P < 0.001), attributed to competitive exclusion despite ecological similarity.

- **Factor IV — [[Green Sunfish Group]]**: All *Lepomis* species plus *Ictalurus natalis* and *Minytrema melanops*; marginally *Percina caprodes* and *Moxostoma erythrurum*. Pool-inhabiting assemblage, primarily in moderate-sized streams; *L. gulosus* and *L. microlophus* restricted to lower drainage, others more widespread.

- **Factor V — [[Silvery Minnow Group]]**: Concentrated in lower-drainage tributaries; species (*Hybognathus nuchalis*, *Notropis venustus*, *N. lutrensis*, marginally *N. atrocaudalis*) likely originated from the lower Mississippi/Gulf Coastal Plain, associated with clear, silt-bottomed lowland streams.

- **Factor VI — [[Golden Shiner Group]]**: Concentrated in lower-drainage tributaries but widely distributed overall. Species include *Notemigonus crysoleucas*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Ictalurus melas* (tolerant of intermittent streams), and *Etheostoma gracile*, *Notropis amnis* (more restricted to permanently flowing water). Group typifies sloughs, backwaters, and turbid, silty low-gradient streams.

**Broader ecological pattern**: Occurrence in the lower Kiamichi correlates positively with a species' westward range into Oklahoma's prairie environments, likely reflecting tolerance of lentic conditions and/or general ecological flexibility. Complementary/competitive distributions were also documented between *Notropis whipplei* and *N. venustus* (subgenus *Cyprinella*; r = -0.38, P < 0.01), interpreted as reflecting differing biogeographic origins (Ozark uplift vs. Gulf Coastal Plain) paralleling habitat segregation.

**Species without strong factor associations** (correlation <0.40; 11 of 48 species) were attributed to four causes:
1. Sporadic/rare occurrence (e.g., *Erimyzon oblongus*)
2. Broad, generalist distribution (e.g., *Esox americanus*, *Micropterus salmoides*, *M. punctulatus*, *Percina caprodes*)
3. Competitive exclusion depressing correlations (e.g., *Fundulus* spp.)
4. Highly localized specific habitat (e.g., *Aphredoderus sayanus*, *Notropis atrocaudalis*, restricted to lowland swamp habitats between Antlers and Hugo)

## Key Findings
- **Longitudinal zonation**: Fish community structure in the Kiamichi River is strongly structured along an upstream (clear, high-gradient, rocky) to downstream (turbid, sluggish, soft-bottomed) gradient, mirrored by the six factor groups.
- **Complementary species pairs**: Significant negative correlations were found between ecologically similar sibling/congeneric species pairs — *[[Fundulus notatus]]* vs *[[Fundulus olivaceus]]* (r = -0.63) and *[[Notropis whipplei]]* vs *[[Notropis venustus]]* (r = -0.38) — suggesting competitive exclusion shaping fine-scale distribution despite habitat overlap.
- **Biogeographic linkage**: Species restricted to or common in the lower Kiamichi tend to also range farther west into Oklahoma's prairie streams, linking local habitat tolerance (lentic adaptability) to broader biogeographic distribution patterns.
- **Surface vs. bottom orientation**: Within upper-drainage taxa, Factor III (Brook Silverside Group) skews toward surface-oriented species while Factor II (Steelcolor Shiner Group) skews toward bottom-oriented species, reflecting physical differences between fast and quiet-water habitats.
- **Reservoir impact predictions**: Construction of [[Hugo Reservoir]] (conservation pool 5,370 ha; max pool 17,250 ha) and two additional proposed impoundments (lower Jackfork Creek; upper Kiamichi above Jackfork confluence) are predicted to:
  - Increase abundance of Factor I and IV (lentic-tolerant) species
  - Decrease abundance of Factor II and III (lotic-specialist) species
  - Directly threaten swamp-restricted species *[[Aphredoderus sayanus]]* and *[[Elassoma zonatum]]* via habitat flooding
  - Potentially disrupt "island" stream communities in headwaters via increased invasion pressure from reservoir-adapted species, especially during drought stress periods, consistent with island biogeography theory (MacArthur and Wilson 1967)
- **Methodological note**: Comparison with the broader-scale factor analysis of [[Stevenson et al. 1974]] (western/central Oklahoma, coarser sampling units) showed little correspondence in species groupings, illustrating that fine-grained abundance-based analyses (as used here) reveal different — and more ecologically resolved — patterns than coarse presence-absence analyses over larger geographic scales.
