# Endangered and Threatened Species Recovery Planning Guidelines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1981 policy document from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) sets out the procedures for preparing, reviewing, funding, and implementing recovery plans for species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. It covers fish, wildlife, and plants. The guidelines explain how a [[Recovery Plan]] is structured into three parts (Introduction, Recovery outline, and [[Implementation Schedule]]), how recovery tasks and species are ranked through a two-tier [[Priority System]], who may prepare plans and under what conditions, and how plans move through technical, agency, and final review. The document is procedural rather than research-based. It contains no population data, sample sizes, or field results for any species.

## Background & Description

The Act directs the Secretaries of the Interior and Commerce to develop and implement recovery plans for the conservation and survival of listed species. Section 4(g) allows the Secretary to decline a plan only if it would not promote the species' conservation. Responsibility is divided between agencies: the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) handles marine species except birds, FWS handles birds and terrestrial species, and [[Sea Turtles]] are a joint responsibility.

The guidelines frame recovery plans as "vehicles" that accomplish specific objectives and "catalysts" that prompt the actions needed for recovery, not ends in themselves. Recovery efforts end when plan objectives are met, usually at delisting, or when a species goes extinct. Management of a species normally under state jurisdiction returns to that state once it is delisted. The guidelines state that the Service retains flexibility over plan development, and that the guidelines themselves are subject to modification.

Plan format follows three parts plus an appendix:

- **Part 1, Introduction:** habitat requirements, population-limiting factors, past and current distribution and status, and conservation efforts. Lengthy taxonomic discussion is discouraged. Much of this material should come from the species' Listing Package.
- **Part 2, Recovery:** the primary objective, the parameters defining "recovered," a step-down outline of objectives and sub-objectives, and a narrative describing each task. Essential habitat should be identified where possible.
- **Part 3, Implementation Schedule:** the working section that assigns organizations, priorities, timing, and costs. It is used to track tasks and to base funding decisions.

Plans may be prepared by FWS, by a [[Recovery Team]] of three to seven people, by an individual or contractor, by a state (see Appendix E), or by another federal agency. The method depends on the species' range, the complexity of actions, the number of responsible organizations, and available expertise. Typically the preparer completes Parts 1 and 2, while FWS or the Regional Director may prepare Part 3.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidelines require each plan to identify adverse influences and the programs that will reverse them. The categories listed include:

- **Physical:** natural disasters, human activities, pollution, and other environmental contaminants.
- **Biological:** competing species (including conflicts between plans for two or more species), [[Hybridization]], loss of food supply and habitat, predation, and disease.
- **Other:** economic losses (for example, from depredation control), political factors including local ones, and [[Over-Utilization]] such as overfishing, hunting, or trapping.

Recovery programs are grouped under habitat identification and protection, regulation and enforcement, [[Captive Propagation]], control of competing species, and research needed to identify management practices. Plans must state whether a recovery objective includes reclassification from Endangered to Threatened or full delisting, and should use quantifiable terms where possible.

The document stresses that for some species, reproductive potential is so low that the realistic goal may be only to halt downward trends. Long-range planning can be limited by inadequate data, and plans may be developed only to the extent current data justify and amended as new data arrive.

Habitat terminology matters. The term [[Critical Habitat]] should not be applied to any habitat in a recovery plan unless the Secretary has officially designated it and it has been published in the Federal Register. Plans should instead use [[Essential Habitat]].

## Key Findings

**Plan structure and content**
- The step-down outline must agree with the Introduction, and the item numbers in the outline and narrative must correspond.
- Each terminal objective should be a single identifiable task, with no unnecessary tasks, actions, or sub-objectives.
- Research tasks must be specific (for example, not just "conduct life history studies").
- The [[Office of Management and Budget]] requires that land acquisition sites be identified, with location and acreage where available, in an approved plan. "Abbreviated" plans with only general site descriptions will not qualify as FWS recovery plans.

**Implementation schedule and priorities**
- The schedule should cover at least three years beyond the current fiscal year. The first phase covers roughly the first three to five years.
- Task priorities are:
  - **Priority 1:** actions absolutely essential to prevent extinction (example: peregrine falcon law enforcement to prevent taking).
  - **Priority 2:** actions needed to maintain current population status (example: maintaining existing peregrine falcon nest sites).
  - **Priority 3:** all other actions needed for full recovery (example: establishing new nest sites).
- The schedule's general categories include information gathering (research or operational), habitat maintenance and acquisition, predator and competitor control, disease control, education, regulations, enforcement, and administration.
- Research tasks are distinguished from operational data gathering by the need for sampling design, controls, new methods, or statistical interpretation.

**Two-tier priority system**
- The [[Listing Priority Table]] ranks species and subspecies in twelve categories by threat level (high, medium, or low), information availability, and taxonomic status. Species rank above subspecies.
- The [[Recovery Priority Table]] uses degree of threat and recovery potential instead of information availability. Recovery potential depends on how well the biology is understood, how easily threats can be alleviated, and how management-intensive recovery will be.
- Threat categories are defined by time: high means imminent threat (within two years), medium means a threat within two to five years, and low means no known deleterious threats within five years or threats not conclusively established.
- A priority 1 task for a priority 12 species can take precedence over a priority 3 task for a priority 1 species.

**Review and approval**
- The sequence is: technical review draft (Regional Director to Director, 45-day review), agency review draft (sent within 60 days of receiving technical comments, 45-day review), and final draft (sent within 60 days of receiving agency comments, 45-day review).
- Only the Director can approve a plan. Approved plans are printed with 25 copies sent to the Director.
- Public reviews may be scheduled at the Regional Director's discretion, with Part 3 omitted if cooperating agencies have not had a chance to review it.

**Recovery teams**
- Teams of three to seven members are appointed by Regional Directors in consultation with states and cooperating organizations. Members are chosen for expertise, involvement, willingness, and availability.
- Teams are autonomous planning bodies. They may not implement recovery actions, lobby, speak for an agency, or determine Critical Habitat. Their technical assistance is kept separate from plan preparation.
- Team funding is handled through Regional Directors. Team business uses a Memorandum of Agreement or purchase order where the leader is not a federal employee.

**Contracting**
- Contracts are generally used for services over $5,000 with multiple payment schedules and up to one year of delivery. Purchase orders cover short-term services under $5,000.
- [[Cooperative Agreement]] and [[Memorandum of Understanding]] options exist for work with other federal or state agencies. Cooperative agreements with funding must be genuinely cooperative or the contracting office will reject them.
- Non-competitive procurements require a detailed written justification with factual, not generalized, reasons.

**State preparation**
- A state may prepare a plan for a species that resides entirely within its borders if it has a Section 6 Cooperative Agreement or a special agreement with the Regional Director. The agreement must set review intervals of no more than one year, target dates, and funding levels. If state action is inadequate within one year, the Service directs the effort.

**Responsibilities**
- The Regional Director's duties include emergency protection, selecting the preparation method, reviewing drafts, printing and distributing approved plans, budget proposals, and annual work plan advice.
- The Director's duties include setting plan schedules, providing guidelines and training, approving plans, reviewing drafts, and allocating funds through Program Advices.

**Distribution**
- Approved plans are distributed to Washington offices (including Program Plans, Refuge Management, Federal Aid, Federal Wildlife Permit Office, Division of Wildlife Ecology, Division of Fishery Ecology, Heritage Conservation and Recreation Service, and the Environmental Protection Agency), the National Wildlife Laboratory, the Fish and Wildlife Reference Service, and other cooperating agencies. The list includes roughly fourteen entries.
