# Taxonomic Status of the Controversial Genera and Species of Parrotfishes (Family Scaridae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scaridae

## Executive Summary

This Smithsonian Contributions to Zoology monograph (Number 17, 1969) by [[Leonard P. Schultz]] revises the taxonomy of the parrotfish family Scaridae. It resolves long-standing confusion over names based on early descriptions and type specimens, and it brings together males and females previously treated as separate species. The paper provides a descriptive list of 11 genera, 5 subgenera, 68 species and 5 subspecies, illustrated with 38 color figures. Its main taxonomic moves include the synonymy of several nominal species, the division of [[Scarus]] and [[Sparisoma]] into subgenera, and the designation of neotypes and lectotypes to stabilize nomenclature.

## Background & Description

Parrotfishes are reef-associated labroid fishes in which species identification has been difficult because early descriptions (e.g., [[Forskal]] 1775, [[Bloch]] 1786–1791, [[Ruppell]] 1828, 1835, and [[Cuvier and Valenciennes]] 1839) often lack color-pattern and anatomical detail, and many types have deteriorated. Schultz's stated aims were to (1) present information on difficult species, (2) reconcile his conclusions with those of [[J. L. B. Smith]] (1956, 1959), and (3) provide a current descriptive list.

Taxonomic characters used throughout include:
- Number of median predorsal scales (commonly 4–7)
- Number of scale rows on the cheek (1–4)
- Pectoral fin ray formula (e.g., ii,12; ii,13; ii,14)
- Upper pharyngeal tooth rows (one main row with or without a rudimentary row, or three rows in [[Bolbometopon]] and the Sparisomatinae)
- Color pattern, sexual dichromatism and color phase
- Gill raker counts

Schultz notes that many characters overlap between closely related taxa, so he treats some traditional generic characters as of limited value at the genus level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species covered occur across the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans, the Red Sea, and in parts of the Mediterranean (via the Suez Canal, for *Leptoscarus vaigiensis*). Specimens were collected largely during the International Indian Ocean Expedition (IIOE, 1964–1967, including the [[Anton Bruun]] cruises), the [[Te Vega]] expeditions in the western Pacific, and collections at Diego Garcia, One Tree Island (Queensland), and Papeete, Tahiti. Schultz reports that several species occur in very large schools, notably the juveniles of *Scarus sordidus* and the Indian Ocean populations of *Xanothon erythrodon*, and that some species are among the most abundant parrotfishes in the Indian and Pacific Oceans.

Ecological observations in the text are limited. The author cites behavioral and natural-history work on color change, night habits (Stark and Davis 1966), and diurnal-nocturnal activity (Hobson 1965), and notes that sex reversal and color change with age and sex complicate identification in living populations.

## Key Findings

**Scope and results**
- The descriptive list comprises 11 genera, 5 subgenera, 68 species and 5 subspecies.
- The paper includes 38 color illustrations on plates; the author says the work relies on specimens collected by the IIOE and other expeditions.

**Major nomenclatural changes**
- [[Scarops]] is monotypic; *Scarops rubroviolaceus* absorbs [[Pseudoscarus jordani]], *Callyodon africanus*, and *Scarus paluca* as synonyms. *S. jordani* is treated as the mature male and *S. rubroviolaceus* as the female.
- [[Bolbometopon]] contains two species, *B. muricatus* and *B. bicolor*. *Cetoscarus* is a junior synonym. *Scarus pulchellus* is the adult female of *B. bicolor* (following [[Randall]] 1963).
- [[Ypsiscarus]] is elevated from subgenus to full genus, with *Y. ovifrons* and *Y. oedema*. *Callyodon viridifucatus* is a synonym of *Y. ovifrons*.
- *Scarus harid* has three subspecies: *harid* (Red Sea), *vexillus* (western Indian Ocean) and *longiceps* (tropical western and central Pacific). *Hipposcarus schultzi* and *Callyodon toshi* are synonyms of *S. h. longiceps*.
- *Chlorurus* is a junior synonym of *Scarus*, which is divided into the subgenera *Scarus*, *Xenoscarops* and *Callyodon*. *Sparisoma* is divided into the subgenera *Callyodontichthys* and *Sparisoma*.
- *Scarus gibbus* absorbs *S. microrhinos* and *S. strongylocephalus* as synonyms, based on overlapping counts and color patterns.
- *Scarus sordidus* absorbs *Xanothon bipallidus* and related names; no significant Red Sea–Indian–Pacific differences were found in fin ray or scale counts.
- *Scarus taeniurus* is supported as a valid species, with *S. fowleri* treated as its male, and *S. carifanus*, *S. bataviensis* and *S. baliensis* (in part) as synonyms.
- *Scarus forsteri* is retained as valid, distinct from *S. taeniurus* (see Table 8 comparison).
- *Scarus ghobban* absorbs *S. dussumieri* (Cuvier and Valenciennes), with *S. apridentatus* as the adult male.
- *Scarus mus* is recognized as valid, with *Callyodon speigleri* and several other names as synonyms.
- Among species previously unidentified, *Scarus enneacanthus* is recognized as valid, and *S. capitaneus* is treated as its objective synonym.

**New species**
- [[Scarus fehlmanni]] is described from the Red Sea (holotype USNM 202419, 248 mm standard length, immature male). It has 6 predorsal scales, 2 rows of cheek scales, ii,13 pectoral rays and 25+33 gill rakers on the first arch. Schultz places it in the subgenus *Xenoscarops* while noting that this arrangement is open to question.

**Nomenclatural actions**
- Neotypes were designated for *Scarus psittacus* Forskal (USNM 201805, Red Sea, 182 mm), *S. forsteri* Cuvier and Valenciennes (USNM 202607, Tahiti, 220 mm) and *S. sordidus* Forskal (USNM 202297, Red Sea, 210 mm).
- Lectotypes were selected for *S. quoyi*, *S. baliensis*, *S. bataviensis* and other names.

**Unidentifiable names**
- Schultz (1958) had listed 34 nominal species as unidentifiable; several have since been identified. Ten further *Pseudoscarus* names described in 1875–1885 by Castelnau, Alleyne and Macleay, and De Vis are listed as unidentifiable, pending examination of their types.

**Methodological notes**
- Table 1 and Table 2 measure cheek-scale patch width against interorbital width and body proportions. The author concludes that these ratios overlap and cannot separate the *harid* forms alone.
- Table 3 shows that *S. h. vexillus* has 22–31 cheek scales, whereas *harid* and *longiceps* have 15–22, which Schultz considers a useful character.
- Table 5 shows that the snout profile angle increases with standard length in *S. gibbus*, so the author regards it as of doubtful taxonomic value.
- Tables 13 (a large count table of median predorsal scales, cheek scale rows and pectoral rays across all species) summarizes data for the descriptive list.

**Conclusions on character use**
- Pectoral ray number is "of considerable value" for species but "of little or no importance" at the generic level.
- Cheek scale rows are useful for species but of lesser importance for genera.
- The pharyngeal dentition (number of tooth rows on upper pharyngeals) is treated as a key character for generic placement, including in *Bolbometopon*.
