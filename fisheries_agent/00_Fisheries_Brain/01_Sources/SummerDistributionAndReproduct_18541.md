## Executive Summary

This 1976 technical report presents findings from a 1975 summer survey of fish in the Colorado River and its tributaries within [[Grand Canyon National Park]], conducted to assess distribution and reproductive status. Twenty-two localities were sampled via seining in July and August 1975. Ten fish species were collected, comprising 40% native and 60% exotic (introduced) taxa. Reproduction was confirmed in all four native species and in three of six exotic species via collection of young-of-the-year individuals. With the notable exception of the rare [[Bonytail Chub]], native fish outnumbered exotic fish in both total abundance and number of localities occupied. Native fish population levels appeared comparable to those recorded in 1968, suggesting relative stability of the native ichthyofauna despite increasing exotic species pressure. No ectoparasites were detected on any examined specimens.

## Background & Description

The study was commissioned by the National Park Service under the Colorado River Research Program to build a scientific foundation for visitor-use planning along the Colorado River corridor from Lee's Ferry to Grand Wash Cliffs. It builds on earlier ichthyofaunal work by [[Robert Rush Miller]] (1944, 1946, 1961, 1972, 1975) and systematic/distributional studies by Holden and Stalnaker (1970, 1975).

**Methodology:** Fish were collected by seine (6.5m × 1.5m × 6mm mesh, or 3.0m × 1.0m × 6mm mesh) at 22 tributary and mainstem localities, including Little Colorado River, Carbon Creek, Chuar Creek, Lava Creek, Cardenas Wash, Bright Angel Creek, Crystal Creek, Elves Chasm, Tapeats Creek, Kanab Creek, Havasu Creek, Stone Creek, Deer Creek, Parashont Wash, National Canyon, Diamond Creek, and others. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin, later transferred to 50% isopropanol, measured to the nearest mm (total length), and examined for ectoparasites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River within [[Grand Canyon National Park]] functions as one of the last major refugia for endemic Southwestern fish fauna, which is increasingly imperiled across the American Southwest due to expanding recreational and commercial water use (Deacon & Minckley 1974). The river's mainstem and tributary network — including cool, spring-fed side canyons and the warmer, turbid [[Little Colorado River]] — provide heterogeneous thermal and hydrological habitats supporting both native and non-native fish.

The [[Little Colorado River]] emerged as a critical, apparently unique spawning refuge for the [[Bonytail Chub]], likely due to elevated water temperatures and reduced turbulence relative to other tributaries. This same tributary is historically significant as the primary collection locality for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), raising concern about potential hybridization between the two congeners, a phenomenon previously documented by Holden and Stalnaker (1970).

Rainbow trout stocking by Arizona, Nevada, and Utah Game and Fish Departments (both upstream and downstream of the canyon) has established self-sustaining reproducing populations in select cold, clear tributaries — notably [[Tapeats Creek]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]] — creating a naturalized salmonid fishery within an otherwise warmwater native fish system. This represents a direct example of exotic species establishment via stocking that could pose long-term competitive risk to native cyprinids and catostomids.

Notably, no ectoparasites were found on any specimens examined, contrasting with documented parasite burdens (e.g., *Lernaea* copepods) affecting other native Southwestern fish populations (James 1968; Wilson et al. 1966).

## Key Findings

- **Species composition:** 10 species collected; 40% native, 60% exotic, across 22 survey localities in July–August 1975.
- **Native fish dominance:** Except for the bonytail chub, native fish were more abundant than exotics in both total numbers and localities occupied.
- **Occurrence rates (% of 22 localities):**
  - Speckled Dace: 86.5%
  - Bluehead Sucker: 68.1%
  - Flannelmouth Sucker: 45.0%
  - Fathead Minnow: 40.9%
  - Rio Grande Killifish: 31.8%
  - Rainbow Trout: 23.6%
  - Carp: 9.0%
  - Bonytail Chub: 4.5%
  - Channel Catfish: 4.5%
  - Black Bullhead: 4.5%

- **Reproduction confirmed (via young-of-year/juveniles) in:**
  - All four native species: [[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]]
  - Three exotic species: [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Rio Grande Killifish]], [[Rainbow Trout]]
  - Reproducing rainbow trout populations specifically confirmed in [[Tapeats Creek]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]]
  - Reproduction suspected but unverified in [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Black Bullhead]]

- **Size/juvenile data (July vs. August mean total length & % juveniles):**
  - Rainbow Trout: 105.7mm (90.0% juv., July) → 128.4mm (83.0% juv., Aug.)
  - Speckled Dace: 47.9mm (50.2% juv.) → 65.2mm (50.9% juv.)
  - Fathead Minnow: 56.2mm (28.5% juv.) → 50.6mm (26.9% juv.)
  - Flannelmouth Sucker: 106.1mm (83.0% juv.) → 79.4mm (93.8% juv.)
  - Bluehead Sucker: 107.9mm (92.8% juv.) → 82.4mm (96.2% juv.)
  - Channel Catfish: no July data → 273.0mm (Aug., all adults)
  - Rio Grande Killifish: 47.4mm (51.1% juv.) → 40.0mm (57.4% juv.)

- **Bonytail Chub status:** Collected exclusively from the [[Little Colorado River]]; considered rare and geographically restricted within Grand Canyon. July specimens measured ~140–200mm; August specimens ~60–100mm. No adults were collected or observed — only juveniles — strongly suggesting the Little Colorado River serves as a critical spawning/nursery area for this species.

- **Population stability:** Native fish populations in 1975 appeared similar in abundance to 1968 levels (per Miller 1975), indicating relative short-term stability despite ongoing exotic species establishment.

- **Health status:** All examined fish were negative for ectoparasites.

- **Conservation concerns raised:**
  - Potential hybridization risk between [[Bonytail Chub]] and the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the Little Colorado River.
  - Recommendation to collect 20+ adult bonytail chub for a brood stock at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery.
  - Recommendation for similar brood stock action for humpback chub if collected.
  - Proposed visitor education initiatives (brochures, interpretive talks, a 15-minute mandatory film) to reduce angler-caused mortality of rare/endangered species, including mention of the [[Peregrine Falcon]] as a co-occurring rare species of conservation concern.
  - Recommendation against further expansion of recreational/industrial river use beyond 1975 levels to preserve system integrity.

- **Research recommendations:** Monthly systematic surveys (e.g., near Phantom Ranch), four seasonal river-length surveys (summer, fall, winter, spring) from Lee's Ferry to Pierce's Ferry, and benthic sampling to correlate diet/stomach content data with tributary use patterns.
