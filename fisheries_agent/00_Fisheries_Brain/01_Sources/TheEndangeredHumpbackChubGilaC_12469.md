# The Endangered Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in Arizona: A Review of Past Studies and Suggestions for Future Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 draft report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Research Branch), submitted to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Upper Colorado Region), compiles and reanalyzes the scattered "grey literature," agency reports and unpublished data on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the Colorado River and its tributaries below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. It was prepared to support the ongoing Section 7 consultation on dam operations. The review covers hydrology, limnology, benthic and zooplankton communities, distribution and abundance, reproduction, thermal tolerance, age and growth, habitat use, movement, parasites, and food habits, and closes with a list of research gaps and proactive management options. The document is a draft; its recommendations section is explicitly incomplete.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Gila cypha]] (Miller, 1946), a cyprinid described from the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.
- **Status:** Listed as endangered on March 11, 1967. Listing reflected a restricted, fragmented distribution, small population size, and threats from hypolimnial-release dams, reservoirs and cold tailwaters.
- **Regulatory history:** The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] issued a jeopardy opinion in 1978. Dam operations were also found to limit recovery of the [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program began in December 1982. Consultation then moved toward Conservation Measures in place of Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives.
- **Data sources:** Requests were sent to agencies and collectors, and literature was searched through the DIALOG databases (BIOSIS, NTIS, ASFA). Four large Department databases were compiled in dBASE III PLUS, drawn from [[Carothers et al. 1981]], [[Kaeding and Zimmerman]] (1982, 1983), [[Maddux et al. 1987]], and the 1987–1989 monitoring program.
- **Analytical constraints:** Gear types, effort, seasons and sites varied among data sets. Most samples contained no humpback chub, which produced zero-inflated, non-normal catch-rate distributions. Parametric tests therefore had limited applicability.
- **Study area conventions:** Locations are given as river miles from Lee's Ferry, with kilometres as the primary distance measure. The mainstream is divided into reaches 10 to 50 following Maddux et al. (1987), and seasons are Spring (March–May), Summer (June–August), Autumn (September–November) and Winter (December–February).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The Colorado River drains 635,000 km², and annual flow at Lee's Ferry has ranged from 2.4 to 20.5 maf since 1963. Pre-dam flow was unimodal with a June peak from snowmelt. Post-dam flows were regulated, with daily fluctuations of up to 31,500 cfs and stage changes of up to 13 vertical feet. In 1983–1986 high releases were steady, and drought after 1987 shifted releases toward a bimodal pattern. Tributaries fall into two groups: sediment-laden, intermittent streams (Paria River, [[Little Colorado River]], Kanab Creek), and clear, spring-fed streams from karst formations (Bright Angel, Tapeats, Deer and Havasu creeks). The Little Colorado River gets a perennial base flow of about 225 cfs from [[Blue Springs]].

**Thermal regime.** Releases from the hypolimnion (about 70 m depth) hold Lee's Ferry at roughly 7–12 °C. Mainstream temperatures seldom exceed 16 °C about 250 river miles downstream. Backwaters and tributary mouths warm under steady flows (above 25 °C in some cases), but daily fluctuations under peaking operations drain and refill backwaters, which reduces warming.

**Water chemistry.** Impoundment raised mean annual salinity by about 50 mg/L, to about 600 mg/L. Blue Springs water is highly charged with CO₂ and oversaturated with calcite. Calcite precipitation forms travertine dams in the lower Little Colorado River. Phosphorus appears to limit primary production in the upper tailwater, and suspended sediments from tributary floods reduce light penetration.

**Productivity and invertebrates.** Only rough primary-production estimates exist (Cole and Kubly 1977). In 1988–1989, backwater Ponar samples yielded 17 benthic taxa, with mean densities of 3,592 ind/0.1 m² above the Little Colorado River and 208 ind/0.1 m² below. Oligochaetes made up more than 95% of benthic organisms. Backwater zooplankton (mean 1,363 ind/m³) were much denser than mainstream zooplankton (341 ind/m³), and cladocerans were relatively more abundant in backwaters.

**Riparian zones.** Pre-dam Old High Water Zone vegetation (mesquite, catclaw acacia) is distinguished from post-dam New High Water Zone vegetation (tamarisk, coyote willow, arrowweed). Their role in river productivity is poorly studied.

**Nursery habitat.** Backwaters are described as "very important nursery habitats" for native and introduced fishes.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- Pre-dam records are very limited: three individuals used to describe the species, plus archaeological bones.
- Below Glen Canyon Dam, humpback chub were rare to common in the first 26 km (16 mi) in the early 1967–1969 surveys, but were not collected there during 1984–1989 GCES work.
- About 77.5% of all chub caught (466 individuals, all gear combined) came from the 10-mile reach RM 60–70, the Little Colorado River confluence zone. Only 1.3% came from above that reach.
- No humpback chub were taken from mainstream nearshore habitats above the Little Colorado River in 1987–1989.
- Trammel net catch rates in the Little Colorado River in 1987–1989 were significantly different among years (ANOVA F = 7.188, P < 0.001). Zero catches made up 42% of 430 samples. Percentages of zero catches were 20% (1987), 52% (1988) and 44% (1989).

**Population estimates**
- Kaeding and Zimmerman (1982) estimated 7,000–8,000 adults (>200 mm TL) in 1980–1981, a "ball park" figure.
- Minckley (1988, 1989) used Peterson estimates for the lower 1.2 km of the Little Colorado River: 5,783 (SE 679) in 1987, 7,060 (SE 574) in 1988, and 10,120 for the same reach in 1989. For the 15 km reach in 1989 the estimate was 18,253.
- The report concludes that the population-estimation assumptions of demographic and geographic closure were violated, so the precision and bias of these estimates are highly questionable.

**Reproduction and early development**
- Reproduction probably occurs from spring into summer. Spawning is thought to coincide with peak spring flows at 12–23 °C.
- Adult fecundity was estimated at 5,262 eggs/kg body weight (Hamman 1982).
- Carothers et al. (1981) captured young-of-the-year almost exclusively in the Little Colorado River. The report suggests that the Little Colorado River is the principal spawning and nursery area, although rare mainstream reproduction is possible.
- Marsh (1985) found complete embryo mortality at 5 °C, 10 °C and 30 °C. Hatch peaked at 20 °C (60%).

**Thermal tolerance and growth**
- In 1988–1989 thermal-shock experiments, mortality of native larval fishes was significantly higher in mainstream cages (1988, combined species). In 1989 this effect was not seen, possibly because larvae were larger.
- Von Bertalanffy asymptotic length estimates were 442.7 mm (length-at-age) and 434.9 mm (tag–recapture). Few chub over 450 mm TL have been recorded.
- Preliminary otolith analysis of 49 fish (104–476 mm TL) suggests ages of 1 to 22 years.

**Movement**
- In 1980–1981, Kaeding and Zimmerman recaptured 3.9–5.4% of tagged fish. Maximum movement was 17.1 km.
- Maddux et al. (1987) reported a median recapture distance of 0.5 km, with the greatest distance 10 km. Most recaptures occurred in spring and early summer.
- Seven recaptures in 1987–1989 showed mainstream-to-tributary movement.

**Biological interactions**
- The parasitic copepod [[Lernaea cyprinacea]] was found in 54% of 65 juveniles in October 1978. Infection was more common in Little Colorado River fish, which was interpreted as evidence for tributary–mainstream movement.
- Adults in the 1981 spawning season were heavily infected with *Aeromonas hydrophila*.
- Channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) may prey on chub. Crescent-shaped wounds were observed on adult chub.

**Food habits**
- Young-of-the-year fed on dipterans and possibly attached diatoms.
- Adult stomachs from the Little Colorado River (n = 17, 1985–1986) were dominated by filamentous algae (77% mean volumetric share, presumably *Cladophora glomerata*). Five of the 17 stomachs were empty.

**Research recommendations (incomplete list)**
- Factors affecting reproductive success and early survival, including habitat quantification in tributaries and mainstream.
- Thermal shock effects on early life stages.
- Relationships among growth, survival and fecundity under controlled thermal regimes.
- Links between flood timing and magnitude in the Little Colorado River and reproductive success.
- Effects of competitors, predators and parasites.
- Proactive management: modifying mainstream hydrology and temperatures, augmenting habitat, and introducing fish into new or modified habitats.
- The report notes that the authors intended to expand this list in the final report.

**Data appendices.** Appendices I–III hold database file structures and seasonal catch statistics. Tables 1–9 cover tributary discharges, benthic and zooplankton taxa, humpback chub records, statistical tests and growth parameters. Full figures and tables are omitted here.
