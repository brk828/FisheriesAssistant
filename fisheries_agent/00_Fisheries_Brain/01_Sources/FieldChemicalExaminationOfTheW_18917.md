# Field Chemical Examination of the Waters in Tennessee Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1950 paper by Charles S. Shoup (Department of Biology, Vanderbilt University) reports field water-chemistry data from roughly 430 localities on the principal streams of [[Tennessee]]. Sampling was carried out mainly in summer, drawing on a biological survey begun in 1938 by the Tennessee Department of Conservation, Division of Game and Fish. The paper explains the chemical parameters that govern fish and aquatic life (dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, alkalinity, pH, temperature) and tabulates results by stream and station. Its central message is that water chemistry, especially buffering capacity set by bedrock geology, should guide fisheries management and stocking decisions.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Supplement earlier survey papers (Shoup 1940, 1941, 1943, 1944, 1948) and provide a practical guide for field water-chemistry studies tied to fisheries biology.
- **Historical context:** The paper traces freshwater biology from [[S. A. Forbes]] (Illinois River), [[E. A. Birge]] and Chancey Juday (Wisconsin lakes), J. Reighard (Michigan), and C. A. Kofoid (Illinois).
- **Methods:** Titration for total alkalinity with 0.02N sulphuric acid (methyl orange), phenolphthalein alkalinity with N/44 NaOH, free carbon dioxide by titration to a "vanishing" pink, dissolved oxygen, and pH measured before and after aeration (pH₁ and pH₂). Temperatures are in °F. Methods followed [[Standard Methods for Water Analysis]] (American Public Health Association, 1936) and Theroux, Eldridge, and Mallmann (1936).
- **Data tables:** About 430 stations are condensed into 20+ table pages, organized by stream and reported with turbidity, free CO₂, dissolved oxygen, air and water temperature, pH, and alkalinity. Field notes describe cover, pool grade, pollution sources, and fishability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dissolved oxygen.** Most game fishes prefer at least 3–4 ppm dissolved oxygen (Needham 1938). Flowing, riffled water is usually saturated, but deep pools and bottom waters of warm lakes and southern streams can become oxygen-deficient in midsummer, which constrains stocking.

**Carbon dioxide.** Atmospheric CO₂ (0.03%) has little direct effect on fish. Elevated CO₂ can stimulate respiration, reduce hemoglobin's oxygen-carrying capacity, and signal pollution. In limestone regions, free CO₂ is converted to bicarbonate; in soft-water siliceous streams, small amounts can produce acidity.

**Alkalinity and buffering.** Total alkalinity is mostly bicarbonate (titratable with methyl orange). Phenolphthalein alkalinity indicates carbonates. Streams on limestone (Ordovician Central Basin, Knox Dolomite, Great Valley of East Tennessee) are strongly buffered and fertile. Streams on the Cumberland Plateau, Highland Rim sands and clays, and Smoky Mountain uplands are poorly buffered and vulnerable to acid pollution.

**pH.** Natural waters range from pH 3.2 to 10.5, with most between about 5.0 and 8.5. Shelford (1925) relationships are cited: pH falls with rising CO₂, rises with bicarbonate, and streams above pH 8.0 contain almost no free CO₂.

**Temperature.** Each species has tolerance limits that vary with life stage. Trout are optimally held near 65 °F. Needham (1938) gives maximum tolerable temperatures of 75 °F (brook trout), 81 °F (brown trout), and 83 °F (rainbow trout). Thermal stratification in lakes can trap cool, oxygen-poor water below a warm surface layer.

**Acid mine drainage.** Coal-mine discharges are the main unnatural acid source. The Cumberland Plateau streams are most at risk because they have little alkali reserve. Limestone bedrock can neutralize acid over short distances, but spring floods can overwhelm this buffering.

**Regional patterns.** Fertile, warm-water streams with moderate to high alkalinity support aquatic vegetation and rich bottom fauna. The Sequatchie River is alkaline through most of its length because it is cut into limestone, in contrast to neutral neighboring Plateau streams.

## Key Findings

- **Survey scope:** About 430 localities across Tennessee, in streams underlain by 16 geological formations, sampled in summer over more than five years.
- **Oxygen threshold:** Game fish generally need at least 3–4 ppm dissolved oxygen; deep and bottom waters in warm months may fall below this.
- **Alkalinity as a fertility index:** Moderate-to-high total alkalinity marks productive warm-water streams. Examples include Beaverdam Creek, Holston/Watauga tributaries, and Central Basin rivers, where values often exceed 100–200 ppm.
- **Vulnerable streams:** Low-alkalinity streams of the Cumberland Plateau and the Eastern Highland Rim, such as New River (Scott County) and the upper Caney Fork, are highly susceptible to acid mine drainage.
- **Neutralization limits:** Limestone can neutralize acid mine drainage only over short reaches, and spring floods can carry acid past it.
- **Obey River system:** The Obey River, once a stream of marked alkali reserve (average pH 8.1, M.O. alkalinity 101 ppm, dissolved oxygen 7.93 ppm before impoundment), was submerged by [[Dale Hollow Reservoir]].
- **Temperature limits (per Needham 1938):** 75 °F (brook trout), 81 °F (brown trout), 83 °F (rainbow trout), with the caveat that these require confirmation.
- **Recommendations for field work:** Measure maximum water temperature on the hottest clear afternoons, test for oxygen stratification before stocking, use both aerated and unaerated pH readings, and treat total alkalinity and free CO₂ as key indicators of pollution.
- **Methodological caution:** Titrating to a "vanishing" pink endpoint is more reliable for free CO₂ in low-concentration streams than a permanent faint pink (Peters, Williams, and Mitchell 1940).
- **Management principle:** Water chemistry is an integral part of fisheries survey work. Knowing local chemistry lets biologists manage the environment (fertility, oxygen, temperature) and set sound stocking policy.
