# Letter from Town of Truckee to Cui-ui Recovery Team Regarding Recovery Plan

## Executive Summary
This document is a policy/advocacy letter from the Mayor of Truckee, California, addressed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [[Cui-ui Recovery Team]], rather than a primary research paper. It concerns the recovery status and management strategy for the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a sucker species endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]] and the [[Lower Truckee River]] (Nevada). The letter reports a notably successful 1994 spawning run, describes a tenfold increase in population since the early 1980s, and advocates for integrating upstream (California) water rights, ecosystem management, and flow-management practices (e.g., "ramping" of reservoir releases) into the species' recovery planning. It also raises awareness of co-occurring imperiled taxa in the watershed, including [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] and Tahoe yellow cress.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Cui-ui]] are anadromous-like lake residents of [[Pyramid Lake]] (a terminal, endorheic lake in the Great Basin) that migrate into the [[Lower Truckee River]] to spawn.
- **Spawning habitat**: Identified as "the critical factor in Cui-ui recovery" — the letter recommends prompt designation of appropriate lower Truckee River reaches as critical habitat.
- **Flow dependency**: Spawning success is tied to river discharge dynamics, specifically:
  - Minimum flow maintenance throughout the year in the main stem and tributaries.
  - "Ramping" (gradual, controlled adjustment) of reservoir releases, particularly fall flood-control drawdowns managed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, which affects water availability for subsequent spring spawning.
- **Watershed context**: The Cui-ui's fate is linked to broader [[Truckee River Basin]] ecosystem health, water quality/quantity, and coexists with other sensitive riparian-dependent species (e.g., [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], Tahoe yellow cress) that may also require Endangered Species Act protections within 5–20 years.
- **Water allocation**: California's total entitlement in the basin is fixed at 32,000 acre-feet (69% groundwater), much of which returns to the river as high-quality surface flow, effectively augmenting river discharge available for spawning-related management.
- **Restoration potential**: Rehabilitation of native riparian vegetative canopy on the lower Truckee River combined with Delta improvements is cited as having potential water savings of 50,000–71,000 acre-feet annually (per USDI Senate testimony, 1994), which could reduce reliance on reservoir releases for spawning flows during dry years.

## Key Findings
- **1994 spawning run**: Described as "the most successful spawning run in decades."
- **Population trend**: Cui-ui numbers have increased approximately tenfold since the early 1980s, indicating a shift from "crisis stage" to a more stable management phase.
- **Recommended management action — Essential Spawning Years**: The letter proposes that recovery planning identify and delineate specific "essential spawning years" based on age-class structure and genetic considerations (e.g., ensuring spawning opportunities for cohorts from the strong 1986, 1993, and 1994 runs), allowing reservoir managers to optimize water releases in critical years and conserve supplies in non-critical years.
- **Critical habitat designation**: Recommended as a priority — appropriate lower Truckee River sections should be formally designated to benefit both the [[Cui-ui]] and regional water-management interests.
- **Flood-control flow management**: Current Army Corps of Engineers fall drawdown policy is flagged as a constraint on water availability for spring spawning in low-precipitation years; "ramping" of releases between reservoirs is cited as beneficial to both Cui-ui recovery and upstream fisheries (referenced in a companion "Enclosure A," not included in this excerpt).
- **Multi-species/ecosystem context**: The letter references a March 1994 USFWS memorandum, *"An Ecosystem Approach to Fish and Wildlife Conservation,"* emphasizing partnership-based, ecosystem-level conservation rather than single-species management, and cites a Draft EIS for "Eight Eastside Rivers" addressing other sensitive upstream species.
- **Policy stance**: Advocates that Cui-ui recovery and upstream (California) economic/ecological interests are not inherently conflicting, and that coordinated water management could yield co-benefits for water quality, quantity, and wildlife across the [[Truckee River Basin]].
