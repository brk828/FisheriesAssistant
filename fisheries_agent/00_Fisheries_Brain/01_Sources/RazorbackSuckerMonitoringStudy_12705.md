# 1988 razorback sucker monitoring study progress reports I, III, & IV

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report documents a 1988 rearing and monitoring study for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) along the lower [[Colorado River]]. The study established three isolated, netted coves at **Goose Flats Backwater (C-5)** to rear juvenile razorback suckers stocked from hatchery ponds ([[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] and [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]]). Despite predator removal efforts prior to stocking, substantial population losses occurred in enclosed coves, attributed to net barrier breaches (beaver damage), transport stress mortality, avian predation, and residual piscivorous fish that evaded removal. The study highlights significant methodological challenges in using semi-natural backwater enclosures for headstarting an endangered catostomid species.

## Background & Description

**Stocked Juvenile Razorback Suckers (Niland-reared, 28 July 1988):**
- Mean Total Length (TL): 113 mm (range 88–137 mm)
- Mean Weight: 18 g (~31 fish/lb)
- Condition: Excellent; minimal mortality during transport (<5 individuals across ~3,007 fish stocked)

- Larger size class: 114 fish ≥250 mm TL (mean 268 mm; range 250–343 mm)

Marking methods included fin-clipping (right pelvic fin = 1988 year class; left pelvic = 1986/1987 year class), cove-specific secondary marks (caudal/dorsal fin punches or clips), and white Floy tags. An intended tetracycline bone/scale marking method failed due to hatchery miscommunication.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat requirements:** Juvenile razorback suckers were reared in shallow backwater coves (0.10–0.13 ha, 2.0–2.5 m depth) containing rooted aquatic vegetation (*[[Myriophyllum]]* sp., water milfoil) for predator escape cover, and sand/silt substrate with organic/algal accumulation for foraging.
- **Predator community:** Enclosed coves harbored numerous non-native piscivores and competitors, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and *[[Tilapia]]* sp. — reflecting a heavily invaded, altered lower Colorado River fish assemblage.
- **Documented predation:** Stomach content analysis confirmed direct predation on stocked razorbacks by [[Channel Catfish]] (all three sampled individuals contained razorbacks, one with three) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (1 of 5 sampled). [[Flathead Catfish]] stomach was empty.
- **Avian predation pressure:** Great blue herons and egrets were frequently observed hunting along cove shorelines, representing a likely major source of juvenile mortality.
- **Anthropogenic/faunal barrier interference:** Resident beavers repeatedly damaged net barriers, requiring reinforcement with chicken wire; breaches likely permitted mass escapes of stocked fish.
- **River channelization context:** Habitat availability for backwater rearing sites was severely limited — 66% of the 127-mile study reach was excluded due to reservation/refuge land ownership, and the remaining reach was 93% channelized, underscoring the scarcity of suitable backwater habitat for [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery in the lower [[Colorado River]].

## Key Findings

1. **Site constraints:** Suitable rearing backwaters were extremely limited due to land ownership restrictions and river channelization; only three coves at Goose Flats (C-5) Backwater met all siting criteria (depth ≥2 m, vegetation cover, organic substrate, low human disturbance).
2. **Predator removal was incomplete:** Trammel and gill netting prior to stocking removed relatively few large predators, and residual predators (notably catfish in Cove 1) caused substantial post-stocking losses.
3. **Rotenone unavailable:** The report explicitly notes it was "extremely unfortunate" that rotenone treatment could not be used to fully sterilize coves of predators prior to stocking.
4. **Differential cove survival:** Cove 1, which retained large channel/flathead catfish and bass, yielded far fewer recovered razorbacks (N=132) at inventory compared to Cove 2 (N=394) and Cove 3 (N=290), directly implicating predation in reduced survival.
5. **Growth performance:** Surviving razorback suckers grew substantially over ~3 months in enclosures, from a stocking mean of 113 mm TL to 180–252 mm TL, suggesting enclosed backwater coves — when adequately predator-free — can support strong growth rates for headstarting juveniles.
6. **Population decline drivers (ranked hypotheses):** (i) beaver-caused net barrier breaches enabling escape, (ii) acute transport/stocking stress mortality, (iii) wading bird predation, (iv) residual fish predation.
7. **Marking protocol failure:** Intended tetracycline-based hatchery feed marking failed due to a communication breakdown between hatchery and fisheries staff, necessitating fallback fin-clip marking.
8. **Multiple stocking sources integrated:** The 1988 program combined newly-reared Niland juveniles (2-cove/pond origin) with older 1986–1987 cohort fish reared at [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]], indicating a multi-year, multi-site stocking strategy for [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery.
9. **Planned next steps:** Mass release of enclosure survivors, dismantling of barrier structures, and electro-fishing surveys of the lower 40 miles of the Colorado River to assess recovery/dispersal of stocked razorbacks.
