# Fish and Wildlife Management on Watershed Projects

## Executive Summary
This paper, presented at the 25th North American Wildlife Conference (1959), describes fish and wildlife management practices integrated into the U.S. Soil Conservation Service's (SCS) small watershed program, authorized under the Flood Control Act of 1944, the Pilot Watershed Program (1954), and the [[Watershed Protection And Flood Prevention Act]] (Public Law 566, 83rd Congress). The program's upstream approach emphasizes soil and water conservation via floodwater retarding structures, farm ponds, and vegetative treatments, contrasted with downstream flood control (dams, levees). The document details fish stocking and pool management strategies for [[Floodwater Retarding Reservoirs]], waterfowl habitat enhancement through drawdown techniques, and upland game habitat development, while noting that Public Law 85-865 later authorized federal cost-sharing (up to 50%) for fish and wildlife measures in these projects.

## Background & Description
While this is a management-focused paper rather than a morphological study, it references distinguishing ecological/functional traits relevant to species selection for stocking:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]**: Predatory sport fish; used as primary game species in warm water impoundments; spawning success used as an indicator before permitting fishing.
- **[[Bluegill]]** and **[[Redear Sunfish]]**: Panfish species stocked alongside bass to establish balanced warm-water sport fisheries.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Under field trial consideration in Georgia for supplemental stocking; noted for natural spawning behavior in/above reservoirs observed in Texas and Oklahoma; readily uses artificial spawning structures (kegs, barrels, drums, rock slab caves, boxes) placed in 2–6 ft of water.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** and **[[Brook Trout]]**: Cold water species stocked in Southeastern mountain impoundments at 200–300 fish/acre; dependent on bottom-draw water management to maintain thermal habitat.
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]**: Cold water species managed in conjunction with trout in mountain stream/reservoir systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper distinguishes two major reservoir management categories based on thermal regime:

### Warm Water Habitat
- Majority of floodwater retarding reservoir conservation pools (avg. ~10 acres, range 4–100+ acres) fall into this category.
- Prior to stocking, wild fish are typically removed via **rotenone** application, or pools are drained/dried through the spawning season before restocking.
- Stocked cooperatively with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and State Fisheries Commissions.
- Fertilization trials (W.W. Neely, SCS South Carolina) demonstrated that drawing overflow from the reservoir bottom—rather than the top—makes fertilization practical despite high water turnover.

### Cold Water Habitat
- Found predominantly in Southeastern mountain watersheds; smaller retarding pools than warm water systems.
- Bottom-draw overflow structures preserve downstream cold water temperature for [[Trout]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]], while simultaneously deepening the cold, oxygenated layer within the impoundment itself.
- Developed via cooperative engineering between SCS and USFWS (modified drawdown well).

### Multiple-Purpose Drawdown Structures
A key ecological engineering tool: floodwater retarding structures fitted with a 30–48 inch drawdown slot/gate, enabling:
1. Suppression of undesirable woody vegetation (alder, willow, buttonbush, briars) via water level manipulation.
2. Exposure of shallow mudflats for planting waterfowl food crops.
3. Periodic drawdown for fish population management.
4. Mosquito control (public health).
5. Downstream domestic/agricultural water supply.

### Waterfowl Habitat
- Reservoirs attract ducks, geese, marsh, and shorebirds (53 species documented at one North Georgia project), but sustained use requires managed food production.
- Natural submerged aquatics (naiad, [[Potamogeton]]) and edge plants (smartweed, barnyardgrass) are insufficient alone and vulnerable to siltation without watershed stabilization.
- Managed drawdown (summer lowering via slot, planting, winter reflooding) is the most effective food production method.
- Successful food plants trialed at Noonday and Settingdown Creek projects: browntop millet, Japanese millet, chicken corn, Texas millet, and various smartweeds.
- Biennial drawdowns preferred over annual where mechanical ground prep is unavailable, to limit weed competition.
- Woodland duck ponds managed via low-level dikes; mast-producing trees (oaks, beech) favored; corn avoided due to raccoon predation; ponds drained each spring to prevent tree mortality.
- Wood duck nest boxes deployed where natural cavity trees are scarce.

### Upland Game Habitat
- Critical, erosion-prone areas stabilized with vegetation doubling as food/cover: bicolor and sericea lespedeza (quail), perennial grasses and clover (deer, turkey).
- Channel excavation spoil banks planted with quail mixtures (browntop millet, Korean lespedeza, cowpeas, perennial tickclover).

## Key Findings
- **Scale of program (as of Dec 1, 1959)**: 1,114 floodwater retarding structures, 79,198 farm ponds, 287,773 acres of wildlife area improvement, and 334,114 acres of critical area vegetation completed nationwide.
- Not all watershed projects can support full multi-purpose fish and wildlife management, but maximum benefit requires deliberate multi-use planning.
- A 48-inch drawdown slot/gate (upgraded from 30 inches) is now recommended for greater management flexibility.
- Rotenone use is confirmed practical for removing wild fish populations in small Georgia streams prior to stocking.
- Landowners are advised to withhold fishing pressure for at least one year post-stocking, until bass demonstrate successful natural spawning.
- Fertilization of reservoirs, previously considered impractical due to water turnover, is now feasible when overflow is drawn from the reservoir bottom.
- Improved watershed treatment measurably restores stream fisheries: the [[Settingdown Creek]] project (NE Georgia) converted a silted, seasonally dry stream with poor/non-existent fishing into a clear, perennial stream with excellent fishing.
- Public Law 85-865 (amending Public Law 566) established federal cost-sharing (up to 50%) for fish and wildlife measures — including reservoir storage capacity, structure modification, stream channel improvement, and marsh/pit development — contingent on public access requirements.
- The paper anticipates expanded, geographically broader applications: goose range development, pheasant habitat, northern waterfowl nesting/marsh enhancement, and furbearer habitat establishment.
