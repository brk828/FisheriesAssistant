# Inventory, Distribution and Abundance of Selected Aquatic Species in the Yuma Division

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetology #mugilidae #elopidae #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) field operations staff, provides baseline ecological data for three aquatic and semi-aquatic species in the Yuma Division of the [[Colorado River]]. The goal was to predict biological effects of future changes in discharge and hydrology. The report has three parts: (1) the [[American Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), an introduced amphibian that was the main focus; (2) the [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*); and (3) the [[Pacific Ten-Pounder]] (*Elops affinis*). Bullfrog work comprised visual night counts on 32 transects, collection of 100 adults for diet analysis, bank-vegetation mapping, and life-history review. Mullet and ten-pounder sections rely mainly on state survey and literature records.

## Background & Description

**American Bullfrog (*Rana catesbeiana*)**
- A large, widespread North American frog, historically found in permanent ponds and slow streams east of the Rocky Mountains, from Canada to northeastern Mexico.
- Introduced to California and elsewhere in the early 1900s and rapidly spread across the West, where several native amphibians were displaced or extirpated.
- Breeds March to July. Males defend territories and call from choruses; the species uses [[Male-Defended Oviposition]] and [[Territorial Behavior]].
- Females may produce up to 48,000 eggs, but 10,000 to 20,000 is more typical. Two clutches per season are presumed.
- Eggs hatch in 2 to 4 days. Tadpoles transform in 3 to 4 months in southern localities and take more than two years in the north. Maximum adult snout-vent length is usually about 180 mm.

**Striped Mullet (*Mugil cephalus*)**
- A catadromous species with worldwide distribution in tropical and subtropical seas. Spawning occurs offshore in the Gulf of California in December and January.
- Young move into the Colorado River and often develop into yearlings there before returning to the Gulf.

**Pacific Ten-Pounder (*Elops affinis*)**
- A primitive marine species first recorded in the Colorado River at Laguna Dam by Glidden (1941). It is mainly piscivorous in the sea and can reach about 2 m at sea.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and habitat structure**
- Thirty-two transects of 0.42 km (0.25 mi) were set at regular intervals from 2 km below [[Laguna Dam]] to 1.3 km above [[Morelos Dam]].
- Bank cover was classified into five categories: [[Carrizo Cane]], emergents, [[Salt Cedar]], [[Riprap]], and open bank.
- Across the division (Sept. 22–26, 1981), [[Phragmites]] was the dominant bank cover on both banks (49%), slightly more so on the California side. Open bank made up 36%. Emergents, salt cedar, and riprap were locally abundant but minor overall.

**Bullfrog habitat use and environmental constraints**
- Adults were seen mostly at night, usually perched on mid-sized banks within 1 to 2 m of the shore. Larvae were not observed during the study despite extensive seine and dip-net searches.
- Frogs were rarely found in dense Phragmites away from edges. They were typically found in short open-bank reaches between dense vegetation patches, which provide escape routes to water and cover.
- Thermoregulation may hide some frogs. Lilleywhite (1969) reported body temperatures of 25–33 °C and a critical thermal maximum of 39.2 °C; shuttling between air and water could lower night counts.
- Hunting seasons opened July 1 in Arizona and California. Boat access sites were adjacent to areas with low bullfrog densities, so hunter removal may have influenced the density patterns.

**Striped Mullet and Pacific Ten-Pounder status**
- Mullet make up about 20% of the fauna by number in the 1980–81 AGFD and California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) surveys, but can be locally dominant. Abundance depends on flows reaching the Gulf in wet years.
- Mullet were considered sporadic near Yuma until canal ditches were built (Dill 1944). They are most abundant in backwaters just below Laguna Dam. Mullet are uncommon above the Pirot Knob Wasteway, and Imperial Dam is considered a barrier.
- Ten-pounders are sporadic and typically collected as single individuals. One collection at the Pirot Knob Wasteway in 1979 yielded 39 specimens (30% relative abundance) in 10 minutes of electrofishing. Overall, ten-pounders made up less than 2% of the total catch.
- Their occurrence coincides with high-flow periods, and numbers decline after flows recede.

## Key Findings

**Bullfrog distribution and abundance**
- Three visual counts (July 10–August 13, 1981) agreed on distribution and abundance; separate counts were not statistically different at the 0.05 level.
- Average density was about 9 per km (14.8 per mi) with both banks included, roughly 4.7/km on the Arizona side and 4.4/km on the California side.
- The estimated adult population in the Yuma Division ranged from 588 to 714 frogs. These estimates exclude backwater frogs beyond 5 m inland, so they are conservative.
- Densities were highest near the 4th Avenue and Interstate 8 bridges and adjacent to the Gila Rapids.

**Bullfrog habitat relationships**
- Densities were greatest where Phragmites covered more than 50% of the bank (5.9/km), significantly higher than in areas with less than 50% Phragmites cover (4.6/km; t = 4.549, df = 190).
- Densities were inversely related to open bank. Areas with 50% or more open bank held 2.3/km, significantly lower than 25–50% open bank (11.6/km; t = 3.178, df = 112) and <25% open bank (5.9/km; t = 4.250, df = 130).
- The authors concluded that a certain amount of open bank within dense cover is necessary for prime bullfrog habitat.

**Bullfrog population biology**
- Adult sex ratio was about 1:1 when based on internal examination (N = 61). External tympanum-based estimates were about 3:1 (N = 57), but the internal result was judged more reliable because some frogs had not reached sexual maturity.
- Snout-vent lengths overlapped broadly across age classes. Figure 2 likely represents five or six age classes, but class separation was not clearly defined.
- Two specimens (SVL = 54.47 mm) appeared to be recently metamorphosed adults.

**Bullfrog diet (100 adults, Aug. 15–Sept. 24, 1981)**
- Stomachs averaged 29% full. Seventeen percent contained 5% or less of their potential volume.
- Individual frogs contained an average of 1.9 identified taxa, with 32 taxa recorded across all specimens.
- The most important prey in relative volume was crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*), at about 19%, but it occurred in only a few stomachs.
- Wolf spiders (Lycosidae) occurred in 24% of stomachs. Other common prey included long-horned earwigs (Labiduridae), sowbugs (Isopoda), and crickets (Gryllidae). Fish, frogs, reptiles, and mammals were also eaten, including *Gambusia affinis*, a kingsnake, and a rattlesnake (*Crotalus atrox*).
- New dietary records were reported for rattlesnake and muskrat (*Ondatra zibethicus*).
- Frogs were seen to be opportunistic predators on a diverse array of prey.

**Bullfrog life history (Dowe 1979; this study)**
- Breeding began in early April, with peaks in late April and late May. Most larvae from the first clutch metamorphosed within 3 to 4 months (August–October). Some overwintering larvae probably transformed the following spring. Overwintering adults probably bred in their first year, and those from larvae overwintering twice in their second year.

**Striped Mullet**
- Post-larvae (20–40 mm) were collected below Morelos Dam in March (Johnson and McClendon 1970). The authors questioned the assumption that salinity is required for successful reproduction.
- The maximum recorded length of striped mullet from the Colorado River is under 56 cm.

**Pacific Ten-Pounder**
- Collections were infrequent (Table 4). Their occurrence is linked to periods of continued high flow in the lower river.
- The authors concluded that the species is not a perennial component of the Yuma Division fauna.

**Overall conclusion**
- Bullfrogs are now an established component of the lower Colorado River aquatic fauna, with densities comparable to those in California. They have adapted to extreme temperatures and turbid lotic waters that are essentially unknown in their native range.
- The bullfrog appears to favor dense cover (primarily Phragmites) adjacent to short patches of open bank.
- Frequent movements of several kilometers (reported in earlier studies) may explain their widespread distribution away from backwater breeding areas.
