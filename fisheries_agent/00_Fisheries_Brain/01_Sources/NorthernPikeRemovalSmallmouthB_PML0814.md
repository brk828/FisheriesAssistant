# Northern Pike Removal, Smallmouth Bass Monitoring, and Native Fish Monitoring in the Yampa River, Hayden to Craig Reach, 2004–2006

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This synthesis report by [[Finney and Haines]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado River Fish Project, March 2008) evaluates a three-year mechanical removal program for nonnative [[Northern Pike]] in a 37-mile reach of the [[Yampa River]] near Craig, Colorado. The work was funded by the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], which seeks to control nonnative fishes that prey on or compete with endangered species.

The study had six objectives: reduce adult pike numbers; estimate pike abundance and size structure before and after removal; test whether targeting [[Backwater Habitat]] concentration areas is effective; maintain public support by stocking removed pike into off-channel angling ponds; monitor the native fish community and [[Smallmouth Bass]]; and monitor fish movement.

A total of 2,267 northern pike were removed, and adult abundance estimates declined. Catch rates also fell across all years, although no statistically significant trend was detected. Pike movement was mostly downstream. Tag returns from anglers were 25% in 2004, 5% in 2005, and not estimable in 2006. Smallmouth bass recaptures were too few for reliable movement or abundance estimates. The native fish community was poorly represented. A failed fish screen at [[Elkhead Reservoir]] in 2005 likely added escaped fishes to the river and biased several estimates.

## Background & Description

**Northern pike (*Esox lucius*)** is a large, aggressive esocid native to many North American drainages. It has been widely stocked outside its native range for recreational angling. In the Yampa River, pike escaped from Elkhead Reservoir, where they were stocked in 1977, and established a reproducing population. From there they move into the lower Yampa and the [[Green River]], where they overlap with habitat of four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. Pike were identified as a significant threat to these species by a majority of upper basin resource managers in the late 1980s.

**Smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*)** is an exotic predator, abundant in lower-elevation Yampa reaches. It was introduced into Elkhead Reservoir in the late 1970s. It is a known predator of Colorado Pikeminnow in the middle Green River and competes with native fishes in key spawning and nursery habitats.

Native fishes still occur in the upper Yampa, including Colorado Pikeminnow, [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Mountain Whitefish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The Yampa River flows about 320 km from west of the Continental Divide to its confluence with the Green River. The 37-mile study reach runs through low-gradient agricultural land. Flows at the Craig gauge ranged from 100 to 13,000 ft³/s and averaged 1,232 ft³/s. Recent drought lowered flows. [[Elkhead Creek]] joins the Yampa at river mile 148.1. Elkhead Reservoir, 7.25 miles upstream on the creek, was enlarged to supply municipal water to Craig and to boost Yampa base flows.

**Screen failure.** A temporary fish screen on the reservoir outlet, installed during the 2004–2005 enlargement, failed on April 16, 2005, at about 500 ft³/s during the rising limb of the hydrograph. Elkhead Creek peaked at 1,250 ft³/s in 2005 and 1,100 ft³/s in 2006. Flows up to 480 ft³/s were screened in 2006.

**Sampling conditions.** Sampling occurred each spring (2004–2006) during higher flows (2,500–6,500 cfs below the Craig gauge), when boats could access the river and backwaters were flooded. The reach was divided into 2-mile subreaches and sampled six times in 2004 and seven times in each of 2005 and 2006.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Pike were collected with two pulsed-DC [[Electrofishing]] boats (22 mm dip-net mesh) and, in 2004, five [[Fyke Netting]] sets on public land.
- Pike were marked with T-bar tags and pelvic fin-clipped for double tagging. All were scanned for PIT tags. Length, discharge, effort, and capture reach were recorded.
- Abundance used [[Petersen Mark-Recapture]] estimates with 95% confidence intervals, on consecutive passes to meet closure assumptions. Fyke-net removals were added to the final estimates.
- Movement was assessed using the 2-mile reach system. Between-year movements were judged less biased than within-year movements, because released fish may show a [[Fallback Response]] and drift downstream.
- Smallmouth bass abundance was estimated only for 2004, using CAPTURE closed-population models on passes 4–6 downstream of Bluegill Creek. Recapture rates in other years were too low.

**Northern pike**
- 2,267 adult pike were removed. Each year removal reduced the adult population by 47–63%.
- Abundance estimates declined after removal. Estimates were precise in 2004 and 2006, but immigration biased the 2005 estimate.
- Catch rates declined across the study period and tracked population size, but no significant trend was detected (2004: p = 0.06; 2005: p = 0.26; 2006: p = 0.14).
- Targeting concentration areas (low-velocity, vegetated backwaters) was judged effective in 2005 and 2006. Backwater reaches had higher catch rates.
- Mean length varied by year and pass. It differed among passes in 2004 and 2005 but not in 2006. Size structure shifted toward smaller fish in 2005 and recruited into larger classes in 2006.
- Juvenile pike (<300 mm TL) were captured and removed at 55 (2004), 354 (2005), and 159 (2006) individuals, with 52, 284, and 135 removed.
- Movement was mainly downstream. Between-year movement ranged up to 86.4 miles downstream and 32.4 miles upstream. Two pike released in the study reach were recaptured in the Green River more than 125 miles downstream in under four months.
- Of pike captured in the study reach, 81 originated upstream and 22 downstream. Seven of 15 upstream-origin pike captured in 2006 came from [[Catamount Reservoir]], the first year tagged fish from there appeared in the study area.
- Angler tag returns were 25% (2004) and 5% (2005), and not estimable in 2006. Pilot-study returns were 56% (2002) and 41% (2003).
- Escapement signs in 2005 included more pike over 1,000 mm (five captured, versus two in 2004), lacerations possibly from passing a dam and broken screen, and a catch-rate shift above versus below the Elkhead confluence.

**Smallmouth bass**
- Captures were 324 (2004), 34 (2005), and 68 (2006).
- The 2004 population estimate was 1,469 (95% CI 872–2,621). OCR-garbled source values were reconstructed here; no estimates exist for 2005 or 2006.
- Bass were mostly in downstream reaches, and encounters rose later in each sampling season, consistent with an annual upstream migration.
- Of 40 bass previously tagged by other investigators, 11 had been caught previously in the river and rereleased in Elkhead Reservoir.

**Native fish**
- 205 native fish were encountered in 2004–2006, mostly mountain whitefish (202 total across years, with Colorado Pikeminnow (2) and Flannelmouth Sucker (1) making up the rest).
- No Roundtail Chub were captured. Pure-strain native suckers were rare, and hybrid suckers were common.
- Two adult Colorado Pikeminnow were captured, a notable find.
- No tagged native fish were recaptured.

**Ancillary fishes**
- Ancillary captures were low in 2004, peaked in 2005, and declined in 2006. The 2005 peak is consistent with Elkhead escapement.
- Bluegill, black crappie, largemouth bass, black bullhead, channel catfish, and green sunfish were recorded. Nearly all ancillary fish were downstream of the Elkhead confluence.

**Conclusions**
- Northern pike abundance, size, and catch rates were reduced within and among years.
- Pike moved predominantly upstream-to-downstream.
- Smallmouth bass presence was highly variable between years and mainly downstream.
- The native fish fauna is depauperate in the study reach.

**Management recommendations**
1. Continue three-pass removal until a more effective strategy can be defined. Model exploitation to identify a population threshold, and consider targeting large backwaters exclusively.
2. Identify sites for fyke nets and secure landowner permission to set and monitor them.
3. Continue monitoring native fishes at minimal effort, given their rarity in the reach.
4. Expand removal efforts upstream and explore escapement prevention on reservoir outlet structures.

**Caveats noted by the authors**
- Removal success depends on whether invasion is driven by habitat degradation or simply by the presence of the fish, so success must be carefully defined. Success would mean increased native and listed species abundance in the study or downstream areas.
- Escapement from Elkhead Reservoir violated closure assumptions, complicated detection of catch-rate changes, and limited the ability to reduce mean length or target concentration areas.
- Tag-return estimates are likely biased low by angler resentment, pike mortality during stocking, and cannibalism in holding ponds.
