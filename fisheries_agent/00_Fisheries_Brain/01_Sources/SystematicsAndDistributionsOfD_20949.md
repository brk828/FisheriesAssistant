# Systematics and Distributions of Dolly Varden and Bull Trout in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Haas and McPhail (1991) test whether North American [[Dolly Varden]] (*Salvelinus malma*) and [[Bull Trout]] (*Salvelinus confluentus*), long grouped within the Arctic char complex, are two distinct species. Using multivariate morphometric and meristic data from 1,580 museum and field specimens collected at 310 sites, the authors find two morphotypes separable by a linear discriminant function (LDF) built from three field-measurable characters. They interpret the taxa under the [[Biological Species Concept]] and conclude that the two forms are good species, not ecophenotypes, with strict sympatry but no evidence of interbreeding or introgression in the wild.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic context:** The genus *Salvelinus* is notoriously difficult. Dolly Varden were previously part of the Arctic char (*S. alpinus*) complex and had been recognized as a distinct species in North America by several workers (e.g., Morton 1970; Behnke 1980; Cavender 1980).
- **Prior work:** Cavender (1978) proposed the two-species split but relied on museum specimens, a typological concept, and qualitative comparisons of few characters, and gave no field diagnosis for bull trout.
- **Morphological groups:** The study used 30 allopatric specimens per species, sampled from the [[Queen Charlotte Islands]] (Dolly Varden) and the [[Kootenay Region]] (bull trout).
- **Measurements:** 93 measurements and 20 counts were initially taken per fish, with 23 truss measurements from 11 landmark points. The reduced data set contained 51 morphometric and 10 meristic characters, analysed with [[Principal Component Analysis]] (PCA) and [[Linear Discriminant Function Analysis]] (LDFA).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geographic distribution**

- **Dolly Varden:** Range from just south of the Canada–US border (~47°N) to the Seward Peninsula (~60–62°N), largely coastal and often anadromous. They occur in no east-flowing drainages except sites 73 and 74 in the Liard River headwaters, which the authors suspect are a recent crossover. Islands off British Columbia and Alaska are occupied exclusively by Dolly Varden.
- **Bull trout:** Originally ranged to ~41°N, but southern populations are largely extinct; the present southern limit is ~42°N. They are mostly interior, reaching the Pacific coast only in the Puget Sound area and the Fraser River drainage. The northern limit is ~60°N, with a sample from ~61°N in the Yukon River drainage. Bull trout are listed as a US Category 2 species (Williams et al. 1989).
- **Glacial history:** The southern limit of Dolly Varden and the northern/southern bull trout split correspond to the southern extent of the Wisconsin (last Pleistocene) glaciation, consistent with western refugia.

**Habitat and biology**

- Specimens came from lakes, rivers and streams. Dolly Varden are the more anadromous species.
- Laboratory crosses were made with fish collected as migrating spawners in November from the [[Lower Fraser River]] system.
- Under sympatry, Dolly Varden were more numerous, and the two species appear to exclude each other broadly rather than partition resources finely.

**Interactions**

- Bull trout coexist and hybridize with introduced [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*S. fontinalis*) in several areas, and bull trout appear to decline there.

## Key Findings

1. **Two species confirmed.** PCA of both the 51-character morphometric set and the 23 truss measurements separates the two groups clearly. Group separation lies on the second principal component (shape), while the first (size) is not discriminatory. Shape is orthogonal to size.
2. **Field diagnostic (LDF).** The discriminant function uses branchiostegal ray number, anal fin ray number and the ratio of total upper jaw length to standard length:
   - LDF = 0.629 × branchiostegal rays + 0.178 × anal fin rays + 37.310 × (upper jaw length / standard length) − 21.8
   - Values > 0 indicate bull trout; values < 0 indicate Dolly Varden. The function separates all 60 original specimens and the full 1,580-fish data set.
3. **Single characters.** Branchiostegal ray number is the best single character: Dolly Varden median 22 (range mostly 17–23), bull trout median 27 (mostly 26–31). No single character works everywhere.
4. **Sympatry.** Strict sympatry with no evidence of interbreeding was found at 10 sites in four drainages: lower Fraser, Skeena, Nass and Stikine. Dolly Varden predominate in these samples.
5. **Parapatry and syntopy.** Many populations occur parapatrically or syntopically; neither species dominates, suggesting broad-scale competitive exclusion with little character displacement.
6. **Laboratory crosses.** Of 90 reared fish (30 Dolly Varden, 30 bull trout, 30 hybrids), hybrids were morphologically intermediate on the PCA and LDF. Bull trout appeared to grow slightly faster at this small size, though a size difference between parental stocks may confound this.
7. **Natural hybrids.** None were conclusively found, and there is no evidence of introgression, although possible introgressed populations were reported in the Skagit River region of Washington after the paper went to press.
8. **Supporting evidence.** Cytological, mitochondrial DNA and developmental studies cited by the authors indicate the two species are not sister taxa. Dolly Varden are more closely related to Arctic char, and bull trout to white-spotted char (*S. leucomaenis*) of Asia.
9. **Morphological summary.** Bull trout have larger, broader, flatter heads and more slender, ventrally flattened bodies. Dolly Varden have oval, "snake-like" bodies with heads that do not dominate the profile.
10. **Conservation note.** Bull trout are declining in the south, and the authors advise careful management because the species is environmentally sensitive.
11. **Methodological note.** Measurement repeatability was high (ANOVA ratio close to one). Sample size of 30 per group exceeds the common minimum of 25. Log transformation was applied, and data were normally distributed and homoscedastic after transformation.
