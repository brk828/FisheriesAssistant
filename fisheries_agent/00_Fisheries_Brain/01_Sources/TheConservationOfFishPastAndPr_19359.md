# The Conservation of Fish: Past and Present Experience

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #freshwater-ecology #threatened-species #global-fish-fauna

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review by [[Peter S. Maitland]] examines the global status of fish conservation, covering approximately 30,000 described fish species worldwide. Despite fish representing three in every five vertebrate species, they have historically received minimal conservation attention compared to other taxa. The document reviews major threats to fish populations including pollution, habitat loss, river engineering, overfishing, and species introductions. It presents case studies from Africa, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, and South America, demonstrating that progress in fish conservation bears little relation to the importance of indigenous fish communities. The paper proposes comprehensive conservation strategies including legislation, habitat restoration, translocation, captive breeding, and monitoring programs. Key findings indicate that 292 North American species (28%) are threatened, while in Europe 144 of 191 indigenous species require some form of protection.

## Background & Description

Fish represent the most abundant yet least known vertebrate class globally. About 100 new species are described annually, suggesting the final total will exceed 30,000 species. They inhabit virtually all aquatic habitats and display remarkable morphological and functional diversity. However, public support for fish conservation has lagged behind other wildlife groups, primarily because fish are difficult to observe in the wild and lack the charismatic appeal of warm-blooded vertebrates.

Freshwater fish possess several characteristics particularly relevant to conservation. Their habitats are discrete, confining populations within particular bounds and leading to differentiation of independent populations with individual stock characteristics. Because many populations are confined to single (often small) aquatic systems with significant water movement, entire populations are vulnerable to pollution, disease, and other threats. For any species, the number of separate populations is usually far more important than the number of individuals.

Migrations characterize many fish life cycles, creating periods of particular vulnerability. In diadromous riverine species, entire populations must pass through lower river sections to and from the sea. If these sections are polluted, obstructed, or subject to heavy predation, entire populations of several species may disappear, leaving upstream communities permanently impoverished with approximately 50% of their previous species complement.

The [[IUCN Red Data Book]] classification system provides the major international framework for assessing conservation status. Categories include: **Extinct** (no longer existing in wild after repeated searches), **Endangered** (in danger of extinction if causal factors continue), **Vulnerable** (likely to become endangered), **Rare** (small world populations at risk), and **Indeterminate** (insufficient information to classify). For fish, the number of sites occupied is more critical than total individual numbers, since one catastrophic event could eliminate an entire population confined to a single water body.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Major Threats

**Pollution and Land Use**: Pollution represents the single most significant factor causing major declines in fish populations. Sources include domestic, agricultural, and industrial wastes, which can be totally toxic or selective. Eutrophication and acidification from atmospheric pollution affect even remote waters. The [[River Thames]] and [[River Clyde]] became completely fishless in lower reaches during peak industrial periods but are now recovering following pollution control measures.

**Habitat Loss**: Fish face enormous habitat loss across all aquatic habitat types. Many smaller lakes have been drained or filled, streams piped, and rivers converted to repositories of human waste. However, some new habitats have been created through reservoir construction and canal development, though these are rarely exploited for conservation purposes.

**River Engineering**: Dams and obstructions particularly threaten migratory species. If unable to reach spawning grounds, populations may become extinct within a few years. Engineering works can destroy habitat through dredging, siltation, or exposure to intolerable water level fluctuations. Fish-pass design technology has improved, allowing most problems to be solved at project-planning stages if appropriate legislation exists.

**Species Introductions**: Introduction of non-native species can radically alter community structure and lead to extinction of sensitive native species. The [[Ruffe]] (*Gymnocephalus cernua*) appeared in [[Loch Lomond]] in 1982, 100 km north of its former range, and is now one of the loch's commonest fish, potentially threatening the vulnerable [[Powan]] (*Coregonus lavaretus*) whose eggs it consumes. The Ruffe has also established in [[Lake Superior]] after accidental introduction via ballast water from Europe.

[[Magnuson]] (1976) noted that lake faunas resemble island populations—isolated, small, and relatively young. Species number may result from equilibrium between immigration rates of new species and extinction of old ones. Human activities moving fish increase instability and uncertainty in fishery management outcomes.

### Geographic Variation

Enormous differences exist between fish faunas of different world regions due to geological, climatic, and historical factors. Diversity increases from poles to tropics, with most striking differences among major continents and even between major river basins. Unfortunately, conservation progress bears little relation to the relative importance of indigenous fish communities.

**Africa** has over 2,700 freshwater species. In southern Africa (270 species, 20 alien), at least 24 freshwater and 7 estuarine species are threatened. Major threats include introduced alien fishes and habitat destruction. [[Madagascar]]'s endemic freshwater fauna has undergone severe attrition from exotic species and forest clearance.

**Asia** possesses very rich tropical fish fauna, but continent-wide conservation reviews are lacking. [[India]] faces numerous pressures including pollution, physical obstructions, habitat loss, overfishing, and species introductions.

**Australia** has a depauperate freshwater fauna with only 192 species. Of these, 6 are endangered, 5 vulnerable, 4 potentially threatened, 2 indeterminate, 32 restricted, and 16 of unknown status—totaling 65 species potentially needing conservation action. Major threats include water conservation schemes, hydro-electric development, and irrigation projects.

**New Zealand** has just 27 indigenous freshwater species. One species is extinct and several others are conservation concerns, mainly from habitat degradation. Introduced salmonids have had harmful impacts through predation and competitive exclusion.

**Europe** has 191 indigenous species. Of these, 22 require urgent conservation measures, 144 require some protection, and 25 appear in no immediate danger. Many species have declined in range and numbers over the last century. In the [[British Isles]], 42 native species include 10 under threat (2 now extinct). Of 235 National Nature Reserves in Great Britain, 75% contain fresh waters but only 20% of these have fish. Only 3 of the 10 most threatened species occur in NNRs.

**North America** has 1,033 native freshwater species. Of these, 292 (28%) are threatened: 74 endangered, 85 vulnerable, 101 rare, 5 indeterminate, and 27 extinct. Human-caused extinction rates have increased dramatically since 1950, with status continuing to decline especially in arid western USA and northern Mexico. The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, established 1974, maintains protected gene pools and has successfully handled over 20 species.

**South America** has over 2,000 species, centered on the [[Amazon Basin]]—the world's richest river system for fish diversity. Despite enormous pressure from land use changes and forest clearance, and the economic importance of fish to local populations, little attention has been paid to fish conservation or habitat protection.

## Key Findings

### Conservation Assessment and Status

1. **Global Diversity**: Approximately 30,000 fish species described, with 100 new species described annually. Three in every five vertebrate species are fish, yet they receive minimal conservation attention compared to birds, mammals, and plants.

2. **Population Characteristics**: For fish conservation, the number of separate populations is far more important than total individual numbers. Entire populations confined to single water bodies are vulnerable to single catastrophic events (pollution, disease, landslides).

3. **Threat Categories**: Distinctly threatened species are characteristically those belonging to very defined taxonomic units of restricted geographic range, particularly sensitive to human threats, whose populations or ranges have undergone significant decline likely to continue.

4. **Regional Status**: North America: 28% of 1,033 species threatened; Europe: 144 of 191 species require protection; Australia: 65 of 192 species potentially need conservation action; British Isles: 10 of 42 native species under threat.

### Conservation Options and Management

**Legislation**: Legal protection varies widely across countries. Most freshwater fish legislation targets exploitable resources rather than native fauna elements. Important international frameworks include the [[Bern Convention]] (Appendix II: 4 fish species fully protected; Appendix III: 118 species partially protected) and the [[EC Directive on Conservation of Natural and Semi-Natural Habitats]].

**Habitat Restoration**: Represents the principal long-term means for successful fish conservation. Examples include:
- [[River Thames]] and [[River Clyde]]: formerly fishless, now recovering with diverse communities following pollution control
- [[Lake Trummen]] (Sweden): 300,000 m³ of nutrient-rich sediment removed 1970-1971, leading to improved water quality and fish community recovery
- Acidification amelioration: calcium addition to water bodies or catchments, pioneered in Scandinavia

**Translocation**: Can create new populations without threatening existing stocks if specific criteria are followed (Table 3). Stock may be transferred as eggs, fry, juveniles, or adults. At least 30 adults of each sex should be used to maximize genetic diversity. Current projects include:
- [[Vendace]] (*Coregonus albula*): formerly in 2 Scottish and 2 English lochs; extinct in one Scottish loch from sewage, apparently extinct in the other
- [[Powan]] (*Coregonus lavaretus*): 6 populations in Great Britain; adults netted from [[Loch Lomond]] spawning grounds, stripped for eggs, hatched indoors, and released into two selected lochs. 1991 sampling showed excellent growth.

**Captive Breeding**: Widely used for endangered animals but should generally be regarded as short-term emergency measure due to genetic difficulties over multiple generations. Short-term captive breeding (one generation) has advantages where obtaining eggs or young is difficult. Modern cryopreservation techniques successful for sperm but not yet for eggs, limiting current conservation value.

**Monitoring**: All populations worthy of conservation must be monitored adequately. Systems should be standardized for temporal and spatial comparisons. Methods should be non-damaging, cheap, routine, and potentially carried out by local wardening or volunteer staff. Regular reviews of distribution and status for all fish species are essential, with maintenance of registers of valuable stocks.

### Management Recommendations

The document proposes comprehensive management strategies (Table 5):

1. Strong legislation controlling importation of potentially disruptive fish
2. Controls on movement and release of fish within countries on catchment basis
3. Improved fishery management with complete ban on live baiting
4. Active conservation management for threatened species
5. International support for transnational species
6. Management plans for major sites of threatened species
7. Habitat restoration as major long-term objective
8. Translocation to create safeguard populations
9. Regular monitoring of existing and new sites
10. Research on poorly known species
11. Substantial resources for implementation
12. Wise renewable use of safeguarded stocks (angling, commercial exploitation)
13. Public education programs, especially for anglers
14. Regular reviews of distribution and status with maintained registers

### Future Directions

The optimum solution requires a series of small international centres for promoting freshwater fish conservation research—one per continent. These units would:
- Initiate detailed mapping schemes
- Collate exploitation statistics
- Coordinate and direct necessary research
- Propose international management programmes for threatened species

The general picture shows fish conservation has been sadly neglected globally compared to other taxa. However, recent years have seen increasing international attention with several symposia devoted to this topic and reviews of conservation status in large geographic areas. These initiatives should lead to practical conservation management schemes to save threatened species and restore important habitats worldwide.

Human activities have interacted with fish populations for thousands of years, but the last 200 years—particularly recent decades—have seen new and intense pressures applied to fresh waters. Many species have declined in range and numbers. The way forward requires each country to develop rational conservation-oriented policies for managing fish stocks, though basic information on distribution and status is often lacking. Where available, realistic management plans can allow conservation and exploitation to coexist for commoner species while providing full protection for threatened species through ecosystem conservation, supplemented by translocation and other active management measures where necessary.
