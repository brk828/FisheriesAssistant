# Willow Beach - Razorback Suckers 1994

## Executive Summary
This 1994 internal memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Parker Fisheries Resource Office) addresses operational concerns regarding the retrofitting of the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] for propagation of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The document is not a primary morphological or ecological research paper, but rather a management protocol memo advocating for expert oversight (from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]) during early culturing stages, and outlining a proposed rearing protocol. It also references comparative data from a concurrent, successful wild-larvae rearing program on [[Lake Mohave]].

## Background & Description
The source text contains no direct morphological description (e.g., meristics, fin ray counts, body proportions) of the [[Razorback Sucker]]. Only indirect size/growth data is provided:
- **Stocking size (Lake Mohave wild larvae):** ~0.5–1.0 inches (total length) at time of release into isolated habitats.
- **Projected growth (Lake Mohave cohort):** Anticipated to reach **10–12 inches** total length by late summer, following early release into natural habitat.
- **Willow Beach protocol (proposed):** Fry to be held in captivity only until they are "on feed," rather than being retained indoors until reaching ~2 inches TL, as originally planned — a practice the author argues will stunt growth potential compared to the Lake Mohave cohort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Larval/Fry Habitat:** Newly hatched razorback sucker fry are described as vulnerable to fungal infection and require careful water quality management (via zooplankton-rich raceways) during early rearing.
- **Trophic Ecology (juvenile):** Early life stages depend heavily on **zooplankton** (natural, via fertilized/plankton-bloomed raceways) and **brine shrimp** supplementation alongside appropriately-sized dry feed — indicating a zooplanktivorous feeding strategy in early ontogeny.
- **Habitat Use Strategy:** The [[Lake Mohave]] native fish program utilizes an approach of collecting wild larvae, briefly holding them until feeding independently, then releasing them into **unoccupied isolated backwater habitats** — presumably refugia protecting juveniles from predation by non-native fishes, a known major threat to [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment in the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Disease Pressure:** Fungal infection of eggs is identified as a key early-life mortality risk, requiring monitoring every 6 hours through swim-up and potential prophylactic treatment.

## Key Findings
- **Expertise Gap:** No personnel experienced in razorback sucker culture were slated to be on-site at Willow Beach during the critical early hatchery phase, a deficiency the author strongly argues should be remedied by involving [[Roger Hamman]] (species expert, Dexter National Fish Hatchery) and dedicated fish health staff.
- **Growth-Rate Risk:** Prolonged indoor holding (to ~2 inches TL) is hypothesized to produce smaller fish by the end of the growing season compared to the Lake Mohave program's early-release strategy, which achieves 10–12 inch fish by late summer from much smaller (0.5–1 inch) release sizes.
- **Programmatic Stakes:** The author states that failure of the 1994 Willow Beach razorback sucker propagation effort could jeopardize continued institutional investment in and development of the hatchery for this purpose.
- **Proposed Culture Protocol** (9-step outline):
  1. Fish health specialist present through hatching.
  2. Pre-fertilize/fill outdoor raceways to establish zooplankton blooms in advance.
  3. Use heath trays for egg incubation; cull non-viable eggs.
  4. Monitor eggs at ≥6-hour intervals through swim-up.
  5. Treat/prevent fungal infection proactively.
  6. Rear fry in small, uncrowded containers (not large raceways) until independent feeding.
  7. Use appropriately-sized dry feed, heavily supplemented with brine shrimp.
  8. Transfer to outdoor raceways once on feed (est. 7–20 days at warmer temperatures).
  9. Daily disease monitoring and treatment post-transfer.
- **Comparative Model:** The [[Lake Mohave]] wild-larvae program is cited as a successful, lower-intervention model for boosting recruitment of this endangered catostomid, contrasting with the more intensive, indoor-based approach initially proposed for Willow Beach.
