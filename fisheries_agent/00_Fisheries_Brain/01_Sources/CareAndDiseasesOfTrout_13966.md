# Care and Diseases of Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service Research Report (revised edition, 1946, by H. S. Davis) is a practical manual for [[Trout Hatchery Management]]. It covers the rearing of fingerling trout in troughs, [[Raceway]]s and [[Circular Pool]]s, the nutrition and feeding of hatchery trout, brood stock improvement, and a systematic account of the parasites and diseases that cause losses. Its central message is that heavy hatchery mortality is largely preventable through crowding control, adequate oxygen and temperature, sound diet, sanitation, and early diagnosis.

## Background & Description

The report is the third and most extensive revision of an earlier work. Demand had shifted toward larger trout for stocking, with fish of 6 to 12 inches or more planted annually, because heavily fished streams cannot produce enough food to sustain required trout populations. Keeping trout alive through summer is the main difficulty. Losses are typically heaviest between yolk-sac absorption and late summer or fall, and eggs before eyeing may lose 20 to 30 percent or more. The author cites an exceptional lot carried through its first year with under 10 percent loss from the egg stage.

The report is organized into: care of fingerlings and ponds; trout foods and feeding methods; improvement of stock; and parasites and diseases, grouped as external animal parasites (trematodes, protozoa, copepods, mussel glochidia), internal animal parasites (worms, protozoa, Myxosporidia, coccidia), bacterial diseases, and miscellaneous diseases (fungi, popeye, goiter, nutritional disorders).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Trout naturally spawn in riffles of small, swiftly flowing streams, and young fish remain in shallow riffles or side channels with well-aerated water and a perceptible current. The author argues that hatchery rearing should approximate these conditions. Wild trout are rarely seriously harmed by parasites because swift, cold streams are poorly suited to parasite spread; the problem arises in crowded hatchery conditions.

Key environmental variables discussed:

- **Temperature:** Trout grow best at 55 to 60°F; rearing pools should not exceed 65°F. Brook trout tolerated up to 80°F for short periods in rapid streams, but hatchery warming reduces dissolved oxygen and increases harm.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Crowded fish consume substantial oxygen, and feeding sharply increases demand. In one trough with 1,500 brook trout, feeding 300 g of food raised oxygen consumption to 70.08 percent, then 81.04 percent, of the available supply after a second feeding.
- **Water source:** Springs are preferred because they carry no fish, which reduces infection risk. Stream water may carry [[Mussel Glochidia]] and parasitic copepod larvae. The author also notes that stream and lake water may contain a growth-promoting "conditioned water" factor.

The report also describes self-purification in raceways: bacteria, algae, protozoa and insect larvae break down excrement, and chironomid larvae form part of the young trout's food.

## Key Findings

**Rearing systems and crowding**

- In a controlled trough experiment (March 1, 1933), 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 3,000 rainbow fingerlings were held in 48.27-gallon troughs. Growth slowed in the larger lots at water temperatures of 54 to 56°F. The 3,000-fish lot was ended by a [[Gill Disease]] outbreak, and the 2,000-fish lot was discontinued on August 2. The author concludes that 1,000 is the maximum for a standard trough through summer under favorable growth conditions, and that two or three times that number can be held where growth is slower.
- Oxygen depletion by fish was measured in several trials. Examples: 1,500 brook trout (about 14 g) removed about 45 percent of available oxygen; 17,680 rainbow fingerlings (0.26 g) removed 24 percent; 9,975 rainbow fingerlings (0.59 g) removed 34.89 percent; 3,900 black-spotted trout (2.30 g) removed 18.02 percent; 41,600 one-day-old [[Loch Leven Trout]] fry consumed only 3 percent.
- Raceways are recommended over troughs: they are cheaper per fish held and provide more natural conditions. Recommended dimensions for fingerlings are 3 to 5 feet wide, up to 50 to 75 feet long, with water 20 to 25 inches deep at most, and a sand or gravel bottom where possible. At the Pittsford station, about 6,000 brook fingerlings in a 4 × 37 ft raceway had virtually no mortality from April to October without cleaning.
- Circular pools, 15 to 25 feet in diameter and 18 to 20 inches deep at the center, were judged superior for fingerlings. At Leetown, 20,000 rainbow fingerlings (2.3 g) stocked June 11 in a 15-foot pool had practically no mortality until September 6, when average weight was 8.8 g and total weight about 387 pounds. A second pool with 15,000 fish (1.9 g) reached 11.5 g average by September 2, with similar total weight. A self-cleaning outlet device was developed at Leetown.

**Sanitation and chemical treatment**

- Lime (milk of lime, 1 part slaked lime to 4 parts water) is an effective pond disinfectant when drained. Chlorine is preferred for sterilizing filled ponds, with about 5 parts per million held for several hours. Liquid chlorine is more economical for large volumes, and a sodium thiosulphate (“hypo”) solution neutralizes residual chlorine.
- The [[Formalin]] treatment (1 part to 4,000 for 1 hour, stock flow stopped) was judged the most effective method for external protozoan parasites and gyrodactylid worms, and much cheaper than salt.
- [[Copper Sulphate]] (1 part to 2,000, dipped 1 to 2 minutes) is the main bacterial treatment, but it is toxic to weakened fish and less effective in hard water.
- [[Calomel]] at 0.2 percent in food was the standard treatment for [[Octomitus Salmonis]]. [[Carbarsone]] at the same concentration cleared flagellated forms within 4 days, with no toxicity observed over 7 days, at a cost of about $3.00 per ounce.

**Nutrition**

- Trout diets fall into three groups: fresh meats (horse meat, cattle, sheep and hog liver, lungs, spleen), dried animal meals, and plant products (wheat middlings, cottonseed, soybean and peanut meal).
- Beef liver was judged the best single meat for young fingerlings. Beef heart was superior to liver for advanced fry because it grinds into fine particles, while a mixture of heart and liver gave growth close to liver alone with mortality comparable to heart.
- Beef and pig melts are cheap but produce slow growth. [[Cataract]] developed in fish fed melts alone, attributed to a probable vitamin A deficiency.
- Frozen or decayed fish can cause serious losses, especially when fed above 20 percent of the diet. Certain fish species contain an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1 (thiaminase), while others (including rainbow, brown and lake trout) do not.
- Dry animal meals (salmon, white fish, sardine, meat meal, dried skim milk and buttermilk) can make up to about 70 percent of the ration for fish 4 to 5 inches long, but use above 50 percent is less efficient. Fresh meat is about 70 percent water and dry meals about 10 percent, so the fresh-meat price should be multiplied by three for comparison.
- Plant meals produced slower growth than animal meals; soybean and peanut meals gave better growth than cottonseed meal but poorer fingerling survival. The author advises against plant meals for small fingerlings.
- Rainbow trout appear more susceptible to dietary deficiencies than brook trout.

**Brood stock**

- Selective breeding at the Hackettstown (NJ) hatchery from 1919 raised maximum length at the end of the second year from 10 to 13.75 inches over four generations, and minimum length from 7 to 10.5 inches.
- At Pittsford (VT), the third selected generation grew markedly faster; at 29.5 weeks, Pittsford fish averaged 14.7 g versus 4.9 g and 4.6 g in two York Pond lots.
- Egg production in 2-year-old fish rose from 958 (1928) to 1,779 (1932) after selection.
- Spawning date was found to be largely heritable.
- Hatchery-produced eggs generally performed better than imported eggs, probably because of environmental acclimatization.
- Breeding for [[Furunculosis]] resistance showed promise.

**Parasites and diseases**

- **[[Gyrodactylus]] (fluke):** Found at virtually all hatcheries; controlled by a 1-minute dip in 1 to 500 acetic acid or a 1-hour formalin bath (1 to 4,000).
- **[[Discocotyle Salmonis]]:** A gill trematode 3 to 5 mm long, known from Long Island. Acetic acid is ineffective. Zonite spray (1 part to 5 parts water) was recommended.
- **[[Salmincola Edwardsii]]:** A copepod found only on brook trout. Free larvae survive about 2 days, and the full life cycle takes about 2.5 months. Sand filters and removal of 2-year-old brood fish were suggested.
- **[[Mussel Glochidia]]:** Infestation of rainbow fingerlings at the Truckee River (California) pools was linked to heavy annual losses. Glochidia remained viable for 11 days, and infections of 600 to 1,200 per fish killed fingerlings within a day or two.
- **[[Costia Necatrix]] and [[Costia Pyriformis]]:** Flagellate protozoans causing costiasis, most common when fingerlings are overcrowded or poorly fed. Controlled with formalin (1 to 4,000 for 1 hour).
- **[[Chilodon]] and [[Trichodina]]:** Ciliate protozoans. Trichodina causes white blotches and epidermal thickening; controlled by a 3 percent salt bath or 1 to 500 acetic acid.
- **[[Ichthyophthirius Multifilis]] ("ich"):** Rarely injurious to trout because it develops slowly at low temperatures and cannot complete its life cycle in rapid flow. Removing infected fish to fast-flowing water helps.
- **[[Octomitus Salmonis]]:** An intestinal flagellate responsible for chronic wasting ("pinheads") and sometimes acute epidemics among fingerlings, with losses reaching 50 to 75 percent. Overcrowding, poor oxygen and unsuitable food are key triggers.
- **[[Schizamoeba Salmonis]]:** Common and generally not harmful in hatchery trout.
- **Helminths:** Eye flukes (Strigeidae, including Diplostomum flexicaudum) caused blindness and losses at Hackettstown; control is by destroying snail hosts. Tapeworms were removed with kamala (1½ to 2 percent in food). Myxosporidia and a coccidian (probably Eimeria) were noted without major injury.
- **[[Furunculosis]]:** A septicemia caused by *Bacterium salmonicida*, most severe in brown and brook trout. Rainbow trout are rarely affected. A vaccine trial by Duff gave about 25 percent mortality in vaccinated fish versus 75 percent in controls. Infected ponds should be sterilized with chlorine (3 parts per million) or lime.
- **[[Ulcer Disease]]:** Primarily affects brook trout. Its bacterial cause is uncertain, and it may be a less virulent strain of *B. salmonicida*.
- **[[Peduncle Disease]]:** A rod-shaped bacterial disease at Leetown in 1941, recurring in 1945, starting in the adipose fin and destroying the caudal peduncle. Overcrowding was a predisposing factor.
- **[[Fin Rot]]:** Named for several confused infections; the typical form is a rod-shaped bacterium, treated with copper sulphate dips at 24-hour intervals.
- **[[Gill Disease]]:** Eastern form caused by long bacterial filaments; treated with copper sulphate or potassium permanganate (1 to 400,000 flushed over 4 successive days). A western form, linked to pantothenic acid deficiency by Wolf (1944), was not cured by the same methods.
- **[[Cytophaga Columnaris]]:** A myxobacterium favoring temperatures above 70°F, with a heavy outbreak among brook trout at Leetown in 1940 and 1944. Prevention is by copper sulphate dip when fish are handled in warm water.
- **Fungus ([[Saprolegnia]]):** Usually a secondary infection, but it can kill eggs in bulk. Saprolegnia invaderis was described as a stomach-invading fungus.

**Conclusions**

The author argues that most hatchery losses can be reduced through early diagnosis, prevention of overcrowding, adequate oxygen and temperature control, varied diets with animal protein, careful sanitation and separation of fingerlings from older fish, and locally bred stock. Chemical treatments are secondary: they are effective against external parasites and some bacteria but may injure fish, and they rarely eliminate infections once established.
