# Multi-Species Conservation Program for the Lower Colorado River: Notice of Intent to Prepare an EIS/EIR

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-conservation

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register notice (Volume 65, Number 134, July 12, 2000) announces that the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] and the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] will jointly prepare an Environmental Impact Statement/Environmental Impact Report (EIS/EIR) for a proposed [[Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (MSCP) on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The MSCP is intended to conserve habitat, move threatened and endangered species toward recovery, reduce the likelihood of additional listings under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and the California Endangered Species Act, accommodate current water diversions and power production, and provide the basis for take authorization. The notice is mainly procedural: it lists preliminary alternatives, probable environmental effects, public scoping history, and contacts. It contains no original field data on fish populations.

## Background & Description

The MSCP is a partnership of federal, state, tribal and other public and private stakeholders with an interest in managing water and related resources in the Lower Colorado River basin. A 1995 Memorandum of Agreement (with a later Memorandum of Clarification) among the Department of the Interior and the states of Arizona, Nevada and California established the program. The MSCP would cover the 100-year floodplain from Glen Canyon Dam to the southerly international boundary with Mexico, for a period of 50 years. Participants agreed to develop, implement and fund the program, pursuing a single approach for interim and long-term compliance with the [[Endangered Species Act]] and [[National Environmental Policy Act]] requirements.

The Bureau of Reclamation and the Fish and Wildlife Service are joint federal lead agencies. The EIS/EIR would underpin Reclamation's Record of Decision on its portion of the MSCP and the Service's Record of Decision on issuing a section 10 incidental take permit. Reclamation would also prepare a biological assessment, which the Service would use for a biological opinion under section 7. The [[Metropolitan Water District Of Southern California]] is the designated CEQA lead agency.

Public scoping began with a notice published May 18, 1999. Scoping meetings were held at six locations from June 15 to July 1, 1999, and a scoping summary report is available online. The notice invites written comments on conservation alternatives and issues by August 11, 2000.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Five preliminary MSCP alternatives were under consideration. Each addresses riparian, marsh and aquatic conservation strategies, though the number of covered species differs:

- **No Action/No Project:** Projects would proceed without a coordinated plan. Effects on listed and sensitive species would be evaluated and mitigated project by project, through individual section 10 permits or section 7 consultations.
- **Large Core/Minimized Management:** Covers about 100 species. Uses large core habitat creation (1,250 acres or greater), corridors and low-management restoration areas. The aquatic strategy creates off-stream refugia and allows periodic reconnection to the river. Non-native control is included. Delivery of water to core areas would use canals, weirs and other devices. Upstream reservoir releases of up to 40,000 cubic feet per second, combined with bankline and levee modification, are also considered. Acreage suggestions range from about 2,000 to 80,000 acres.
- **Smaller Core/Active Management:** Covers about 100 species. Uses smaller core habitat (250 to 1,250 acres) with active management. Its aquatic strategy creates off-stream refugia without periodic reconnection to the river. Non-native control is included.
- **Habitat Restoration and Enhancement:** Covers about 100 species. Emphasizes preservation and creation of habitat anywhere in the species' U.S. breeding range. Its aquatic strategy centers on self-sustaining fish populations in the [[Upper Salt River]] and other tributaries, through habitat renovation, fish stocking and removal of non-native fish. Studies indicate about 58,000 acres of land in the southwestern willow flycatcher's breeding range may be available for acquisition, though this would need supplementing for species residing only within the Lower Colorado River.
- **Species-Based (Smaller Number of Species):** Covers a limited set of species, including ESA-listed species and those likely to be listed. It borrows elements of the large and small core alternatives to meet recovery goals case by case. Actual acreage depends on the species covered.

The notice identifies probable environmental effects in four areas:

- **Biological resources:** Endangered species using the project area include the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], with [[Bald Eagle]] listed as being considered for change. The [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]] is under review for listing. The MSCP would benefit biological resources overall.
- **Hydrology and water quality:** Some conservation strategies may alter onsite waters of the United States under [[Clean Water Act]] section 404, which the [[Army Corps Of Engineers]] regulates. Effects are unknown until specific alternatives are developed.
- **Floodplains and wetlands:** The program would benefit floodplains and wetlands, especially by maintaining or creating backwaters and restoring native riparian habitat. Several thousand acres of floodplain sites have been identified for possible restoration. Conversion of developed cropland to native habitat is an important component under consideration. The partnership is also studying the economics of long-term leases or purchases of private lands.
- **Cultural resources:** The program could disturb or affect cultural resources, which the notice flags for review.

## Key Findings

- The MSCP is a 50-year, coordinated conservation plan for the Lower Colorado River floodplain, from Glen Canyon Dam to Mexico, developed by a multi-jurisdictional partnership.
- Five preliminary alternatives were under consideration, four of which cover about 100 species or a subset of listed species. Each uses riparian, marsh and aquatic strategies.
- Fish-specific measures include off-stream refugia, periodic river reconnection, non-native fish removal, and fish stocking to establish self-sustaining populations in the Upper Salt River and other tributaries.
- Habitat acreage suggestions range from about 2,000 to 80,000 acres, and roughly 58,000 acres of potentially available land in the southwestern willow flycatcher's breeding range were identified.
- The notice does not report any original population data, sample sizes or statistics for the listed fish species. Its findings are procedural and preliminary.
- Public involvement is central to the process, since many federal, state, local, tribal and private entities are involved. Comments on alternatives were due by August 11, 2000.
- Contacts for further information: Tom Shrader (Bureau of Reclamation, ecologist), Sam Spiller (Fish and Wildlife Service, Lower Colorado River coordinator), and Laura Simonek (Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, CEQA questions).
