# The Role of Competition in the Replacement of Native Fishes by Introduced Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by Allan A. Schoenherr (Fullerton College), examines why native desert fishes in North America have declined as introduced species became established. The central case is the replacement of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) by the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other introduced species. The author argues that competition is commonly assumed to cause replacement but is rarely demonstrated. Life-history comparisons suggest that predation of Gambusia females on young Poeciliopsis, together with behavioral interactions, better explains the pattern.

## Background & Description

- Deacon et al. (1979) listed 151 fish taxa in North America as endangered, threatened, or of special concern. About 20 percent (65) were considered desert species, and more than two-thirds of these had declined in number while interacting with introduced forms.
- Reduction of a native population by an introduced species can occur through hybridization, predation, or competition. Hybridization and predation are usually obvious; competition is nearly impossible to prove and is often assumed by default.
- Since the 1930s, introduced species have formed a large part of the desert fauna as native species declined.
- Gambusia affinis was introduced worldwide as a mosquito control agent, although native larvivorous fishes already inhabited many of these waters. Danielson (1968) showed that [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis*) captured mosquito larvae more effectively than Gambusia.
- Table 1 of the source lists about 30 fish species reported as reduced in number by Gambusia across Asia, Africa, and North America, drawn from Myers (1965), Hubbs and colleagues, and others. Entries include Gila Topminnow, Desert Pupfish, Pecos and Rio Conchos taxa of the Gambusia nobilis group, and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in California.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Gila Topminnow decline:** Mosquitofish were introduced to California in 1922 and reached Arizona soon after. By 1976, Gambusia had been collected from the Gila River near its mouth and from the Salt River near Tempe. Decline began before mosquitofish arrived, through diversion, channelization, and drainage of marshes (Miller 1961). From the 1930s onward, Gambusia increased as Poeciliopsis decreased.
- **Case studies:** Aravaipa Creek (Pinal County, Arizona) was fishless until about 1936, when it was stocked with mosquitofish that were in fact Poeciliopsis. By March 1959 Gila topminnows were gone and mosquitofish were abundant. Near Safford, Arizona, Poeciliopsis dominated artesian ponds in 1962; by 1966 the system yielded only mosquitofish. At the Deer Valley pond, a 1969 collection of 143 Poeciliopsis included only one male and one female Gambusia, and by December 1970 Gila topminnows were nearly eliminated.
- **Spring refugia:** Mosquitofish appear to be selected against in clear, carbonate-rich spring-fed waters occupied by Poeciliopsis. Gambusia has a selective advantage in other waters.
- **Desert pupfish:** Its decline in Arizona followed a pattern similar to that of the Gila topminnow. Introduced species included Gambusia, golden shiner (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), and largemouth bass, which extirpated the Monkey Spring pupfish in 1969.
- **Salton Sea:** Quarterly surveys since March 1978 found desert pupfish made up less than 5 percent of the fauna. At least 22 introduced species occur in freshwater tributaries. Sailfin mollies and [[Zill's Cichlid]] (*Tilapia zillii*) have displaced pupfish from nearly all habitats except shallow, warm portions of irrigation ditches and San Felipe Creek.
- **King Street Canal:** In March 1977 pupfish were common in water 5 to 10 cm deep alongside four introduced species, with habitat partitioning evident. By March 1978 habitat had been reduced to essentially two types, and by March 1980 Tilapia zillii occupied the shallow wash habitat. Replacement there may now be complete.

## Key Findings

- **Competition is rarely demonstrated.** Life-history data from cohabitation experiments showed no evidence of competition for a common resource between Poeciliopsis and Gambusia.
- **Predation is the proposed mode of replacement.** Gambusia females preyed on young Poeciliopsis. Schoenherr (1974, 1977) reported that Poeciliopsis females were smaller and had fewer embryos under cohabitation. Gambusia females matured faster and reached larger size, while Poeciliopsis continued to grow at normal rates.
- **Similarities drove the initial assumption.** Both species prefer marshes and quiet waters, share similar morphology, and tolerate a range of conditions. Gambusia tolerates colder water than Poeciliopsis.
- **Behavior matters in replacement.** In the Salton Sea area, sailfin mollies interfere with pupfish reproduction through male-male interactions. Tilapia juveniles and adults chase pupfish and eat their eggs and young.
- **Niche partitioning and character displacement** are the likely outcomes of coexistence. The author argues that competitive exclusion may be inferred from niche partitioning and character displacement rather than directly observed.
- **Pristine desert habitats** were typically occupied by few species, and introduced generalists with wide tolerances (goldfish, mosquitofish, sailfin mollies) readily establish themselves. Refugia include carbonate- or CO2-rich headsprings and extreme habitats.
- **Conservation recommendation:** The author calls for expanded life-history and behavioral research, and for preventing unnecessary introductions. He asks why Gambusia should be introduced for mosquito control when native species can perform the task.
