# Rates of Development in Embryos of a Cyprinodont Fish Exposed to Different Temperature-Salinity-Oxygen Combinations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Otto Kinne]] and Eva Maria Kinne examines how embryonic development in the euryhaline teleost [[Cyprinodon Macularius]] (desert pupfish) responds to combined changes in temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen. Eggs were transferred about 4 hours after oviposition into 48 constant temperatures (10–37 °C), eight salinities (distilled water to 85‰) and three oxygen levels (approximately 70%, 100% and 300% air saturation). Development was slowed by higher salinity and by lower oxygen, and these effects grew stronger as temperature rose. The authors conclude that dissolved oxygen, which changes with temperature and salinity, is the main controlling factor, while salinity per se has limited direct physiological effect.

## Background & Description

*Cyprinodon macularius* is a small cyprinodontid fish, described by [[Baird and Girard]] (1853). The broodstock used came from near the Salton Sea, California. Adults were held in aerated, filtered water at 26–28 °C with a 14-hour photoperiod and fed on worms, brine shrimp (*Artemia salina*), liver and commercial fish food. Seventeen spawning pairs were available; most experiments used eggs from three pairs. Eggs were usually laid individually on attached substrate and were separated during incubation to prevent clumping, which otherwise reduced development rates.

The study is part of a wider research programme on the [[Euryplastic Fish]] concept, which seeks to understand organisms' responses to combined environmental factors rather than single factors alone.

**Methods overview**
- Incubation temperatures: 48 constant temperatures, 10–37 °C, held within ±0.2 °C (up to 30 °C) and ±0.1 °C (30–37 °C).
- Salinities: glass-distilled water, ½ freshwater, freshwater, 35‰, 45‰, 55‰, 70‰, 85‰. Seawater or sea salt was used for higher salinities.
- Oxygen: 70% (stagnant, non-aerated), 100% (aerated) and 300% (oxygenated) air saturation, measured by the [[Winkler Method]].
- Development rate: time from fertilization to hatching ("incubation period"), plus time to defined embryonic stages. Mortality was expressed as a percentage of eggs per batch.
- Lighting: 14-hour day under combined daylight and fluorescent light.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors place their work in the context of known temperature–salinity interactions in marine and brackish organisms. They note that a single environmental factor only becomes important when it reaches critical intensities. Temperature and salinity are linked because changes in one alter the solubility of gases, osmotic pressure and pH of water.

Key environmental relationships identified:
- **Oxygen solubility:** Within the tested temperature and salinity range, air-saturated dissolved oxygen varied by up to 65%. This is mainly due to differing absorption coefficients of oxygen in water. Oxygen content of 70%-saturated water fell from about 8.0 ml/L in distilled water to 2.8 ml/L at 85‰ across 10–32 °C.
- **pH and carbonate:** Heavy aeration raised pH sharply, to about 9.3 in distilled water and 8.6 in 70‰ (versus 8.1 initially), by removing free CO₂. Seawater CO₂ equilibria are buffered and were not fully removed during the study.
- **Light:** Light intensity at the water surface was about 20 ft-c in incubators and 40–60 ft-c in spawning aquaria.

The authors also discuss the osmotic permeability of the fish egg chorion and plasma membrane, citing studies on [[Fundulus Heteroclitus]], salmonids and herring. They argue that the egg becomes relatively impermeable to salts shortly after oviposition, which limits the direct effect of external salinity.

## Key Findings

**1. Temperature, salinity and oxygen interact**
- Increasing salinity progressively retarded development, and increasing oxygen accelerated it. Both effects became stronger at higher temperatures.
- Hatching lengths also decreased with temperature. Average hatching length was 5.3 mm between 24 and 28 °C, falling to 3.9 mm at 34 °C and 3.65 mm at 35 °C.

**2. Development rate and salinity**
- Incubation periods were shortest in ½ freshwater and lengthened with increasing salinity (Fig. 4).
- Development was similar in all salinities at 25 °C or below. Retardation became noticeable at higher temperatures, particularly above 30 °C.
- At 70‰ and 32–33 °C, many embryos developed fully but failed to hatch. Their heart rates declined before stopping.
- No embryos hatched at 85‰. Some reached the early pigmented-eye stage at 28 °C, but all died within days, showing underdeveloped tails.

**3. Thermal limits**
- Lower lethal temperature of incubation was about 12 °C.
- Upper critical temperature shifted with salinity: about 35 °C in ½ freshwater, 34 °C in 35‰, and 31.8 °C in 70‰ (inflections on the development curves). The upper lethal temperature for hatching was about 36 °C in freshwater, 34.2 °C in 45‰ and about 32 °C in 70‰.
- Development arrested at high temperatures with reversible effects, and a sudden temperature drop to 10 °C caused arrest in some embryos.

**4. Oxygen as the controlling factor**
- At 70% saturation, development was similar to 100% saturation up to 16–18 °C. Above this, rates slowed increasingly. At 35‰ and 34–35 °C, incubation time was about 25% longer than at 100% saturation.
- Hypoxia-induced arrest, typically between the early and late pigmented-eye stages, was reversible if oxygen was restored early.
- At 300% saturation, development accelerated markedly at 35‰ and 70‰, showing that the salinity effect can be largely offset by oxygen.

**5. Mortality**
- Mortality in 35‰ was higher than in ½ freshwater and rose above 34 °C, reaching 100% at 36 °C.
- Mortality was generally higher in 70% oxygen. The data show that embryos transferred into experimental conditions shortly after oviposition were more vulnerable.

**6. Temperature coefficient (Q₁₀)**
- The temperature coefficient decreased with increasing temperature, so the van 't Hoff rule did not apply well. The authors doubt that Q₁₀ values offer much insight into complex physiological processes.
- The authors also suggest that empirical rate–temperature models, such as the logistic equation, must be adjusted for oxygen and salinity.

**7. Conclusions**
- Salinity effects on early development are small because the egg membranes limit osmotic exchange after oviposition. Oxygen supply, which depends on temperature and salinity, is the main factor controlling development rates.
- Water movement from aeration likely improved gas exchange at the egg surface, reducing hypoxic retardation.
