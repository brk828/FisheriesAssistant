## Executive Summary
Cole and Whiteside (1965) present a physical-limnological reconnaissance (June and August 1964) of two small, closed-basin saline ponds -- **Green Pond** and **Red Pond** -- at the Kiatuthlanna archaeological site, Apache County, Arizona (~6,100 ft elevation, Upper Sonoran Life Zone). Both ponds lack surface outlets and are concentrated by evaporation; salinities are extreme (up to ~258 g/L total dissolved solids in Red Pond). In early summer both ponds display unusual **dichothermy** (temperature inversion, warmer at depth than at surface) stabilized by density stratification from dissolved solids, suggesting apparent meromixis. However, by late summer, evaporative concentration of surface waters and lowering water levels raised the center of gravity and triggered a **mid-summer overturn**, rendering both ponds thermally and chemically homogeneous. The authors propose an unusual **monomictic cycle inverted relative to typical warm monomictic lakes**: winter dilution from seeps/snowmelt establishes a fresh surface lens (stratification), while summer evaporation destabilizes and mixes the water column. The temporary hypolimnion (monimolimnion) of Red Pond hosts a dense bloom of purple sulfur bacteria, imparting a red color and sharp light-attenuation layer, likely the source of the pond's name and its significance in [[Zuni Mythology]] concerning Kiatuthlanna, the "Place of the Big Water."

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Two small, closed, saline, spring/seep-fed ponds occupying solution-like depressions in Tertiary sands (Bidahochi Formation), intercepting the local water table; no surface outlets. Surrounded by juniper-pinyon woodland (Navahonian biotic province, Upper Sonoran Life Zone).
- **Salinity regime:** Extremely saline, permanent habitat -- Red Pond TDS ranged from 120 g/L (March, surface) to 258 g/L (June, 1.5 m depth) to ~221 g/L (August, uniform); Green Pond TDS ranged ~61–112 g/L. This is the first record of strongly saline, permanent Arizona waters supporting *[[Artemia salina]]*, distinct chemically from previously described Arizona aquatic habitats (Cole 1963).
- **Thermal/density stratification niche:** Early summer dichothermy created a **cryptic monimolimnion** in Red Pond (from ~0.6 m depth) and Green Pond (from ~1.5 m depth), with bottom water up to 23.2°C warmer than surface in Red Pond -- an inverted analog of the classic warm monomictic thermocline, driven by solar heating of hypersaline deep water rather than surface warming.
- **Sulfur bacteria niche:** Purple sulfur bacteria occupy the anaerobic, H₂S-rich monimolimnion of Red Pond, performing anoxygenic photosynthesis (oxidizing H₂S to S, reducing CO₂) — a classic **chemocline/anoxic photic zone niche** analogous to that described in other meromictic and stratified saline lakes.
- **Seasonal hydrologic cycle:** Winter/spring seep and snowmelt input is hypothesized to create a dilute surface lens that re-establishes stratification annually; this dilute layer would be reinforced by nocturnal ice formation and freeze-concentration effects (surface waters observed to slush-freeze at –9°C to –14°C).
- **Faunal context:** *Artemia salina* recovered from these ponds by V. Rhoton represents the first Arizona record of the species (Koehn and Cole 1964), implying the ponds function as a stable, hyperhaline refuge habitat -- ecologically comparable to other Anostracan/brine-shrimp saline lake systems (see [[Great Salt Lake Limnology]], [[Meromictic Lakes]]).

## Key Findings
- **Apparent but transient meromixis:** June 1964 profiles showed strong dichothermy and density stratification in both ponds (specific densities: Green Pond surface 1.075 vs. 2 m 1.086; Red Pond surface 1.125 vs. 1.5 m 1.180), suggesting stable meromictic layering.
- **Mid-summer overturn:** By August, both ponds had become thermally and chemically homogeneous (Green Pond mean 28.4°C, +5.26°C from June; Red Pond mean 28.25°C, +3.45°C), driven by evaporative concentration of surface water, lowered water levels raising the center of gravity, and surface warming -- overturning the "unusual monomixis."
- **Inverse seasonal stratification model:** The authors propose that these ponds stratify in **winter** (via dilute seep/snowmelt inflow over denser saline water) and circulate in **summer** (via evaporative concentration) -- the reverse of typical warm monomictic lake dynamics.
- **Heat budget:** Areal heat content was nearly equal between ponds in June (Red: 2,942 cal/cm²; Green: 2,973 cal/cm²) despite very different temperature profiles; by August, Green Pond's heat content rose to 3,486 cal/cm² while Red Pond's fell to 2,785 cal/cm², consistent with the loss of its deep warm layer upon overturn.
- **Light regime and sulfur bacteria bloom:** In June, Red Pond showed sharp light attenuation between 1–1.5 m (Secchi depth 1.4 m) linked to a purple sulfur bacteria bloom producing red water and a lavender filtrate residue at depth; Green Pond showed no comparable stratified light-absorbing layer (Secchi depth 1.8 m). By August, light penetrated to the bottom of both ponds (Secchi: Red 0.56 m, Green 1.9 m) as the ponds mixed and the bacterial layer disappeared/dispersed.
- **Freezing behavior:** August (concentrated) waters froze/slushed at unusually low temperatures -- Green Pond at –9°C, Red Pond at –14°C -- temperatures realistically reached on winter nights at nearby Saint Johns, AZ, supporting the hypothesized freeze-concentration mechanism contributing to winter stratification.
- **Cultural-ecological linkage:** The red coloration from purple sulfur bacteria in the shallow monimolimnion of Red Pond may explain the pond's vernacular name and its distinct role in [[Zuni Migration Legend]] and ceremonial practices (offerings, corn pollen, prayer feathers) recorded by Roberts (1931), tying the site's physical/biological limnology to its archaeological and ethnographic significance at [[Kiatuthlanna]].
- **Faunal significance:** Documentation of *Artemia salina* in these ponds (Koehn and Cole 1964) marks the first Arizona occurrence of the species and establishes Kiatuthlanna's ponds as chemically distinctive, strongly saline, permanent habitats relative to other known Arizona aquatic systems.
