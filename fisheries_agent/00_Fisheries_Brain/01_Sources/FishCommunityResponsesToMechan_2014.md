# Fish Community Responses to Mechanical Removal of Nonnative Fishes in a Large Southwestern River

#ichthyology #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #nonnative_species_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary:** This study focused on two abundant nonnative species—[[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*)—and four native species in the [[San Juan River]]. Channel Catfish were classified as juveniles (≤300 mm TL) and adults (>300 mm TL); fish in diets of Channel Catfish increase when individuals reach 300 mm TL. Common Carp adults were classified as ≥250 mm TL. Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) juveniles were ≤410 mm TL, [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) juveniles ≤300 mm TL. Between 1991–1997, Flannelmouth Sucker averaged 59% of annual electrofishing catch, Channel Catfish 13%, Bluehead Sucker 12%, and Common Carp 9%. Federally endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were extremely rare or extirpated; restoration via stocking began mid-1990s (114,649 Razorback Suckers, mostly subadult/adult; >4,000,000 Colorado Pikeminnows, mostly age-0, stocked 1994–2011). Channel Catfish and Common Carp identified as predators on early life stages of native fishes and potential competitors for resources.

**Habitat Associations:** The San Juan River drains 99,200 km² in Colorado, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico; mean annual discharge 56.5 m³/s (1935–2012). Upper reach (rkm 268.1–199.6) characterized by extensive channel braiding, cobble-gravel-sand substrates, riffles, runs, backwater mesohabitats. Lower reach (rkm 103.0–65.9) has more sand substrates, fewer riffles and backwaters, minimal channel braiding. Longitudinal variation in fish densities: nonnative adult Common Carp and adult Channel Catfish densities generally increased upstream; juvenile Channel Catfish densities increased downstream but were temporally variable. Water temperature and sand substrates increase, channel complexity decreases moving downstream. [[Navajo Reservoir]] (constructed 1962) eliminated native fish habitat via inundation, lowered water temperatures with hypolimnetic releases. Piscicide applied to 64.4 km below dam in 1961 purged most fishes (six native, eight nonnative species). Invasive [[Tamarix]] spp. (salt cedar) and [[Elaeagnus angustifolia]] (Russian olive) have confined, narrowed, and reduced channel braiding. Flow regime partially altered: spring peaks attenuated, summer flows elevated compared to historical levels; managed since 1998 to mimic natural regime when possible.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration:** Channel Catfish can have large home ranges in rivers; generally make upstream movements in spring, localized movement in summer, downstream movements in fall. Limited mark–recapture data from San Juan River show extensive migrations (>160 km in summer). Movement of fish into subreaches after nonnative removal and before fall monitoring (average 15 days upper reach, 39 days lower reach) may have obscured removal effects. General pattern of higher adult Channel Catfish densities upstream implies adults tend to move upstream. Migration from [[Lake Powell]] into San Juan River limited by sediment waterfall at rkm 0.0 (formed 2002 when lake elevation dropped; breached only ~2 weeks in 2011). Weir and selective fish passage at upstream limit of subreach A limits upstream movement of nonnative fishes during base flows. Recolonization rates from migrants likely influenced removal effectiveness.

**Trophic & Community Interactions:** Juveniles of Channel Catfish, Flannelmouth Sucker, and Bluehead Sucker highly omnivorous, foraging mainly on detritus, invertebrates, and algae; likely occupy same habitats where they co-occur. Such low-trophic-level resources rarely limiting in aquatic systems. Adult Channel Catfish (≥300 mm TL) increasingly piscivorous. Weak negative trend identified between annual densities of adult Channel Catfish and other small-bodied fishes. Potential predation by adult Channel Catfish on juvenile Flannelmouth Sucker hypothesized but not confirmed. Increased densities of omnivorous Razorback Suckers and invertivorous juvenile Colorado Pikeminnows from stocking may have reduced resources freed by nonnative removal, leaving little extra for other native fishes.

**Environmental Drivers:** Annual flow variation likely drives spawning and recruitment success in Flannelmouth Sucker. Longitudinal variation in Channel Catfish larval drift and age-0 densities indicates middle reaches are most productive spawning habitats. Between 1991–1997, larval Channel Catfish densities 10× higher at Mexican Hat (near upper limit subreach G) compared to Four Corners (downstream of upper reach). Annual small-bodied fish surveys (2003–2012) showed age-0 Channel Catfish densities increased downstream, peaking in lower river portions. Habitat heterogeneity, longitudinal connectivity, and sources of recruiting juveniles influenced removal success. Increased flows and turbidity may increase Rainbow Trout mortality in other Colorado River reaches.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory:** Common Carp densities declined river-wide after removal (year effect η²=0.88 upper reach, η²=0.75 lower reach), with steeper declines in subreaches with longer removal periods. Channel Catfish densities decreased only in upper reaches (subreaches A and B showed strongest declines); no temporal trends evident at lower reach. Juvenile Channel Catfish densities remained variable; adult Channel Catfish showed significant declines at upper reach (year effect η²=0.13) but not lower reach. Native fish responses equivocal: juvenile Flannelmouth Sucker densities increased over time at upper reach (year effect η²=0.07) but decreased at lower reach (η²=0.33); adult Flannelmouth Sucker declined both reaches (η²=0.28 upper, η²=0.60 lower). Bluehead Sucker densities relatively stable through study period, maintaining strong longitudinal patterns. No marked positive response by most native fishes despite reduced nonnative densities. Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker numbers increased river-wide over study period through stocking; natural spawning and recruitment extremely limited to date.

**Threat & Risk Assessment:** Establishment of nonnative fishes cited 68% of time as contributing factor in extinction of 40 North American fish taxa over last 100 years. Approximately 40% of North American fishes currently at risk (endangered, threatened, vulnerable); native southwestern U.S. fishes exceptionally imperiled due to high endemism. Channel Catfish and Common Carp identified as predators on early life stages and competitors with native fishes. Over 9,000,000 nonnative Channel Catfish stocked 1910–1986 by New Mexico Department of Game and Fish. Common Carp introduced to New Mexico waters 1883, present in Colorado River Basin by late 1880s. Nineteen nonnative fish species documented in recent San Juan River investigations versus historical depauperate fauna of ≤8 native species. Habitat degradation, loss, fragmentation and altered flow regimes act in concert with nonnative species to threaten native fishes. Complexity of large river systems limits ability to completely remove Channel Catfish and document positive native fish response.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations:** Nonnative removal in lower reach had little effect on lowering nonnative fish densities for appreciable time; transfer of efforts to middle reaches during peak Channel Catfish spawning (June–July) may stem successful spawning and recruitment. Targeting specific life stages and habitats might increase viability of sustained removal program. Managers should strive to implement removal efforts with controls (no-removal reaches) to enable effectiveness evaluation. Continued monitoring and experimentation will help untangle program efficacy and benefits for native fishes. Spatial extent of barriers plays large part in effectiveness of removal to reduce nonnative densities for substantial time periods. Electrofishing will likely never totally eliminate Channel Catfish from river. Management actions would benefit from developing strategies to evaluate how nonnative removal directly or indirectly influences native fish population dynamics. Multiple management activities operating simultaneously (flow manipulation, nonnative removal, fish stocking) make direct attribution problematic.

**Biological Reference Points:** Total cost of large-scale nonnative removal program 2001–2012: $4,218,000, resulting in removal of 314,710 Channel Catfish and 20,830 Common Carp. Upper reach removal efforts: 2–30 passes annually per subreach (each pass = two electrofishing rafts, one per shoreline); effort ranged 2–15 passes, becoming more similar among reaches over time. Lower reach: 1–9 passes per subreach annually. River-wide removal began 2008 with ≥6 yearly electrofishing passes all reaches. Electrofishing settings standardized: pulsed direct current on high range, output current maintained at 4 A. Fall monitoring conducted annually (September–October) 1994–2012 via raft-mounted electrofishing; two rafts sampled each shoreline, effort summed at ends of each 1.6 rkm (three of every four 1.6 rkm sampled yearly). CPUE calculated as fish per hour. No spatial or temporal trends in median size of Channel Catfish detected at upper reach; moderate declines in size at lower reach over study period despite variable temporal removal efforts. Size-biased gear may inadvertently increase abundance of smaller nonnative individuals, potentially increasing negative interactions with native fishes.
