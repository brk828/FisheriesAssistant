## Executive Summary

This 1993 synthesis by David M. Lodge (*Trends in Ecology & Evolution*) reviews the state of research on biological invasions, using several aquatic case studies — most notably the [[Zebra Mussel]] (*Dreissena polymorpha*), the [[Rusty Crayfish]] (*Orconectes rusticus*), and the [[Opossum Shrimp]] (*Mysis relicta*) — to illustrate how introduced species reshape community structure, food webs, and ecosystem processes. Although the paper is fundamentally a conceptual review of invasion ecology (colonization success rates, invasibility, disturbance, and predation), it draws heavily on freshwater and fish-adjacent systems to support its arguments. Fish populations appear as both **agents** (selective predators mediating crayfish competition) and **victims** (salmon decline via trophic cascade from *Mysis* introduction) of invasion dynamics, making this a key conceptual touchstone for understanding non-native species impacts in aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a conceptual/ecological paper rather than a morphological description of fish taxa, it does detail identifying and functional traits of the aquatic invertebrate invaders central to its fish-related case studies:

- **[[Zebra Mussel]]** (*Dreissena polymorpha*):
  - Small filter-feeding Eurasian bivalve; adults reach **4–5 cm**.
  - Uniquely possesses **free-swimming larvae** (veligers) among North American freshwater bivalves — enabling rapid dispersal.
  - Adults attach to firm substrates (e.g., boat hulls) via **byssal threads**, allowing occupation of hard-substrate niches unavailable to native unionids.
  - Occurs at extreme densities: **100,000–400,000 individuals/m²** in Lakes St. Clair and Erie.

- **[[Rusty Crayfish]]** (*Orconectes rusticus*):
  - Competitively superior decapod relative to native congeneric crayfish; success is mediated by aggressive shelter competition combined with **fish-mediated selective predation** favoring the invader's behavioral phenotype.

- **[[Opossum Shrimp]]** (*Mysis relicta*):
  - Small pelagic mysid decapod; introduced deliberately as a forage supplement for salmonids but instead altered zooplankton availability, disrupting the trophic base salmon depended upon.

No direct fish morphological data (body plan, fin counts, etc.) are reported in this text; fish are discussed functionally, as predators or trophic endpoints.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames aquatic invaders within a broader theoretical model of **colonization success**, **community invasibility**, and **keystone impact**, with fish playing structurally important roles in several documented food webs:

- **Zebra Mussel invasion (Great Lakes system, established ~1985)**:
  - Introduced via ballast water from the Caspian/Black Sea region into the Great Lakes.
  - Filtration of phytoplankton dramatically increases water transparency, promoting rooted macrophyte colonization.
  - Restructures the pelagic–benthic food web: shunts carbon/nutrients from **pelagic zooplankton and phytoplankton** pathways toward **benthic macroinvertebrate and periphyton/macrophyte** pathways.
  - Native [[Unionid Clams]] are smothered and decline sharply.
  - Total macroinvertebrate abundance increases ~10-fold in mussel-dominated areas.
  - **Predicted negative impacts on fish populations have not yet been documented**, and zebra mussels appear to have few predators (a possible "trophic dead end").
  - Simplified food web presented: *Zooplankton → Fish*; *Native Invertebrates → Mussel*; *Periphyton/Macrophytes*; *Nutrients* interlinking pelagic and benthic compartments.

- **Rusty Crayfish invasion (Northern Wisconsin lakes)**:
  - *O. rusticus* drives native congeneric crayfish to local extinction via combined **competition for shelter** and **selective fish predation** favoring the invader.
  - Reduces macrophyte and invertebrate abundance by up to **100%**.
  - Indirectly reduces populations of **some fish species** and alters lake-wide energy/nutrient flow — establishing the crayfish as a functional **keystone species**.

- **Opossum Shrimp invasion (Flathead Lake, Montana)**:
  - Introduction of *Mysis relicta* intended to enhance fish forage instead triggered a **trophic cascade**: reduced zooplankton availability → **decline in salmon numbers** → collapse of eagle and grizzly bear populations that fed on spawning salmon.
  - Demonstrates cross-ecosystem linkage between aquatic invaders and terrestrial consumers via a fish intermediary.

- **General predation theory**: Predation (particularly by vertebrates, including fish) is identified as one of the two leading causes of colonization *failure*, yet paradoxically, established vertebrate/invertebrate predators are also cited as a primary mechanism by which invaders exert **large community-level impact** (e.g., island lizard extinctions, African Great Lakes [[Cichlid]] extinctions via introduced predatory fish).

## Key Findings

- **Colonization success rates** are low: estimates range from ~10% (Williamson) up to a likely-overestimated 35%; paleobiological estimates (Vermeij) for Miocene–Pleistocene biotic exchanges show most taxa achieving <1–24% establishment, rarely up to 46%.
- **Impact prevalence**: Only an estimated 2–40% of established exotics have detectable ecological impact, though this figure is highly uncertain due to publication bias and difficulty detecting subtle effects (e.g., competition).
- **Two leading causes of colonization failure**: inappropriate climate and predation; competition and disease impacts are likely underestimated due to measurement difficulty.
- **Predation as a dominant impact mechanism**: cited as the primary driver of extinction in island lizards and African Great Lakes cichlids, and central to the rusty crayfish and zebra mussel case studies.
- **Keystone invaders**: *Dreissena polymorpha*, *Orconectes rusticus*, and *Mysis relicta* are highlighted as invaders whose effects propagate across population, community, and ecosystem levels — not merely adding species richness but restructuring entire trophic networks.
- **Community invasibility paradox**: predation by natives can both *resist* invasion (by directly killing colonists) and *enhance* invasibility (by suppressing competitors, thus permitting coexistence) — an unresolved tension flagged for future research.
- **Economic impact**: Zebra mussel biofouling of water intake structures is forecast to cost **$2–4 billion annually** in the American Great Lakes alone.
- **Predictive limits**: Despite strong general statistical patterns (e.g., species-poor communities are more invasible than species-rich ones), the paper concludes that **outcome of any single introduction event cannot be reliably predicted** without intensive, near-dissertation-level study of both the colonist and target community — a conclusion directly relevant to fisheries and aquatic invasive species management.
