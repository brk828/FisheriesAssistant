## Executive Summary

This paper, presented in 1964, describes the use of **ammonium nitrate/fuel oil (AN/FO)** blasting mixtures as a cost-effective alternative to dynamite for creating and restoring open water habitat ("potholes") in emergent marsh vegetation. While the primary focus is **waterfowl habitat management**, the technique has direct relevance to aquatic and fishery habitat engineering, as one documented blast explicitly created a **fishing pond** (60 ft × 120 ft, 8–10 ft deep). The paper is not a fish-species study but a **habitat modification and explosives-engineering report**, useful to aquatic ecologists for understanding historical methods of pond/wetland creation that shape fish and waterfowl habitat structure.

## Background & Description

- **Problem addressed**: Dense, continuous stands of emergent vegetation (sedges, grasses, bulrush-cattail communities, brush) reduce marsh productivity for wildlife by eliminating open water needed for waterfowl pair isolation, courtship, and brood rearing.
- **Prior methods**: Draglines and bulldozers (effective but limited by wet soil/access issues); dynamite blasting (Scott & Dever 1940; Provost 1948), which is costly.
- **New technique**: Ammonium nitrate (AN), a common agricultural fertilizer, mixed with **No. 2 fuel oil** (5.4–6% by weight; standard field mix = 1 gallon fuel oil per 100 lbs AN) forms an inexpensive, insensitive **blasting agent (AN/FO)**, detonated via a dynamite primer and detonating cord network.
- **Cost comparison**: AN/FO costs approximately **one-fifth to one-third** the cost of equivalent dynamite blasting work.
- **Safety classification**: AN/FO is officially a "blasting agent," not an explosive; it cannot be detonated by a standard No. 8 blasting cap alone, requiring a dynamite primer/booster.
- **Field preparation**: Best results use **uncoated prilled AN** (~140–325 mesh); agricultural AN coated with diatomaceous earth performs poorly (incomplete detonation, toxic nitrogen oxide smoke). Charges are sealed in 10-mil polyethylene bags for use in wet marsh conditions, since AN is less dense than water and must be prevented from floating.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Target ecosystems**: Freshwater emergent marshes and woodland potholes in Minnesota (Chippewa National Forest, Tamarac National Wildlife Refuge), Michigan (Michigan Dept. of Conservation sites), Wisconsin, and salt marsh at **Parker River National Wildlife Refuge, Massachusetts**.
- **Habitat value driver**: Wildlife productivity depends on the **interspersion of cover and open water** (Low, Scott & Dever 1941); continuous emergent vegetation without open water is functionally unproductive for waterfowl.
- **Woodland potholes**: Serve as secluded courtship/pairing sites for ducks; broods typically move to adjacent larger water bodies (0.5–1 mile away) for rearing (Evans & Black 1956).
- **Natural succession**: Identified as the primary driver of pothole loss, converting open water to dense sedge/grass/brush stands over time.
- **Soil-dependent outcomes**: Blasting efficiency varies significantly by substrate:
  - Peat marshes perform best when mineral soil underlies the peat at 3–4 ft depth.
  - Heavy clay soils produce wider, shallower craters at shallower charge depths.
- **Salt marsh application**: The Parker River NWR project combined **mosquito abatement** with waterfowl habitat creation using military cratering charges, indicating a dual ecological/public-health rationale for marsh blasting in coastal systems.
- **Fishery-relevant outcome**: A large-scale blast (972 lbs AN/FO, 12 charges) created an **8–10 ft deep, 60 × 120 ft fishing pond**, demonstrating that this technique can generate aquatic habitat with sufficient depth to support fish populations, not just shallow waterfowl potholes.

## Key Findings

- **Charge depth controls crater morphology**:
  - Optimal charge depth: **3–4 feet** below surface.
  - Charges shallower than **2 feet** dissipate force upward, producing poor results.
  - Deeper charges → narrower, deeper holes; shallower charges (2–3 ft) → wider, shallower holes (especially in clay).

- **Representative excavation outcomes** (Minnesota & Michigan):
  | Charge Configuration | Soil Type | Resulting Pothole | Cost |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Two 50-lb charges, 12 ft apart, 3–4 ft deep | Heavy clay | 25 × 35 ft, 7 ft deep | $11.00 |
  | Six 50-lb charges, two rows, 10-ft centers | — | 30 × 40 ft, 6 ft deep | — |
  | Two 8-lb charges | Peat (Michigan) | 14 × 19 ft, 4 ft deep | $4.00 |
  | One 50-lb charge | Peat | 25 ft diameter, 7 ft deep | $5.60 |
  | 972-lb charge (12 × 27-lb units, 3 rows, 10-ft centers) | — | **Fishing pond**: 60 × 120 ft, 8–10 ft deep | ~$50.00 |

- **Military cratering charges** (Parker River NWR, salt marsh):
  - 42 lbs AN + TNT booster per charge, contained in a 6-in × 30-in metal cylinder.
  - Single charges most efficient; average crater = 25 ft diameter × 4 ft deep.
  - Material cost ≈ **$12.00 per crater**, versus ≈ **$3.00 per 40-lb AN/FO charge** — indicating standard AN/FO remains substantially cheaper than military cratering charges for equivalent habitat creation.

- **Detonation engineering**:
  - Detonating cord (rather than individual electric caps) preferred for connecting multiple charges spaced 10–12 ft apart, exceeding standard electric cap lead-wire lengths.
  - Clove-hitch knots at right angles ensure reliable simultaneous detonation across the main line.
  - Wind direction management reduces debris fallback into excavated craters, particularly for large-diameter holes.

- **Comparative reclamation success**: Heavy equipment (draglines/bulldozers) at Tamarac NWR achieved **>90% mated pair use** of reclaimed wetlands (Hunt, 1963), providing a benchmark against which blasting-derived habitats can be ecologically compared, though this paper does not report direct pair-use data for AN/FO-blasted sites.
