# Fishes of the Lower Green River Drainage, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Kentucky Nature Preserves Commission report (Scientific and Technical Series No. 3, January 1983) by Retzer, Burr, and Warren presents an annotated checklist and ecological analysis of the fishes of the lower [[Green River]] drainage. Collections at 112 stations, mostly in the summers of 1979 and 1980, supplemented by verified museum and agency records, yielded **94 species in 19 families**. The authors interpret the fauna in terms of Pleistocene glaciation, post-glacial change, historic swamp drainage, and modern [[Strip Mining]], and they identify ten Kentucky fishes of conservation concern present in the area.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The study area covers most of the lower Green River drainage, from the confluence with the [[Ohio River]] upstream to river kilometer 174.4 at Rochester, Butler County. It drains approximately 5,211 sq km within eleven counties. The Green River basin as a whole drains 22,895.6 sq km of west-central Kentucky and 984.2 sq km of north-central Tennessee. Major tributaries include [[Cypress Creek]], Deer Creek, [[Panther Creek]], Pond Creek, and [[Pond River]].

**Physical setting.**
- The area spans a broad lowland alluvial floodplain on the Western Coal Field and an upland escarpment area rising 180–200 m above the floodplain.
- Bedrock is Pennsylvanian shale, siltstone, and sandstone in the lowlands, and lower Pennsylvanian to upper Mississippian sandstone in the uplands.
- Rainfall averages 111.8 cm/year, and mean annual temperature is 14 °C.
- Natural vegetation is mainly Oak-Hickory Forest, with Southern Floodplain forest (blackgum, oaks, bald cypress) in parts of Cypress Creek and the extreme lower Green River.

**Habitat types.**
1. **Green River proper:** a moderately large river with an average channel depth of 2.8 m, maintained by three dams. Discharge at river kilometer 161.2 averages 239.9 cu m/s. The bottom is mostly soft clay and silt, and submergent vegetation was not observed at any collecting site.
2. **Lowland streams and swamps:** the dominant habitat, typically channelized, turbid, sluggish, and organic-rich. Swamps are rich in aquatic vegetation such as *Chara*, *Potamogeton*, and *Typha*.
3. **Upland streams:** found only in hilly areas of Pond Creek, upper Pond River, and South Fork Panther Creek. They are clear, fast-flowing, rocky, and riffled.

**Methods.** Common minnow and bag seines were the main gear. An ichthyocide was used occasionally, and boat-mounted electrofishing was used at Sites 70 and 109. Sampling continued until no new species appeared. Abundance was not quantified, and deep-dwelling species were probably under-represented. Voucher specimens are deposited at the Southern Illinois University Ichthyology Collection and the KNPC.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical baseline.** Woolman (1892) reported 17 species from lower Pond Creek, three of which the authors consider questionable. Early settlers found the area dominated by swamps. Clearing and drainage, channelization, and field runoff increased turbidity and nearly eliminated aquatic vegetation, leaving only remnant swamps in Cypress Creek and near the Green River mouth.

**Pleistocene and post-glacial history.** The authors reconstruct the fauna by assuming that present-day species' habitat tolerances match those of their ancestral stocks. Wisconsin-age lakes and marshes covered the lowlands. Mixed molluscan and fish faunas suggest a cool climate that allowed northern and southern species to coexist. Relict populations of *Percopsis omiscomaycus* and *Esox masquinongy* in the upper Green River are interpreted as remnants of glacially displaced northern fishes. The fauna is now dominated by lowland and large-river species.

**Human pressures.**
- *Strip mining:* In 1977 there were about 91 surface and 14 underground mines in the relevant counties, roughly 25% of them in the study area. Eleven sampling sites produced no fishes, and strip mining was evident at nine of them.
- *Oil wells:* Streams near wells had faunas comparable to similar streams away from wells, but swamps and backwaters receiving oil-field brines yielded meager catches.
- *Dams:* The three dams on Green River proper may impede spawning migrations of species such as *Anguilla rostrata*, *Alosa chrysochloris*, *Cycleptus elongatus*, and *Stizostedion vitreum*.
- *Invasions:* Threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) has recently invaded the Green River following stocking and natural range expansion in the Mississippi and Ohio valleys.

## Key Findings

**Diversity and composition**
- The study area holds 94 of the 149 fish species known from the Green River drainage (63%), though it covers only about one-fifth of the drainage. No species were endemic.
- The 94 species fall in 19 families. Five families account for 75 species (about 80%): Cyprinidae (25), Percidae (16), Centrarchidae (13), Catostomidae (12), and Ictaluridae (9). The other 19 species belong to 14 additional families.
- Three species were new records for the drainage: [[Spotted Gar]] (*Lepisosteus oculatus*), [[Cypress Minnow]] (*Hybognathus hayi*), and [[Spotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis punctatus*).
- The authors report 112 species for the lower drainage after adding three new records to Burr's 109. This exceeds the 85 known from the western Kentucky embayment and the 99 from the Licking River.

**Distribution patterns**
- Most species occurred in lowland areas (53 species) and Green River proper (49 species). Upland areas held 28 species.
- Cyprinids dominated every habitat type. The four species found in all habitat units included three centrarchids (*Lepomis cyanellus*, *L. macrochirus*, *L. megalotis*) and *Pimephales notatus*.

**Conservation concern**
- Ten Kentucky fishes of threatened, special-concern, or undetermined status occur in the lower drainage: *Ichthyomyzon unicuspis*, *Lepisosteus oculatus*, *Hybognathus hayi*, *Cycleptus elongatus*, *Erimyzon sucetta*, *Chologaster agassizi*, *Elassoma zonatum*, *Lepomis punctatus*, *Ammocrypta pellucida*, and *Percina phoxocephala*.
- Seven of these are at risk of extirpation from the lower drainage if their habitats are not protected. *Lepisosteus oculatus*, *Hybognathus hayi*, and *Lepomis punctatus* are considered especially vulnerable, each known from only one or two localities.
- Several species are linked to swamp drainage, including *Lepisosteus oculatus*, *Amia calva*, *Erimyzon sucetta*, *Centrarchus macropterus*, *Elassoma zonatum*, and *Lepomis punctatus*.
- Population status of *Elassoma zonatum* is a particular concern. The Cypress Creek population is the easternmost in the Ohio Valley and may be threatened by strip mining.

**Habitat and water quality**
- Strip mining may affect as much as 25% of the study area. Strip-mined sites showed red-orange water, extremely soft bottoms, very low pH, and few or no fishes or invertebrates.
- The survival of *Ammocrypta pellucida* and *Percina phoxocephala* depends mainly on maintaining high water quality in the Green River.

**Recommendations**
- The authors call for habitat protection at the few known localities of the rarest species and for effective land management to preserve the fish fauna.
- Further study is needed to assess the status of *Ichthyomyzon unicuspis*, *Cycleptus elongatus*, and *Chologaster agassizi* in the lower drainage.
