# Botanical Explorations in the Southwest (1904)

#botany #arid-ecology #plant-taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This 1904 report from the [[New York Botanical Garden]], published in the *Journal of the New York Botanical Garden*, records a botanical collecting expedition across Texas, Arizona, Sonora, and Baja California. The party surveyed desert vegetation, descended the [[Colorado River]] by sloop from [[Yuma]] to the [[Gulf of California]], and collected living plants for the Garden. The document also includes a short essay, written for teachers, on protecting native plants. Its aquatic content is limited to river-delta and coastal-bay habitats. No fish or aquatic fauna are recorded.

## Background & Description

The expedition began in New York on January 13, 1904, and reached Langtry, Texas, three days later. Stops included Tucson, Arizona, where the party consulted with the [[Desert Botanical Laboratory]] and gave a public lecture. The party also received living indigenous plants from the University of Arizona grounds and from J. J. Thornber's private collection.

The expedition vessel was a flat-bottomed sloop built at Yuma by G. Sykes, a civil engineer of Flagstaff. It was fitted with a centerboard, mainsail, and jib. Loaded with about 1,200 pounds of camping gear, provisions, instruments, and collecting outfit, it drew 9 to 12 inches. The party included Prof. R. H. Forbes, Director of the Tucson Experiment Station, an assistant, and E. C. Cushman, who joined at Lerdo.

The report's author is N. L. Britton, Director-in-Chief. The document is signed by D. T. MacDougal, Director of the Laboratories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Delta and river habitats.** The Colorado River delta is an alluvial plain about the size of Massachusetts. Its vegetation changes along the river:
- Upper delta: dense mesquite, willow, and poplar, with giant cane (*Phragmites*) on newly formed low land.
- Lower course: cattail (*Typha angustifolia*) and flood plains of salt grass and saltbush, grading to mudflats with salt grass and a network of salt sloughs.
- Santa Clara Slough: a flowing salt stream crossing a sandy, gravelly desert plain.

The lower river is subject to strong tidal waves that flood the plain and make navigation hazardous. The river also shifts channels within a single season, which creates a boundary dispute risk under the [[Gadsden Purchase]] treaty.

**Coastal desert.** Near [[San Felipe Bay]], hot springs emerge at low tide, and algae grow on the rocks. Fresh water can be dug from a sandy depression at about 85°F. It is slightly alkaline and salty. Local Indigenous peoples had known of this source for about two centuries. Coastal slopes rise to 500–600 feet. Desert species there include [[Ocotillo]], Parkinsonia as a tree, the "copal quien" (*Veatchia*), opuntias, and the great tree cactus (*Cereus pecten-aboriginum*), which may reach its northern limit here.

**Mountains and aridity.** An ascent of a granite mountain of about 4,000 feet revealed extreme aridity and low relative humidity. Ephedra, Euphorbia, Asclepias, Opuntia, and Mammillaria grew at lower levels. Agave and Yucca occurred on the summit ridgelines. The report notes many latex-bearing plants.

**Unexplored terrain.** The Tablita range, the central ridge of northern Baja California, rises gradually to the Calamahue peak (also called Santa Catalina or San Pedro Martyr). Its estimated height is over 10,000 feet. No passes are known between its northern end and Agua Dulce, about 125 miles south. The author describes this area as terra incognita.

## Key Findings

- **Collections:** Living plants from the sotol region, Tucson, and the Baja California mountains were brought to the Garden. Rope made from lechuguilla fibers was also collected. The author considers this fiber worth commercial consideration.
- **Expedition logistics:** The river descent began at Yuma on January 28. It covered about 100 river miles in six days. The return to Yuma took the sloop from Lerdo on February 22; the land party covered 72 miles in two days.
- **Humidity:** Relative humidity, even in the delta, was extremely low. Temperature and humidity were recorded throughout. A separate paper on this topic was in preparation.
- **Institutional exchange:** Arrangements were made to receive a large set of Arizona herbarium specimens in exchange for Garden material.
- **Boundary issue:** Shifting Colorado River channels could move large tracts of land between the United States and Baja California.
- **Navigation hazard:** The lower river has repeatedly caused accidents and hardship for explorers.
- **Exploration gap:** The Calamahue and Tablita region remained largely unmapped. The author argues it would reward a well-organized naturalist expedition.

### Essay: The Protection of Our Native Plants (for Teachers)

This short essay, awarded second prize in the 1904 competition of the [[Caroline and Olivia Phelps Stokes Fund]], argues that calls to protect native plants persist despite strong economic and aesthetic arguments. The author's central claim is that the people who destroy native vegetation have not yet been reached by these arguments. Plants with direct or indirect economic value will need protection as long as business competition continues. The excerpt ends before the full analysis of destructive classes.
