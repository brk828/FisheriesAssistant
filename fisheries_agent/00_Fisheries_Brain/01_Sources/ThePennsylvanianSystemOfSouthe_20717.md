# Pennsylvanian and Permian Stratigraphy of Southeastern Arizona

#geology #stratigraphy #paleontology #pennsylvanian-permian

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of two Arizona Geological Society-style papers on Upper Paleozoic rocks, plus an extensive bibliography. The first, by K. C. Havenor, covers the [[Pennsylvanian System]] of southeastern Arizona. The second, by Donald L. Bryant, covers [[Permian Marker Zones]] in southern Arizona. The rocks are predominantly marine carbonates. The main topics are nomenclature changes, formation boundaries, thickness variations, fossil-based age dating, and correlation with adjacent areas. The document contains no fish or aquatic-ecosystem data. Its marine fauna (brachiopods, fusulinids, gastropods, echinoids, corals) is used only to date and correlate rock units.

## Background & Description

Pennsylvanian rocks crop out in most mountain ranges of southeastern Arizona. They form part of a carbonate-dominated Upper Paleozoic sequence. They rest conformably on Mississippian beds and grade upward into Permian rocks, and exposures are discontinuous because of faulting, erosion and burial.

Nomenclature has changed repeatedly:

- **[[Naco Formation]]**: Ransome (1904) first named the thick marine section the "Naco limestone" from exposures in the Naco Hills south of Bisbee. Girty's fossil identifications showed both Early Pennsylvanian and younger, Hueco-like faunas.
- **Stoyanow (1936)** tried to restrict "Naco" to Pennsylvanian beds, but this was impractical because no lithologic break separates the Pennsylvanian and Permian parts.
- **[[Naco Group]]**: Gilluly, Cooper, and Williams (1954) elevated Naco to group rank to include all Permian and Pennsylvanian units in the region. The group contains the [[Horquilla Limestone]], the [[Earp Formation]], [[Colina Limestone]], [[Epitaph Dolomite]], [[Scherrer Formation]] and [[Concha Limestone]].
- **[[Tule Spring Limestone]]**: Lindgren (1905b) defined this unit in the Clifton-Morenci area for Pennsylvanian and Lower Mississippian strata. It is at least 500 feet thick at its type locality and rests on the Devonian Morenci shale or the Ordovician Longfellow limestone. It contains a considerable hiatus.

**Lithology of key formations:**

- **Horquilla Limestone**: pinkish-gray to dark gray, aphanitic to medium-crystalline, cherty, fossiliferous limestone with gray, black, red or greenish shale beds. Shale becomes more abundant upward. Its contact with the underlying Mississippian beds is disconformable. Where it overlies the [[Escabrosa Limestone]], the contact shows scour-and-fill structures, karst, and red chert pebble conglomerate.
- **Earp Formation**: interbedded limestone, dolomitic limestone, shale and sandstone that form subdued ledge-and-slope topography. Lower beds may be up to 3 feet thick and composed almost entirely of fusulinid tests.
- **[[Andrada Formation]]**: thin-bedded, varicolored sandstone, siltstone and shale with gypsum and marl. Used in western areas where the Naco units cannot be separated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes paleoenvironments rather than living ecosystems. Its ecological content is limited to the following points:

- The Pennsylvanian seas reached their maximum areal extent in Arizona by the end of Desmoinesian time.
- The Horquilla-to-Earp transition records little environmental change. It shows a gradual increase in fine-grained clastic input that continues into the Permian [[Wolfcamp Series]].
- The Andrada Formation, with its high sand, marl and gypsum content in the basal part, indicates a shallower and more restricted environment than the underlying Horquilla Limestone.
- Permian faunal biotopes are described as brachiopod-dominated (with corals, bryozoans, echinoderm fragments, pelecypods and gastropods) or gastropod-dominated, where other fossils are scarce.
- Fusulinids are common in the lower Permian and rare in the upper part. Clastic beds are generally barren of fossils.
- The Concha Limestone shows a shift near its top from a brachiopod assemblage to a mollusc-dominated fauna, with abundant gastropods and common pelecypods.

## Key Findings

**Thickness of Pennsylvanian sections**

| Locality | Approximate thickness |
|---|---|
| Chiricahua Mountains | ~2,100 ft |
| Gunnison Hills | ~1,900 ft |
| Naco Hills (south of Bisbee) | ~1,400 ft |
| Whetstone Mountains | ~1,000–1,100 ft |
| Vekol Mountains (~75 mi WNW of Tucson) | ~700 ft |

The Havenor paper notes these thicknesses are hard to establish because the Pennsylvanian top is hidden within lithologically similar beds, and the rocks have been deformed, intruded and eroded since post-Permian time. A northwest-trending thick depositional trough extended across southeastern Arizona. Its wedge of sediment thickens southeastward and may have contained several semi-separate basins.

**Age of Pennsylvanian beds**

- Fusulinella, Fusulina and Triticites zones in the Chiricahua and Naco Hills sections indicate deposition from Atokan through Virgilian time.
- Megafossils from the Vekol Mountains (including *Spirifer rockymontanus*, *Neospirifer cameratus*, *Composita subtilita* and *Dictyoclostus americanus*) suggest a Desmoinesian to Missourian range.
- No undisputed Morrowan fossils are known from Arizona.
- By mid-Atokan time, several hundred feet of Horquilla Limestone had already accumulated near the Chiricahuas and Naco Hills. By mid-Desmoinesian time, all of southeastern Arizona was inundated.

**Pennsylvanian-Permian boundary**

- The boundary lies within the Earp (or Andrada) Formation. It has no lithologic or erosional break and is located by fossil evidence alone.
- In some areas it can be placed within a few feet; elsewhere only within a few hundred feet (see [[Pennsylvanian-Permian Boundary]]).
- Fusulinid zones are critical for placing it. Distinctive colors of fusulinids and matrix may also help correlation in the field.

**Permian section and marker zones (Bryant)**

- Permian thickness may reach 5,000 ft, but no area exceeds 4,000 ft and most are under 2,500 ft.
- Ages range from lowest Wolfcampian to probably middle Guadalupian.
- An orange-to-red chert pebble conglomerate marks the Earp. It occurs about 350 ft below the top in the Gunnison Hills and Swisshelm Mountains, about 150 ft below the top in the Naco Hills, and about 1,400 ft above the Earp base in the Portal section.
- The Colina Limestone is about 600 ft thick at Tombstone. It is marked by large *Omphalotrochus* gastropods with abundant echinoid spines.
- The Epitaph Dolomite is nearly 800 ft thick at Tombstone. In the Sands Ranch area of the southern Whetstones it is about 1,200 ft thick and includes about 200 ft of gypsum.
- The Scherrer Formation is recognizable across southern Arizona. Its type section in the Gunnison Hills has about 65 ft of red siltstone, 300 ft of white sandstone, 165 ft of limestone and 150 ft of white sandstone. A distinctive zone of *Permocidaris* echinoid spines occurs near the middle of its limestone member.
- The Concha Limestone is richly fossiliferous. Its lower cherty unit (about 150 ft) has abundant large *Dictyoclostus* and bryozoans. A "tan chert zone" (about 30 ft) is bracketed by horn corals below and a *Neospirifer* zone about 20 ft above.
- Fusulinids of lower Guadalupian (Word) age (*Parafusulina*) occur in narrow zones about 350 ft above the Concha base.
- The Rainvalley Formation is more than 500 ft of thinner-bedded, more varicolored limestone that overlies the typical Concha. It includes a five-foot chert bed with abundant *Composita* about 200 ft above its base.

**Regional correlation**

- To the north, Pennsylvanian rocks are represented by the Supai Formation and the Paradox basin sequence.
- To the south, in Sonora, the Sierra de Teras limestones are correlated with the Escabrosa and Naco group and are more than 5,000 ft thick.
- In New Mexico, the Pennsylvanian is generally the Magdalena Group. In the Big Hatchet Mountains, the Pennsylvanian-Permian boundary lies within the Horquilla, well below the base of the Earp. This shows a lithologic transition across time lines.
