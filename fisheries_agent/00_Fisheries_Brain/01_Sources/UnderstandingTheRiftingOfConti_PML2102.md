# Environmental Assessment for the Basin and Range Geoscientific Experiment (BARGE) in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percichthyidae #Centrarchidae #Clupeidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This National Park Service environmental assessment (dated December 14, 1993) evaluates Project BARGE, a marine seismic reflection survey of the [[Lake Mead Fault System]] in the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]] (LMNRA). The study aims to image the crust across the [[Breakaway Zone]] between the [[Basin and Range Province]] and the [[Colorado Plateau]] to test whether a weak, flowing lower crust permits continental rifting. The preferred alternative is a towed [[Air Gun]] array operated from a barge over 2–5 days in March 1994, with about 50 [[Seismometer]] stations deployed on land. The document concludes that impacts on fish, native endangered species, water quality, and recreation will be minimal, and it excludes underwater explosives after stakeholder concerns.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Continental margins form by rifting, yet the forces that drive it remain poorly understood. Lake Mead lies across a major boundary where crust has been extended by roughly a factor of two in the Basin and Range but barely deformed in the Colorado Plateau. Estimates suggest extension of 190–300 km across the Basin and Range at this latitude, mostly in the last 15 million years.

**Method.** The marine survey uses [[Seismic Reflection]] equipment: two 300 scfm compressors, a 4,000 in³ air gun array towed at about 10 m depth, a 2.4 km streamer with 120 channels, and a DFS-V recording system. Around 8,400 shots are planned at 25 m intervals. Marine surveys yield data density about 60 times that of land surveys and need charges 60–250 times smaller.

**Lake Setting.** Lake Mead is a large reservoir created in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]]. Key morphometry (Table 1): maximum operating level 367.0 m, maximum depth 180 m, surface area 660 km², volume 36 km³, length 183 km, shoreline development 9.7, retention time 4.0 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishery history.** [[Largemouth Bass]] were stocked in 1935 and boomed until the late 1930s, then declined repeatedly. Threadfin shad were introduced in 1955 as forage, which increased bass growth and produced a harvest peak of about 800,000 in 1963. Glen Canyon Dam (1963) changed river flows and reduced bass spawning success. [[Striped Bass]] and rainbow trout were stocked in 1969 to augment the fishery. Trout collapsed after 1976, and striped bass condition declined through the late 1970s and early 1980s, with die-offs linked to low oxygen (<2 mg/L) in thermal refuges.

**Current fishery.** Striped bass made up an average of 66% of the 1983–1992 catch, followed by channel catfish (19.3%), largemouth bass (6.2%), black crappie (5.4%), and bluegill (2.4%). Rainbow trout were under 1%. Forage is the principal limiting factor: shad are abundant mainly near inflows such as Las Vegas Bay.

**Native fishes.** Before impoundment, only nine native fish species occurred in the mainstream Colorado River. Several are federally listed as endangered:
- **Bonytail Chub** and **Colorado Squawfish**: no evidence of persistence in Lake Mead; last records in the 1950s–1960s.
- **Humpback Chub**: not known from Lake Mead; the nearest population is far upstream in Grand Canyon.
- **Razorback Sucker**: the only one still present. A total of 43 fish were captured at Las Vegas and Echo bays over four years, with no evidence of recruitment.

**Water quality.** Lake Mead is alkaline, hard, and saline (TDS around 630 mg/L; pH about 8.0), with very low phosphorus and oligotrophic productivity. Chlorophyll-a averaged 1.5 µg/L in 1981–1982. Wastewater from Las Vegas Wash raises nutrients in the inner bays. Summer stratification produces a negative heterograde oxygen curve, though oxygen rarely falls below 4 mg/L in most of the reservoir.

**Land and recreation.** The LMNRA covers about 1.5 million acres and recorded 9.34 million recreational visits in 1992. Peak use runs April–September. Main uses are boating, swimming, waterskiing, fishing, and camping.

## Key Findings

- **Project rationale:** Marine seismic imaging gives a complete transect from the Colorado Plateau into the stretched Basin and Range, providing a direct test of whether lower-crustal flow explains crustal re-thickening (Kruse et al. 1991).
- **Preferred alternative:** Marine air gun surveying, with about 210 linear km of transects (about 95 km along transect A–A′–A″, 75 km in Boulder and Virgin basins, and 40 km along D–D′), conducted in March 1994.
- **Land alternative rejected:** Land surveying would have lower data density and signal quality, require dynamite and drilling in desert terrain, and cost many times more.
- **Air gun impacts:** The literature indicates no fish kills from air guns and minimal impacts on eggs and larvae. The authors acknowledge that uncertainty remains within a region near the source. The risk to adult fish is judged low.
- **Threadfin shad:** A 1989 echo-sounding survey found few fish in open water in March–April. Biologists judged that the March timing avoids peak shad concentrations, so impacts on the sport fishery are expected to be negligible.
- **Native fishes (Marsh 1993, Appendix B):** The project is unlikely to affect federally listed endangered fish. Transects do not enter Las Vegas or Echo bays, the closest approach being at least 1.6 km. Recommendations include observer monitoring and tissue sampling for any razorback sucker mortality.
- **Mitigation:** Explosives were dropped from the proposal; only air guns are used. Echo sounding precedes shooting, transects are adjusted to avoid fish, and trained observers in patrol boats monitor for fish mortality. Seismometers are placed in shallow holes (30–40 cm deep) with minimal surface disturbance, and sensitive archaeological and ecological areas are avoided.
- **Other impacts:** Geological effects are negligible, with ground shaking comparable to a magnitude 2.0 earthquake. Water quality effects are minimal because the lake is isothermal in March. No wetlands are affected. Recreation is slightly disrupted for a few days, and about 15–20 scientists and technicians will be involved.
- **No Action:** Without the project, little progress can be made on understanding rifting, since the region is among the few places on Earth where rifting is currently active.
