# Fishes of Colorado: 1967 Progress Report and Rare Species Comments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1967 package from the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]] at Colorado State University. It contains a cover letter from [[Robert Behnke]] to Dr. W.L. Minckley, a *Progress Report on the Fishes of Colorado* by [[Hiram W. Li]] and Robert J. Behnke, and a set of Behnke's comments on the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] list of rare and endangered fishes. The report is a preliminary revision of [[Beckman's Guide to the Fishes of Colorado]]. It proposes name changes, added and deleted species, and corrected distributions, and it argues that the state's fish taxonomy is poorly resolved. The comments focus mainly on cutthroat trout taxonomy, hybridization with introduced rainbow trout, and the status of native Colorado River basin fishes.

## Background & Description

The authors trace the weak state of Colorado ichthyology to the U.S. Geological and Geographical Surveys of the 1860s and 1870s. Those collections produced many newly named genera and species, and their validity was never critically reviewed. Errors were carried forward through later literature. The report is arranged by family, following the order of Beckman's Guide.

Taxonomic changes proposed in the report include:

- **Salmonidae:** Coregonidae is reduced to subfamily Coregoninae, and Thymallidae to subfamily Thymallinae. The American grayling should be *Thymallus arcticus*, with *T. signifer* as a synonym.
- **Catostomidae:** The flannelmouth sucker, *[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]* (*Catostomus latipinnis*), may be two species. Coarse-scaled fish (75–90 lateral-line scales) occur with the typical fine-scaled form (95–125) in the Black Canyon of the Gunnison River. The [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*, formerly *P. delphinus*, a synonym of *P. platyrhynchus*) is added to the native list as *P. platyrhynchus*, found in a Yampa tributary.
- **Cyprinidae:** The [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*) are treated as full species, not subspecies. The [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) is added to the native list, though its systematic position is not settled. The Rio Grande chub should be *G. pandorae*.
- **Ictaluridae:** The catfish family becomes Ictaluridae, and *Ameiurus* becomes *Ictalurus*.
- **Serranidae:** The white bass genus *Lepibema* becomes *Roccus*.
- **Cottidae:** The eagle sculpin *Cottus annae* is a synonym of *C. beldingi*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Behnke's comments describe how introductions and hybridization threaten native fishes. Key points:

- **Hybridization:** Introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] have hybridized with native cutthroat trout across much of the West. Stocking of [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] as "native" hatchery stock is also a risk. Behnke states that introducing rainbows or other cutthroats into waters with access to pure native populations will destroy the native strain.
- **Lower Colorado basin:** Behnke, after a May 4, 1967 meeting in Tempe with Minckley and J. Deacon, reports that the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), introduced by bait fishermen, and the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) cause endemic cyprinids and cyprinodonts to disappear within a few years of reservoir creation.
- **Reservoir fish:** [[Humpback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) increased rapidly in new lower Colorado basin lakes, then declined as reproduction failed. The authors call for life history studies.
- **Distribution anomalies:** A distinctive, undescribed cutthroat with pepper-like spots occurs in the upper Snake River, Wyoming and Idaho. Its range is completely surrounded by large-spotted Yellowstone cutthroat. Its origin is unexplained.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy and nomenclature**

- Behnke's 1967 comments and the progress report recommend that many names in Beckman's Guide be revised.
- *Salmo evermanni* is a synonym of *Salmo clarkii henshawi* (Benson and Behnke, 1961).
- *Salmo bouvieri*, an extinct Waha Lake, Idaho cutthroat, is treated as part of *S. clarkii lewisi*.
- The grass pickerel (*Esox vermiculatus*) should be a subspecies of *E. americanus*.
- Subspecies of the central stoneroller should change from *plumbeum* to *pullum*.
- The plains sand shiner should be *Notropis stramineus missuriensis*, since *deliciosus* is a synonym of *N. texanus*, which does not occur in Colorado.
- The Colorado speckled dace should be *Rhinichthys osculus*.
- The fathead minnow does not separate into the two geographic subspecies the names imply, so subspecies should not be used.

**Introductions and records**

- Lake whitefish (*Coregonus clupeaformis*), northern pike (*Esox lucius*), silver salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), California golden trout (*Salmo aguabonita*), and redsided shiner (*Richardsonius balteatus*) are recorded as introduced to Colorado. Atlantic salmon is deleted from the state list.
- The white sucker is well established in west slope Colorado River tributaries and was recorded in the Rio Grande basin.
- Pure Rio Grande cutthroat were found on the Trinchera Ranch, Costilla County, in 1967.
- Specimens of a *Notropis* minnow new to Colorado, possibly a range extension of *N. volucellus*, were taken from an irrigation ditch east of Fort Collins.
- Rio Grande *Pantosteus* records are the only authentic east-slope Colorado records outside the Colorado basin. Behnke notes that *P. platyrhynchus* is expected in the North Platte basin in Colorado.

**Cutthroat trout conservation**

- Pure populations are rare. The Piute trout (*Salmo clarkii seleniris*) was on the verge of extinction in 1964 from hybridization. Its native range was about 4 miles of Silver King Creek headwaters, Alpine County, California. It was propagated and introduced to new sites.
- The Lahontan cutthroat (*S. c. henshawi*) is listed as endangered in the 1965 and 1966 lists. Pure populations are extremely rare, with only four tiny California streams plus Independence Lake and Summit Lake (Nevada) known. It has 4–6 more gillrakers than other subspecies.
- The Humboldt River cutthroat has about 3 fewer gillrakers and 20 fewer scales than typical Lahontan cutthroat, and it holds up well in Elko County headwaters.
- The Apache trout (Arizona) is distinct from cutthroat and rainbow trout and is at least a subspecies. Its chromosome number, 2N=58, matches California golden trout.
- The Montana westslope cutthroat is not a distinct species. Its diploid number is 64, typical of interior cutthroat. The cytological claim that supported separate species status was an error.
- Snake River cutthroat has 2N=64 (interior), not 2N=70 (coastal).
- The [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] was the first cutthroat used in federal propagation, from Twin Lakes, Colorado, in 1890. Those fish were already hybridizing with 1880s rainbow introductions.
- Black Hollow Creek, a Poudre River tributary, held a small population of relatively pure greenbacks. In 1966, 55 adults were moved to a barren stream. In 1967, a barrier dam was built, brook trout were removed with [[Rotenone]], and 24 adult greenbacks were reintroduced.
- Kern River rainbow (*S. gairdnerii gilberti*) may be extinct. Hatchery rainbow and golden trout introductions in Sequoia National Park produced hybrid populations.
- Relict interior cutthroat in the Sacramento system, around Mt. Shasta, are largely hybridized with rainbows, except in remote headwaters.

**Ongoing work and requests**

- A micro-immunoelectrophoresis method is being tested to separate Yellowstone Lake cutthroat from other native forms.
- Specimens of native east-slope cyprinids, Colorado basin *Gila*, *Carpiodes*, coarse-scaled flannelmouth suckers, and *Pantosteus* are requested. Observations on squawfish and humpback sucker life history are also wanted.
- The authors recommend that the peripheral category on the 1966 list be applied consistently. Grayling and Atlantic salmon fit that definition but were listed as rare and endangered.
- The authors note that the blueback (*Salvelinus oquassa*) and Sunapee trout (*S. aureolus*) should be treated as subspecies within the *Salvelinus alpinus* complex.
