# Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Colorado River Basin, 1908–1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, synthesizes literature and field data on the [[Humpback Chub]], an endemic cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]] federally listed as endangered. The report combines a review of "gray" and peer-reviewed literature with an analysis of collections from the Grand Canyon region (1908–1990), focusing on distribution, movements, condition, age and growth, reproduction and population size. Its central message is that the upper-basin populations are recovering under a federal plan, while the lower-basin population, concentrated in the [[Little Colorado River]], lacks a specific recovery plan and faces threats from introduced predators, parasites, and cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

The humpback chub (*Gila cypha* Miller, 1946) is a large minnow with a distinctive dorsal hump, a reduced scale covering, and an inferior mouth. It was the last of the Colorado River "big-river" fishes to be described, alongside the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The holotype was taken near Phantom Ranch in western Grand Canyon. Taxonomic status was contested for decades because of identification difficulties, hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]], and inconsistent common names; the author notes that the species' status "is not resolved to everyone's satisfaction," and that concern persists about genetic swamping by hybridization.

The Colorado River basin is the most species-rich area of endemism among major drainages of the conterminous western United States: 21 of 34 primary and secondary freshwater fishes are endemic. The first listing as federally endangered occurred on 11 March 1967.

Life stages use different habitats: larvae and fry occupy shoreline backwaters in slow water; young-of-the-year use slow to moderate eddies; juveniles use deeper low-velocity eddies and backwaters (<10 m); adults occupy deep eddies and backwaters, including depths above 10 m. Adults are considered sedentary, though exceptions occur.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Five upper-basin populations were identified (Green, Yampa, and Colorado at Black Rocks, Westwater Canyon, and Cataract Canyon). In the lower basin, the species ranges from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Lake Mead]], with the largest population in the [[Little Colorado River]] and occurrences at five other areas of the Grand Canyon corridor. Historically the chub was absent from the Green River above the Yampa confluence after the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure and [[Rotenone]] eradication.

**Diet.** The diet includes aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates and occasionally fish. Adults feed at the surface on emerging mayflies and drifting terrestrial insects.

**Parasites and threats.** Two parasites pose potential threats: the [[Anchorworm]] (*Lernaea* sp.) and the [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), the latter first recorded from the Little Colorado River in May 1990.

**Lower basin habitat.** Flows are bimodal, with winter rains and late-summer monsoons. Canyon reaches alternate rapids with deep pools; eddies and backwaters serve as refugia and nurseries. Tributaries are classified as high-gradient (e.g., [[Bright Angel Creek]], Shinumo, Havasu) or low-gradient (e.g., [[Kanab Creek]], Paria, Little Colorado).

**Introduced fishes.** Non-native species, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Common Carp]], prey on or compete with chub. Channel catfish stomachs contained at least five humpback chubs out of an estimated 200 fish examined, though this was considered an underestimate.

**Dam effects.** Post-dam water temperatures in the Colorado River dropped to a constant ~10 °C, below the 16 °C necessary for successful reproduction, and no eggs or larvae were collected in the 122 km reach from Glen Canyon Dam to the Little Colorado River.

## Key Findings

- **Upper basin status:** Recovery plan in place (USFWS 1990a); goal is five self-sustaining populations. Reproduction and recruitment confirmed in three populations. Taxonomic clarification underway.
- **Lower basin status:** No recovery plan. Population concentrated in Little Colorado River and adjacent mainstream.
- **Movement:** Upper basin fish largely sedentary; Black Rocks tagging recaptured 7% of 219 fish marked in 1980–81, with one fish moving 23 km. Little Colorado River tagging (1984–1990) showed fidelity: 358 individuals recaptured more than once had a mean movement of 6.9 km (range 0–8.7 km). Only seven fish moved between the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers.
- **Age and growth:** Cataract Canyon sample of 23 fish; largest lower-basin chub was nine years old (380 mm TL). Mean annual growth of 19 mm between recaptures (1985–1990, n = 73); mean growth 0.037 mm/day.
- **Condition factors:** Male and female condition ranged 0.64–0.85 (January–April) and 0.42–0.72 (May–December) in the Little Colorado River; Black Rocks chub varied from 0.8 to 1.2.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning not directly observed. Ripe adults collected in the Black Rocks population in June 1980 and May 1981 (water temperatures 11.5–16 °C). Hatching dates back-calculated from a Muth (1990) equation indicated spawning in the Little Colorado River from early February to October.
- **Hatchery propagation:** Black Rocks broodstock produced roughly 18,000 fertilized eggs in May 1980; in 1981, 9 females yielded approximately 30,000 eggs in semi-natural spawning. Hatchery stocks ultimately did not survive as an ex situ population.
- **Population estimate:** Peterson method on 3,878 tagged chubs produced estimates of 5,712–6,747 fish >150 mm TL in the lower 1.5 km of the Little Colorado River (1987–1990), with the author cautioning that the single-census assumptions were violated.
- **Recommendations:** The author recommends a comprehensive lower-basin management plan, rearing facilities (in-canyon ponds and outside-canyon reservoirs), exclusion or removal of trout from Lees Ferry to Lake Mead, a permanent biological station at Phantom Ranch, and biannual multi-agency monitoring of the Little Colorado River population.
