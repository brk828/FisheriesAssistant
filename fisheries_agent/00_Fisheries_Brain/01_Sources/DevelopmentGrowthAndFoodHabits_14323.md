# Development, Growth, and Food Habits of the White Sucker (*Catostomus commersonii*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1926 monograph by Norman Hamilton Stewart, published in the [[Bulletin of the Bureau of Fisheries]] (Vol. XLII, Document 1007), examines the life history of the [[White Sucker]] (*[[Catostomus Commersonii]]*) in the Ithaca, New York region. It covers spawning and egg development, larval and juvenile growth, age determination from scales, and feeding ecology across the species' life stages. Observations were made over roughly a year (June 1922 to August 1923), and the study argues that the sucker's diet shifts with mouth position, making it a significant consumer of stream-bottom invertebrates, particularly [[Chironomid Larvae]].

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The author aimed to document the development and ecological relationships of a single freshwater species, arguing that the interactions of stream organisms can only be understood through detailed life histories of individual species.
- **Study area:** Ithaca and [[Tompkins County]], with observations at [[Beebee Lake]], [[Cayuga Lake]], [[Fall Creek]], [[Cascadilla Creek]], and tributary streams. The region includes cold spring-fed brooks, gravel-bottomed streams, cat-tail marshes, and lake shorelines.
- **Adaptability:** The species is described as remarkably adaptable and generally abundant, making it an important factor in stream environments.
- **Morphology notes:**
  - Eggs are about 2 mm in diameter, telolecithal, and slightly adhesive until water hardened.
  - Spermatozoa measure 24.7 µm in length.
  - Adults have a mouth that shifts from terminal in larvae to inferior in adults, a change that governs diet.
  - Breeding males have "pearl organs" that roughen the sides and can injure females during spawning.
- **Scales:** Cycloid in type. Circuli are concentric ridges whose spacing reflects seasonal growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and temperature**
- In Beebee Lake, spawning was delayed by a cool season. Air temperatures averaged 5.6 °F below normal from April 9 to May 18.
- The peak spawning date was estimated at about May 5, with spawning ending around May 25. The breeding period lasted about 29 days.
- Stream spawning dates varied with water temperature. Upland streams spawned by about May 6 on average, while streams fed by colder [[Cayuga Lake]] water spawned until about June 5. The author attributed this month-long difference to the cold lake water.
- Fish began breaking the surface and leaping when water reached 54 °F (from April 22 onward), a behavior whose significance remains uncertain.

**Spawning behavior**
- Groups of one to five males pursued females over gravel, often in shallow water. Spawning took place in swift water of only a few inches depth in at least one observed case.
- Areas of gravel, 1 to 2 feet across, were swept clean of debris, probably marking egg-laying sites.

**Egg and larval environment**
- Eggs were incubated in a [[Meehan Hatching Jar]] supplied with creek water, approximating natural conditions.
- Fry were reared in a small nursery pond (4 by 6 feet) with seepage, fertilized with manure and dried milk albumen. The pond developed a rich natural fauna and flora.
- Young fry (under 17 mm) avoided still ponds and rapids, preferring intermediate moderate currents near deep, quiet stretches. They were often associated with *Elodea*.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction and Egg Development
- **Ripe fish timing:** Ripe males were taken as early as March 23, and a male from February 7 yielded spermatozoa that were active when placed in water. No ripe females were found before April 27.
- **Egg characteristics:** Eggs are 2 mm in diameter when laid and become about 3 mm after [[Water Hardening]] (within 30 minutes). The egg membrane is about 15 µm thick.
- **Fecundity:**
  - A 12-inch female yielded 150 cc (165 g) of eggs, estimated at 18,000 eggs using von Bayer's tables.
  - A 15-inch female contained 31,200 eggs.
  - Individual eggs weigh 6 to 10 mg.
- **Fertilization:** Over 90 percent of eggs were fertilized in laboratory trials. The author could not confirm whether fertilization occurred before or after water was added.
- **Sperm motility:** Sperm activity lasted 40 s in tap water, 45 s in goldfish aquarium water and melted snow, and 5 s in human saliva. Sperm from a fish dead for one hour were nearly as active, but those from a fish dead four hours were inactive.
- **Incubation:** Eggs hatched in 21 days at a mean water temperature of 50.5 °F. The author estimated 18 to 20 days as normal under natural conditions. About 5 percent hatched after 12 days.

### Embryonic and Larval Development
- **Cleavage:** Development was described from the one-celled stage (2.5 hours) through blastoderm investment (92 hours). The yolk was fully invested by 92 hours.
- **Embryo:** The embryo body was visible at 149 hours. Pigment appeared in the eyes by 246 hours, when 5 percent of eggs hatched.
- **Newly hatched larva:** About 8 mm long, with a cylindrical yolk sac and an unopened mouth. Pectoral fins were present at hatching.
- **Mouth opening:** The mouth opened at about 9 mm, and food was first taken at about 12 mm.
- **Pigmentation:** Chromatophores appeared progressively on the dorsal, caudal, pectoral, and other fins between 12 and 20 mm. Pigment followed the scale outlines by 25 mm.
- **Fin development:** The dorsal fin was defined by 9 mm. Ventral fins appeared at 15 mm.

### The Critical Period and Mouth Migration
- **Mouth descent:** The mouth moved from a terminal position to an inferior one. It was level with the lower rim of the eyes at 14 mm and fully below that level by 16 mm.
- **Critical period:** Between 16 and 18 mm, the fish shifted from feeding at the surface to feeding on the bottom. Stewart called it the critical period because it coincided with major changes in gut structure and feeding.
- **Intestinal change:** The first intestinal loop formed at about 17 mm. The gut was straight up to that size. By 24 mm the digestive tract was nearly twice body length, and in adults (6 to 12 inches) it was about three times body length.
- **Swim bladder:** At 14 mm the swim bladder occupied twice the intestine's cross-sectional area. The author hypothesized this might drive the top-swimming habit under 17 mm.
- **Absence of stomach:** The adult digestive tract has no true stomach, and no glands other than the liver and pancreas.

### Age Determination and Growth
- **Scale formation:** Scales first appeared at about 22 mm (around 40 days from hatching). By autumn of the first year, scales averaged about 10 circuli.
- **Annual zones:** Each annual growth zone holds a band of widely spaced circuli followed by a band of crowded ones. Fingerlings of 1¼ inches had a single zone, confirming it as an annual mark.
- **Circuli counts:**
  - Two-year fish (60 to 80 mm): averaged 19 circuli.
  - Three-year fish (90 to 120 mm): 28 to 32 circuli.
  - Suckers aged 4 to 5 years averaged about 10 circuli per zone.
- **Seasonal irregularities:** Early spring circuli (February to March) were irregular, sinuous, or branched. Scale growth was nearly at a standstill from November to late February.
- **Average lengths by age:**

| Age | Average length (inches) |
|---|---|
| First season | 1½ |
| Second | 3 |
| Third | 4 |
| Fourth | 4½–6 |
| Fifth | 6–8 |
| Sixth | 8–13½ |
| Seventh | 13½–15½ |

- The author noted rapid growth in years six and seven as surprising and recommended tagging studies to verify it. The species is known to reach 22 inches.
- Suckers from a large spring with stable cold temperatures showed virtually no seasonal scale variation.

### Feeding Habits by Life Stage
Stewart identified five feeding periods:

1. **Yolk-food period:** From hatching (8 mm) until the yolk is absorbed, at about 9 to 12 mm.
2. **Top-feeding period:** From first food (12 mm) to the mouth shift (16 to 17 mm). Fry fed on floating organisms, mainly [[Chironomid Larvae]], [[Entomostraca]], diatoms, rotifers, and protozoa.
3. **Critical period:** A brief transitional phase of about nine days (16 to 18 mm), during which the fish began feeding on the bottom.
4. **Fingerling period:** From about 17 or 18 mm to about 75 mm. Fingerlings fed on bottom organisms but ingested large amounts of sand (about 21 to 25 percent of intestinal contents). Fish of the first summer formed schools of a dozen to several hundred.
5. **Adult period:** Fish over 75 mm (3 inches). Sand fell to 0.9 percent of diet, and insects predominated.

**Diet composition**
- **Top-feeding fry (12 to 16 mm), streams:** Chironomids 62%, Entomostraca 4%, diatoms 8%, protozoa 6%, rotifers 7%, arthropod fragments 7%.
- **Adults (52 specimens):** Chironomids 30%, large insect larvae and nymphs 31%, caddis-fly larvae 13%, Odonata 9%, mayfly nymphs 9%. Total insects 66.4%, plants 6%.
- **Averaged across 162 fish:** Chironomids 32.9%, with mollusks, caddis flies, and other insects contributing smaller shares.

**Feeding rates**
- A 5-inch sucker contained about 1,200 chironomids, plus 800 caddis-fly larvae and 60 mayfly nymphs. At that rate, about 36,000 chironomids would be consumed per month.

**Starvation test**
- Suckers of 22 to 25 mm confined in a screened tray in their habitat died within eight days. Dace placed alongside them survived on floating particles. Dissections showed nearly empty guts.

### Economic Status and Health
- **Competition:** The sucker is an insectivorous rival of [[Trout]] and [[Bass]], which are fish of commercial and recreational importance.
- **Fish eggs:** Over 200 sucker stomachs were examined, and none contained fish eggs. The author concluded that eggs are rarely taken, despite earlier reports of "spawn eating."
- **Food value:** The flesh is firm and pleasant in spring but soft and undesirable by June. The species is considered of little food value.
- **Parasites:** One 8-inch specimen contained 145 cestodes and 50 trematodes, with no other gut contents.

### Literature Review
- Previous studies of sucker diet by Forbes (1890), Hankinson (1908), Reighard (1915, 1920), Baker (1916), and Pearse (1918) reported samples of 5 to 42 fish, often lacking size, locality, or seasonal detail.
- Reported mollusk percentages varied from 0 to 42 percent, and chironomid percentages from 3 to 40 percent. Stewart attributed these discrepancies to differences in place, age, and season.
