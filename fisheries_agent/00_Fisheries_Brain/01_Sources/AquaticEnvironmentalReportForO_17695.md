# Aquatic Environmental Report for Morgan Lake, New Mexico (1976)

#ichthyology #fisheries #thermal-ecology #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae #reservoir-management

## Executive Summary

This annual report documents aquatic conditions in [[Morgan Lake]], New Mexico from June through December 1976, focusing on physico-chemical parameters, plankton populations, and fish communities. [[Morgan Lake]] is a cooling water reservoir for the Four Corners Electric Generating Plant, formed in 1963 with a capacity of 39,000 acre-feet and mean depth of 15 meters. The study was designed to monitor conditions before, during, and after an anticipated fish kill based on previous mortalities in 1973 and 1974. No major fish kill occurred in 1976, attributed to brief stratification periods (mid-July to mid-August) and the dominance of highly tolerant species ([[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]]). The fishery has undergone dramatic simplification from a diverse gamefish assemblage to one dominated by two hardy nongame species. Water temperatures reached 31°C in the lake and 45°C in the canal, with severe oxygen depletion below the thermocline during stratification.

## Background & Description

[[Morgan Lake]] was created in 1963 at 1625m elevation to provide cooling water for power generation. The system has experienced periodic thermal and chemical stress leading to changes in biotic communities. Historical fish populations (1961-1973) included [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Tilapia]], [[Bluegill]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Northern Pike]], and various other species through stocking or natural colonization. Major fish kills in summers 1973 and 1974 were attributed to high temperature-low oxygen conditions, drastically reducing species diversity.

The 1976 study employed monthly sampling at two stations: one in the hot canal 140m upstream from the settling pond, and one mid-lake between towers 3 and 6. Physico-chemical measurements followed standard APHA methods, including temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity, nutrients (PO₄, NO₃, Si), and major ions. Phytoplankton and zooplankton were sampled using Kemmerer bottles and plankton nets respectively. Fish were collected using experimental gill nets (21.3 x 1.8m, four mesh sizes) set at surface, thermocline, and bottom depths, checked at dawn, noon, and dusk. Stomach contents, length-weight relationships, condition factors (K), and gonadosomatic indices were determined for all fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Morgan Lake]] system exhibits extreme thermal conditions due to its function as a cooling reservoir. Maximum summer surface temperature of 31°C (lake) and 45°C (canal) occurred on August 3, 1976. Thermal stratification developed between 10-12m by mid-July, creating a sharp thermocline that persisted until mid-August when periodic mixing began. By October 1, the water column was fully mixed with uniform temperature profiles.

Dissolved oxygen conditions were severely depressed during stratification. By July 7, oxygen saturation dropped below 1% below 12m, creating anoxic conditions in the hypolimnion. During peak stratification (August 3), negligible dissolved oxygen occurred below 14m. The brief stratification period (mid-July to mid-August) was interrupted by mild regional weather and periodic plant shutdowns, allowing wind-driven mixing. By late September, oxygen was redistributed throughout the water column with 52% saturation at 20m depth.

Nutrient concentrations (PO₄, NO₃, Si) displayed inverse relationships with phytoplankton density. Silica increased as temperature-sensitive diatom populations declined. pH remained relatively constant (8.1-8.5 at surface) with slight depression in the hypolimnion during decomposition periods. Secchi disc transparency remained low throughout summer (0.6-2.2m), decreasing with increases in blue-green algae and fly ash. Total dissolved solids were approximately four times higher in late August than June, suggesting material release from sediments during turnover.

Phytoplankton populations were slightly lower in 1976 than 1975 but showed similar seasonal trends. Blue-green algae dominated late summer, with [[Aphanizomenon ovalisporum]], [[Aphanocapsa delicatissima]], and [[Lyngbya]] sp. comprising the greatest proportion. Peak densities exceeded 90,000 cells ml⁻¹ in October. Diatoms ([[Fragilaria crotonensis]]) were present only in moderate numbers in June and absent or restricted to bottom waters thereafter. Green algae ([[Scenedesmus bijuga]], [[Coelastrum microporum]], [[Franceia ovalis]], [[Oocystis pusilla]]) dominated June-July before declining. Blue-green algae densities were consistently higher in the hot canal than the lake, documenting their warm-tolerant nature. An unidentified [[Lyngbya]] species emerged in October (74,000 cells ml⁻¹ in canal, 64,000 in lake) and warrants monitoring as a potential bloom species.

Zooplankton populations peaked in June at 97.5 organisms liter⁻¹ then declined sharply through summer. By early July, over 80% of zooplankton were restricted above the thermocline due to oxygen depletion below. Mean populations decreased from 58.8 to 13.5 organisms liter⁻¹ above the thermocline and from 38.7 to 3.7 organisms liter⁻¹ below during the sampling period. Cladocerans were the most abundant group throughout most of summer. [[Daphnia]] spp. dominated in June (15.2 organisms liter⁻¹), replaced by [[Ceriodaphnia]] spp. in July (17.5 organisms liter⁻¹), then [[Diaphanosoma brachyurum]] toward summer's end. By August 3, total zooplankton dropped to 50 organisms liter⁻¹. Immature copepods were fairly abundant by late August (up to 16.5 organisms liter⁻¹). Adult copepods ([[Cyclops]] spp., [[Diaptomus]] sp.) ranged from 12.6 organisms liter⁻¹ (July) to 2.1 organisms liter⁻¹ (October). Rotifers occurred sporadically in low numbers.

## Key Findings

**Species Composition and Fishery Status**: The 1976 fish community consisted of four species: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, dominant), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*, subdominant), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Chaenobryttus cyanellus*, rare), and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*, rare). This represents dramatic simplification from the diverse assemblage present 1961-1973. Total collections: 192 carp, 69 channel catfish, 3 green sunfish, 3 mountain suckers. The two dominant species are among the most tolerant fish known to high temperature and low oxygen conditions.

**Food Habits and Nutritional Status**: Both [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]] were omnivorous but exhibited poor feeding. In June, 80% of channel catfish contained diatoms and green algae, with [[Oscillatoria]] spp. (95.2%), [[Daphnia]] (57.1%), and [[Diaptomus]] (19%) present. By August, food diversity decreased markedly. Carp consumed primarily fragmented crustacea remains (51.5% in June), chlorophytes (22.6%), and macrophytes (13.6%). Fish scales occurred frequently in stomachs of both species—a poor nutrient source and not a preferred food item, indicating nutritional stress. Stomach fullness was critically low: most fish had stomachs <5% full after June, with many at 1% or less. By late August, 26.9% of channel catfish had empty stomachs. The absence of substantial food and occurrence of non-nutritive items (scales) indicates an inadequate food base.

**Length-Weight Relationships and Condition**: Length-weight analysis revealed abnormal growth patterns. The constant N (normally ~3.0 for isometric growth) fluctuated monthly, indicating fish increased in length versus weight during stress periods. For channel catfish, N dropped from 2.66 (June) to 1.80 (first August trip) before rising to 3.76 (late August). Overall population N was 3.16 (within normal range 2.96-3.52), but monthly variation was extreme. Carp showed similar patterns with overall N of 1.67, far below normal ranges (3.45-3.52). These fluctuations occurred during increasing water temperature and declining food availability.

Condition factors (K) for channel catfish averaged 0.7-0.8 throughout the study (normal range 0.5-1.2), placing them at the lower end of acceptable values. Channel catfish appeared emaciated by late August. Carp condition factors averaged 1.2 overall (normal range 1.0-3.0), lower than populations in Iowa, Utah, and three other New Mexico lakes (Caballo, Conchas, Navajo), matching only [[Elephant Butte Lake]]. No sex differences in condition were observed. Mean channel catfish total length was 269mm (range 105-412mm), weight 162g (range 10-400g). Mean carp length was 293mm (range 206-560mm), weight 304g (range 114-1544g).

**Vertical Distribution and Behavior**: Fish distribution was determined primarily by temperature, dissolved oxygen, and species behavior patterns. Despite adverse conditions, most fish were collected in overnight bottom sets regardless of temperature and oxygen levels, reflecting the bottom-dwelling, crepuscular nature of both species. In June, 53 fish were taken from bottom nets, 40 from thermocline, demonstrating distribution throughout the water column before severe stratification. By July-August, fish concentrated in bottom nets despite oxygen <2.0 ppm, with 22-24 fish collected. During the second August trip, an unusual distribution occurred: 50 fish at surface, 22 at thermocline, likely due to strong winds forcing fish from littoral areas and noxious materials released during turnover (TDS four times higher than June).

Size segregation was evident: larger fish (carp ca. 600mm) were observed in littoral areas and seining collections, while mid-lake gill nets captured smaller individuals. This may reflect higher oxygen consumption with increased size and temperature, making it more efficient for larger fish to remain in littoral zones with more diverse feeding substrates. Feeding periodicity showed channel catfish most active overnight (consistent with crepuscular behavior), with reduced activity during morning and afternoon. Carp fed primarily overnight and more in afternoon than morning.

**Reproductive Condition**: Reproductive data were limited. Gonadosomatic indices for carp declined from June (males 2.8, females 4.5) through October (both sexes 0.5), indicating spawning had largely occurred before sampling began. Gravid channel catfish were collected only in June. The reproductive season appears prolonged due to elevated water temperatures, with most activity preceding June collections.

**Absence of Fish Kill**: No major fish kill occurred in 1976 despite conditions (high temperature, low oxygen, stratification) that caused mortalities in 1973-1974. This is attributed to: (1) brief stratification period (mid-July to mid-August only) due to mild weather and periodic plant shutdowns allowing wind mixing; (2) species composition limited to two highly tolerant species. Previous gamefish ([[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Tilapia]]) are far less tolerant to low oxygen and high temperature. The current assemblage of [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]] represents a "survivor" community capable of withstanding extreme conditions that would kill less tolerant species.

**Management Recommendations**: The report recommends: (1) Install subsurface bubbling system in spring 1977 to prevent stratification; (2) Monitor benthic populations before and during bubbling system operation; (3) Continue monitoring program to assess bubbling system effects; (4) Introduce [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) at low levels (~1000 fish) to establish forage base, with mid-winter sampling to evaluate overwintering tolerance; (5) If threadfin shad succeed, reintroduce [[Largemouth Bass]] to complement channel catfish; (6) Implement carp control through commercial fishing; (7) Conduct similar fish sampling plus littoral zone gill netting.

**Comparative Context**: The 1976 conditions were similar to 1975 in major trends but with slightly less severe oxygen depletion and shorter stratification. Phytoplankton and zooplankton patterns paralleled 1975. The dramatic shift from a diverse gamefish assemblage (1961-1973) to a depauperate tolerant-species community represents a case study in thermal pollution effects on reservoir fisheries. [[Morgan Lake]] demonstrates how extreme thermal regimes can filter fish communities to only the hardiest species, with cascading effects on food webs and ecosystem function.
