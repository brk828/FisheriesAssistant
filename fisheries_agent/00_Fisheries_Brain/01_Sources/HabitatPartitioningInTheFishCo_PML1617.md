# Habitat partitioning in the fish community of a southwestern river

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[William J. Matthews]] and [[Loren G. Hinz]] (Biological Station and Department of Zoology, University of Oklahoma) quantifies microhabitat use by the four numerically dominant fishes of the [[South Canadian River]] during one annual wet-dry cycle (May 1976 to January 1977). The dominant species were [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (Red Shiner), [[Notropis Girardi]] (Arkansas River Shiner), [[Hybognathus Placitus]] (Plains Minnow) and [[Gambusia Affinis]] (Mosquitofish). The authors found that habitat partitioning was transitory and weakly structured: species used wide habitat under mild conditions and contracted into narrow refugia when physico-chemical conditions became stressful. No species pair consistently overlapped strongly, and the fish assemblage was characterized by ecological plasticity rather than stable partitioning.

## Background & Description

The South Canadian River is wide and shallow, with seasonal flow that reduces it to pools during most summers. Its water chemistry (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, total dissolved solids) fluctuates widely and can limit fish distribution. Earlier partitioning studies, including [[Moyle 1973]], [[Mendelson 1975]], [[Werner et al. 1977]] and [[Hall and Werner 1977]], described microhabitat segregation in relatively stable environments. [[Zaret and Rand 1971]] studied a fluctuating tropical system without quantitative microhabitat data, and [[Harrell 1978]] documented habitat shifts after a flood on the Devil's River, Texas. The authors state that no earlier study had quantified microhabitat partitioning in an environmentally unstable southwestern river.

The study asked four questions:

1. Did dominant species use microhabitats that differed physico-chemically?
2. Was microhabitat partitioning consistent despite environmental fluctuation?
3. Were species associations permanent?
4. Were there habitat-use patterns of the whole assemblage with adaptive significance?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The site was a 600 m segment of the South Canadian River near Norman, Oklahoma, with steep banks and dense vegetation, plus lower [[Pond Creek]] (about 10 m wide, no deeper than 1 m, with algal mats and no permanent riffles). The river was easily seined because few snags were present.

**Study periods and conditions.** Each period lasted 2 days.

- **May 1976:** Mild conditions. River volume was the highest of any period, and no factor approached stressful levels. Pond Creek was flowing.
- **August 1976:** The river was a series of pools connected by channels as narrow as 3 m, with pool depths of 0.3 to 1 m. Temperatures reached 37 °C, close to the critical thermal maximum for several species, and oxygen and pH approached stressful values. Upper Pond Creek was dry.
- **October 1976:** Low water persisted after a dry August–October. Backwater pH was 8.2–8.5, while mainstream pH reached 8.7–9.5, near the tolerance limit of N. lutrensis. Backwater daily temperature fluctuation was 1–2 °C, versus up to 9 °C in the mainstream.
- **December 1976–January 1977:** Rains restored water levels near May levels, Pond Creek flowed again, and air temperatures were usually cold.

**Physico-chemical ranges (Table 1).**

| Variable | May 1976 | Aug 1976 | Oct 1976 | Dec 1976–Jan 1977 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 11.5–30.0 | 21.0–37.0 | 7.5–19.5 | 0.0–12.0 |
| Oxygen (ppm) | 4.1–17.2 | 3.3–12.9 | 6.6–19.0 | 10.0–24.0 |
| pH | 7.5–8.7 | 8.0–9.6 | 8.2–9.5 | 7.7–8.4 |
| TDS (ppm) | 470–1160 | 550–700 | 500–680 | 430–700 |

Turbidity, current speed and depth ranges were also reported.

**Methods.** Sampling used a 1.22 × 3.05 m seine of 0.32 cm bar mesh, with a "best effort" downstream haul of 5 m at each point. Twenty-five points were selected by random pacing per subsampling period, and the actual totals were 100, 89, 97 and 98 points across the four periods. Ten physico-chemical variables were measured at each point. Principal components analysis (PCA) ordinated the points, and species were plotted on the resulting axes. Habitat breadth used the Levins-type index (range 1.0–4.0 with four habitat quadrants), and overlap used the [[Pianka Overlap Index]] (range 0.0–1.0).

## Key Findings

**Dominant species and abundance.** The four species made up nearly all specimens collected:

- N. lutrensis: 38,624
- N. girardi: 18,475
- H. placitus: 5,113
- G. affinis: 4,437

Larger fishes (centrarchids, etc.) were few, and other small species were rare. Adults and large juveniles were analysed together, and young-of-year were excluded. Table 2 lists about 20 species by period, with minor taxa such as *Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Cyprinus carpio* and several *Lepomis* species in small numbers.

**Habitat distribution.** Each species was heterogeneously distributed among habitat quadrants in every period (G-tests, p < 0.005). Each species' distribution also differed significantly from the other three, except that N. girardi and H. placitus did not differ in August.

**Habitat breadth (Table 5).**

|---|---|---|---|---|
| N. lutrensis | 3.05 | 2.43 | 1.15 | 1.59 |
| N. girardi | 1.86 | 2.85 | 1.94 | 1.98 |
| H. placitus | 2.41 | 2.90 | 1.99 | — |
| G. affinis | 3.03 | 2.35 | 1.57 | 2.53 |
| Mean | 2.51 | 2.63 | 1.66 | 2.03 |
| Overall (lumped) | 3.15 | 2.67 | 1.61 | 2.33 |

- N. lutrensis had the widest breadth in May and the narrowest in October, confined almost entirely to backwater pools.
- N. girardi was narrow in May, when it occupied the main channel, widened in August, and was in deeper, non-flowing pools in December.
- G. affinis was broad in May, moderate in August, narrow in October, and broader again in December.
- Breadth did not correlate with abundance (product-moment correlation).
- Mean breadth changed significantly among periods (ANOVA, p < 0.05). The largest change was from August to October (t-test, p < 0.01).

The text gives some values (for example, H. placitus in May, B = 2.11, and mean August breadth, 2.62) that differ slightly from Table 5.

| N. lutrensis – H. placitus | 0.836 | 0.619 | 0.673 | — |
| N. lutrensis – G. affinis | 0.913 | 0.446 | 0.987 | 0.937 |
| N. girardi – H. placitus | 0.262 | 0.986 | 0.943 | — |
| N. girardi – G. affinis | 0.333 | 0.978 | 0.723 | 0.755 |
| H. placitus – G. affinis | 0.958 | 0.963 | 0.774 | — |
| Mean | 0.622 | 0.768 | 0.830 | 0.740 |

- No species pair consistently overlapped highly. The authors read this as the absence of close-knit microhabitat guilds.
- N. lutrensis and G. affinis were the only pair with high overlap in three of four periods, though overlap fell to 0.446 in August.
- Overlap between N. lutrensis and the other cyprinids never reached 0.900.
- Mean overlap was lowest in May and highest in October, when N. lutrensis, N. girardi and G. affinis crowded into backwaters even though space was available.
- Seasonal differences in mean overlap were not statistically significant because of high within-season variance.

**Single-variable separation.** No consistent separation of species occurred across subranges of any single physico-chemical variable (Kruskal-Wallis tests, p > 0.10).

**Ordination.** The first three principal components explained 61.18, 61.17, 61.04 and 55.34% of total variance across the four periods. The two-dimensional ordinations explained 47.86, 47.36, 47.47 and 38.36%.

**Overwintering.** Deeper-pool use by Notropis in October and December–January did not indicate a consistent overwintering site, since the pools differed between the two periods. In January 1976, 11,249 N. lutrensis had been collected from a wide variety of microhabitats.

**Interpretation and conclusions.**

- Under mild conditions, the assemblage dispersed and segregated. Under harsh conditions, it converged on refugia such as backwaters with lower pH and more stable temperatures.
- The authors attribute overlap to physico-chemical limiting factors more than to biotic interactions.
- Wide microhabitat use may aid trophic diversification and continual sampling of habitat quality, but it is advantageous only while stress stays low. N. lutrensis combined broad use in benign conditions with specialization in stressful ones, which may explain its success.
- Inger and Colwell (1977) are cited for the idea that unpredictable environments prevent guild formation.
- Competition could intensify as food dwindles in late autumn, combined with physico-chemical stress, possibly contributing to the decline observed between October and December.
- Partitioning appears far less structured here than in stable systems. The authors conclude that partitioning may matter in stable environments but is of lesser importance in the South Canadian River.
- Two survival strategies are proposed for Great Plains fishes: (1) broad tolerance and fixed location, or (2) selective responses and frequent relocation. The authors suggest the most successful species combine both.

**Cross-references.** [[Matthews and Hill 1979]] and [[Matthews and Maness]] (in press) provide the methodological base and the thermal and oxygen tolerance data that this study draws on.
