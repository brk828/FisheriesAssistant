# Effect of Dissolved Oxygen Tension on the Toxicity of Un-ionized Ammonia to Several Fish Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by [[J. C. Merkens]] and [[K. M. Downing]], published in the *Annals of Applied Biology* (Vol. 45, No. 3, pp. 521-527), examines how dissolved oxygen (DO) tension modifies the lethal effect of [[Un-Ionized Ammonia]] on four freshwater fish species: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[European Perch]], [[Roach]], and [[Gudgeon]]. Laboratory bioassays in [[Static Bioassay]] aquaria tested rapidly lethal concentrations (about 2.0 to 8.8 p.p.m. N) and low, sub-lethal-for-trout concentrations over periods from hours to 13 days. The authors found that lower oxygen tension increased ammonia toxicity in all species except gudgeon, and that a low ammonia concentration significantly reduced the oxygen resistance of rainbow trout but not of perch or roach.

## Background & Description

Ammonia occurs in many wastewaters, including [[Domestic Sewage]] effluents and [[Coke Oven Effluent]]. Ammonium compounds are more toxic to fish in alkaline solutions because a larger share of total ammonia exists as the more toxic un-ionized form at higher pH. Earlier work by [[Downing and Merkens 1955]] showed that reduced DO shortens survival of rainbow trout exposed to relatively high un-ionized ammonia concentrations. The present study extends this to coarse fish and to the lower concentrations likely in polluted streams.

Methods included:
- **Test species and sizes:** rainbow trout (length 8.0 ± 0.9 cm in Exp. 1; 12.2 ± 1.0 cm in Exp. 2 and 3 for trout, roach and perch, with gudgeon at 10.0 ± 0.3 cm in Exp. 2); roach, perch and gudgeon at similar sizes.
- **Test vessels:** aquaria of 40 to 150 litres, holding 7 to 10 fish per test.
- **Water:** borehole and dechlorinated tap water; unchlorinated bore-hole water in Exp. 1.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** measured by [[Winkler Method]]; percentage saturation calculated from [[Truesdale Oxygen Solubility Data]].
- **Endpoint:** death defined as loss of equilibrium and no respiratory movement for 15 seconds.
- **Concentration basis:** equivalent ammonium chloride and un-ionized ammonia values were calculated for average pH and temperature, with about 489 p.p.m. dissolved solids accounted for.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work addresses effluent management for fisheries. The authors stress that pH strongly determines whether an ammonia-containing effluent will harm a fishery, because pH governs the un-ionized fraction. Dissolved oxygen is a second modifier: low DO intensifies ammonia toxicity, so polluted, oxygen-depleted streams pose combined risks. Species responses differed, with gudgeon less affected by oxygen level and perch and roach showing no significant ammonia effect on oxygen resistance. The study is freshwater and laboratory-based; no marine data are reported.

## Key Findings

- **Survival and concentration:** survival of all species declined as un-ionized ammonia concentration rose.
- **Oxygen effect:** reduced DO increased ammonia toxicity in rainbow trout, perch and roach. Gudgeon showed no significant difference in survival between the two oxygen levels.
- **Greatest oxygen effect at low ammonia:** the effect of oxygen tension on survival was largest at the lowest ammonia concentrations.
- **Experiment 1 (trout, 20.1 °C, pH about 8.0):** survival periods from 2 hr to 13 days were recorded at 45.7% and 100.3% air saturation. Mortality curves were fitted to log concentration, and [[Table 2]] gives concentrations separating complete survival from complete mortality at each time point.
- **Experiment 2 (four species, 15.2 °C, pH about 7.9):** tested at 53.4% and 96.7% air saturation over high un-ionized ammonia concentrations, with median survival plotted for each species.
- **Experiment 3 (trout, perch, roach at low ammonia):** the presence of un-ionized ammonia significantly reduced survival of rainbow trout under low oxygen. It had no significant effect on perch or roach. A slight reduction in roach resistance was significant only at the 20% probability level.
- **Discussion:** pH is a key controlling factor for effluent harm. Toxicity data at different pH values for a given un-ionized concentration were not yet available.
