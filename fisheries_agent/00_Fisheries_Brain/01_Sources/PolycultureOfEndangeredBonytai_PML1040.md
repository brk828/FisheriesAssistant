# Polyculture of Endangered Bonytails and Razorback Suckers in Recirculated Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether co-culturing endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in a [[Recirculating Aquaculture System]] could reduce labor and affect fish performance. The experiment ran for 120 days (26 June to 24 October 2002) at the [[Mora National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center in New Mexico. Razorback suckers reduced the frequency of bottom siphoning needed to clean tanks, without increasing standpipe screen cleaning. Bonytail size-class and razorback density did not significantly change bonytail or razorback growth, except as reported below. Bonytail survival was high overall, but was significantly lower in the small bonytail, low razorback density treatment. The authors conclude that polyculture is feasible and that the work is a first step toward defining its requirements.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Both species are federally listed as endangered in the [[Colorado River Basin]] of the southwestern United States. Their decline is attributed to habitat loss and degradation.
- **Recovery context:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) and partners have recovery plans that include captive propagation and stocking augmentation. Growing captive stocks and water conservation goals have increased interest in husbandry practices for water reuse systems.
- **Feeding ecology:** Bonytails shift from predominantly benthic foraging as juveniles to midwater and surface feeding as adults, feeding mainly on aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, plant matter and algae. Razorback suckers shift from midwater plankton feeding as larvae and juveniles to bottom feeding, and adults feed mainly on detritus, algae and invertebrates.
- **Rationale for polyculture:** Polyculture, the rearing of multiple species together, can improve fish production and environmental outcomes in ponds. It is less common in recirculating systems. Bonytails are sometimes cultured with razorback suckers because of the latter's perceived ability to resuspend and reprocess solids that settle on tank bottoms. Before this study, no published work had tested this effect for these two species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Facility:** Tanks were 432 L, supplied with aeration and filtered reuse water at about 7.5 L/min (approximately 25 exchanges/day). Treatment components included a rotating drum microscreen filter, a propeller-wash bead filter for ammonia nitrification, and a boiler-assisted heat exchanger for temperature control. Lighting followed a natural photoperiod of 14.5 h light in late June, reduced to 9 h light by late October.
- **Water temperature:** Temperatures were held within a range of 21.9–22.2 °C as an energy-saving measure, because well water at the site was cold. The authors considered this suboptimal for growth.
- **Water quality:** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, total ammonia nitrogen and nitrite were monitored. Nitrite was described as a potential hazard in such systems, since unremoved solids break down into ammonia and nitrite.
- **Waste dynamics:** In polyculture tanks, a positively buoyant flocculent material resembling fish waste collected at the water surface around standpipes. The authors interpreted this as the product of razorback sucker waste reprocessing and resuspension.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- **Design:** A 3 × 2 factorial design of three razorback sucker densities (0, 2 and 5 fish/tank) and two bonytail size-classes, with 24 tanks and four replicate tanks per treatment.
- **Bonytails:** 240 captive-reared juveniles from the 2000 year-class. Small size-class averaged 17.6 ± 0.5 g and 127.2 ± 1.1 mm TL. Large size-class averaged 43.9 ± 1.2 g and 166.9 ± 1.7 mm TL.
- **Razorback suckers:** 56 captive-reared fish averaging 285.4 ± 3.4 mm TL.
- **Feed:** Commercial sinking shrimp-production feed (40% protein, 15% lipid), hand-fed four times daily.
- **Response variables:** Growth (relative and specific growth rates, RGR and SGR), size, survival, tank cleaning frequency and water quality.

**Growth and size**
- Small bonytails had significantly higher RGR (0.90%) than large bonytails (0.31%). SGR followed the same pattern.
- By day 120, mean TL and weight remained greater for large bonytails (191.0 ± 2.5 mm; 59.5 ± 2.4 g) than for small bonytails (163.3 ± 2.5 mm; 31.0 ± 2.2 g). Within each size-class, bonytail size did not differ among razorback densities.
- Razorback sucker growth was significantly greater at low density (RGR 0.22 ± 0.03%; SGR 0.26 ± 0.04%) than at high density (RGR 0.11 ± 0.03%; SGR 0.13 ± 0.0x%).
- Razorback final weight was greatest in the low-density, large-bonytail tanks (433.5 ± 51.6 g), and lower in high-density, large-bonytail (316.2 ± 17.8 g) and high-density, small-bonytail (295.0 ± 16.2 g) tanks.
- Bonytail size variation was significantly lower in polyculture for small bonytails (weight variation 19.1 g in polyculture vs 29.0 g in monoculture).

**Survival**
- Razorback suckers had 100% survival, with no mortalities during the 120 days.
- Bonytail survival in the small bonytail, low razorback density treatment was significantly lower than in the other five treatments, with a suggested interaction (P = 0.0435). Survival differences among the other treatments were under 3%.
- Mean bonytail survival in a previous monoculture study under similar conditions was 94% (range 94–97%).

**Tank cleaning labor**
- Polyculture tanks needed significantly less bottom siphoning than bonytail-only tanks, despite higher biomass and waste production.
- Frequency of standpipe screen cleaning did not differ between monoculture and polyculture.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- Razorback suckers reduced manual solids removal by resuspending and reprocessing settled waste, which was then removed through the system. The authors suggest this is the mechanism for the labor reduction.
- Neither species' size or growth was negatively affected by co-culture, although bonytail growth was generally low, probably because of suboptimal temperatures.
- Future work should quantify removed waste directly (by collecting, drying and weighing it) to confirm the labor finding.
- Further research is needed to determine the minimum bonytail size suitable for polyculture and to adjust razorback density to protect small bonytail survival.
- The authors conclude that the lack of increased growth should not be viewed as a negative result, and that the study supports the feasibility of bonytail–razorback sucker polyculture as a first step toward developing its requirements.
