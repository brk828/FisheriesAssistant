# Proposal to Develop New Aquatic Invasive Species Control Technologies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This proposal, prepared by [[Robert W. Clarkson]] (Bureau of Reclamation, Phoenix Area Office) and [[Shaula Hedwall]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Ecological Services), argues that non-native aquatic species are the most important obstacle to conserving native fishes and aquatic herpetofauna of the western United States, particularly the [[Colorado River]] system. The authors call for new, more selective control tools and urge national leadership and rapid action. Two development pathways are costed: taxon-specific [[Piscicide]] development (including registration of [[Supaverm]]) and [[Genetic Biocontrol]] using transgenic or ploidy-manipulation methods.

## Background & Description

Many native fishes and aquatic herptiles of the Colorado River system are imperiled, and several are federally listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Habitat degradation, water development and invasion by non-native fishes, crayfish and bullfrogs are the primary causes. Non-native species are described as the single most important deterrent to native fish conservation, and existing control options (chemical or mechanical removal, barriers, and theoretical methods) are limited and often non-selective.

The document contains two technical sections:

- **Taxon-Specific Pesticide Development:** Background on existing piscicides ([[Rotenone]] and [[Antimycin-A]]), their non-target lethality, and the steps for registering new selective compounds.
- **Genetic Biocontrol Development:** Background on transgenic and chromosome-set (ploidy) manipulation approaches, with development steps, timelines and costs.

The authors also note that the proposal draws on [[Dawson and Kolar 2003]] and [[Kapuscinski and Patronski 2005]], and that partnerships with agencies and organizations such as the [[Western Native Trout Initiative]] would be valuable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Rotenone and antimycin-A are generic piscicides, lethal to most gill-breathing organisms including fishes and aquatic invertebrates. Renovation projects combined with barriers have restored native faunas, especially native trouts, but non-target mortality is high because salvage is inefficient and recovery areas are remote and mountainous.

In the Great Lakes, selective lampricides [[TFM]] and [[Bayluscide]] reduced sea lamprey reproduction in tributaries with minimal non-target impact. [[Squoxin]] is selectively toxic to northern pikeminnow, a predator and competitor of Pacific Northwest salmonids, but is not EPA-registered for that use.

Supaverm, an anti-parasitic used in koi ponds, was screened by Ward (2005). At 13 µg/L it was effective against fathead minnow, red shiner, yellow bullhead, largemouth bass and smallmouth bass, while leaving longfin dace, Gila chub and Gila topminnow unharmed.

## Key Findings

**Taxon-specific piscicide development**
- Only two piscicides are currently registered in the southwest: rotenone and antimycin-A, both generic and non-selective.
- Supaverm showed selective potential in Ward (2005) screening; the authors cite it as evidence that taxon-specific chemicals are feasible.
- Dawson and Kolar (2003) found little potential for life-stage-specific targeting of Gila basin non-natives, so new chemicals would target all life stages.
- Registration under the full EPA Section 3(c) process requires a full data battery under Good Laboratory Practice standards. Testing may take 6 to 7 years, with up to an additional year of review per study.
- Costs: re-registration or re-labeling of an existing chemical is roughly $5 million over about 5 years; a completely new chemical may cost $35–50 million over 8–10 years.

**Genetic biocontrol development**
- Muir and Howard (1999) modeled a transgene introduced via a small number of donor Japanese medaka, predicting eventual local extinction of donor and receiving populations through reduced offspring viability.
- Transgenic methods could achieve complete control with little non-target impact, but face public acceptance and risk assessment uncertainty. Repeated directed releases would be required, limiting risk of incidental translocation.
- Ploidy manipulation produces sterile or near-sterile organisms; it is unsuitable where threatened species live because non-native populations must first be increased by releasing sterile fish.
- Priority research species proposed: green sunfish, mosquitofish, red shiner and northern crayfish, which have strong negative impacts and little sport value.
- Research and development timelines and costs:
  - Ploidy manipulation: about 5 years; total $3–5 million.
  - Transgenic: a 20-year program; research and efficacy costs of roughly $2.5 million plus $12 million, with additional deliberation (about $725,000) and regulatory costs (about $540,000). Total estimated at $15–20 million.

**Recommendations**
- Pursue the most promising approaches, namely piscicide development and genetic methods, as rapidly as possible.
- Establish national leadership, since States cannot fund the effort alone.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is urged to take a lead regulatory role in genetic biocontrol.
- Pursue cooperative partnerships with agencies and organizations across the West.
