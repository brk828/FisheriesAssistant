# Survey for Loach Minnow in Big Bonito and Tonto Creeks, Fort Apache Indian Reservation (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tiaroga-cobitis

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report from the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] of the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] documents a July 1993 survey for [[Loach Minnow]], a threatened fish, in remote reaches of [[Tonto Creek]] and [[Big Bonito Creek]] on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. Five sites were sampled on Tonto Creek and two on Big Bonito Creek using [[Backpack Electrofishing]]. **No Loach Minnow were captured.** Fish assemblages were dominated by [[Speckled Dace]], with [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] also recorded. Big Bonito Creek was markedly depauperate. The author concludes that the survey results are preliminary and that continued surveys are needed to clarify the status of the species on the Reservation.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small bottom-dwelling cyprinid associated with riffle habitat, as described in the document's preferred-habitat discussion.
- **Regulatory status:** Listed as threatened in 1985 (USFWS 1986). A 1992 USFWS jeopardy opinion concluded that a proposed water diversion and water-management action was likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the [[White River Population]]. The opinion was based on potential habitat modifications and was reinforced by scientific support for the White River population's decline.
- **Knowledge gap:** Efforts to address the jeopardy opinion were hindered by a lack of knowledge about the species' distribution. Earlier surveys had concentrated on a few easily accessible streams that did not necessarily have suitable habitat (Propst et al. 1985). Many streams on the Reservation had never been surveyed.
- **Rationale:** The author states that the status of any new Loach Minnow populations discovered on the Reservation could affect how the species is managed, and that new populations might have implications for proposed water-related activities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Big Bonito Creek and Tonto Creek lie in the White Mountains geographic subprovince, in the east and south-central portions of the Reservation. Both streams drain the southern slopes of the [[Mount Baldy Wilderness]] and flow through ponderosa pine and spruce-fir forest before joining the [[Black River]] on the southern Reservation boundary. Much of the drainage lies above 2000 m elevation, which the report identifies as the uppermost limit of Loach Minnow distribution in New Mexico (Propst and Bestgen 1991). All survey sites were at or below 2000 m.

**Site characteristics:**
- **Tonto Creek:** Sites were characterized by an analogous stream channel and floodplain with abrupt rises in elevation. Habitats were dominated by pools and runs over cobble and boulder substrates, and riffles of any significance were rare. Site 4 was located near the confluence of Tonto Creek and a tributary, and Site 5 was located downstream of Fort Apache Road Y4T.
- **Big Bonito Creek:** Site 1 was about 500 m east of the K L Ranch, in a reach with a relatively broad floodplain and confined channel. Riffles were cobble and gravel but were heavily embedded. Site 2 lay in the ID Ranch area, close to the confluence with the Black River, where pools were boulder-strewn and runs were long and sand-bottomed. No sites were surveyed between Sites 1 and 2 because the channel is bordered by steep canyon walls with few riffle habitats.

**Riffle habitat.** Riffles were small at Tonto Creek Sites 1 and 2, and were only marginally suitable for Loach Minnow at Sites 3 to 5. Riffle length was greatly reduced at Big Bonito Creek Site 2.

## Key Findings

### Methods
- **Gear:** A Coffelt Mark 10 backpack electrofishing unit was used at all sites. The frequency selector switch was set to CPS (pulses per second) to reduce mortality.
- **Handling:** Stunned fish were collected with 3/8-inch mesh dipnets, identified, measured to the nearest millimeter, weighed and released.
- **Effort:** All riffle habitats within each predetermined survey area were sampled intensively. Pools, runs and other habitats were sampled less intensively. Multiple passes were usually required to complete each sampling event.
- **Schedule:** Table 1 lists sampling dates from 13 July to 5 August 1993 for Tonto Creek. The Big Bonito Creek dates in the scan appear as October to November, which conflicts with the text stating that surveys occurred in July 1993. The discrepancy should be checked against the original.

### Results
- **Loach Minnow:** None were captured at any site.
- **Tonto Creek:** Fish were collected at all five sites. [[Speckled Dace]] comprised 79.3% of all fish taken. Other species included Sonora Sucker (about 1.3%), Rainbow Trout and Brown Trout, which were present at low relative abundance; their exact percentages are partly illegible in the scan. The author notes that Sonora Sucker and trout were likely underrepresented because sampling focused on riffles, which these species do not favor.
- **Big Bonito Creek:** Sampling effort was similar to Tonto Creek, but only 38 specimens were collected. Speckled Dace predominated at Site 1 (reported as 85.8%), and Rainbow Trout and Brown Trout were each represented by a single individual (2.6%). Site 2 yielded no fish despite repeated sampling.
- **Length data (Table 2):** Total length statistics (sample size, mean length and range) are provided for five species at each site across the Big Bonito and Tonto creek drainages. Table 2 is partly illegible in the scan, so the original should be consulted for exact values. One specimen was seen but not collected.

### Discussion
- The decline of Loach Minnow is attributed to habitat disturbance. The author notes that the surveyed streams were selected for accessibility and that their riffle habitats were limited, which constrains their suitability for the species.
- The absence of Loach Minnow is discussed in relation to the quality and quantity of riffle habitat in the lower Big Bonito and Tonto creeks. Disturbance history in the drainage is also discussed, though the relevant details are illegible in the scan.
- The author discusses the native fish community of Tonto Creek and the possible effects of nonnative trout. The conclusions are not fully legible in the scan.
- The sparse fish catch on Big Bonito Creek could not be explained based on the available data.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- The 1993 survey did not find Loach Minnow at any site, but the author states that the results are preliminary.
- Continued surveys are necessary to determine the status of the species on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and to address the knowledge gaps identified by the 1992 jeopardy opinion.
