# Final Rule Listing Rio Yaqui Fishes: Yaqui Chub Endangered, Beautiful Shiner and Yaqui Catfish Threatened, with Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final rule, effective October 1, 1984, lists three freshwater fishes of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) is determined to be endangered. The [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Notropis formosus*) and the [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*) are determined to be threatened. Critical habitat is designated for all three, centered on the aquatic habitats of the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] in Cochise County, Arizona. The rule also includes a special rule allowing take for educational, scientific, or conservation purposes under state law. The stated reason for listing is that populations have been seriously reduced by habitat modification and face an imminent threat from the release of exotic fishes.

The document is a regulatory notice, not a research study. It draws on earlier surveys and public comments. A short plant listing from the same issue is also included (see Key Findings).

## Background & Description

**Species and origin.** All three Rio Yaqui fishes were first collected and described from San Bernardino Creek, just south of the Arizona-Sonora border, in the latter half of the 19th century (Girard, 1856; Rutter, 1896). Historically they occurred throughout the Rio Yaqui system and in a few smaller adjacent drainages.

**Habitat preferences.**
- Adult Yaqui chubs inhabit pools and undercut banks in permanent streams. The largest populations occur in the riffles of small streams.
- Yaqui catfish are usually found in permanent streams with medium to slow current.
- The beautiful shiner is found in a variety of habitats.

**Biology.** Little is known about the biology of these fishes. The beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish are thought to be biologically similar to the [[Red Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]], respectively.

**Regulatory and survey history.**
- The Yaqui chub was considered for listing in 1966 and 1973, but no action was taken because its status in Mexico was undetermined.
- A 1979 published account by Deacon et al. described the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish and proposed the Yaqui chub as endangered.
- The three species were placed in Category 1 of the December 30, 1982, Vertebrate Notice of Review (47 FR 58454).
- On April 12, 1983, the [[Desert Fishes Council]] petitioned the Service to list the Yaqui chub. The Service found substantial information supporting the petition (notice June 14, 1983, 48 FR 27273). It then found the petitioned action warranted and published a proposed rule on July 15, 1983 (48 FR 32527).
- Hendrickson et al. (1980) surveyed the Rio Yaqui system in Mexico and found only one Yaqui chub specimen after extensive collecting. They also reported range reductions for the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish.

**Public comment.** Five written comments were received on the proposal, from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Arizona Department of Water Resources, the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), the U.S. Forest Service, and Mexico's Direccion de Flora y Fauna Silvestre. No public hearing was requested or held.
- Arizona Game and Fish supported the proposal but raised concern about geothermal exploration on BLM land in the San Bernardino Valley. The Service responded that any further actions, such as drilling, would require consultation under Section 7 of the Act.
- The Arizona Department of Water Resources and BLM knew of no affected activities.
- The Forest Service had no objection and knew of no suitable reintroduction habitat on its lands.
- Mexico supported the conservation efforts and described problems these species face in Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The Rio Yaqui fishes occur in the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]], which drains western Sonora, portions of eastern Chihuahua (Mexico), and the extreme southeastern corner of Arizona. The Yaqui chub has also been recorded from several other localities, including sites in the closed Guzman basin, and the beautiful shiner has been recorded from small drainages of the region.

**Remaining U.S. populations.**
- Yaqui chub: limited to a few springs on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and to [[Leslie Creek]], both in southeastern Arizona. Its flow is gradually dwindling.
- Beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish: extirpated from the United States.

**Threat factors under Section 4(a)(1).** The Service evaluated five factors.

| Factor | Summary of finding |
|---|---|
| A. Habitat destruction or modification | Most serious. [[Arroyo Cutting]], stream headwater diversion, impoundments, [[Groundwater Pumping]], and canal systems for irrigated agriculture in Mexico have reduced permanent stream habitat and failing springs. |
| B. Overutilization | Not a threat. The fishes have no commercial use, and past scientific collecting had no impact on populations. |
| C. Disease or predation | Minor in the U.S. Introduced predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, black bullhead, channel catfish, and green sunfish occur in parts of the range and likely prey on these fishes. The threat is expected to grow in Mexico. |
| D. Inadequate regulatory mechanisms | The Yaqui chub is Group II on the Arizona threatened and unique wildlife list, allowing take under a scientific permit or angling license. The shiner and catfish are Group I, so Arizona law does not protect them. No protection exists in Mexico. |
| E. Other natural or manmade factors | Introduced exotics are the major threat. Red shiner, channel catfish, and blue catfish could cause competition or genetic swamping. The [[Mosquitofish]] caused the rapid elimination of the native [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (listed as endangered in the same drainage). Exotic introductions in Mexico are expected to increase as the interior of Sonora and Chihuahua develops. |

**Geothermal potential.** The San Bernardino Valley has potential geothermal resources (Hahman, 1979), but the area is not a Known Geothermal Resource Area. BLM has issued leases on lands adjacent to the refuge. Exploration or development could deplete or pollute the aquifers feeding refuge springs. The Service expects no such effects if activities are properly designed and regulated.

## Key Findings

**1. Listing status**
- Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*): **endangered**. Its restricted Mexican distribution and the same threats faced by the other species justify this status.
- Beautiful shiner (*Notropis formosus*) and Yaqui catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*): **threatened**, based on their status and distribution in Mexico and threats to remaining habitat in the U.S. and Mexico.
- Without listing, the Service concluded these species' status could continue to decline.

**2. Critical habitat**
- The aquatic habitats of San Bernardino NWR (Cochise County) are designated critical habitat. They hold one of the two existing Yaqui chub populations and provide expansion habitat and prime reintroduction sites for the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish.
- Yaqui chub constituent elements: permanent streams and spring pools without exotic fishes, deep pools separated by riffles, flowing water with moderate current, and backwaters with overgrown vegetation and sand or detritus for feeding and shelter.
- Beautiful shiner constituent elements: small permanent streams with riffles, or intermittent creeks with pools and riffles, with clean unpolluted water free of exotic fishes.
- The Service considered economic and other impacts under Section 4(b)(2). These did not warrant changing the proposed designation.

**3. Federal protection (Section 7)**
- Federal agencies must ensure their actions are not likely to jeopardize the species. Consultation is required where a "may affect" determination is made.
- The only Federal activity that may affect the critical habitat is geothermal exploration, which occurs outside the refuge boundary but could affect aquifers. BLM regulates and licenses it. The designation does not preclude geothermal development, which may proceed if refuge artesian and surface water supplies are adequately protected.

**4. Prohibitions and permits**
- Standard Section 9 prohibitions apply to all three species, including take, import, export, interstate sale, and possession of illegally taken wildlife.
- Permits under 50 CFR 17.22, 17.23, and 17.32 may be issued for scientific purposes, species propagation or survival, or to relieve undue economic hardship.
- A special rule (proposed for the two threatened species) allows take for educational, scientific, or conservation purposes under applicable state law. No state law currently applies because the species are absent from Arizona. Without the special rule, endangered-level prohibitions would apply.

**5. Other determinations**
- The Service will consider whether these species should be placed on the Annex of the Convention on Nature Protection and Wildlife Preservation in the Western Hemisphere.
- An Environmental Assessment under NEPA was not required (notice October 25, 1983, 48 FR 49244).
- The Department of the Interior found the designation is not a major rule under Executive Order 12291. It will not have a significant economic effect on small entities. Agriculture, mainly cattle production, is the primary local economic activity and is not expected to be affected.
- The rule contains no information collection requirements under the Paperwork Reduction Act.

**6. Plant rule in the same issue**
- A second Federal Register rule (Portland, Oregon office) determines endangered status for *Thelypodium stenopetalum* (slender-petaled thelypody) and *Sidalcea pedata* (pedate checker-mallow).
- Over 85 percent of historic meadowland habitat for these plants has been eliminated by dam construction and urban and commercial development. Most remaining habitat is subject to development or adverse modification.
