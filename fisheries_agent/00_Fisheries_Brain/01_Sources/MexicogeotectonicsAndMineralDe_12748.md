# Mexico Geotectonics and Mineral Deposits

#geology #economic-geology #geotectonics #mineral-deposits

## Executive Summary

Zoltan de Cserna (Instituto de Geología, U.N.A.M.) presents a tentative review of how Mexican metallic and hydrocarbon deposits relate to the country's geotectonic evolution from the Precambrian through the Tertiary. The paper is built around a series of palinspastic reconstruction maps showing the positions of the main structural belts and the mineral occurrences believed to be genetically tied to them. The central argument is that most deposits originated in their host rocks during specific geological phases (synsedimentary, synvolcanic, or related to magmatism), but that Tertiary orogeny and magmatism remobilized and reshaped most of them into the deposits mined today. The paper is a synthesis rather than a field study, and the author describes it as "merely a tentative summary."

*Note: the source is a geology review, not an ichthyology or aquatic-ecology document. The sections below follow the standard note format, with geological settings in place of habitat information.*

## Background & Description

**Scope and approach.** The paper argues that studying a mineral deposit should separate its present form and age from its original mode of accumulation. The author notes that petroleum geology routinely makes this distinction (traps, source beds, migration), whereas metallic deposit studies often focus on paragenesis and late-stage features. The paper was inspired by Cady, McKelvey, and Wells (1950) and reflects the author's work accumulated over many years, including visits to numerous mines in Mexico and abroad.

**Source base.** Maps were compiled from [[Palinspastic Reconstruction]] work and from earlier publications by the author (de Cserna, 1958 to 1971c). Other inputs included the Tectonic Map of North America (King, 1968, 1969), Geology and Economic Minerals of Canada (Douglas, 1970), unpublished state maps compiled by Petróleos Mexicanos, quadrangle maps, and ERTS space images from the U.S. Geological Survey. The reference list is long (roughly 150 entries) and spans mining, petroleum, and tectonic literature from 1889 to 1975.

**Format.** The paper contains seven palinspastic reconstruction figures (Figs. 1 to 8, covering the Precambrian, Paleozoic, Late Triassic to Early Jurassic, Middle Jurassic, Late Jurassic to Early Cretaceous, late Early Cretaceous, and Late Cretaceous to Paleocene). A Tertiary section closes the text. The maps are described as "greatly simplified" because of sparse data and scale limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address biological communities, fish, or aquatic habitats. Its environmental content concerns depositional and tectonic settings:

- **Marine and continental settings.** Synsedimentary deposits formed in marine basins, shelves, reef complexes, and basins, and in some cases in shallow gulf waters. Continental redbed and molasse deposits, coal-bearing coastal sequences, and evaporite basins are also described.
- **Plate-scale tectonics.** The paper proposes that the southern part of the North American continent was divided into blocks by WNW-trending transcurrent faults, with differential westward displacement. An Early to Middle Triassic age was suggested for this event, though other authors proposed late Paleozoic or late Proterozoic ages.
- **Oceanic and ophiolitic settings.** Ophiolites and massive sulfide deposits are linked to eugeosynclinal (volcanic-sedimentary) settings. Some sulfide accumulations are compared to Kuroko-type deposits.
- **Gulf of California.** The paper treats the gulf as a Cenozoic feature formed by separation of the Baja California Peninsula from mainland Mexico, driven by right-lateral movement along the San Andreas fault system and transform faulting near the East Pacific Rise. Late Tertiary synsedimentary copper and manganese formed on its floor at Santa Rosalía, and marine phosphorites occur on the west coast of southern Baja California.

## Key Findings

**1. Deposit types by age and setting**

- **Precambrian Y terrain:** weakly metamorphosed rocks containing synvolcanic and synsedimentary lead-zinc-copper, with silver and gold. Titanium is most abundant here and in associated anorthositic rocks.
- **Precambrian Z terrain (east-central Mexico):** synsedimentary copper-zinc.
- **Paleozoic Huastecan belt:** synsedimentary lead-zinc in nonmetamorphosed sediments. Ophiolites in the crystalline rocks contain asbestos and possibly chromite.
- **Paleozoic Jaliscoan belt:** massive sulfide deposits in southern Mexico and possibly southern Baja California.
- **Upper Triassic coastal sequence (northwest Mexico):** coal.
- **Lower Jurassic marine sequence (east-central Mexico):** one of the source-rock sequences for hydrocarbons in the region.
- **Middle Jurassic coastal sequence (south-central Mexico):** coal, notably in northwest Oaxaca.
- **Middle to Upper Jurassic evaporites (northeast and southeast Mexico):** important salt accumulations. The southeastern basin also contains potassium salts.
- **Upper Jurassic to lower Lower Cretaceous miogeosyncline:** synsedimentary copper, phosphorite, manganese (e.g., Molango), and lead-zinc-copper with silver and gold.
- **Upper Lower Cretaceous basin carbonates around reef complexes:** Mississippi Valley-type or Pine Point-type lead-zinc deposits. The author argues Pine Point is the closer analogue.
- **Upper Cretaceous coastal deposits (northern Mexico):** important coal, including the most important coal reserves of Mexico in Coahuila.

**2. Magmatic deposits**

- Granitic magmatism that pierced Precambrian continental crust produced disseminated copper-molybdenum deposits. Examples include the Upper Cretaceous plutons in Sonora (granitic to quartz monzonitic) and the deposits at Inguarán, Michoacán, and Placeres del Oro, Guerrero.
- Dioritic magmatism next to oceanic crust is associated with a disseminated copper-gold deposit at El Arco (Calmalli), Baja California.
- Tin and mercury deposits are closely linked to medial Tertiary volcanism. The tin origin is proposed as remelting of Precambrian crust.

**3. Relation of deposits to Precambrian crust**

The author states that the distribution of tungsten (tactite) deposits, which are confined to the oldest and next-oldest Precambrian belts, and of Upper Cretaceous copper-molybdenum deposits centered over the oldest Precambrian terrain, suggests a close relation between Precambrian crust and the origin of these metals in younger intrusives.

**4. Hydrocarbons**

- Lower Jurassic marine shales and limestones are a source-rock sequence for east-central Mexican oil fields.
- Late Cretaceous to Paleocene sinking in the east drove the first updip (westward) migration of hydrocarbons from the Upper Jurassic to Lower Cretaceous miogeosynclinal prism. The author argues this migration also helped form the Lower Cretaceous lead-zinc deposits.
- Salt domes, oil field structures, and stratigraphic traps in presently producing fields date from the Tertiary.

**5. Tertiary reworking**

Orogeny and magmatism during the Tertiary remobilized most previously formed mineral concentrations into economically exploitable deposits. Early Eocene folding and thrusting, gravity sliding over décollement surfaces, and Miocene magmatism (lavas, tuffs, and ignimbrite sheets) in the western half of the country all contributed. The author stresses that most present-day morphology, structure, and age relations are the product of these late processes.

**6. Named deposits and districts cited**

- **Lead-zinc:** Parral-Santa Bárbara, Sombrerete-Fresnillo and southern Zacatecas, Santa Fe (northern Chiapas), San José (southeast of Monterrey), Concepción de Oro (southwest of Monterrey), Plomosas (northern Chihuahua).
- **Massive sulfide:** La Dicha (north of Acapulco), El Triunfo and San Antonio (south of La Paz), Guale (northwest of Manzanillo), Petatlán, Bastán (Michoacán).
- **Copper:** Teziutlán (Puebla), Santa Rosalía (Gulf of California floor), Las Vigas Formation (northeastern Chihuahua).
- **Asbestos:** Catorce, Ciudad Victoria, Tecomatlán, north of Oaxaca City, Guerrero.
- **Coal:** Moradillas and Santa Clara (Sonora), Sabinas and Coahuila basins.

**7. Conclusions**

The author concludes that mineral deposits are "intimately related" to geotectonics. Their original accumulation can be linked to the rocks that make up each geotectonic element, but their present form and age reflect Tertiary processes. The reconstructions are presented as a tentative summary of the author's accumulated data and thinking.
