# Kontinentalverschiebungstheorie und Fischverbreitung

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biogeography #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Ameiuridae #paleoichthyology #continental-drift

## Executive Summary

This 1948 paper by Curt Kosswig applies [[Continental Drift Theory]] (Kontinentalverschiebungstheorie) to explain historical patterns of primary freshwater fish distribution between North America, Eurasia, and Africa. Kosswig builds on Myers' Eocene faunal inventory of North America to argue that the classical "Bering Strait migration" model for the arrival of [[Cyprinidae|Cyprinids]] in North America during the Miocene is climatically implausible, since polar migration had pushed the North Pole into the immediate vicinity of the Bering region by that time, creating unsuitable (cold) conditions for a temperate-to-tropical freshwater fauna. Instead, Kosswig proposes that Cyprinids invaded North America from Europe via a transatlantic filter route (England-Newfoundland Bridge or Scandinavia-Greenland Bridge), analogous to the well-documented Pliocene Indian-to-African migration of Cyprinids across a similarly restrictive filter zone. The paper is a foundational example of using paleogeographic reconstructions (continental positions, pole locations) to test and refine historical biogeography hypotheses for [[Primary Freshwater Fish|primary freshwater fishes]].

## Background & Description

While the paper is primarily biogeographic/paleontological rather than morphological, it references key diagnostic and taxonomic groupings relevant to identifying "primary division" freshwater fish lineages:

- **[[Acipenseridae|Sturgeons]]** — genera *Polyodon* and *Scaphirhynchus* (Eocene North America); Palearctic representatives include *Psephurus* and *Scaphirhynchus*.
- **[[Amiidae]]** (*Amia*) and **[[Lepisosteidae]]** (*Lepisosteus*, "gar pikes") — archaic holostean lineages, extinct in the Palearctic today but known there as fossils, and also found as fossils on southern continents.
- **[[Osteoglossidae]]** — Eocene North American representative now entirely absent from the continent; a lineage generally associated with Gondwanan/tropical freshwater distributions.
- **[[Catostomidae]]** (suckers) and **[[Ameiuridae]]** (=Ictaluridae, bullhead catfishes) — considered the only Eocene North American forms that can be confidently identified as immigrants from Asia, derived from the southern differentiation area of the Cyprinids/Siluriformes.
- **[[Aphredoderidae]]** and primitive **Cyprinodontiformes** — survive today as the cave-adapted **[[Amblyopsidae|Amblyopsids]]** of North American caves.
- **[[Percopsidae]]**, endemic **sunfishes** (Centrarchidae, implied), and **Percidae** — considered old North American Eocene endemics.
- **[[Umbridae]]** and **[[Esocidae]]** (pikes) — taxa with poor or absent paleontological documentation in North America but known from the Eocene/Oligocene of Europe, suggesting trans-Atlantic rather than trans-Beringian origins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames its argument around **"primary division" freshwater fishes** — taxa physiologically restricted to freshwater and highly intolerant of marine dispersal, making them sensitive indicators of historical continental connectivity.

- **Eocene Palearctic freshwater fauna** is described as sparse relative to the diverse North American Eocene endemic fauna, since many American lineages never successfully colonized Europe or Siberia.
- **Climatic filter zones**: The Bering Strait region, though temperate in the Eocene, underwent rapid climatic deterioration in the Miocene due to polar migration (pole positioned near 75°N, 150°S at the time), rendering the corridor unsuitable for temperate/warm-adapted primary freshwater fish dispersal.
- **Transatlantic corridor**: Kosswig proposes the England-Newfoundland Bridge or a Scandinavia-Greenland Bridge as viable dispersal routes for Cyprinids into North America during the Miocene, while continents were still connected under continental drift reconstructions.
- **Analogous filter migration — India to Africa**: The Pliocene migration zone between India and Africa is cited as a direct ecological analog — a narrow, climatically restrictive corridor traversable only by a few highly adaptable and aggressive (dispersal-capable) taxa, explaining the relative faunal monotony of Cyprinids that successfully colonized Africa compared to the explosive generic diversification seen in North America.
- **Asymmetric faunal exchange**: Older North American endemic lineages (Osteoglossidae, Amiidae, Lepisosteidae, *Psephurus*, etc.) partly succeeded in reaching Europe, but younger/derived groups — Centrarchidae, Percopsidae, Aphredoderidae — failed to disperse to Europe, just as Ameiuridae and Catostomidae failed to disperse from Asia into a reverse direction. This asymmetry is explained via corridor timing and ecological filtering rather than pure chance.

## Key Findings

1. **Rejection of the Bering Strait hypothesis for Miocene Cyprinid entry into North America**: Continental drift-based pole reconstructions show the Bering region had become too cold by the Miocene for the required influx of Cyprinids, contradicting the standard literature view (with the partial exception of Kyle 1926, who proposed a Mesozoic European connection for the Leuciscinae).
2. **Proposed transatlantic invasion route**: Cyprinids more plausibly reached North America via a North Atlantic land bridge (England-Newfoundland or Scandinavia-Greenland) while these connections still existed under the continental drift model.
3. **Greater generic diversification of North American vs. African Cyprinids** is attributed to the greater geologic age of the family in North America (colonization began earlier, allowing more time for radiation), not merely to a more favorable dispersal route — since the African corridor (India→Africa, Pliocene) is considered ecologically comparable in restrictiveness to the proposed European→North America corridor.
4. **Filter-bridge model generalized**: Both the India-Africa Pliocene corridor and the proposed Europe-North America Miocene corridor represent narrow, climatically stressful "Landenge" (isthmus-like) filter zones passable only by a small number of highly adaptable, aggressive fish lineages — with Cyprinids being the paradigmatic example, having successfully colonized freshwater systems on every continent except South America and Australia since the Eocene.
5. **Faunal exchange was asymmetric and lineage-selective**: Only Catostomidae and Ameiuridae are confidently identified as Asian invaders into Eocene North America; other groups (Centrarchidae, Percopsidae, Aphredoderidae, Umbridae, Esocidae) either originated endemically in North America or failed to cross intercontinental corridors in either direction, paralleling the pattern seen in the India-Africa exchange.
