# Explosive Speciation in African Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 lecture by [[P. H. Greenwood]], delivered to the Royal Institution-style weekly evening meeting and published in the Proceedings of the Royal Institution of Great Britain, examines "explosive speciation" in the fish faunas of East and Central African lakes. The lecture argues that the rapid diversification of [[Cichlidae]], particularly the genus [[Haplochromis]], produced species flocks that fill nearly every available niche. Greenwood proposes that tectonic instability of shallow lake basins, fragmenting and reuniting water bodies, drove repeated spatial isolation and speciation in Lake Victoria. He also highlights breeding biology (mouth brooding and continuous spawning) as a likely facilitator of rapid evolution.

## Background & Description

The term "explosive speciation" describes very rapid evolution of many closely related species within a restricted geographic area. Darwin's work on [[Galapagos Finches]] provided the first example, and other centres include islands, lakes and caves.

Cichlids dominate the fish faunas of most east and central African lakes. Their radiations vary between lakes:

- **Lake Rudolf:** 2 endemic species, neither in endemic genera (least diversification).
- **Lake Tanganyika:** 132 endemic species in 30 endemic and 4 non-endemic genera.
- **Lake Nyasa:** 189 species in 20 endemic and 3 non-endemic genera.
- **Lake Victoria:** 120 endemic species in one non-endemic genus, plus 3 endemic species in 3 endemic genera (intermediate diversity).

Lake Victoria's *Haplochromis* are small, typically 3 to 4 inches long, with the largest species rarely exceeding a foot. Females practise mouth brooding, carrying the developing young for a period of about three weeks. Males are polygamous, brightly coloured during spawning, and establish territories and spawning pits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Victoria's *Haplochromis* have occupied nearly all available niches. The exceptions are open surface waters, deep waters, and dense marginal papyrus swamps with very low oxygen, which are colonised mainly by specialised air-breathing fishes or poorly colonised by any fishes. Major habitats include papyrus-fringed bays with thick organic mud, exposed sandy shores with wind-agitated water, and rocky cliff shores that are mostly associated with offshore islands. The average depth about 140 ft offshore is followed by a gentle slope. Most fish life is concentrated in shallow littoral waters. The deepest basin, averaging 200 ft, remained poorly sampled at the time.

Greenwood contrasts this with marine tropical shores, where similar adaptive diversity is spread across many genera and families. In marine fishes, a genus rarely covers more than one habitat and food source.

The geological history of the Victoria basin is central to the argument. The basin formed during the early Pleistocene (about 1 to 1.4 million years ago) as part of [[East African Rift Valley]] formation. Tectonic tilting first to the south-west and later to the north-east caused the lake to overflow into the [[Nile]] system during the upper Pleistocene. Brief connection with Lake Edward is reflected in shared species.

## Key Findings

**Trophic specialisation and adaptive radiation**

- *Haplochromis* have evolved specialisations in dentition, skull and jaw form and digestive physiology, exploiting almost every food source in the lake.
- **Insectivores and detritus feeders** are the least specialised and may make up about 30% of known species. Insectivores resemble river *Haplochromis*, the probable modern representatives of ancestral forms.
- **Mollusc-eaters** (about 12 species) follow two routes. One group enlarged the pharyngeal bones to crush shells. The other developed specialised oral dentition to wrench snails from their shells.
- **Plant-eaters** are poorly represented, with about four algal grazers and one species that chews submerged plant leaves. Both groups have long, convoluted guts.
- **Piscivores** are the largest group, about 40% of Lake Victoria species, often preying on other *Haplochromis*. Increased jaw size, grasping-piercing teeth and streamlined bodies are typical.
- **Paedophagous species** feed on embryos and larvae from brooding females' mouths. They have large expansible mouths and reduced oral dentition.
- Specialisation often arises through relatively simple changes in dental morphology and differential growth of head skeleton. Several species often reach the same level of specialisation from different ancestral stocks.

**Speciation mechanisms**

- Repeated tectonic fragmentation and reunion of the basin would isolate populations, permitting divergence and further speciation.
- Competition among species with similar potential would accelerate divergence.
- Complex breeding behaviour, with male colouration and courtship acting as reproductive barriers, could facilitate speciation.
- Continuous spawning, unlike non-cichlids that spawn only in flooded rivers, may have increased reproductive potential and the rate at which favourable gene combinations spread.

**Natural experiment: Lake Nabugabo**

- Cut off from Lake Victoria about 4,000 years ago, Lake Nabugabo has only seven *Haplochromis* species, five of which are endemic.
- Its fishes are more distinct from Victoria relatives than Victoria species are from one another, demonstrating that rapid divergence is possible.

**Comparative and unresolved points**

- The fractioning-and-reunion model fits shallow, expansive lakes (Victoria, Edward) but not deep, narrow lakes such as Tanganyika and Nyasa.
- Tanganyika's flocks derive from a different ancestral stem, and Nyasa and Victoria radiated only within *Haplochromis* and locally derived genera.
- Greenwood states that no single explanation can account for the similar end results across lakes.
- The factors that first triggered feeding specialisation remain unknown.
