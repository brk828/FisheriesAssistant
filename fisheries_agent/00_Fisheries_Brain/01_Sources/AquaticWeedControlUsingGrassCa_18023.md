# Grass Carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) as a Biological Agent for Aquatic Weed Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1969 article by D. G. Cross of the [[Salmon and Freshwater Fisheries Laboratory]] (Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food, London) describes the [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) and assesses whether it can be imported into Britain for controlling nuisance water weeds. The author summarises the species' feeding anatomy, growth, temperature and oxygen tolerance, breeding requirements, and the plants it eats, then sets out three conditions that must be met before introduction: proven weed consumption, controllable population size, and no harm to native fish. The conclusion is that no insurmountable problems appear likely, but on-site testing is required before any decision.

## Background & Description

The [[Grass Carp]] is a cyprinid native to rivers of Siberia, Manchuria and China flowing to the Pacific between 50° N and 23° N. It has been established in several countries, including [[Malaysia]], [[Taiwan]], [[Japan]], the [[United States]], Eastern Europe, Holland and Germany.

Two importation attempts are described:

- **Hong Kong and Taiwan fingerlings (3–5 cm):** 95% survived transport but arrived in poor condition and were not feeding. Most died within months, mainly from [[White Spot Disease]] (*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*). The author notes that white spot in healthy, feeding fish can be cured with [[Chloramine-T]].
- **Hungarian State Fish Farm, Dinnyés (near Budapest):** 1,900 fish of 10–15 cm and 150 of 30–40 cm were transported by lorry in eleven 60-gallon tanks over a thousand-mile journey, with water changes and cylinder oxygenation. All arrived in excellent condition and fed vigorously within days.

Morphology: the grass carp resembles the native chub, with an elongated body, large head and mouth, olive-brown upper parts and silver lower parts. It has no oral teeth. Instead, toothed falciform lower pharyngeal bones work against a horny pad on the pharynx roof, grinding food. Plants are gripped and torn by lateral body movements. The gut is only about 2.25 times body length, roughly a fifth of the herbivore norm; for comparison, [[Silver Carp]] (*Hypophthalmichthys molitrix*) has a gut about 12 times body length. Faeces contain about one-half undigested food (Hickling, 1966), so large food quantities are needed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tolerances and growth**
- Water temperature: 0–35 °C (Stevenson, 1965).
- Salinity: up to 10,000 p.p.m. (Doroshev, 1963).
- Dissolved oxygen: as low as 0.5 p.p.m. (Yeh, 1959).
- Growth is strongly temperature-dependent. Mature fish can reach 50 kg where the season is long (Nikolsky, 1956). In British ponds near Maidstone, Kent, fish grew from 5 oz (140 g) to 14 oz (410 g), and from 7.5 in (19 cm) to 11 in (28 cm), over 14 weeks (June–October 1964) at 15–20 °C (Stott, 1967).

**Reproduction**
- Sexual maturity takes five to nine years, depending on growing-season length.
- Spawning in the native range requires temperatures above 20 °C and current of two to five feet per second (Nikolsky, 1956). Such conditions occur locally in British power-station effluents.
- Fish reared outside the native range develop mature gonads but do not spawn. Induced breeding uses [[Pituitary Hormone Injection]] from common carp, a method common at Eastern European and Far Eastern fish farms.
- Eggs hatch in 24–30 hours at about 25 °C (Tang et al., 1963).

**Diet**
- Larvae feed on minute animals and planktonic algae. By about two inches, diet shifts to vegetation, though the change may be incomplete.
- At about nine inches, fish in the laboratory ate [[Daphnia]], tubificid worms and [[Asellus]] as well as plants.
- Below 10 °C, only tender plants are eaten. As temperature rises, feeding becomes less selective, including rushes (Verigin et al., 1963). A fish can eat more than its own weight of plant material per day.

**Economic motivation**
About £1.25 million per year is spent by River Authorities and Drainage Boards on aquatic weed clearance, mostly labour, which is becoming harder to recruit. A natural agent would be valuable.

## Key Findings

**1. Weed-eating potential is supported by Table I.** Plants eaten by grass carp (approximate order of preference) include [[Canadian Pondweed]] (*Elodea canadensis*), [[Hornwort]] (*Ceratophyllum demersum*), [[Stonewort]] (*Chara* sp.), [[Lesser Duckweed]] (*Lemna minor*), [[Broad-Leaved Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton natans*), [[Water Milfoil]] (*Myriophyllum* sp.), [[Great Reedmace]] (*Typha latifolia*), [[Common Reed]] (*Phragmites communis*), [[Common Rush]] (*Juncus effusus*), [[Frogbit]] (*Hydrocharis morsus-ranae*) and [[Watercress]] (*Nasturtium officinale*), among others. Most nuisance weeds in Britain fall into this list. Sources are Verigin et al. (1963), Stroganov (1963), Penzes & Tolg (1966) and Krupauer (1967).

**2. Conditions for introduction**

- *(a) Weed consumption:* Stroganov (1963) reported that grass carp reared with young sturgeon cleared a pond of weeds. Weed control after introduction has been reported elsewhere (Singh et al., 1967).
- *(b) Population control:* The main concern is possible breeding in Britain. The author cites the [[Common Carp]] pest outbreak in the USA as a warning, while noting that rainbow trout introductions to the British Isles and New Zealand were a success.
- *(c) Habitat and native fish:* Weeds shelter fish food and provide spawning sites. Stocking density must balance weed control with protection of native species. This is simpler if grass carp do not breed in Britain.

**3. Overall conclusion:** Most evidence comes from conditions unlike Britain's. No insurmountable problems are apparent, but tests in Britain are required before any decision.

**4. Operational lessons from transport:** Fingerlings from distant sources arrived weak and died of disease. Larger, robust fish transported with water changes and oxygen survived, and the author recommends this approach for laboratory and field trials.

**5. Health note:** White spot is treatable with chloramine-T in feeding fish, so disease risk is manageable during quarantine.
