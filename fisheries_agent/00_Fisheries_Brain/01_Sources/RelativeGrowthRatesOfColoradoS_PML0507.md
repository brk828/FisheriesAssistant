# Relative Growth Rates of Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Suckers in Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study monitored the growth of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) for one year in earthen ponds at the [[Chino Fish and Wildlife Base]]. Both species are state-listed endangered, and the squawfish is also federally listed. Fish were sampled monthly with a [[Relative Growth Rate Method]], in which a subsample is weighed and measured and then returned to the pond. The squawfish gained about 270% in weight in its first year and continued to grow in its second year. The humpback sucker gained 42.6% in weight over the monitoring period. Predictive [[Growth Equation]]s were developed for each species, and the authors stress that the humpback curve is only a gross approximation. Age estimation for the humpback by size or [[Scale Annuli]] was judged unreliable and needs verification by [[Otolith Analysis]].

## Background & Description

Colorado squawfish and humpback suckers live mainly in backwaters and impoundments of the [[Colorado River]]. Natural growth data for these species were lacking. Published growth work existed for related species, including the [[Sacramento Squawfish]] studied by [[Taft and Murphy 1950]] and the humpback sucker studied by [[McAda 1977]], but these used age-length or age-weight relationships from wild populations. The authors found no study of either species using actual or relative growth methods.

Growth can be measured in several ways. Actual growth requires repeated measurement of tagged individuals over long periods. Estimated growth from scales or otoliths is quicker but assumes that back-calculated growth is proportional to scale or otolith growth. The relative growth method combines elements of both: a group is periodically subsampled to estimate average changes in weight and length, and the subsample is returned to the group without selecting particular individuals.

Fish came from the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Nevada, per source). Colorado squawfish (n=101) were progeny of captive spawning in 1975 and represented the 2+ year age class. Humpback suckers (n=55) were progeny from 1974 and represented the 4+ year age class at the start of monitoring. Fish were transported to Chino and released into 1,000 m² earthen ponds containing forage fish, including [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*). They were fed dry trout chow weekly, and aquatic insects and vegetation were also present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Monthly random samples of fish were seined from each pond, measured for total length and weighed, and returned to the pond. Individuals were not recaptured on purpose. Water temperature, pH, alkalinity, hardness and dissolved oxygen were recorded in each pond before seining.

Growth was split into growing seasons, defined as June through January. This period matched water temperatures of 20–30°C, when growth should be at its maximum. The other water quality parameters appeared adequate for growth.

Comparison data were scarce. The only published growth data for a comparable species were from the Sacramento squawfish (Taft and Murphy 1950). Comparison is difficult because of differences in species, measurement units (standard length and inches versus total length in millimetres) and methods (length and scale annuli versus weight-length data). Humpback data from [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] wild fish (Region 5 unpublished field data) were combined with the Chino and Willow Beach data to build a general curve.

## Key Findings

**Colorado squawfish growth**
- First-year average increase: about 270% in weight and about 47% in length (OCR-affected value; source text reads "47.8" or similar).
- Second-year average increases: 35.5% in weight and 10.8% in length.
- Growth followed the Sacramento squawfish curve through its third, fourth and fifth growing seasons. Growth rate remained roughly constant across the third to fifth seasons. As fish grew larger, length increased more slowly and weight increased more.
- Three weight-length equations were fitted, one for each sampling year:
  - 1977: log W = 3.1205 log TL − 5.4870, R² = 0.99
  - 1978: log W = 2.7864 log TL − 4.6199, R² = 0.94
  - 1979: log W = 2.0826 log TL − 4.1745, R² = 0.26
- Results from 1977 and 1979 overlapped. The 1977 data stopped at 310 mm and the 1979 data began at 300 mm.
- Vanicek and Kramer (1959) aged 182 squawfish from the Green River. Their mean total lengths for 3, 4 and 5 year olds were 188, 229 and 329 mm. These closely match the size ranges measured here (180–320, 240–400 and 300–400 mm).

**Humpback sucker growth**
- Monitored average increase: 42.6% in weight and 14.4% in length over one year (the abstract gives a different weight figure, so the results section should be checked against the original).
- Combined weight-length equation from all available data: log W = 2.815 log TL − 4.553, R² = 0.95.
- Wide variation in weight and length occurred within each sampling period. A slow upward trend in growth rate was seen over the study.
- The authors found that size and scale-annuli age estimates were poor. McAda's scale annuli were indistinct and did not grow in proportion to body length. McAda's data covered larger fish (442–618 mm), so his equation would not describe weight-length for smaller fish.
- The only otolith available (one 600 mm individual) showed 17 growth zones, interpreted as annuli (Fitch 1977). More age data are needed before age classes can be assigned to the curve.

**Maturity and reproduction**
- Humpback suckers at Chino in their fifth year showed secondary sexual characteristics (tubercles on the anal, pelvic and lower caudal fin) and released reproductive products (males, spring 1979).
- The slowed growth of the broodstock may reflect a shift of energy from growth to reproduction after sexual maturity (Love 1970).
- If sexual maturity occurs in the fifth or sixth year, the Willow Beach broodstock could be at least 10 to 11 years old. Larger fish (560–600 mm) may be females and intermediate fish (420–500 mm) males. This is tentative because neither group was spawning when measured.
- It is not known whether humpback suckers are reproductively viable at the same time they show physical signs of maturity.

**Management implications**
- The study provides reasonable estimates of growth rates for both species, useful as a general guide for managers of endangered populations.
- Further work is needed on time to sexual maturity, longevity and sex-related physical characteristics, especially for the humpback sucker.
