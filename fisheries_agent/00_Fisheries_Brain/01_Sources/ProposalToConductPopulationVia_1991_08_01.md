# Proposal to Conduct Population Viability Analysis for the Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology #PopulationViabilityAnalysis

## Executive Summary

This 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum and draft discussion paper proposes conducting a formal **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** for the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) to establish scientifically defensible, quantitative recovery goals under the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program]]. The document argues that existing recovery plans lack quantitative targets for population number, size, and spatial distribution. It proposes engaging PVA expert Mike Gilpin to build a space-based demographic model (in BASIC) synthesizing demographic, genetic, environmental, and catastrophic-event data. This effort is explicitly framed as a pilot template applicable to three other endangered Colorado River Basin fishes: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The accompanying draft "Recovery Goals" discussion paper frames foundational conservation biology questions (What is recovery? How many populations are needed? How large must they be?) using a question-and-answer format, while acknowledging that formal PVA had never previously been applied to large-river fish species.

## Background & Description

The source document is a policy/planning paper and contains **no morphological, meristic, or anatomical data** on any of the four species discussed. It focuses exclusively on population status, distribution, and conservation planning methodology rather than taxonomy or physical description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: A large-river piscivore requiring extensive, undammed river segments to complete its life cycle. Notably does **not** concentrate in discrete locales; adults undertake long-distance migrations to spawning grounds, and larvae drift long distances to reach nursery habitats. This life-history strategy means the species cannot be recovered via management of short, isolated river reaches, unlike many other imperiled species.
  - Historic/target recovery subbasins:
    - [[Green River]] subbasin (lower 345 mi), including lower [[Yampa River]] (137.5 mi) and lower [[White River]] (150 mi)
    - [[Colorado River]] subbasin, Lake Powell to Palisade (River Mile 185)
    - Lower 198 miles of the [[San Juan River]]
    - [[Salt River]] or [[Verde River]] (contingent on future feasibility studies)

- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Recovery plan identifies candidate habitats including the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers in [[Grand Canyon]], Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon (Colorado River), the Yampa and Green rivers within [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Gray and Desolation canyons (Green River), and Cataract Canyon (Colorado River).

- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Considered virtually extirpated from the wild at time of writing. No candidate recovery sites identified; habitat quality/quantity in historically occupied range considered potentially insufficient, requiring further research before reintroduction.

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Historically Basin-wide; by 1991 reduced to small populations in the upper [[Green River]] and lower [[Yampa River]], plus the [[Colorado River]] below [[Hoover Dam]]. Occasional individuals recorded in the Grand Valley (Colorado River), Cataract Canyon, San Juan River, and [[Paria River]]. No recovery sites yet identified; habitat restoration research needed.

- **Major identified threats/mortality factors** across species: altered flow volume/pattern (deviation from natural hydrograph), habitat alteration and channelization, predation/competition from nonnative (exotic) fishes, contaminants, and habitat fragmentation via passage barriers (dams).

## Key Findings

- **PVA as a novel tool for fish conservation**: At the time of writing, a comprehensive, formal PVA had never been conducted for any fish species. Prior partial analyses (based on genetics or habitat parameters only) existed for [[Gila Topminnow]], pupfish species, and striped bass, but none integrated the full suite of factors proposed here.
- **Study design**: The proposed Colorado Squawfish PVA would incorporate demographics, genetics, environmental variability, catastrophic events, systematic perturbations, metapopulation dynamics, and feedback loops into a space- and time-based simulation model.
- **Key management questions the PVA aims to answer**:
  1. Number of populations required for a given persistence probability (e.g., 95% over 200 years)
  2. Required population size per life stage per population
  3. Optimal spatial arrangement of populations
  4. Sensitivity of persistence probability to deterministic factor changes
  5. Explicit articulation of underlying model assumptions
- **Population size cascade concept**: A hypothesized order-of-magnitude relationship across life stages — if breeding adult population must number in the thousands, juvenile populations should be an order of magnitude larger, and larval/young-of-year cohorts may need to reach the hundreds of thousands to sustain that adult population size.
- **Genetic/spatial recovery criteria (draft standard)**: At minimum, two viable, self-sustaining populations per genetically distinct "stock," located in uncorrelated (independent) environments, to guard against simultaneous catastrophic loss.
- **Monitoring limitation**: No reliable index of abundance existed for these species due to their rarity, mobility, and dispersal in large, turbid rivers, complicating both baseline assessment and long-term trend monitoring.
- **Policy tension identified**: A documented debate between (a) prioritizing immediate aggressive management (flow restoration, habitat restoration, exotic species control, contaminant reduction, passage restoration) to halt decline versus (b) immediately refining quantitative recovery goals to guide resource allocation — with consensus leaning toward halting decline first unless goal-refinement doesn't divert resources from urgent action.
- **Budget/logistics**: FY1992 proposed project budget totaled $18,860 (including 15% overhead), structured across three tasks: data compilation ($4,700), model synthesis/development ($7,200), and findings/report writing ($4,500), scheduled for a June–September 1992 timeframe.
- **Programmatic significance**: Results were intended to serve as a methodological template extensible to the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], representing a foundational effort in applying [[Population Viability Analysis]] to large-river fish conservation.
