# Three New Intergeneric Hybrids of Cyprinid Fishes from Western Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by George F. Weisel (Montana State University, Missoula), published in the *American Midland Naturalist*, reports three intergeneric minnow hybrids from the [[Columbia River Drainage]] of western Montana that had not been recorded before:

- [[Mylocheilus Caurinum]] × [[Ptychocheilus Oregonense]] (Columbia River chub × northern squawfish)
- [[Ptychocheilus Oregonense]] × [[Richardsonius Balteatus]] (northern squawfish × redside shiner)
- [[Rhinichthys Cataractae]] × [[Richardsonius Balteatus]] (longnose dace × redside shiner)

Hybrid identity rests on intermediacy in morphological characters, quantified with the [[Hybrid Index]]. The author judges the fish to be naturally occurring hybrids, though the evidence is circumstantial. The paper also proposes that habitat change, especially the post-1938 level change of [[Flathead Lake]] following [[Kerr Dam]], promotes hybridization through crowding of spawning fish.

## Background & Description

Hybridization between fish species and genera is well documented in the families [[Cyprinidae]], [[Catostomidae]], and [[Centrarchidae]]. Hybrid studies help clarify species relationships and inform genetics, speciation research, and wildlife management. The paper follows the measurement and counting methods of Hubbs and Lagler (1947), with ratios calculated by division.

Parental species are distinguished as follows:

- **Columbia River chub:** smaller mouth, barbels present, blunt grinding pharyngeal teeth (formula typically 1, 5-5, 1), 8 dorsal rays, bright red coloration on the jaw and cheek.
- **Northern squawfish:** longer, pike-like head, larger mouth, no barbels, sharp hooked pharyngeal teeth (typically 2, 5-4, 2), 9 dorsal rays, no red coloration.
- **Redside shiner:** large eye, deep body, many anal rays (about 14–15), brilliant colors, fewer lateral-line scales.
- **Longnose dace:** ventral, horizontal mouth overhung by the snout, barbels at each jaw angle, well-developed frenum, large internarial flap.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flathead Lake (Montana).** Chubs and squawfish are among the commonest native fishes here, and their spawning periods overlap (milt and eggs were stripped from both in July and early August). Gill-net hauls also included [[Bull Trout]] (*Salvelinus malma*), [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*), [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*), [[Largescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus macrocheilus*), and [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*). The author attributes the hybridization to crowding of spawning fish. Before the dam, sloping gravel shorelines served as spawning grounds. After [[Kerr Dam]] was built in 1938, spring and summer lake levels rose substantially, flooding these areas. Remaining shores are mostly mud or steep rock. Lakes with long, shallow sand-and-gravel shores yielded no hybrids.

**Warm spring slough near Bearmouth.** This slough drains into the [[Clark Fork River]]. Its mud banks offer spawning gravel only at the mouths of several warm springs, and it holds a large fish population. Squawfish and shiners cross in from the river during spawning. The water stays at about 17–18 °C, which may bring the two species' ripening periods into alignment. Shiners ripen several weeks to a month earlier than squawfish in typical western Montana streams. The author notes that the river is polluted by wastes from the copper smelter at Anaconda, and that tributaries are blocked by flumes and viaducts.

**Irrigation ditches (Post Creek and west of Missoula).** The water is milky with clay, which gives the fish an unusually pale color. Crowding is not a plausible explanation here because spawning grounds are ample. The author suggests hybridization is instead favored by the rarity of the dace relative to the abundant shiners.

## Key Findings

### 1. Mylocheilus caurinum × Ptychocheilus oregonense (Adults, Flathead Lake)

- **Sample:** 20 adult hybrids gill-netted in summer 1950–1951. Twenty specimens of each parental species were used for comparison.
- **Relative abundance in catches:** Chubs made up 67.7%, hybrids 2.8%, and squawfish 29.3% of all catches. In the summer of 1951, the hauls contained 363 chubs, 16 hybrids, and 163 squawfish.
- **Size (standard length):** Chubs 150–250 mm (mean 186.2); squawfish 139–232 mm (mean 179.3); hybrids 160–214 mm (mean 193.2).
- **Hybrid indices (adults):**
  - Mouth/jaw proportion: 54 (almost exactly intermediate)
  - Head length/standard length: 49
  - Dorsal fin position: 45
  - Scales above lateral line: 43
  - Scales below lateral line: 267 (described as "not very trustworthy")
  - Lateral line scales: 67
  - Dorsal rays: 10
  - Total pharyngeal teeth: 54
- **Barbels:** 14 of 20 hybrids have none; 4 have a rudiment on the right jaw only; 1 on the left only; 1 has a small barbel on each side.
- **Sex ratio:** 15 males and 5 females. The 3:1 male bias is suggestive but not conclusive, because the sexes of spawning fish often arrive in unequal numbers.
- **Gonads:** Hybrids of both sexes were present. Eight males had large, apparently ripe testes; seven were immature or spawned out. One female had large, ripe-looking ovaries. The author sees no evidence of sterility from gross examination.
- **Grand average hybrid index:** 78 across all adult characters, falling to 46 when doubtful characters (scales below the lateral line and scale radii) are omitted.

### 2. Yearling Hybrids (Yellow Bay, Flathead Lake)

- On July 8, 1950, [[Reeve M. Bailey]] collected 37 yearling hybrids in a seine haul in [[Yellow Bay]], along with 25 chubs and 130 squawfish. The specimens were loaned to the author (UMMZ 161874).
- A second set of six yearling hybrids was seined in the summer of 1951.
- Yearling hybrid indices were 43 (jaw/head length), 75 (relative head length), and 41 (dorsal fin position). The grand averages were 42 and 45 for the two yearling sets.
- The paper describes this as mass hybridization of fishes in the wild, which the author notes is rarely seen. Earlier mass hybridization reports (Hubbs and Miller 1943, Gila × Siphateles) were attributed to an introduced exotic species.

### 3. Ptychocheilus oregonense × Richardsonius balteatus (Warm Spring Slough)

- **Sample:** 3 hybrids, seined March 20 and April 8, 1950, alongside 219 squawfish and 169 redside shiners. Four specimens of each parental species were measured.
- **Key indices:** Head length/standard length 108; snout length 74; eye width 70; greatest depth 69; supraorbital pores 57; anal fin rays 68.
- **Grand average hybrid index:** 69.
- The hybrids are intermediate in nearly all characters except relative head length, which is closer to squawfish.
- Color is intermediate: lighter than squawfish, with black blotches and specks, and lacking the red and brassy sheen of the shiner.

### 4. Rhinichthys cataractae × Richardsonius balteatus (Irrigation Ditches)

- **Sample:** 5 hybrids. Four came from the Post Creek ditch (March 26 and May 20, 1950), and one came from an irrigation ditch about 5 miles west of Missoula (December 11, 1951).
- **Associated fish:** At Post Creek, 7 dace and 719 shiners were taken. At the Missoula ditch, 2 dace and 320 shiners were taken.
- **Grand average hybrid index:** 52, very close to exact intermediacy.
- Hybrids have a ventral-to-terminal mouth that is intermediate between the two species. Some have barbels, and frenum development varies.
- Hybrids lack melanophores on the lower jaw, like the dace, and retain a black back stripe, like the shiner.

### 5. Hybrid Status and Reproduction

- The author concludes that the specimens are "naturally occurring hybrids," though the evidence is circumstantial. Intermediacy in most characters and co-occurrence of the parental species in the same waters support this.
- The parental species show concurrent breeding seasons, since milt could be stripped from mature males of each.
- The author found **little indication of backcrossing**. No fish intermediate between a hybrid and a parental species was identified. The author concludes that hybrids probably do not reproduce extensively.
- Hybrids were found only in specific localities. In Flathead Lake, the chub–squawfish combination was taken on nine occasions over two years and nowhere else.

### 6. Management Implications

- The author recommends that wildlife managers consider how dam construction and other environmental changes affect hybridization in the Northwest, where dam construction is proceeding rapidly.
- He argues that particular ecological conditions, rather than chance alone, determine whether genetically compatible species hybridize.
