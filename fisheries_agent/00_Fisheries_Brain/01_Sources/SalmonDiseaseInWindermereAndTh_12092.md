# Salmon Disease in Windermere and the River Leven: The Fungal Aspect

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This article examines the fungal component of the salmon disease known as [[Ulcerative Dermal Necrosis]] (U.D.N.), first recorded in autumn 1964. The author, a mycologist at the Freshwater Biological Association, argues that the disease is multi-factorial, but that the presence of an active [[Saprolegnia]] fungus is essential to fatal outcomes. Isolates from diseased fish were dominated by a single, sexually sterile strain, [[Saprolegnia Type I]]. Water sampling in summer 1968 showed this strain was common in the River Leven, present at lower levels in Windermere, and nearly absent from saline estuarine water. Fish slime appeared to suppress the fungus, and laboratory work showed strain variation could arise under chlorine stress. The author concludes that controlling the fungus might be tantamount to controlling the disease.

## Background & Description

- **Disease:** [[Ulcerative Dermal Necrosis]] (U.D.N.), first recorded in autumn 1964 in the [[Windermere]] and [[River Leven]] system.
- **Earlier explanations:** single-cause hypotheses ("columnaris infection", "radioactive pollution", "unknown virus") failed to gain full acceptance.
- **Proposed multi-factorial model:** host species, age, breeding condition, predisposing chemical or physical environmental factors, immunological barriers, and exposure to pathogens (algae, bacteria, fungi, viruses).
- **Host species:** [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Sea Trout]], [[Trout]] and [[Char]] are susceptible. [[Bullhead]] is considered ostensibly immune.
- **Disease stages:** [[Saprolegnia]] is always established on fin bases or the head in advanced cases. Irish (Carbery, 1968) and Scottish (Elson, 1968) observers reported a transient "pre-fungal" stage with small blister-like snout lesions. English observers could not confirm this, and reported fungus is usually part of the lesion from the start.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Inoculum and identity.** Many [[Saprolegnia]] species and strains occur in all freshwater, so only those linked to disease matter. Isolates from many diseased fish were almost all one strain, Saprolegnia Type I, which is sexually sterile and cannot be named by classical criteria. The author notes that [[Saprolegnia Ferax]], a common freshwater species, never took part in the infection, which suggests high selectivity.

**Water sampling (summer 1968).** Samples were taken from Windermere through the River Leven to Bardsea, at a time of low visible infection.
- The River Leven had Saprolegnia Type I as a definite part of the fungal microflora.
- Windermere had lower but demonstrable levels.
- Estuarine water with measurable salinity was almost entirely free of the strain.
- One exceptional recovery occurred at Greenodd, on a falling tide, from brackish water with salinity 11 (about 30% sea water and 70% freshwater).
- Fungal spore counts per 10 ml were mapped at stations including Windermere, Newby Bridge, Backbarrow, Low Wood Bridge and Greenodd (Fig. 1).

**Control site.** The [[River Frome]] in Dorset, a river free of the disease, yielded [[Saprolegnia Ferax]] and many unidentified isolates but no Saprolegnia Type I. The author cautions that one set of samples gives limited reliance.

**Parasites of the fungus.** Other fungi attacking Saprolegnia are common in Windermere, but their ecological significance is unknown, and their use in biological control appears unlikely. Plate 1 documents *Rozellopsis* and *Woronina polycystis* infections of Saprolegnia Type I.

## Key Findings

1. **Fungal identity:** Nearly all diseased-fish isolates yielded one recognisable, sexually sterile strain, Saprolegnia Type I.
2. **Environmental distribution:** The strain was a definite part of the River Leven microflora, present at lower levels in Windermere, and almost absent from saline estuarine water.
3. **Lower lake risk (hypothesis):** Lower spore levels in Windermere may explain lower infection in resident trout and char than in migratory salmon and sea trout. The author notes some evidence for this.
4. **Negative control:** The River Frome, which is disease-free, lacked Saprolegnia Type I (single sample set only).
5. **Fish slime barrier:** Swabs of disease-free salmonids and coarse fish carried many fungi but very little Saprolegnia Type I, suggesting anti-fungal action by slime. Filter-paper swabbing with narcotized fish was a non-lethal sampling method.
6. **Bullhead experiment:** Bullhead exposed to about 3.5 million zoospores picked up spores from the water but showed no disease. A physically damaged specimen two weeks later died of Saprolegnia Type I, showing the strain persisted in the environment.
7. **Strain variation:** At 1.5 ppm chlorine, a few zoospores survived from thousands, producing colonies with slower growth rates. Chlorine at 2 ppm or more killed all zoospores. This indicates that virulent strains could arise and establish.
8. **Central conclusion:** Fatally diseased fish may never occur without active Saprolegnia. Controlling the fungus may therefore amount to controlling the disease, and this approach was judged potentially fruitful.
