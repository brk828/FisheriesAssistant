# Estimation of Effective Population Size by Determination of Accumulated Genetic Distance Due to Drift

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmo-gairdneri

## Executive Summary

This paper by William B. Schill, Raymond C. Simon, and Stevan R. Phelps (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) proposes a method for estimating [[Effective Population Size]] (Ne) from accumulated [[Genetic Drift]], quantified as [[Nei's Standard Genetic Distance]] (Nei's D). The authors derive formulas linking Nei's D to Ne, which can be computed from electrophoretic data. They apply the method to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) brood stocks and argue that common guidelines for captive population size, which focus on retaining alleles regardless of frequency, are inadequate. The authors propose that the unique array of allele frequencies in a population should be the object of conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Effective population size is central to genetic management. Prior estimation methods include allelism of lethal genes (Wright et al. 1942; Nei 1968), rates of chromosomal rearrangement (Lande 1979), and gene frequency change (Krimbas and Tsakas 1971; Familo and Varvio-Aho 1980). [[Nei and Tajima Method]] (1981) improved this by using the standardized variance of gene frequency change, F.
- **Genetic distance:** Nei's D (Nei 1972) is widely accepted as a measure of population dissimilarity. Its biological significance and sampling properties have been studied (Nei 1972, 1978; Nei and Roychoudhury 1974). The authors argue that quantifying drift as accumulated Nei's D is advantageous because of these well-documented properties.
- **Problem addressed:** Most captive-population size recommendations rest on an arbitrary fixation rate rather than on maintaining gene frequency constancy (Franklin 1980; Ryman and Stahl 1980).
- **Stated purposes:**
  1. Show that Ne can be estimated by measuring the standard genetic distance between two samples of the same population separated by a number of generations.
  2. Estimate the minimum Ne required to maintain a given level of genetic similarity over time.
- **Model components:** Observed genetic distance between repeat samples has three components: mutation (D_m), sampling error at fertilization (D_f), and sampling error at the gene frequency survey (D_s). The authors assume D_m is zero or negligible. The model assumes discrete generations, no selection, and no migration or mutation.
- **Subdivided populations:** An index of divergence (i.d.) is defined from the observed mean interdeme genetic distance and the mean genetic distance from generation zero to generation t, with s demes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** The work concerns hatchery and captive populations managed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Management questions include how many individuals must be kept to restrict loss of genetic variability, and whether a population's characterized attributes (for example, traits used for economic value) remain stable over time.
- **Genetic health:** The authors state that Ne reflects the genetic "health" of a population and that temporal stability can be improved by increasing Ne or by monitoring it.
- **Rainbow trout baseline:** Rainbow trout have an average heterozygosity of about 6% (Allendorf and Utter 1979). A strain brought into hatchery culture from a large donor population is the illustrative case.
- **Simulation design:** The simulation used 10 polymorphic loci per population, 10 demes, binomial Monte Carlo sampling, and sample sizes from 5 to 120. Genetic diversity (H) ranged from 1% to 10%, and generations from 1 to 20. Survey sampling error was excluded by design, since the aim was to model drift itself.

## Key Findings

1. **Method validation (simulation):** The theoretical genetic distances (Equation 4) agreed well with observed drift-induced distances across the tested ranges of Ne, H (1 to 10%), and generations (1 to 20).
2. **Scaling with diversity and time:** The Ne needed to hold a given drift distance increases almost linearly with average genetic diversity and with the number of generations. Variance of the estimate also increases with H because the proportion of polymorphic loci rises.
3. **Distribution:** Interdeme genetic distances approximated a normal distribution at all tested levels of H (1 to 10%) and generations (1 to 20). The authors expect the central limit theorem to apply and recommend many loci for accurate Ne and breeding-structure estimates. They did not test smaller or larger numbers of loci or alleles.
4. **Numerical example (rainbow trout):** Four strains of rainbow trout held by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service had electrophoretic characterizations repeated one generation apart by independent groups, using a subset of 10 polymorphic loci.
   - Ne was estimated four ways. Results are in Table 1, with rows Winter Standard, Winter Formalin, Fish Lake, and New Zealand. Table values as printed: Winter Standard 21, 14, 59, 40; Winter Formalin 5, 6, 7, 8; Fish Lake 26, 31, 26, 40; New Zealand 3, 3, 2, 3. Column headers are not legible in the scan.
   - For Winter Standard, two estimates were substantially higher than the others. The authors attribute this to sensitivity of the F-based estimates to unusually large gene frequency deviations at the AGP-1 and SOD-1 loci (data not shown). Agreement between the other estimates was generally good.
5. **Drift threshold guideline:** If drift-induced genetic distance is to increase by no more than 0.0005 per generation:
   - An Ne of at least 64 is needed to maintain strain integrity for one generation.
   - To extend the "genetic lifetime" to 20 generations, an Ne of at least 1271 is required (value as printed; scan quality makes this figure uncertain). The authors note this is far above the range recommended in the literature for long-term maintenance (printed as "So to soo," OCR-unclear).
6. **Critique of existing guidelines:** Literature estimates are based on arbitrary fixation rates and consider mainly allele loss. The authors propose conserving the particular array of allele frequencies that characterizes a population, not simply preserving alleles regardless of frequency.
7. **Practical recommendation:** Temporal stability of characterized or economically valued populations can be improved by raising Ne to an appropriate level and by monitoring Ne over time.
