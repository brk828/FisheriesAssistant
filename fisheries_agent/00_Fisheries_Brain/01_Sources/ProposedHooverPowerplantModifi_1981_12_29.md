## Executive Summary
This document is an inter-agency memorandum from the Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, dated December 1981, regarding the Hoover Powerplant Modification Project and its Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act. The core scientific content concerns competing hypotheses about the population decline and recruitment failure of the [[Bonytail Chub]] in [[Lake Mohave]], and whether age-structure data from the co-occurring [[Razorback Sucker]] can serve as a valid proxy for bonytail chub population dynamics. The memo also addresses concerns about thermal stratification and cold-water discharge effects from Hoover Dam operations on spawning habitat.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, body measurements) are provided in this administrative correspondence. The document instead references **age structure studies** (aging methodology, presumably via otolith or fin-ray analysis) being conducted by Dr. W.L. Minckley on both species as a proxy for demographic/recruitment history rather than physical morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both species inhabit [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Colorado River formed by [[Davis Dam]] (closed 1951), located downstream of [[Hoover Dam]].
- **Thermal stratification**: Lake Mohave exhibits a thermocline; water temperature variance is depth-dependent.
  - Above 10 m depth: ~1°C weekly variance expected (per UNLV 1978 data) during the May–June spawning period.
  - Below 10 m depth (below the thermocline): potential temperature fluctuations up to 3°C weekly due to cold-water releases from Hoover Dam following the historical Colorado River channel contour.
- **Spawning period**: May through June is identified as the critical spawning window for these species in Lake Mohave.
- **Species status differentiation**:
  - [[Bonytail Chub]]: Federally listed (endangered), presumed to have failed recruitment in Lake Mohave.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: Not federally listed at time of writing; successfully hatches (recruits) in Lake Mohave, suggesting different habitat and spawning requirements than the bonytail chub.
- **Competing recruitment hypotheses**:
  - **Habitat modification hypothesis**: Closure of Davis Dam (1951) altered riverine habitat conditions, eliminating bonytail chub recruitment.
  - **Biotic interaction hypothesis (Minckley)**: Competitive and predatory pressure from **non-native fish species** introduced into Lake Mohave — rather than physical habitat change — may be the primary driver of native fish decline. Under this hypothesis, limited bonytail recruitment could have persisted after 1951 until non-native fish populations reached carrying capacity/"optimum level" in the mid-1960s, after which recruitment ceased.

## Key Findings
- The Bureau of Reclamation agreed to extend the formal Section 7 consultation deadline from January 8, 1982 to March 31, 1982 to allow completion of [[Dr. Minckley]]'s aging studies on bonytail chub and razorback sucker.
- The Bureau explicitly disputes the scientific validity of using [[Razorback Sucker]] age-structure data as a basis for [[Bonytail Chub]] consultation, citing the razorback's differing (non-listed) conservation status, successful natural hatching in Lake Mohave, and likely divergent habitat/spawning requirements.
- The Bureau challenges the assumption that Davis Dam closure (1951) directly and solely eliminated bonytail chub recruitment, favoring further consideration of Minckley's non-native species competition/predation hypothesis.
- If correct, this hypothesis implies the current Lake Mohave bonytail chub population may be **younger** than previously assumed (i.e., recruitment continued into the 1960s) but recruitment has still failed since that time.
- Predicted operational changes from the Hoover Dam Modification Project are expected to have **no significant impact** on the lake's thermal stratification regime in the upper water column (<10 m), since cold-water discharges follow the pre-existing river channel contour.
- Deeper spawning (>10 m, below the thermocline) could expose bonytail chub eggs/larvae to greater weekly thermal fluctuations (up to 3°C) as a result of dam-related cold-water releases — a potential ecological risk factor for [[Bonytail Chub]] recruitment success.
- Key contacts/researchers referenced: [[Dr. W.L. Minckley]] (aging studies), Gordon Mueller (Bureau of Reclamation, Lower Colorado Region).
