# California's Endangered and Rare Fish and Wildlife (1972 Report): At the Crossroads

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

*At the Crossroads* is the January 1972 inaugural report of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (DFG) to Governor Ronald Reagan on the state's endangered and rare fish and wildlife. It was prepared under the [[California Species Preservation Act]] of 1970 and the [[California Endangered Species Act]], which require biennial inventories and recommendations to the Governor and Legislature. The report lists species that the [[Fish and Game Commission]] declared endangered or rare on May 21, 1971, based on Department inventories, criteria, and expert review of 129 candidate life forms. It states that 43 forms of nongame fish and wildlife are threatened with extinction or endangerment, and it argues that these species cannot survive without public funding and habitat stewardship.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a Governor's transmittal letter, a foreword, and a statement of legislative intent. Its legal framework defines an **endangered** animal as one whose survival and reproduction are in immediate jeopardy, and a **rare** animal as one that is in small numbers or limited range such that it may become endangered if its environment worsens. Under Fish and Game Code Section 2052, importing, possessing, or selling listed animals or their parts is prohibited.

The main body consists of species accounts. Each account covers description, distribution, status, protective measures taken, recommendations, and references, with range maps. Endangered taxa include birds, mammals, fishes, amphibians, and reptiles; rare taxa are listed in a separate section. The report notes that the 1966–1970 timeline of legislation (from the [[Endangered Species Preservation Act]] and [[Ecological Reserve Act]] to the state acts) established the legal basis for the list.

The Commission's regulation (Title 14, Section 670.5) formally declares the listed species. Endangered species total 27 entries across birds, mammals, fishes, amphibians, and reptiles. Rare species total 26 entries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames threats as habitat loss, drainage and filling of wetlands, water diversion, channelization, reservoir construction, competition or predation by introduced species (such as [[Mosquitofish]], house cats, and old-world rats), pesticide contamination, and human disturbance. It highlights [[DDT]] and other persistent pesticides as a food-chain problem for fish-eating birds, which show thin-shelled egg failure, and for raptors such as the [[Peregrine Falcon]].

Fish-related accounts emphasize the Colorado River system, the Owens Valley and Death Valley drainages, the Mojave River, and the Klamath basin. Several fishes, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail]], may already be extinct in California. Salamanders and toads are restricted to very small springs, seeps, or limestone outcrops.

The report also stresses the broader ecological value of endangered species, citing the genetic and evolutionary information that each species holds.

## Key Findings

- **Policy basis:** The state's legal authority for species protection is [[Fish and Game Code]] Sections 900–903 and 2050–2052. The first biennial report was due by January 1, 1972.
- **Listed species count:** The Commission listed 27 endangered and 26 rare species/subspecies, drawn from 129 candidate life forms reviewed.
- **Endangered examples:**
  - [[California Condor]]: about 50 birds (slightly more than 50), declining from low recruitment and habitat loss; condors breed at 5–6 years and lay one egg every two years.
  - [[California Brown Pelican]]: 32,000 total population; 300 pairs at Anacapa Island; only 7 young produced from 600 nesting attempts in California in 1971.
  - [[American Peregrine Falcon]]: breeding population fell from 100 pairs in the 1940s to 10 birds in 1970 (2 pairs produced 4 young).
  - [[Southern Bald Eagle]]: 40 recorded nest sites; 15 active in 1971, producing 14 young; 87 bald eagles counted in the 1971 Midwinter Eagle Inventory.
  - [[California Least Tern]]: 300 pairs in 15 sites in 1970.
  - [[Morro Bay Kangaroo Rat]]: range reduced from 4.8 to 1.7 square miles; about 3,000 animals.
  - [[Owens Pupfish]] and [[Tecopa Pupfish]]: restricted to small areas; Tecopa pupfish last known from one artificial pond and creek.
  - [[Desert Slender Salamander]]: known only from Hidden Palm Canyon, Riverside County.
- **Rare examples:**
  - [[San Joaquin Kit Fox]]: 1,000–3,000 animals.
  - [[California Bighorn Sheep]]: about 250 animals in the Sierra Nevada.
  - [[Peninsular Bighorn Sheep]]: about 400 animals in California.
  - [[Guadalupe Fur Seal]]: 600 in 1965; breeding colony on Guadalupe Island, Mexico, slowly increasing.
  - [[Wolverine]]: 15 reported in 1933; recent sightings indicate increasing numbers.
- **Clapper rails:** Southern San Francisco Bay population of the [[California Clapper Rail]] is 1,300 pairs; [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] exceeds 300 pairs along the Colorado River.
- **Protective actions taken:** Full protection under state law for most listed taxa; sanctuaries and refuges (Sespe and Sisquoc condor sanctuaries, Huntington Beach tern sanctuary, Fish Slough for Owens pupfish); DDT restrictions for household and garden use; DFG discontinued use of 1080 poison by federal agencies in July 1970 (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife).
- **Recommendations (common themes):**
  1. Public funds to share the burden of preserving non-hunted wildlife.
  2. Research into current status and survival requirements.
  3. Acquisition, easement, or stewardship of critical habitat.
  4. Immediate species-specific protection and management programs.
  5. Pollution control, especially pesticides and sewer outfalls.
  6. Ecological reserves, national wildlife refuges, and monitoring programs.
- **Conclusion:** The report concludes that endangered wildlife "cannot survive on their own" in today's world, and that continued survival depends on public support and funding. It explicitly rejects extinction as inevitable, citing earlier predictions of extinction for species that remain extant.
