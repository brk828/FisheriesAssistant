# The Plankton of North Twin Lake, with Particular Reference to the Summer of 1955

#limnology #freshwater-biology #plankton

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by [[Joseph H Kutkuhn]] (Iowa State College) describes the plankton of [[North Twin Lake]], a shallow, wind-exposed prairie lake in [[Calhoun County Iowa]]. Based on 59 samples collected from 1951 to 1956, the paper provides an annotated checklist of phytoplankton, protozoa, rotifers and microcrustaceans, and evaluates the lake's trophic status. The author concludes that the lake is eutrophic, supported by both general limnological criteria and the [[Nygaard Quotient]] (12.8). The study also tests a [[Centrifuge Method]] for concentrating plankton, which captured the nannoplankton that net sampling typically misses. [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) were abundant in 1954 and 1955, and their gut contents helped document the plankton community.

## Background & Description

North Twin Lake lies in north-central Calhoun County, Iowa. In 1939 and 1940 the Iowa State Conservation Commission dredged the lake to improve recreational value. The dredged portion covers 135 acres and is 13 to 15 feet deep, while the undredged northern portion reaches a maximum depth of 6 to 8 feet. The lake is about 2.5 miles long (northeast-southwest) with a maximum area of 569 acres. During recent drought years the water level fell almost 3 feet below the outlet into South Twin Lake.

Macrophyte growth is sparse and patchy. Emergent species include [[Cattail]] (*Typha latifolia*), soft-stem bulrush (*Scirpus validus*) and river bulrush (*Scirpus fluviatilis*), all in small, scattered stands. The only submersed plant recorded was *Potamogeton filiformis*, which grew luxuriantly in spring and early summer, then declined, probably as turbidity increased. Attached filamentous algae, mainly *Cladophora* and *Stigeoclonium*, developed in the shallows.

The study was part of Project No. 1374 of the Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit, sponsored by the Iowa State Conservation Commission and Iowa State College, with the cooperation of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- Constant wind action and little shelter prevent permanent thermal stratification. Temporary stratification probably occurred but was never detected.
- Dissolved oxygen was adequate at all depths in summer, except possibly near the mud-water interface in the deep areas. Winterkill can occur during rare winters with thick, persistent ice and snow combined with low water levels.
- The water is alkaline, with pH ranging from about 7.6 to 8.4. Methane ebullition is common in deep water at the south end.
- Surface temperatures approached 85°F in exceptionally hot summers (e.g., 1955). Bottom temperatures stayed within a few degrees of surface temperatures.
- Using [[Forel-Whipple Lake Classification]], the lake falls in the Temperate Lake, Order 3 category. The author classifies it as [[Eutrophication|eutrophic]] following Welch (1952).

**Sampling methods**
- Samples from 1951 to 1954 were collected by filtering measured surface water through a plankton net. Samples from 1955 and 1956 (2-liter volumes) were taken at 6 inches depth and concentrated by [[Centrifuge Method|continuous-flow centrifugation]] to about 15 ml, then fixed with hot 10% formalin.
- Counts used a [[Sedgwick-Rafter Counting Cell]] following American Public Health Association (1946) procedures. Phytoplankton and protozoa were counted at 210X and larger zooplankton at 100X.
- Most samples came from littoral areas, but the author judged that limnetic samples contained largely the same forms.
- Centrifugation captures [[Nannoplankton]] (roughly 5 to 60 microns), which make up much of the standing crop. Its drawbacks include low efficiency for gas-vacuolate blue-greens such as *Microcystis* and *Aphanizomenon*, and concentration of all detritus, which complicates counting.

**Trophic assessment**
The [[Nygaard Quotient]] (Myxophyceae + Chlorococcales + Centrales + Euglenida divided by Desmidiaceae) was calculated as (35 + 72 + 8 + 26) / 11 = 12.8. According to Nygaard, this indicates distinct eutrophication and possibly some contamination. The author stresses that quotients should supplement other limnological data, not replace them.

**Trophic role of gizzard shad**
Gizzard shad were very abundant in 1954 and 1955. The author notes that their guts contained a remarkably large number of plankton taxa, so the fish served as an informal net sampler. Species found only in shad guts are marked in the original checklists and are often tychoplanktonic (occasionally planktonic) forms.

## Key Findings

**1. Species inventory**
- Taxa were identified to species where possible. Totals for the 1951 to 1956 period: 236 phytoplankton species (Cyanophyta 35, Chlorophyta 93, Chrysophyta 62, Mastigophora 43, Fungi 3), 122 zooplankton species (Protozoa 44, Rotatoria 49, Crustacea 20, other 9), for 358 species in all.
- The most important blue-green in 1955 was [[Aphanocapsa Delicatissima]], "very common" and at times the bulk of the phytoplankton. Counts reached 19,900 in August to early October. It never formed a bloom.
- [[Microcystis Aeruginosa]], a recognised nuisance species in this lake, was markedly reduced in 1955, with its highest volume on June 24.
- Common green algae included *Oocystis*, *Pediastrum*, *Scenedesmus*, *Coelastrum* and *Golenkinia*. Peak green algal development occurred in October.

**2. Volumetric composition (1955, Table 2)**
- Phytoplankton made up 84.3% of total plankton volume and zooplankton 15.7%, a phytoplankton:zooplankton ratio of 5.4:1.
- Diatoms contributed the most volume (32.5%), dominated by *Stephanodiscus niagarae* and *Melosira granulata* in spring.
- Blue-greens were second (30.9%), peaking in midsummer.
- Flagellates contributed 5.2%, mainly *Euglena* (especially *E. tripteris* and *E. gracilis*), *Phacus* and *Glenodinium quadridens*.
- Within zooplankton, [[Rotifera]] made up 54.9% (8.6% of total plankton). Protozoa made up 27.9% of zooplankton, and Crustacea 16.4%.

**3. Zooplankton patterns**
- Five rotifer genera dominated: *Anuraeopsis*, *Brachionus*, *Filinia*, *Polyarthra* and *Trichocerca*.
- *Cyclops bicuspidatus* was the most common planktonic crustacean, including larval stages. It was the dominant zooplankter in early spring 1956 as well.
- Cladocerans (*Bosmina longirostris*, *Daphnia longispina*, *D. pulex*) and *Diaptomus oregonensis* were important in early spring and early summer but declined in midsummer. The author suggests this is typical of hot summers, while noting that sampling may have under-recorded large crustaceans.
- Sarcodina were prominent in protozoa, with *Difflugia* spp. the most frequently occurring group.

**4. Seasonal cycle**
Samples show the typical annual cycle: high zooplankton and low phytoplankton in early spring, followed by a sharp fall in zooplankton and high phytoplankton (chiefly blue-greens) in summer. Tripton (non-living suspended material) averaged about three times the volume of living plankton (bioseston).

**5. Methodological conclusions**
- Net sampling underestimates consumers relative to producers, because nannoplankton slip through. Centrifugation avoids this bias and gave a phytoplankton-dominated volume ratio that is typical of such lakes.
- Crustacean volumes may be under-sampled, since larger water volumes would reduce error for these bulky but less numerous organisms. The author judges that correcting this would not greatly change the p:z ratio.
- Duplicate centrifugation left no residual material, and the author found no clear loss of plankton forms.

**6. Conclusion**
The author concludes that North Twin Lake is eutrophic, based on the Nygaard Quotient and general lake characteristics. Its plankton is dominated by blue-green and green algae in summer and by diatoms in spring, with rotifers the major zooplankton group. The author argues that centrifugation provides a more representative picture of the plankton than net sampling.
