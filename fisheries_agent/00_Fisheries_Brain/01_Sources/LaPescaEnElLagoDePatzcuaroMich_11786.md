# Artisanal Fishing on Lake Pátzcuaro and Its Regional Economic Importance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae #goodeidae

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language study, written by Aurelio Solórzano Preciado and produced with the Estación Limnológica de Pátzcuaro (under the Comisión para el Fomento de la Piscicultura Rural), documents the artisanal fishery of [[Lake Pátzcuaro]] in Michoacán, Mexico, and its economic role for island and shoreline communities. It describes the three net types in use, the boats and crews, marketing and prices, and catch records for 1939–1953. The author argues that falling lake levels, intensive capture of undersized fish and the lack of enforced mesh and closed-season rules threaten the fishery. The study recommends mesh and seasonal regulations, diversification of livelihoods, and possibly aquaculture of more valuable exotic species.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Lake Pátzcuaro is a mature, closed (endorheic) basin lake. It probably once formed part of the Lerma river system with [[Lake Zirahuén]] and [[Lake Cuitzeo]]. Its inflowing streams have been diverted for domestic and irrigation use, and it has been losing water for roughly 14–15 years. The author notes former islands, such as Jaracuaro and the Urandén settlements, now joined to the mainland.

**Fish fauna.** The author lists four families:
- **Atherinidae:** [[Pescado Blanco]] (*Chirostoma estor*), the prized local fish, and [[Charal]] (*Chirostoma bartoni*). Both are considered native and possibly of marine origin. The charal is eaten whole.
- **Goodeidae:** five species, including *Goodea luitpoldi* (the largest and most abundant, local name "chegua"), *Neoophorus diazi* ("choromu"), and *Allotoca vivipara*, *Alloophorus robustus* and *Skiffia lermae* (grouped as "tiru").
- **Cyprinidae:** [[Algansea Lacustris]] (acimara, or "sardina"), one representative, reaching about 220 mm.
- **Centrarchidae:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*, "huro salmonado", locally miscalled "trucha"), introduced around 1929–1930. It reached up to 600 mm and 3–4 kg.

Local names change with body length. Pescado blanco is called *tripilla* below about 80 mm, *sapichu* at 60–80 mm, and *curucha* above 170 mm (maximum 400 mm). The aquatic urodele *Bathysiredon dumerili* (achoque) is also collected and sold.

**Fishing gears.** Three gear types dominate (details in Key Findings):
- [[Chinchorro Net]]: a seine (surrounding net) used mainly by island fishers.
- [[Cherémicua Gillnet]]: a gillnet set as a barrier, with two mesh sizes.
- [[Mariposa Net]]: a small hand-operated lift net.

Historically, the author suggests fishing was pre-Hispanic and rudimentary (spears and traps). Nets such as the mariposa may derive from Spanish influence introduced during the colonial period.

**Study aims.** The study asks: (1) What is the state of the fishery? (2) How important is fishing to the regional economy and how is its benefit shared? (3) What regulatory basis could raise yields?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology.** Lake level decline is the main concern. Rainfall fluctuations contribute, but deforestation and water extraction for domestic, agricultural and pumping use also reduce inflow. Evaporation adds to the loss.
- **Habitat change.** Rooted vegetation (*Potamogeton foliosus* and *P. lucens*) is spreading across muddy shallows, narrowing navigation channels. Shallowing favours species adapted to shallow, muddy habitats with abundant detritus, while the deep-living pescado blanco is expected to become limited.
- **Trophic effects.** Lower volume and depth reduce the primary productivity that drives the food chain to commercial fish. Physical, chemical and biological conditions shift, harming some species and benefiting others.
- **Spawning.** Pescado blanco and charal aggregate to spawn in shallow areas in January–April (acimara in January–April near Santa Fé and Chupicuaro). Domestic pigs reportedly destroy embryos on the spawning grounds at Punta Santiago.
- **Predation pressure from introduced bass.** The bass population was heavily fished after it was judged a threat to native species. Its catch was reduced to a minimal commercial level by about 1951.

## Key Findings

**Gear and fleet (census):**
- 171 [[Chinchorro Net]] seines, 128 [[Cherémicua Gillnet]] gillnets and 184 [[Mariposa Net]] nets were counted.
- Chinchorros: typically 10–12 panels, 200–300 m long, a bag of about 14–15 m, and a mesh of about 1.5–2.0 cm per side at the wings. Cost was $1,500–$2,500 each.
- Cherémicuas: a "parada" (four panels) costs $60–$90 per panel. Mesh is 2.0–2.5 cm for pescado blanco and 1.0–1.5 cm for charal. Lengths are 40–50 m and 25–30 m, respectively.
- Mariposas: built locally for $18–$20 (one to two weeks' work) or bought for $60–$70. Mesh is 1.0–1.5 cm.
- Dugout canoes of pine cost $700–$1,000. Crews of four row chinchorro boats.

**Distribution by community:**
- Janitzio holds 33.3% of chinchorros, Pacanda 9.3%, San Jerónimo 8.7%, Tarerio 9.9% and Ichupio 7.5%.
- For cherémicuas: Janitzio 19.5%, Yunuén 17.2%, and Pacanda and Tecuena 7.8% each.
- Cucuchucho has 36.9% of mariposas. The Urandén communities together hold 20.1%.

**Fishing practice:**
- Fishing is nearly year-round. Closed seasons exist but are not respected, out of economic necessity.
- The night shift (from about 5–6 pm to 7 am) is the most intense.
- A chinchorro set takes 45–60 minutes to haul. Canoe crews can make about 12 lances per night.
- Owners receive half of each day's catch, and the remaining half is split equally among crew.

**Catch and yield:**
- A chinchorro crew catches about 20–25 kg in roughly 12 hours, mostly pescado blanco in deeper sites. The author estimates 3–4 kg of pescado blanco and 1–2 kg of other species per crew member.
- July–September is the most productive period for chinchorro, when *tripilla* (pescado blanco fry) is landed.
- Catch per unit effort of tripilla has declined. The author records about 0.25 kg per fisher per day in the previous year, versus 2–4 kg in isolated cases in 1954.
- Cherémicuas are the most size-selective gear, catching mainly adult spawners. Some Janitzio fishers reportedly take 5–6 kg per day in the early months of the year.

**Market and prices:**
- Pescado blanco fetches $8–$18 per dozen (up to $30 during Lent). Fresh charal sells for $0.50–$1.80/kg, dried charal for $4–$9/kg from intermediaries. Dried fish at Pátzcuaro market reaches $10–$11/kg. Large bass sells for $2–$3/kg.
- Dried-salted fish is the main processed product, often sold to Mexico City and interior markets.
- Main markets are Pátzcuaro (Friday the busiest), Erongaricuaro and Quiroga. Morelia and Uruapan are minor outlets.

**Catch records 1939–1953:**
- Dried charal is the largest shipped product: 399,809 kg, valued at $1,959,261.00. Of this, 59.9% (239,606 kg) falls in the final four years, which the author attributes more to better record-keeping than to higher extraction.
- Fresh bass shipped 1939–1951: 175,600 kg, valued at $166,725.00. Peak catch was 143,400 kg (81.6%) in 1941–1945, declining sharply after 1946.
- The table's stated total value is $2,571,249.50. The author warns that the charal label mixes in many juvenile pescado blanco.

**Other livelihoods:**
- Waterfowl hunting (for example gallareta and patos such as *Anas acuta*) supplements incomes around the Urandén communities, mostly in winter.
- Tule craft is practised by "trashumant" artisans who combine it with fishing.
- Pottery is important in Ihuatzio, Tzintzuntzan and Santa Fé de la Laguna, where fishing is not economically significant.
- Agriculture is growing on newly exposed lake beds. Island communities depend on fishing year-round.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- Fishing is the primary livelihood for the islands (Janitzio, Tecuena, Yunuén, Pacanda), and for shoreline towns it is mainly a food source and supplementary income.
- Lake shallowing and siltation are reducing the yields of commercially valuable species. Overfishing of juveniles and spawners prevents population recovery.
- Recommended measures:
  1. Enforce seasonal closures (charal and pescado blanco) and regulate mesh sizes, updated by season.
  2. Create alternative income for fishers, which the author sees as a prerequisite for any regulation to work.
  3. Involve institutions in designing rules that account for social and economic conditions.
  4. Consider aquaculture introduction of more valuable exotic species if native habitat quality keeps falling.
  5. Base timing on sufficient observation, research and experimentation.
