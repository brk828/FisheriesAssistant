# Decomposition of Marsh Grass by Aerobic Marine Bacteria

#marine-biology #microbiology #spartina-alterniflora

## Executive Summary

This document is a student reading note (dated April 17, 1973) on a 1957 study by [[Burkholder And Bornside (1957)]] published in the *Bulletin of the Torrey Botanical Club*. The study examines how aerobic, heterotrophic marine bacteria break down the cordgrass [[Spartina Alterniflora]] in salt marshes near Sapelo Island, Georgia. It addresses field and laboratory decomposition, bacterial growth on grass and mud extracts, and bacterial nutrient requirements. The main conclusions are that marine bacteria actively decompose Spartina, that roughly 11% of the annual grass crop may become available as bacterial biomass, and that the isolates did not require specific single amino acids. Marsh mud was a poor growth medium unless enriched with organic material.

## Background & Description

**Study focus.** [[Spartina Alterniflora]] is a major source of organic matter in [[Salt Marsh]] systems. Its residues, after microbial action, become more readily usable by larger marsh and estuarine fauna and flora. The authors aimed to demonstrate aerobic decomposition of marsh grass by [[Marine Bacteria]] in the Sapelo Island region.

**Methods overview.**
- **Field decomposition:** Fresh grass sheets cut at mud level were placed in crates and submerged in shallow water for 300 days. Residual dry matter was measured by periodically removing crates and air-drying them. The crates were placed in the Duplin River from November 28, 1955 to September 19, 1956.
- **Laboratory decomposition:** Bacteria-inoculated flasks of mature dried grass were incubated for 24 days and their residues measured. Flasks were shaken at 30 °C.
- **Bacterial isolation:** Several aerobic heterotrophs were isolated from marsh mud, decomposing grass, and estuarine water. These included many species of [[Pseudomonas]] and one species each of [[Serratia]], [[Aerobacter]], and [[Flavobacterium]].
- **Growth experiments:** Isolates were tested on grass extracts at different concentrations, on mud and enriched mud extracts, and on chemically defined media with amino acids and carbon sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal decomposition.** In the field, decomposition was slow in winter and faster in spring and summer. By June, the leaves had completely disintegrated. The authors proposed that leaves are broken down by bacterial enzymes first, while the cellulose and [[Lignin]] in the stems are broken down later. They also suggested that mechanical fragmentation and animal feeding increase dry matter loss, though these processes were not measured in the study.

**Laboratory dynamics.** In the flask experiments, dry matter loss was rapid during the first 10 days and then slowed.

**Nutrient sources.** Mud alone supported little bacterial growth, which the authors attributed to its "spent" nature. Casein hydrolysate- and grass extract-enriched muds both promoted growth. Grass extract appeared to supply the substrate needed for mud organisms to grow. Among carbon sources, [[Glycerol]], [[Succinate]], and [[Citrate]] were readily available to these bacteria, with [[Dextrose]] and starch used to a lesser degree.

**Nitrogen sources.** Growth was better with amine nitrogen than with inorganic nitrogen, such as ammonium salts. Growth on single amino acids was generally less pronounced than on mixtures. Casein-derived amino acids supported excellent growth, as did a mixture of [[Alanine]], [[Aspartic Acid]], and [[Glutamic Acid]], although these were poorly used when supplied individually.

## Key Findings

1. **Active decomposition:** Aerobic, heterotrophic marine bacteria actively took part in breaking down Spartina in both field and laboratory settings.
2. **Estimated bacterial conversion:** About **11%** of the annual crop of marsh grass (dry weight basis) may be rapidly converted into bacteria.
3. **Feed quality:** The authors suggested that bacterial conversion of substrates may produce better-quality food for marine fauna with demanding nutritional requirements.
4. **Amino acid requirements:** The isolates had no specific requirement for single amino acids. Growth was better on mixtures of amino acids and on hydrolysed casein than on single amino acids. The authors proposed that the amino acid needs of these bacteria are probably met mainly by marsh grass in the natural environment.
5. **Mud as a medium:** Marsh mud was a poor medium for aerobic heterotrophic bacteria unless it was enriched with organic materials such as casein hydrolysate or grass extract.
6. **Growth on extracts:** Growth was greatest at the highest grass extract concentrations, except for *Serratia*.
7. **Seasonal timing:** Decomposition was slowest in winter and fastest in spring and summer, with leaves fully broken down by June.
