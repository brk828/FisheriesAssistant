# Monofilament vs. Multifilament Nylon Gill Net Efficiency for Lake Whitefish in Lake Huron

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonus

## Executive Summary

This study compares the catching efficiency of [[Monofilament Nylon]] and [[Multifilament Nylon]] gill nets for [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*) in [[Lake Huron]], using commercial-type 114 mm mesh nets fished in the [[North Channel]] (1972) and [[South Bay]] (1973). Monofilament nets caught about 1.8 times as many whitefish as multifilament nets, a difference that varied seasonally. The gear change of the early 1970s therefore inflated commercial catch statistics and probably intensified fishing pressure. Both gears selected fish of similar size, and catches in both declined during full moon, which the author attributes most plausibly to increased net visibility, although reduced fish activity is also possible.

## Background & Description

Lake Huron fishermen converted their whitefish gill nets from multifilament to monofilament nylon web in the early 1970s. This was the first major gill-net change since multifilament nylon replaced cotton web in the early 1950s. Earlier catch comparisons for [[Pacific Salmon]] and other marine species had shown monofilament web to be more efficient only for certain species, so the author set out to test relative efficiency for whitefish and associated species using nets typical of the commercial fishery.

**Gear specifications:**
- Mesh size: 114 mm (stretched measure), typical of whitefish nets in the North Channel in 1972.
- Monofilament twine: nominal size 0.23 mm.
- Multifilament twine: 210 denier, two threads with 25 strands each.
- Hanging ratio: about 50% (100 m of twine on 50 m of side line).
- Net dimensions: 91.4 m long, 30 meshes deep.

Relevant gear concepts include [[Gill Net Selectivity]] and [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CUE), defined here as number of fish per km of net set for one night.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Two field studies provided the data:

1. **North Channel, 1972 (May–October):** Experimental gangs of four alternating mono-multi panels (366 m) were incorporated into a fisherman's regular strings, which averaged 2,100 m. Nets were set in the afternoon and lifted the next morning in 57 trials, with 15 trials left out for two nights. A total of 13.2 km of each net type was lifted.
2. **South Bay, 1973 (April–November):** Two 366 m gangs, one of each type, were set overnight at paired sites in the inner and outer basins 54 times (18.8 km of each gear). Gear types were alternated between sites. Same-day catches were not directly comparable because whitefish were 66% more abundant at one site of each pair (chi-square, P < 0.001), so monofilament catches were compared with the most recent multifilament catch at the same site.

Water conditions were recorded at each set, including temperature, depth, and duration. Water transparency, measured by [[Secchi Disc]], ranged from 4.0 to 10.0 m, with maxima in midsummer.

The author also analyzed long-term commercial data from two North Channel fishermen: 796 multifilament lifts (1964–1968) and 845 monofilament lifts (1972–1976). Some multifilament nets (less than 20%) were still in use until 1974, so the period separation is approximate.

**Lunar light context:** Surface illuminance at full moon (zenith) is about 0.2 lx, roughly sevenfold brighter than at quarter phases ([[Lunar Illuminance]]). A study of [[Atlantic Herring]] estimated a net-avoidance light threshold of 0.006 lx, and light at about 0.2 lx penetrated to around 25 m in ocean water.

**Associated species:** Whitefish dominated the catches. Among 22 other species taken, the most numerous were [[Rainbow Smelt]], [[White Sucker]], [[Longnose Sucker]], [[Alewife]], [[Splake]], and [[Burbot]].

## Key Findings

**1. Monofilament nets caught more whitefish.**
- Grand total whitefish catch was 1,063 in monofilament vs. 598 in multifilament nets (ratio 1.78).
- North Channel ratio: 1.9. South Bay ratio: 1.7.
- Overall, monofilament catches were 78% higher.
- Catch ratios were essentially the same whether the two webs were joined in alternating panels or set in separate gangs (P > 0.10).

**2. Efficiency varied seasonally but not consistently.**
- Mono/multi ratios for whitefish peaked in May in South Bay and in June in the North Channel, but no consistent seasonal trend emerged.
- Monofilament CUE was significantly higher than multifilament CUE in South Bay (P < 0.001) and the North Channel (P < 0.05).
- Correlations between the mono/multi ratio and CUE, mean length, or depth of sets were not significant.

**3. Species responses differed.**
- Monofilament nets were less efficient for [[Rainbow Smelt]] (grand ratio 0.30) and [[White Sucker]] (0.61). Smelt tangled in the twisted strands of multifilament web and were less vulnerable to the single-strand monofilament.
- Alewife (0.73) and most other species were about equal or caught less well in monofilament.
- Longnose sucker was caught more in monofilament (1.90), but pooling of the two sucker species precludes firm conclusions.

**4. Size selectivity was similar for both gears.**
- Length-frequency distributions did not differ significantly between gears in either area (North Channel P > 0.10; South Bay P > 0.50).
- Modal length was 45 cm in the North Channel and 43 cm in South Bay (P < 0.001 when gears were combined).
- Age structure was also similar between gears.

**5. Multiple whitefish stocks were identified.**
- North Channel fish recruited to the gear at age 4 (average length 45.9 cm).
- South Bay had two stocks. The outer-basin migrant stock recruited at ages 4–5, while the inner-basin stock recruited at ages 7–9 and grew more slowly.
- Inner-basin whitefish were significantly lighter for their length than outer-basin fish (ANCOVA, F = 11.042, P < 0.01).

**6. Full moon reduced catches in both gear types.**
- Mean whitefish CUE declined during full moon in the multifilament period (P < 0.01) and the monofilament period (P < 0.05).
- Fishing effort also decreased at full moon.
- The author judged increased net illumination the most tenable explanation, since the transparent monofilament web should have been affected less than the opaque multifilament web if visibility were the main factor. Reduced fish activity under altered light levels is an alternative explanation.

**7. Management implications.**
- Monofilament web increased whitefish netting efficiency and, without compensating constraints, intensified exploitation.
- Raw CUE since the gear change overestimates whitefish abundance by 78% relative to multifilament CUE. The author applied phase-in correction factors to effort: 1.5 (1972), 1.6 (1973), 1.65 (1974), and 1.78 (1975–1976). This follows the [[Effective Effort]] approach of earlier work.
- Corrected CUE (CUEE) suggests fewer fish were vulnerable to the gear in the 1970s than in the 1960s, but the author cautions that changes in growth, recruitment age, and year-class strength must be examined before drawing that conclusion.
- Large mesh alone does not protect stocks from overfishing unless effort is also controlled. Management may need stock-specific strategies.
- Gear depth increases (to 50 meshes in 1977) may add more fishing power than the web change did, so gear improvements must be evaluated continually.

**8. Recommendation.** The author recommends evaluating gear efficiency whenever catch statistics guide management, and controlling the use of more efficient gear where whitefish stocks are heavily exploited. Surveillance and response mechanisms are proposed under the [[Ontario Fisheries Management Strategy]] (Loftus et al. 1978).
