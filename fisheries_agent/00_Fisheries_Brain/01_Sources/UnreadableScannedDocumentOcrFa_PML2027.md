# Thermal Effluent Literature, Blue Tilapia–Largemouth Bass Interactions, and Muskellunge Stocking Mortality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #centrarchidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned document bundles three components: a literature-cited list on the effects of thermal effluents on fish and aquatic invertebrates, a research paper on how an exotic [[Blue Tilapia]] population suppresses [[Largemouth Bass]] reproduction in [[Trinidad Lake]], Texas, and the opening pages of a paper on short-term [[Muskellunge]] stocking mortality tested in isolation coves. The central study found that bass recruitment ceased as tilapia densities rose, and that a pond experiment reproduced this failure at high tilapia density while allowing some spawning at intermediate density. The muskellunge paper introduces a method for measuring stocking mortality with a block-net and SCUBA observations. The muskellunge results are not included in the excerpt.

## Background & Description

**Reference list.** The bibliography contains roughly three dozen works, mostly from the 1950s to the 1970s. They cover thermal discharges from power plants, fish distribution in cooling reservoirs, fish diversity indices, and fish physiology. Examples include Gammon's studies of the [[Wabash River]] thermal inputs (1971, 1973), Trembley's condenser discharge research at Lehigh University (1960), and Wrenn's work on the Colbert Steam Plant on the Tennessee River (1974).

**Blue tilapia and bass study.** Blue tilapia (*Oreochromis aureus*, cited as *T. aurea*) were inadvertently introduced into Trinidad Lake in the late 1960s. Their population grew to nearly 2000 lb per acre (unpublished data). Northern largemouth bass recruitment appeared to stop, and stocked Florida largemouth bass produced no successful spawns after 1972, despite high survival, good growth, and rapid maturity. The stated objectives were to analyze lake population and reproductive data and to test in ponds whether high tilapia densities inhibit bass reproduction. The work was part of Texas Agricultural Experiment Station Project 1896 and was funded by the Texas Power and Light Company.

**Muskellunge stocking study.** Fingerling muskellunge are stocked periodically by state agencies to maintain year-classes. Hatchery fish make up a large share of the catch in some waters. Reported creel contributions were 2% to 60% in Wisconsin lakes (Johnson 1969), an average of up to 63% in [[Little Green Lake]] (Hacker 1966), and 88.5% of yearling fish sampled at [[Lake Chautauqua]] over four years (Mooradian and Shepherd 1973). Some states have recorded initial stocking mortality as high as 70%. The study used [[Isolation Cove]] enclosures in [[Pomme de Terre Reservoir]], Missouri, an 8,000-acre (3238 ha) flood-control impoundment with clear water and Secchi readings commonly of about 6 feet or more. Muskellunge were first stocked there in 1966 and planted yearly through 1974, except in one year that the scan does not make clear.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trinidad Lake.** The lake is a 303 ha cooling reservoir for the Texas Power and Light Company steam station. The main body covers 237 ha and averages 3 m deep. Open-water temperatures range seasonally from about 10 to 35 °C. Thermal stratification is infrequent, and oxygen is generally near saturation. The lake first filled in 1925, exceeds pH 9, and is highly eutrophic. The main fish species are blue tilapia, channel catfish, gizzard shad, carp, freshwater drum, and bluegill. Threadfin shad are abundant in seine samples. Predators besides bass include longnose gar and spotted gar. Blue tilapia, a subtropical species, survives winter by concentrating in the heated effluent of the power plant. This makes the cooling reservoir a thermal refuge, and it may help explain how the population persisted and expanded.

**Competition and spawning.** The authors proposed several mechanisms for reduced bass spawning. Tilapia and bass may compete for nest sites, since tilapia built many nests in the high-density pond. A repressive factor released by forage species at high density, reported for centrarchids by [[Chew 1972]], is another possibility. Unequal sex ratios in the pond may also have played a role. Tilapia also serve as important forage that supports the rapid growth of largemouth bass in Trinidad Lake.

**Sampling methods.** The study used [[Gillnet Sampling]] of ten sites, sampled monthly from January 1973. The two net types used were 150 ft nets with 1.0 to 3.5 in mesh and 130 ft nets with 2.5 to 4.0 in mesh. Fish were measured for length and weight, and sex and maturity were recorded. Ovaries from Florida bass were collected from February to July 1975. The [[Gonosomatic Index]] was calculated as ovary weight divided by (fish weight minus ovary weight), following Kaya and Hasler (1972).

## Key Findings

**Lake population decline.** Standard fixed-site gillnet catches, with five nets per month, were as follows (1973, 1974, 1975):
- Northern largemouth bass: 22, 18, 6
- Florida largemouth bass: 4, 9, 34

All-net totals were as follows:
- Northern largemouth bass: 60, 50, 18
- Florida largemouth bass: 9, 15, 61

Northern bass catches declined to under one-third of their 1973 level, while Florida bass increased as more stocked year-classes were recruited. Length-frequency data showed few small northern bass, which supports the conclusion that their recruitment failed. The authors linked this to suppressed reproduction, possibly caused by the tilapia population that developed in the early 1970s.

**Pond experiment (1975).** Three half-acre ponds were stocked with adult tilapia (mean weight 0.4 lb) at 0, 500, and 1000 lb (Ponds 1, 2, and 3). Ten adult Florida bass were added on 21 March 1975, followed by 500 threadfin shad per pond.
- **Pond 1 (no tilapia):** Schools of bass fry were seen within a month. A seine sample on 10 May contained 35 bass fingerlings averaging 35 mm. This pond was not drained because of the high density of young bass. Eight of the 10 adults were recovered.
- **Pond 2 (intermediate tilapia):** Draining produced 2007 bass fingerlings (mean 51 mm). Adult tilapia recovered totaled 409 lb, and 208 tilapia nests were counted.
- **Pond 3 (lake-level tilapia):** No bass fingerlings were produced. Adult tilapia recovered totaled 976 lb, and 516 tilapia nests were counted, with no bass nests in either pond.

The authors note that Pond 3 was more turbid, probably from tilapia nesting. The scanned text is ambiguous about whether Pond 1 received tilapia, so the design should be checked against the original.

**Gonosomatic indices.** Mean indices for the ponds were 0.0153 for Pond 1 and 0.0222 for Pond 2, and 0.0366 for Pond 3 (May–June). Lake values for Florida bass were 0.0536 (February), 0.0650 (March), 0.0116 (May), 0.0056 (June), and 0.0057 (July). Ponds 1 and 2 were similar to the lake in May–June, while Pond 3 was much higher. The authors interpret the high Pond 3 value as eggs retained in the ovaries because no suitable nest sites were available, not as a sign of spawning.

**Conclusions on bass and tilapia.** The authors concluded that high tilapia density (about 2000 lb per acre in the lake) prevents successful bass reproduction. At about half that density, spawning did occur. The intermediate-density result suggests that a self-sustaining bass population and a tilapia population with harvestable production could coexist, which would be useful for management of the reservoir.

**Muskellunge stocking mortality.** The paper's stated aims are to assess short-term mortality of stocked fingerlings and their behavior after release. The method released fingerlings behind a block-net in isolation coves, which allowed feeding and movement while limiting movement out of the cove. SCUBA observations made mortality readily measurable. The abstract reports that mortality from handling and transport was minimal, and it recommends stocking in areas with extensive weed cover. Quantitative mortality results are not in the excerpt.
