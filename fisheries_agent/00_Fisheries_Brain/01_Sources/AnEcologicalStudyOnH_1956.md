# Hysteromorpha triloba at Oakwood Lakes, South Dakota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Trematoda #Strigeidae #Parasitology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This study by Ernest J. Hugghins examines the ecology and life cycle dynamics of the strigeid trematode **[[Hysteromorpha Triloba]]** at East Oakwood Lake, South Dakota, building on earlier life history work conducted in Illinois. The parasite utilizes a three-host life cycle: the snail **[[Gyraulus Hirsutus]]** (first intermediate host), the **[[Black Bullhead]]** (*Ameiurus melas*, second intermediate host), and the **[[Double-Crested Cormorant]]** (*Phalacrocorax auritus*, definitive host). The research demonstrates a striking ecological dependency between definitive host presence (a cormorant rookery) and the maintenance of the parasite's transmission cycle in the snail population, with infection rates collapsing following cormorant abandonment of the nesting site.

## Background & Description
- **Life stages examined:** Cercariae (emerging from snail host) and metacercariae (encysted in fish host); adult morphology referenced from prior life-history studies (Hugghins 1953, 1954, 1956).
- **Metacercarial cysts:** Found encysted within tissues of black bullheads; cyst burden varied by individual fish.
- **Host specificity noted:**
  - First intermediate host: *Gyraulus hirsutus* (planorbid snail)
  - Second intermediate host: *Ameiurus melas* (Black Bullhead)
  - Definitive host: *Phalacrocorax auritus* (Double-crested Cormorant)
- No detailed morphometric data on the trematode itself is provided in this abstract; morphological descriptions were published in the author's earlier Illinois-based studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Geographic/Habitat context:** East Oakwood Lake, eastern South Dakota, near a nesting rookery of double-crested cormorants.
- **Snail collection & seasonality:**
  - July 1953: 194 *Gyraulus* snails collected near rookery — nearly all shed *H. triloba* cercariae.
  - Spring 1954 (through June 6): ~1,000 snails collected — **no cercarial shedding observed**, indicating the parasite did not overwinter successfully in snail hosts.
  - June 13, 1954: First snails of the season began shedding cercariae, suggesting a seasonal reinfection cycle dependent on returning definitive hosts.
- **Fish host infection data (Spring 1954):**
  - 82 **[[Black Bullhead]]** individuals examined from the lake.
  - All but 2 very small fish harbored metacercariae of *H. triloba*.
  - **Direct positive correlation between fish size and metacercarial cyst burden** — larger bullheads carried more cysts, consistent with cumulative exposure over time.
- **Definitive host diet linkage:**
  - Trautman (1951) previously found bullheads present in 41% of 157 cormorant food/diet samples from the same lake, confirming active trophic transmission from fish to bird.
- **Rookery disturbance and cycle collapse (1955):**
  - Cormorants abandoned the rookery due to development of a nearby public recreation area.
  - Snail infection rates **virtually disappeared** in the same season following cormorant departure — direct evidence of definitive host presence driving parasite transmission dynamics at the site.

## Key Findings
- **[[Hysteromorpha Triloba]]** completes a classic three-host digenean life cycle involving a planorbid snail, a freshwater fish, and a fish-eating bird.
- Infection in the snail intermediate host is **strongly seasonal** and does not persist through winter months without re-seeding from the definitive host population.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** populations in East Oakwood Lake showed near-universal infection prevalence (only 2/82 uninfected), indicating a highly efficient transmission cycle while cormorants were present.
- Metacercarial burden in bullheads scales with fish size/age, reflecting cumulative parasite acquisition over the fish's lifespan.
- The study provides a natural experiment demonstrating **top-down ecological control** of parasite transmission: removal of the definitive host (cormorant rookery abandonment due to human recreational development) led to rapid, near-total collapse of infection in the snail host population within a single season.
- Findings underscore the fragility of complex parasite life cycles to disturbances in definitive host colony stability, with direct implications for **[[Host-Parasite Coevolution]]** and wildlife disease ecology in lake ecosystems.
- Full quantitative data intended for publication in *Proceedings of the South Dakota Academy of Science*, Vol. 35 (1956).
