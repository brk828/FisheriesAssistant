# Arizona Game and Fish Non-Game Program and Native Fish Recovery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is a newspaper outdoor column, not a research paper. It describes the non-game branch of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], which is funded in part by the state income tax checkoff. The column covers native fish recovery, reintroduction plans, the status of several reptiles, amphibians, and fish, staffing, and budget needs. It also mentions unrelated items such as a new handgun, a grizzly bear film, a survival course, a bass tournament, and a fishing report. The main message is that non-game wildlife interest is growing and that the branch needs more money to protect native species.

## Background & Description

- **Source type:** Outdoor column by the outdoor editor, with photographs of a [[Great Horned Owl]], a prairie dog, and a [[Thick-Billed Parrot]] credited to the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
- **Program leader:** Terry Johnson, supervisor of the non-game branch.
- **Purpose:** The branch covers non-game species, including reptiles, amphibians, birds of prey, mammals, and native fish. Staff also run the [[Adobe Mountain Center]], which rehabilitates injured animals.
- **Staffing:** Eight full-time employees, three full-time contract employees, and four to five part-time contract employees.
- **Budget:** $566,000 this year, of which $313,000 (more than half) comes from the [[Income Tax Checkoff]]. The column notes that the checkoff money is used only for non-game programs and can draw matching [[Federal Matching Funds]].
- **Funding request:** Johnson said a budget of $1.4 million would allow a fuller non-game program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish recovery.** The column describes a [[Native Fish Recovery Program]] with wide implications for the state. Biologists conduct surveys, stockings, and follow-up work.
- Razorback suckers and bonytail chubs were surveyed in Mohave Lake on the [[Colorado River]].
- Colorado squawfish, woundfin, and razorback suckers were to be stocked in the Verde, Salt, and Gila rivers. The fish will be monitored to learn why these species declined.
- Johnson said the goal is to "isolate problems and fix them."

**Shared species with Mexico.** Several endangered fish are shared with Mexico, including the Yaqui chub, Yaqui catfish, stoneroller, and Sonoran chub. A cooperative agreement lets Arizona biologists survey in Mexico and Mexican biologists work in Arizona.

**Bullfrog impacts.** Declines of five leopard frog species and the Mexican garter snake may be caused by the non-native [[Bullfrog]]. The branch proposed unlimited bullfrog take during open season in southeastern Arizona and along the Verde and Gila rivers.

**Desert tortoise.** The [[Desert Tortoise]] was recommended for the endangered species list in California and Utah. In Arizona, the column says it is probably a subspecies and not endangered. Johnson noted that there are genetic differences but that the staff does not yet know enough about Arizona's population.

**Reintroductions.** Studies on reintroducing the [[Mexican Wolf]] and [[Grizzly Bear]] are at an early stage.

**Urban wildlife.** Most urban projects involve nuisance animals, such as javelinas in yards and great horned owls preying on pet cats. Johnson wants to work with developers, planners, and zoning commissions to use native plants that support wildlife.

**Public interest.** The column states that non-consumptive wildlife users (people who watch or photograph wildlife rather than hunt) are growing in number, while the share of hunters among outdoor enthusiasts is falling. Fishing is holding steady. The column also says that many of these users do not oppose hunting if it is biologically sound.

## Key Findings

1. **Non-game funding is limited.** The current $566,000 budget is well below the $1.4 million that Johnson says would support a full program. The checkoff supplies more than half the current funding.
2. **Checkoff matching is valuable.** State checkoff money can attract federal matching funds, so each contribution has a larger effect.
3. **Program priorities are shifting.** The branch wants to spend less time on introducing new species and more on restoring native species that are endangered or lost.
4. **Native fish recovery is active.** Stockings and monitoring are underway on the Colorado River system, with a focus on understanding why these fish declined.
5. **Binational cooperation exists.** A cooperative agreement with Mexico allows survey work by biologists from both countries.
6. **Invasive predators and competitors are a concern.** The bullfrog may be harming frogs and snakes, and the branch proposed unlimited take.
7. **Taxonomic status matters.** The desert tortoise's listing status depends on whether Arizona populations are a distinct subspecies, which the branch has not yet resolved.
8. **Public support is growing.** The column cites strong backing for school environmental study areas and continued public demand for wildlife viewing.
