# Notes on Some Captive Minnows, *Pimephales promelas* Rafinesque

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This paper by Bryan R. Burrage (1961, *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*) presents a long-term (1957–1960) captive observational study of four wild-caught [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) specimens from the Kansas River. The study documents feeding behavior, growth, courtship, spawning, parental care, and larval development in a controlled six-gallon aquarium environment. The findings contribute to and partially reconcile earlier conflicting accounts (Coyle 1930; Kraatz 1928) regarding the dietary preferences of *Pimephales* species, and provide detailed first-hand behavioral observations of nesting, courtship typology, and brood-guarding behavior in this species. The note also references the beginning of a separate, related paper on nuptial tubercle distribution in [[Catostomidae]] (sucker fishes), by Branley A. Branson, included on the final page of the source document.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Pimephales promelas* Rafinesque (Fathead Minnow), family [[Cyprinidae]].
- **Initial capture sizes** (March 1957, four specimens): 35 mm (with vertebral anomaly), 40 mm, 30 mm, 42 mm.
- **Growth and longevity observations**:
  - Specimen with vertebral anomaly: died May 1959 at 41 mm (survived ~2 years in captivity).
  - Second specimen (42 mm at capture): died August 1959 at 50 mm.
  - Adult male at death (June 4, 1960): 75 mm — indicates substantial growth and long captive survival (~3+ years from capture).
  - Adult female at death (June 4, 1960): 63 mm.
- **Larval/juvenile size progression** (from May 8, 1960 spawning event):
  - Hatchlings (May 13–14, 1960): ~4–6 mm, estimated brood size ~80 individuals.
  - May 26: range 10–15 mm ("miniature adults" in appearance).
  - June 17: range 12–19 mm, average 17 mm.
  - June 25: largest individual 22 mm.
  - July 3: range 20–26 mm, average 22–23.5 mm.
- **Breeding male characteristics**: Developed distinct nuptial/breeding coloration ("mating colours") that faded rapidly (within a day) after cessation of parental duties — consistent with hormonally-driven secondary sexual traits typical of cyprinids, related to [[Nuptial Tubercles]] phenomena described elsewhere in the source in catostomids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat (captive)**: Six-gallon aquarium, unfiltered/unaerated, split between a densely vegetated half (*Elodea*, *Sagittaria*, *Vallisneria*, sand substrate 2 in. deep) and an open-water half (thin sand layer <1 in.). A slate slab (4×4 in.) placed obliquely provided a nesting substrate. Floating *Lemna* and green crepe paper reduced glare.
- **Feeding ecology**:
  - Classified behaviorally as **bottom-feeders** and **"mud-eaters,"** consistent with Coyle's (1930) characterization.
  - Also grazed algae from tank walls and nibbled rooted vegetation.
  - **Preferred animal prey over plant matter when available** — contrasting with Coyle's (1930) conclusion that *P. promelas* consumes more plant than animal matter, but aligning more with Kraatz's (1928) findings for the congener *P. notatus*.
  - Diet composition (adults, in order of preference): copepods (*Cyclops*) > cladocerans (*Daphnia*) > ostracods and amphipods (lesser amounts) > chironomid (insect) larvae. Also readily consumed boiled egg white.
  - **Juvenile diet differed from adults**: young fish (post-May 17) fed on bottom material and small crustaceans, but preferred cladocerans (*Daphnia*) over copepods — the reverse of adult preference.
- **Reproductive ecology / spawning behavior**:
  - Nest site: excavated depression (~1 inch deep) in sand beneath the slate slab, cleaned thoroughly by the male.
  - **Three distinct courtship behaviors** observed:
    1. Passive admission of female into nest.
    2. Aggressive chasing/coercion of female into nest.
    3. "Entreating" behavior — male approaches female, faces her, then returns to nest repeatedly until she follows and enters (most complex, and only courtship type followed by observed spawning).
  - Spawning event (May 8, 1960) was comparable to spawning behavior described for the [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) by Hubbs and Cooper (1936).
  - Eggs laid on the undersurface of the slate, consistent with descriptions by Markus (1934) and Wynne-Edwards (1932/1933).
  - **Paternal care**: Male exclusively guarded eggs post-spawning, expelling the female and snails from the nest area, feeding only briefly and cautiously, remaining vigilant near the nest — classic male-only brood-guarding behavior typical of *Pimephales*.
  - Post-hatch: Male's guarding behavior and nuptial coloration rapidly declined within a day; adults subsequently lived apart from offspring until their deaths on June 4, 1960.
  - Juveniles congregated near the surface in vegetation immediately post-hatch, later transitioning to bottom-feeding by day 4 post-hatch (May 17).

## Key Findings
- *Pimephales promelas* functions as a **generalist/opportunistic feeder**, behaviorally a bottom/mud-feeder but showing a clear preference for animal prey (crustaceans, especially copepods) over plant material when both are available — nuancing the "mud-eater"/plant-dominant conclusion of Coyle (1930) and aligning more closely with Kraatz's (1928) "general feeder" model proposed for the related [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*P. notatus*).
- **Dietary shift with life stage**: adults favor copepods (*Cyclops*); juveniles favor cladocerans (*Daphnia*) — suggesting ontogenetic dietary partitioning possibly linked to prey size/capture efficiency.
- Courtship in *P. promelas* is behaviorally variable, ranging from passive to coercive to an elaborate "entreating" display; only the entreating behavior was directly linked to successful spawning in this study.
- Confirms male-exclusive parental/nest-guarding behavior, egg-laying substrate preference (undersurface of submerged flat structures), and rapid post-reproductive decline of nuptial coloration — consistent with previously published life history accounts (Markus 1934; Wynne-Edwards 1933).
- Demonstrates significant captive longevity in wild-caught *P. promelas* (over 3 years) and substantial growth (up to 75 mm), useful baseline data for aquarium-based [[Life History]] studies of small [[Cyprinidae]].
- The broader source document also includes the introduction to a separate study by Branson (1961) on **nuptial tubercle distribution in [[Catostomidae]]** (e.g., *Cycleptus*), noting tubercle patterns on the opercles, subopercles, and cheek region as taxonomically and phylogenetically informative traits — relevant comparative context for secondary sexual characteristics across [[Cypriniformes]].
