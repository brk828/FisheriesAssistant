# The Biology and Chemistry of a Warm-Water Stream

#aquatic-ecology #freshwater-biology #benthic-macroinvertebrates #water-quality #pollution-assessment

## Executive Summary

This 1968 technical report by Brehmer, Ball, and Kevern examines the biological and chemical characteristics of the [[Red Cedar River]] near [[Williamston, Michigan]], focusing on the impacts of domestic sewage pollution on water quality and benthic invertebrate communities. The study employed six sampling stations above and below sewage outfalls, conducting weekly chemical analyses (total phosphorus, ammonia nitrogen, biochemical oxygen demand) and quantitative bottom fauna surveys from April through June 1955. Results demonstrated significant increases in nutrient concentrations and altered benthic community structure immediately below pollution sources, followed by recovery and enhanced biological productivity downstream. The research provides a comprehensive assessment of sub-lethal pollution effects on warm-water stream ecosystems and validates the use of benthic invertebrates as biological indicators of water quality.

## Background & Description

The [[Red Cedar River]] originates from [[Cedar Lake]] in Livingston County, Michigan, flowing 49.25 miles through agricultural and urban areas before joining the [[Grand River]]. The drainage basin covers approximately 472 square miles of rolling agricultural land with pasture and small grain farming. Three dams create artificial impoundments at Williamston, Okemos, and East Lansing. The stream exhibits characteristics typical of warm-water systems: slightly alkaline pH (7.2-7.8), highly buffered water (100-300 ppm methyl orange alkalinity), and dissolved oxygen levels above 90% saturation during the study period. Water temperatures ranged from 12°C to 22°C during sampling.

The study area near Williamston received raw sewage and septic tank effluents from six outfalls delivering an estimated 100 gallons per minute (0.2 second-feet) into a river with mean flow of 94.7 second-feet during May 1955, creating a dilution factor of 1:474. Bottom substrates varied from fine sand in upper reaches to gravel and rocks in middle and lower sections, with occasional silt deposition. Aquatic vegetation, particularly [[Vallisneria americana]] (wild celery), grew luxuriantly in some areas.

Six sampling stations were established: Station I (control, upstream from Williamston), Station II (above Williamston Reservoir), Station III (0.3 mile below dam and sewage outfalls), Station IV (0.6 mile below dam), Station V (3.6 miles downstream), and Station VI (6.6 miles downstream). Stations were selected to represent comparable ecological conditions (Stratum I: gravel/small rocks, 12-18 inches depth) for quantitative comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses stream self-purification processes and pollution zones as described by Whipple (1948) and Brinley (1942): clean water, degradation zone, active decomposition zone, recovery zone, and cleaner water. The [[Red Cedar River]] system demonstrates these classical pollution dynamics under sub-lethal contamination conditions where dissolved oxygen remains adequate but nutrient loading and organic matter alter community structure.

Benthic invertebrate communities serve as biological indicators because they are relatively immobile, have multi-year life cycles (e.g., [[Hexagenia]] mayflies: 2 years; [[Corydalis]] dobsonfly larvae: 3 years), and must tolerate prevailing conditions or perish. The study identified 33 families of aquatic invertebrates across all stations, representing diverse ecological tolerances and feeding strategies.

The warm-water classification reflects thermal regime, chemical characteristics, and biological assemblages distinct from cold-water trout streams. Natural nutrient sources include phosphate-bearing parent material leaching and decomposition of organic matter. Anthropogenic inputs from domestic sewage significantly elevate phosphorus and nitrogen concentrations, potentially stimulating primary and secondary production downstream after initial adverse impacts near outfalls.

Agricultural practices in the watershed (pasture, small grains) and marsh drainage influence sediment loads and nutrient inputs. The relatively gradual stream gradient (2.5 feet per mile average fall) and stable banks minimize erosion. Spring floods typically occur during snowmelt when watersheds remain frozen; lowest flows occur in fall before winter precipitation.

## Key Findings

**Water Chemistry Impacts:**

- **Total Phosphorus**: Increased significantly below sewage outfalls, with highest concentrations at Station III (52-214 μg/L across sampling dates) compared to upstream Station I (37-130 μg/L). Concentrations decreased progressively downstream as nutrients were utilized by aquatic flora. Station VI showed slightly lower phosphorus than upstream controls, suggesting enhanced biological uptake in the recovery zone.

- **Ammonia Nitrogen**: Trace amounts detected upstream; maximum concentration of 147 μg/L recorded at Station III on May 27, 1955 (following heavy rainfall). Concentrations decreased by approximately 50% within 0.3 mile downstream (Station IV: 70 μg/L) and returned to trace levels by Station V (3.6 miles downstream). Values remained well below toxicity thresholds throughout the study period.

- **Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)**: Increased from approximately 2 ppm upstream to 4.6-8.8 ppm below outfalls. Highest BOD (8.8 ppm) occurred on May 27, 1955 during high flow conditions that kept organic solids in suspension. Despite elevated BOD, dissolved oxygen remained above 90% saturation due to photosynthetic oxygen production, surface reaeration, and dilution.

- **General Water Quality**: pH remained stable (7.2-7.8), methyl orange alkalinity 100-300 ppm, free CO₂ 4-7 ppm. No phenolphthalein alkalinity detected. Water temperature increased from 12°C to 22°C during the study period (April-June 1955).

**Benthic Invertebrate Community Structure:**

- **Station I (Control)**: Mean of 9.8 ± 0.35 families per square foot; 18 total families recorded with 12 families present in ≥30% of samples. Mean organism density: 120 ± 57.3 individuals/ft². High variability (range: 78-216 organisms/ft²) associated with periphyton-covered rocks supporting large midge larvae populations. Community included pollution-sensitive taxa: [[Perlidae]] (stoneflies), [[Ephemeroptera]] (mayflies), [[Trichoptera]] (caddisflies).

- **Station III (0.3 mile below outfalls)**: Mean of 6.3 ± 0.3 families per square foot; 18 total families but only 7 present in ≥30% of samples. Significant decrease from Station I (t = 7.45, p < 0.01). Mean density: 66 ± 39.0 organisms/ft² (range: 20-160). [[Perlidae]] absent. Marked increase in [[Oligochaeta]] (tubificid worms: 19.8 ± 18.0/ft² vs. lower numbers upstream), indicating organic enrichment. Brown, fetid material observed during sampling, attributed to sewage and bacterial origin. Despite elevated nutrients, overall community complexity decreased.

- **Station IV (0.6 mile below outfalls)**: Mean of 7.2 ± 0.58 families per square foot; 22 total families with 8 present in ≥30% of samples. No significant difference from Station III (t = 1.36, p > 0.05) but significantly lower than Station I (t = 3.82, p < 0.01). Mean density: 46 ± 39.2 organisms/ft². Oligochaeta decreased to 5.20 ± 3.7/ft². All four caddisfly families ([[Hydroptilidae]], [[Hydropsychidae]], [[Psychomyiidae]], [[Leptoceridae]]) present but at low densities (<1/ft²). Identified as transition zone between pollution impact and recovery.

- **Station VI (6.6 miles downstream)**: Mean of 12.9 ± 1.22 families per square foot; highest family richness of all stations. Significantly greater than Stations III and IV (p < 0.01) and Station I (t = 2.44, p < 0.05). Mean density: 66 ± 14.8 organisms/ft² (equal to Station III but with greater taxonomic diversity). Community supported larger organisms including [[Trichoptera]] and [[Ephemeroptera]]. Ammonia nitrogen reduced to trace levels; total phosphorus lower than upstream controls, indicating nutrient conversion through food web. Enhanced biological productivity attributed to nutrient enrichment after completion of organic matter mineralization.

**Taxonomic Composition Across All Stations:**

- **Mollusca**: Present at all stations. [[Lymnaeidae]] (pond snails) at all except Station VI; [[Sphaeridae]] and [[Unionidae]] at all stations, with sphaerids most abundant at Station III. [[Ancylidae]] (limpet snails) rare (2 individuals at Station IV only).

- **Oligochaeta**: Found at all stations; highest densities at Stations III and IV, consistent with organic enrichment and pollution tolerance.

- **Crustacea**: [[Hyallela azteca]] ([[Talitridae]]) present at all stations; [[Gammaridae]] also recorded. Greatest numbers at Station VI.

- **Diptera**: [[Tendipedidae]] (midge larvae) ubiquitous at all stations, all samples. Other families: [[Tipulidae]] (crane flies) at all except Station III; [[Simuliidae]], [[Rhagionidae]], [[Heleidae]], [[Ephyridae]] at Station I; [[Culicidae]] (mosquito larvae) only at Station III.

- **Plecoptera**: [[Perlidae]] ([[Perlesta placida]]) at all stations except III, indicating sensitivity to pollution conditions.

- **Ephemeroptera**: [[Caenidae]], [[Ephemeridae]], [[Heptageniidae]], or [[Baetidae]] represented at all stations; only [[Heptageniidae]] found at all locations.

- **Trichoptera**: Four families recorded ([[Hydroptilidae]], [[Hydropsychidae]], [[Psychomyiidae]], [[Leptoceridae]]); all present at Stations IV and VI. Greatest total numbers at Stations I and VI. Generally considered pollution-sensitive; absence from Station III significant.

- **Hemiptera**: [[Notonectidae]] and [[Corixidae]] adults collected at Stations IV and VI (likely underrepresented due to mobility).

- **Odonata**: [[Coenagrionidae]] (damselfly nymph) at Station VI only.

- **Coleoptera**: [[Elmidae]] (riffle beetles) at all stations, most abundant at Station III; [[Dytiscidae]], [[Haliplidae]], [[Curculionidae]] at Stations I and III only.

**Statistical Significance of Community Differences:**

All station comparisons showed significant differences in mean family richness except Stations III vs. IV (p > 0.05), confirming these as ecologically similar zones of pollution impact. The progression from Station I (control) → Station III (impact) → Station IV (transition) → Station VI (recovery/enhancement) demonstrates classical pollution zonation with downstream recovery and fertilization effects.

**Stream Self-Purification and Recovery:**

The study documented complete recovery of benthic community structure within 6.6 miles downstream, with enhanced productivity exceeding upstream conditions. This recovery resulted from: (1) dilution (1:474 ratio), (2) sedimentation of suspended solids, (3) mineralization of organic matter releasing bioavailable nutrients, (4) photosynthetic oxygen production by aquatic vegetation (particularly [[Vallisneria americana]]), (5) surface reaeration, and (6) utilization of nutrients by phytoplankton and higher plants supporting complex food webs.

**Methodological Contributions:**

The study validated family-level identification of benthic invertebrates for pollution assessment, avoiding the time-consuming species-level taxonomy while maintaining ecological resolution. The [[Surber Sampler]] (square-foot bottom sampler) provided quantitative, replicable samples from comparable habitats (Stratum I: gravel/small rocks, 12-18 inches depth). Statistical analysis using t-tests demonstrated significant differences in community structure between pollution zones. The approach proved widely applicable to gross pollution studies and sub-lethal impact assessment.

**Management Implications:**

The research demonstrated that sub-lethal domestic pollution can reduce aesthetic value and temporarily alter benthic communities while ultimately enhancing downstream productivity through nutrient enrichment. During the study period (spring 1955, above-average flow), dissolved oxygen remained adequate and toxic compounds below harmful levels. However, the authors noted that during low flow or warm water conditions, adverse impacts likely extend further downstream. The presence of larger mayflies and caddisflies at downstream stations indicated recovery of recreational fishery potential, as these organisms are essential forage for game fish. The study supported the need for sewage treatment to protect water quality in the metropolitan Lansing recreational area, while documenting the river's substantial self-purification capacity under favorable flow conditions.
