# Effect of Passive Integrated Transponder Tag Size on Survival, Tag Loss, and Growth of Santa Ana Sucker

#ichthyology #Catostomidae #PIT_tagging #threatened_species

## Survey Objectives & Target Population

**Primary Sampling Purpose**: This study evaluated the suitability of passive integrated transponder (PIT) tags for marking [[Santa Ana Sucker]] to support long-term population monitoring. The objective was to test the effect of PIT tag size (8-mm vs. 12-mm) on survival, tag loss, and growth to determine appropriate tagging protocols for this federally threatened species. The study addresses critical knowledge gaps in population dynamics that result from seasonal sampling limitations and inconsistent survey methodologies in the [[Santa Ana River]], [[San Gabriel River]], and [[Los Angeles River]] systems.

**Target Population & Spatial Boundaries**: The target population was captive [[Santa Ana Sucker]] (Pantosteus santaanae) originating from the [[Santa Ana River]], California. Fish ranged from 60–135 mm fork length (FL) and 2.7–32.4 g weight, representing multiple size classes: small (60–79 mm standard length [SL]), medium (80–99 mm SL), and large (≥100 mm SL). The species is a small, short-lived catostomid typically living just over 3 years and rarely exceeding 200 mm FL. The study was conducted in controlled outdoor raceways at the Riverside-Corona Resource Conservation District facility in Riverside, California, simulating natural stream habitat with gravel and cobble substrates.

## Gear Specifications & Standardization

**Equipment & Mesh/Gear Parameters**: Two sizes of full-duplex PIT tags were tested: (1) 8-mm tags measuring 8.4 × 1.4 mm, weighing 0.03 g, operating at 134.2 kHz; and (2) 12-mm tags measuring 12.5 × 2.03 mm, weighing 0.106 g, operating at 134.2 kHz (Biomark, Boise, Idaho). Tags were preloaded into single-use needles. Detection equipment included an HPR Lite Handheld PIT Tag Reader for individual fish scanning and a BP Plus Portable Antenna for raceway scanning to locate ejected tags. Fish were anesthetized using 0.5-mg/L buffered tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) solution (approximately 49 s sedation time). Raceways were approximately 0.5 m wide and 30 m long with gravel and cobble substrate, watercress (Nasturtium officinale), and common rush (Juncus effusus), covered by 70% shade cloth and entirely enclosed to prevent predation.

**Deployment & Effort Standardization**: Tags were injected into the peritoneal cavity lateral to the midline of the ventral surface, posterior to the pectoral fins, with the needle facing toward the caudal fin and bevel facing down to prevent heart damage and minimize wound size. An experienced fish tagger performed all injections. Following tagging, standard length, fork length, and total length were recorded to the nearest millimeter, and weight to the nearest 0.1 g. Each fish was scanned immediately post-tagging to verify tag insertion and unique identification number. Fish recovered in an observation bucket of aerated water for 15 min, then in a large aquarium for 12 h (overnight) before transfer to raceways. Raceways were divided into 10 sections (mean 7 m length) using weighted seine mesh nets and PVC pipe. Each section was stocked with 5–6 fish (two individuals from each treatment group). Fish were fed 2–3 algae wafers per week, and raceways were monitored daily for mortalities. After 36 days, all fish were retrieved, scanned, remeasured for length and weight, and inspected for tag retention.

**Selectivity & Bias Considerations**: The study followed established tagging guidelines: tags should not exceed 2% of fish body weight (Winter, 1983) or 17.5% of total fish length (Vollset et al., 2020). Mean tag length to fish total length ratios were 8.7 ± 1.7% (range 6.4–12.5%) for 8-mm tags and 12.5 ± 2.3% (range 9.2–19.2%) for 12-mm tags. Mean tag weight to fish weight ratios were 0.36 ± 0.25% (range 0.09–1.04%) for 8-mm tags and 1.1 ± 0.77% (range 0.37–3.9%) for 12-mm tags. The smallest fish that survived with an 8-mm tag was 67 mm total length (TL) and 2.88 g (1.04% body weight; 12.5% TL). The smallest fish that survived with a 12-mm tag was 75 mm TL and 4.89 g (2.2% body weight; 16.6% TL). One mortality occurred in a fish (60 mm FL) tagged with a 12-mm tag that exceeded recommended guidelines (3.7% body weight; 19.2% TL). Control fish were handled identically to tagged fish (sedation, handling time) to isolate tagging effects from handling stress.

## Experimental & Spatial Design Framework

**Stratification & Allocation**: Sixty-one [[Santa Ana Sucker]] were randomly assigned to three treatments: control (N=20), 8-mm PIT tag (N=21), and 12-mm PIT tag (N=20). Fish were initially grouped into size categories (30 small, 24 medium, 7 large) to maintain similar size distributions across treatment groups, though size categories were only used for treatment assignment and not as analytical strata. Treatments were randomly distributed across 10 raceway sections, with each section receiving two individuals from each treatment group. Three raceways were used: raceway 1 divided into four sections, raceways 2 and 3 each divided into three sections. Control fish of different sizes were paired in each section to ensure individual identification at study end.

**Temporal Scheduling**: The study duration was 36 days. Initial measurements and tagging occurred on day 0, with fish held overnight (12 h) in aquariums before transfer to raceways on day 1. Raceways were monitored daily for mortalities throughout the 36-day period. Final measurements were collected on day 36. Mortalities were recorded as occurring within 1 h of tagging (two 8-mm tag fish), within 48 h of tagging (three 12-mm tag fish), or during the study period. The short study duration was designed to assess acute tagging effects but limits conclusions about long-term survival and chronic tag loss.

**Detection & Bias Mitigation**: All fish were scanned with a PIT tag reader at three time points: (1) immediately after tagging to verify insertion and unique ID, (2) before placement in raceways, and (3) at study end (day 36). Fish without detectable tags at study end were scanned a third time and inspected for tagging scars to distinguish between control fish and fish that had ejected tags. All raceways were scanned with a BP Plus Portable Antenna to locate ejected tags. One 12-mm tag was recovered; one 8-mm tag was not recovered but the fish showed evidence of tagging (healed scar). Tag loss was attributed to specific causes when possible: one 12-mm tag loss occurred in a fish where the implanter malfunctioned, requiring a second injection. Dead fish were removed immediately, scanned, and retained for examination.

## Field Data Schema & Variable Definitions

**Primary Biological Measurements**: Standard length (SL), fork length (FL), and total length (TL) were measured to the nearest millimeter. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 g. Measurements were recorded at initial tagging (day 0) and at study end (day 36). Growth was calculated as the change in fork length (mm) and change in weight (g) over the 36-day period. Survival was recorded as binary (alive/dead) with timing of mortality noted (within 1 h, within 48 h, or during study period). Tag retention was recorded as binary (retained/lost) for tagged fish. Dead fish were examined for signs of internal bleeding or organ damage. Fish that lost tags were inspected for tagging scars and wound healing status.

**Environmental & Spatial Covariates**: Water temperature (°C) was recorded continuously (every 15 min) using HOBO Pro v2 data loggers (Onset, Bourne, Massachusetts) positioned near the center of each raceway. Stream depth (±1 cm) and velocity (±0.1 m/s) were measured at 10 points in the thalweg of each section using a top setting wading rod and flow meter (FH 950 Portable Velocity Meter; Hach, Loveland, Colorado). Mean depths ranged from 8.7 to 16.8 cm across sections. Mean velocities ranged from 0.11 to 0.22 m/s. Vegetation was categorized as absent (2 sections), sparse (4 sections), or dense (4 sections). Substrate size was quantified by randomly measuring the longest diameter (cm) of 50 substrate particles per section; mean substrate size was similar across sections. Silt comprised 10–44% of substrate measurements; sand was recorded in only two sections (2–4%). Section length ranged from 5.4 to 9.2 m (mean 7 m).

**QA/QC & Data Entry Protocols**: All tagging was performed by an experienced fish tagger to minimize variability in technique. Each fish was scanned immediately after tagging to verify successful tag insertion and unique identification number. Fish were held in observation buckets for 15 min post-tagging to monitor immediate recovery, then held overnight (12 h) in aquariums before raceway placement to assess short-term survival. Raceways were monitored daily for mortalities, with dead fish removed, scanned, and retained. At study end, all fish were scanned multiple times to confirm tag presence/absence. Fish without detectable tags were inspected for physical evidence of tagging (scars) and identified through process of elimination. All raceways were scanned with portable antenna to locate ejected tags. Data are publicly available from Wulff et al. (2025), U.S. Geological Survey data release (https://doi.org/10.5066/P13FTTW4).

## Analytical Linkages: Downstream Model Compatibility

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tested for differences in initial fork length and weight among treatment groups. Fisher's exact test evaluated survival and tag loss between treatments (control, 8-mm PIT tag, 12-mm PIT tag) due to small sample size; mortalities were excluded from tag loss analysis to assess retention over the full study period. Ordinary least squares regression tested for interaction between treatment effect and initial fish size (fork length and weight) on growth, calculated as change in fork length and change in weight. Results indicated no statistically significant difference in survival (P=0.354) or tag loss (P=0.536) among treatments. Initial fish size significantly affected growth (P<0.001 for length; P=0.0153 for weight), but no significant treatment or interaction effects were detected (P>0.05). Overall survival rate was 87.8% (90.5% for 8-mm tags, 85% for 12-mm tags, 100% for controls). Overall tag loss rate was 5.6% (5.3% for 8-mm tags, 5.9% for 12-mm tags). Results support use of 8-mm and 12-mm PIT tags for [[Santa Ana Sucker]] ≥62 mm and ≥70 mm fork length, respectively. The study provides foundational data for implementing PIT tag technology in mark-recapture studies, survival analysis, movement ecology, habitat use studies, abundance estimation, and population vital rate estimation for this threatened species.
