# Integration of Grand Canyon Physical and Biological Information: A Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report by [[Vicky J. Meretsky]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) and [[Theodore S. Melis]] (U.S. Geological Survey) describes efforts to integrate physical and biological research on the Lower Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. It traces the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program (Phase I, 1983–1988; Phase II, 1988–1995), explains why single-discipline reports left the system only partly understood, and summarizes the August 1995 Fern Mountain integration meeting. That meeting produced a draft integration prospectus organized around four core topics: physical-habitat relations, trophic dynamics, [[Humpback Chub]] population ecology, and the role of science in [[Adaptive Management]]. The authors argue that integrated, management-directed monitoring is needed to address [[Biological Opinion]] issues under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and they note that the work was moving to the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]].

## Background & Description

The document is a conference paper in the Transactions and Proceedings Series of the National Park Service, drawn from the Third Biennial Conference of Research on the Colorado Plateau (1997). Its focus is institutional and scientific: how research on regulated river ecosystems can be integrated and communicated to managers.

Key background elements:

- **Glen Canyon Environmental Studies:** Initiated by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] in 1982 to study effects of Glen Canyon Dam (closed 1963) on downstream resources. Phase I (mainly 1983–1986) produced single-discipline reports. Phase II (from 1988) was accelerated to support an [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS).
- **National Research Council review (1987):** Found Phase I reports lacked integration and recommended earlier involvement of an integration group. It concluded that the studies had met their goals and that dam-related impacts could be reduced.
- **Interim flows:** Set at 8,000–20,000 cfs from August 1991 until a Record of Decision was signed in October 1996.
- **Ad-Hoc Interdisciplinary Integration Work Group:** Formed to integrate existing data; its first meeting (August 1995) included federal and state agencies, academia, the private sector, and [[Native American Tribes]].
- **Species focus:** Grand Canyon and its tributaries support only one viable population of the four endangered big-river fish species, the humpback chub (*Gila cypha*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical controls:** The authors state that physical habitat attributes are controlling factors for many Grand Canyon species, alongside food availability, river temperature, and biotic interactions. Habitats of endangered species change naturally, but regulated releases since 1963 have also shaped them. Geomorphic change is driven by floods, rockfalls, debris flows, flow suppression, unplanned spills, experimental floods, and thermal effects of selective withdrawal.

**Hydroclimatology:** Colorado Plateau flows respond to topography and regional climate, which is linked to global processes such as El Niño and Pacific sea-surface temperatures. The authors cite evidence that daily precipitation intensities increased across the United States during the twentieth century (Karl et al. 1995), with possible geomorphic consequences.

**Humpback chub distribution:** [[Valdez and Ryel]] (1995) hypothesized that the distinct grouped distribution of chub aggregations results from a unique combination of temperature, physical habitat parameters, and life-history requirements. Data come from the Upper Colorado River Basin, the mainstem in Grand Canyon, and the [[Little Colorado River]], but have not been integrated.

**Marsh and backwater habitats:** [[Stevens et al. (1995)]] documented spatial-scale effects of geomorphology and dam operations on marsh development. Predicted impacts of higher flows on these habitats require testing.

**Trophic and benthic systems:** Research covered sediment transport, organic drift, benthic ecology, photosynthetically available radiation, Lake Powell water quality, primary and secondary production, and humpback chub diet. Named "cornerstone species" included *Oscillatoria* (cyanobacteria) and *Simulium* (black flies).

**Native and nonnative fish interactions:** Effects of dam operations on native–nonnative interactions and on parasite and disease loads are poorly understood. Infestation by the copepod parasite *Lernaea cyprinacea* has been documented in native Grand Canyon fishes (Carothers et al. 1981).

## Key Findings

1. **Integration gap:** Phase I and Phase II reports were largely single-discipline. Data on hydrology and organic matter were not linked to humpback chub diet, which limited understanding of food availability over time and space.
2. **Early integration recommended:** The NRC (1987) judged that Phase I would have been stronger with early involvement of an integration group and greater use of historical and external fluvial-system data.
3. **Fern Mountain meeting (August 1995):** Participants agreed that integrating existing biotic and abiotic databases could generate testable hypotheses about habitat relationships. They noted that managing endangered species through dam operations requires first understanding specific habitat–distribution relations.
4. **Core questions on habitat and endangered species:** (1) Which physical and geomorphic attributes limit or control endangered species distributions below Glen Canyon Dam? (2) How do short- and long-term dam operations change those attributes, and how do the changes relate to management objectives? The authors state that neither question can be answered from existing databases alone.
5. **Climate-related priorities:** Suggested topics included climatic non-stationarity and basin hydrology, climate forecasting for reservoir operations, climate-driven tributary processes, climate's role in sediment balance, and climatic forcing of the bottom-up food base.
6. **Trophic synthesis:** The working group proposed large-scale models linking dam operations and physical factors to food-web interactions and population dynamics. Topics were not equally valuable to managers, so management input was needed to prioritize them.
7. **Humpback chub life-history model:** Combining demographic, distribution, movement, and diet data into a conceptual life-history model was seen as a priority. Many researchers believed much of it could be built from existing data.
8. **Native–nonnative interactions:** Four questions were set, covering present effects, which species interact with natives and whether the interactions are negative, neutral, or positive, how future operations will change them, and whether they increase parasites or pathogens. Existing research, especially from outside Grand Canyon, was expected to help answer them only partly.
9. **Adaptive management:** The EIS preferred alternative relies on adaptive management to meet the Endangered Species Act and the Grand Canyon Protection Act (1992). A basin-wide advisory committee was expected to form in 1997 and make recommendations to the Secretary of the Interior. The working group stressed clear, timely communication between scientists and managers, and it supported principles such as independent review of monitoring programs and a forum for stakeholder participation.
10. **Experimental outcomes:** The 1996 [[Beach/Habitat-Building Test Flow]], an experimental flood, involved over 100 scientists. Prior integration reduced redundancy and streamlined planning. Several predicted and unexpected results occurred, and the working group supported incorporating them into the long-term monitoring plan.
11. **Future direction:** Cross-discipline partnerships persisted after the flood, and several integrative papers were in progress. Oversight of research was shifting from the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies office to the Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center, which was expected to reach full operation in 1997.
