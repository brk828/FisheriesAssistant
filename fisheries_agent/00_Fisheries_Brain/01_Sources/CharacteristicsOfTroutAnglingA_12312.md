# Characteristics of Trout Angling at Lawrence Creek, Wisconsin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study presents a complete creel census of [[Angling Pressure]] on [[Lawrence Creek]], a wild [[Brook Trout]] stream in [[Wisconsin]]. It was undertaken as part of a broader program to evaluate how different angling regulations affect a wild brook trout population. The census is described as the first complete assessment of angling pressure on a Wisconsin trout stream. Anglers landed 3,040 legal brook trout over a 19-week season, equivalent to 323.4 fish per acre, and fishing intensity reached 495 man-hours per acre. This is very high compared with other trout streams in the United States and with Wisconsin lakes. The author concludes that wild brook trout may be overexploited and that angler skill, rather than angler numbers, mainly determines success.

## Background & Description

**Methods.** Anglers obtained a permit at a permanent checking station before fishing, with separate permits for each stream section. On leaving, they returned permits and presented their catch. All trout were weighed and measured, scale samples were taken for [[Age Determination]], and time fished and methods used were recorded. A "successful" trip was one in which at least one legal trout was creeled.

**Season and regulations (1955).** The season ran from April 30 to September 7. The minimum size limit was six inches, the daily bag limit was 10 trout, and angling was allowed only from 5 a.m. to 9 p.m. The last point was the only departure from [[Statewide Regulations]].

**Study area.** The 3.3-mile [[Lawrence Creek Project Area]] runs from the headwaters to the upper end of Lawrence Millpond. The stream averages about 23.5 feet wide and covers 9.4 acres. Flow rises from about 12 cubic feet per second one mile below the headwaters to 25 cubic feet per second at the downstream end. The gradient is moderate. The site lies roughly 40 miles south-southeast of the state's geographical centre.

**Fish community.** Non-game species include [[Common Sucker]], [[Blacknose Dace]], [[Brook Stickleback]], muddler (*Cottus* sp.) and [[Creek Chub]]. Only the muddler is abundant. [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] move in from Lawrence Millpond during warmer months and are not year-round residents, though anglers occasionally catch them.

**Stocking history.** The stream was last stocked with rainbow trout in 1945 and with brook trout in 1948. It now supports a small wild [[Rainbow Trout]] population and a large wild brook trout population. No [[Brown Trout]] are present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The catchment's soil is the relatively infertile [[Coloma Sand]]. Much of the original mixed-oak forest has been replaced by pine plantations and abandoned farmland. Streamside cover consists of marsh-meadow, alders and mixed oaks. The local growing season averages 145 days.

Angling pressure and catch followed similar seasonal trends. The author argues that the abrupt drop in pressure after the first two weeks reflects the behaviour of much of the angling public rather than depletion of the trout stock. Recruitment of smaller fish to legal size was strong enough that the number of brook trout at season's end nearly equalled the number at opening.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch and effort**
- Legal brook trout caught: 3,040 on 1,712 trips, totalling 4,653 man-hours.
- Fishing intensity: 495 man-hours per acre. This yielded 323.4 brook trout per acre, or 57.2 lb per acre.
- Legal rainbow trout caught: 177, adding 18.8 fish per acre (4.7 lb per acre).
- Catch rate: 0.67 legal trout per man-hour over the season.

**Seasonal pattern**
- Pressure was extremely heavy in the opening week, then fell rapidly.
- On opening day (April 30), 16.8% of the season's catch and 8.6% of trips were recorded.
- The first week produced 27.1% of the catch and 18.9% of trips. By the fifth week, 48% of both catch and trips had occurred.
- A rise in trips came in the fifth week, which included Memorial Day. No comparable rise occurred in the tenth week, which included the Fourth of July.
- Weekends drew 62% of trips but made up only 29% of trout-season days.

**Intensity in context**
- Intensity ranged from 350 man-hours per acre on Section D to 662 on Section C.
- Among the compared US trout streams (Table 1), Sunkhaze Stream in Maine (1949) recorded 670 man-hours per acre, and Lawrence Creek Section C recorded 662. The entire-stream figure of 495 ranks high against the other streams listed, which range down to 29 per acre on the Swift River, New Hampshire.
- Among Wisconsin waters (Table 2), Lawrence Creek's 495 far exceeds the next highest, [[Escanaba Lake]] at 123. Most other lakes ranged from 24 to 62. The lakes were fished over 12-month seasons, against Lawrence Creek's 19-week season.

**Distribution of catch**
- The most successful 11% of trips took 51% of the catch.
- 36% of trips accounted for 94% of the catch.
- 53% of trips produced no legal trout.
- Three top anglers made 39 trips in total and took 9% of the season's catch. The author estimates that 34 anglers of that proficiency could have taken the full 3,040-fish catch in 442 trips, compared with the 1,712 trips actually made.

**Angler residence**
- Zone 1 (0–25 miles): 19% of trips. Zone 2 (25–50 miles): 20%. Zone 3 (50–100 miles): 47%. Zone 4 (over 100 miles): 3%.
- Non-residents made 11% of trips. Of these, 93% were Illinois residents. Other states included Indiana, New Jersey, California, Minnesota, Texas, Iowa, Ohio, Michigan and Missouri.
- Anglers were mobile. Over 60% of trips came from people living more than 50 miles away, and the densely populated south-east of Wisconsin supplied much of the pressure.

**Methods**
- Natural baits (worms, minnows, insects) were the only method on 61% of trips.
- Artificial lures (flies, spinners, plugs) were the only method on 28%.
- Both were used on 11%.
- The author found bait and fly fishing equally effective in skilled hands.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Heavy use indicates that the Lawrence Creek fishery is a major recreational resource, and the author suggests wild brook trout may be overexploited.
- Angler skill is the strongest determinant of success. Effective pressure could rise substantially, without more anglers, if the existing public became more proficient.
- Relatively high intensity appears typical of Wisconsin trout streams compared with lakes, though the data are limited.
