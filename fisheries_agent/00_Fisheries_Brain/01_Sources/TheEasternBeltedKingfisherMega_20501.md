# Eastern Belted Kingfisher (*Megaceryle alcyon*) and Fish Management in Michigan

#ornithology #fish-predation #megaceryle-alcyon

## Executive Summary

This study examines how the [[Eastern Belted Kingfisher]] (*Megaceryle alcyon*) affects fish production in Michigan, both on natural waters and at outdoor [[Fish Hatcheries]] and rearing stations. The authors combined field observations of kingfisher behavior with laboratory [[Stomach Content Analysis]] of birds collected from 1930 to 1945. They report that the kingfisher is the most common and widely distributed bird predator of fish in Michigan. At hatcheries, it preys heavily on the species being raised. On natural waters, it mainly eats non-game fishes, [[Crayfish]], and insects. The authors conclude that the species is incompatible with hatchery practice but is relatively harmless to fish populations on most natural waters, apart from spawning and nursery areas.

## Background & Description

The study was part of a series on the relationship between fish predators and fish production. Its goal was to determine how kingfisher predation affects food, game, and forage fish, both artificially and naturally produced. Non-lethal controls were designed and tested after substantial losses of valued fish at rearing stations became apparent. The work was supported by the Wildlife Management Institute and the Associated Fishing Tackle Manufacturers.

The authors drew on two data sources:
- **Field observations** of kingfisher habits, distribution, nesting, and fish populations, made in Michigan during all months.
- **Laboratory analyses** of stomach contents from birds collected at fish-cultural establishments and natural waters. Pellet-based methods were not used. Fragmentary fish remains were measured by comparison with whole specimens or skeletons.

Life history was not reviewed in depth, since it had been summarized in earlier accounts. The emphasis was on practical [[Fishery Management]] implications.

Key descriptive points:
- The female is slightly larger, usually weighing a little more than 6 ounces, while the male averages a little less than 6 ounces.
- The female is the more aggressive member of a pair and typically initiates territory selection.
- Mated pairs defend territories closely. They patrol at intervals of roughly 10 to 15 minutes and attack intruders, including people, cats, dogs, and larger birds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migration and distribution.** Michigan is a peninsular state bordered largely by the [[Great Lakes]], and these shorelines serve as the main migration highways for kingfishers. Birds move along the lakeshores and then up major river valleys, dropping off at new or old nesting sites. Observed movement rates included about 15 birds per hour along Lake Huron and 12 per hour at the mouth of the Pentwater River in fall. The species is mainly a summer resident, though some birds remain through winter.

**Territory.** Territory size depends on habitat type:
- On lakes, pairs typically held about ¼ mile of shoreline, usually including a sheltered bay that offered calm feeding water. Wind exposure was nearly as important as turbidity in determining distribution. Smaller Michigan lakes supported more birds per unit of shoreline than large, rough lakes.
- On rivers, feeding was limited by vegetation, deep pools, and fast currents. Territories were correspondingly larger, usually about 1.5 to 3 miles or more. One pair held a 6-mile stretch of the Little Manistee River headwaters, and no other pair was found along that length.

**Feeding behavior.** Kingfishers are strictly diurnal and feed mainly on prey near the surface or in shallow water. Feeding peaks in the morning, afternoon, and early evening. Shallow, moderate-to-slow currents give better fishing success than deep pools.

**Juvenile development.** Young birds follow a predictable dietary sequence:
1. Insects (especially mayflies and caddisflies) for the first 3 to 4 days out of the nest.
2. [[Crayfish]] (*Cambarus*) from roughly the fifth to tenth day on the wing.
3. Fish, beginning about the latter part of the second week on the wing.

Family groups stay together for 10 to 15 days, and the young gradually disperse.

**Habitat avoidance.** Kingfishers are notably absent from:
- Feeder or nursery reaches of streams, where marginal vegetation forms a canopy over the water.
- Turbid lower reaches of large southern Michigan rivers.
- Waters muddied by runoff after heavy rain.
- Lake shores when surf makes feeding impossible.

**Population trends.** Kingfisher numbers on Michigan trout streams appeared to increase with the beaver population in the decade before 1934, since beaver ponds opened stream reaches that had been shaded by overhanging vegetation. On the Jordan River, one bird was counted on 6 miles in 1931, compared with 15 birds on the same stretch in 1934.

## Key Findings

### Diet on Hatchery Waters
- **Trout rearing waters:** Stomach contents from 428 birds (747.8 cc of food) showed that the species being propagated, mainly trout, made up 80.1% of food volume and occurred in 100% of stomachs with food. Birds averaged about two propagated fish each. Crayfish, aquatic insects, and forage fishes were the other main prey.
- **Pondfish rearing waters:** Stomach contents from 27 birds (59.1 cc) showed that pan fishes made up 85.8% of food volume. Propagated fish again averaged about two per bird.
- **Hatchery-derived birds without propagated fish:** 131 birds from trout stations, and additional birds from pondfish stations, contained none of the species being raised. The authors interpreted these as birds that had not yet fed at the hatchery, and they grouped them with natural-water data.

### Fish Size
- Fish eaten averaged less than 3 inches in total length and ranged from 1 to 7 inches. Trout averaged 2.3 inches (651 individuals). Measurements came from 729 specimens.
- Fish over about 5 inches were less commonly eaten, probably because they were harder to swallow. Some 6- to 7-inch fish were carried and then dropped, and marks from the bill were found on larger fish.

### Diet on Natural Waters
- **Trout streams:** Trout were eaten at roughly one per two birds. Forage fishes and crayfish made up nearly two-thirds of food volume and most food organisms by number. Natural-trout-stream samples included 92 individuals, and hatchery-derived samples representing natural trout waters included 181.
- **Non-trout streams:** The study examined 22 birds from natural waters and 95 hatchery-derived birds representing natural non-trout waters. Forage fishes were numerically dominant. Vertebrates were more important than arthropods by volume.
- **Lakes:** Analyses of 45 birds (92.1 cc) found forage fishes at 49.1% of food volume, game and pan fishes at 17.5%, and insects at 21.0%. Crayfish were not a major lake food, which the authors attributed to lower crayfish abundance or higher forage fish availability.
- Earlier records cited in the paper, including a USFWS analysis of 25 stomachs, also documented a broad range of fish, crayfish, and insect prey.

### Hatchery Control and Losses
- Kingfishers can damage hatcheries heavily. One private hatchery near Lake Michigan reported shooting and trapping more than 400 birds in a single spring migration.
- Hatchery predation peaks in May and the first half of June, when birds are establishing territories and nesting.
- Once birds find hatchery ponds, they return repeatedly. The authors suggest that the cumulative kill at hatcheries could reduce regional kingfisher numbers.

### Recommended Control Measures
- **Screening** of ponds was identified as the most effective method, though it is limited to smaller water bodies.
- Poles or supports 4 feet or taller should be kept away from ponds, since they serve as fishing and observation perches.
- **Pole traps** should be discouraged because they capture many songbirds.
- Scarecrows, flashing lights, mirrors, and automatic detonators gave only temporary relief, since birds became accustomed to them.
- New hatcheries should not be sited within 5 miles of Great Lakes shores, which lie on migration flight lanes. Converting headwater streams into rearing pools can also attract fish-eating birds.

### Conclusions
- The authors describe their conclusions as limited. They state that the kingfisher is incompatible with present hatchery practice because a bird can consume more than 50 small fingerlings per day, a figure from White (1936).
- They propose that organizations favoring strict protection should help fund non-lethal protection of hatchery fish.
- On natural waters, the kingfisher is relatively innocuous, except possibly on spawning areas and nursery grounds such as headwater trout streams. Predation on preferred species or their food organisms is a small cost for the bird's aesthetic value.
- Current evidence does not support general control of kingfishers on natural waters.
- Published estimates of total fish consumed are misleading unless they are interpreted against the abundance of predators and prey in specific areas.
