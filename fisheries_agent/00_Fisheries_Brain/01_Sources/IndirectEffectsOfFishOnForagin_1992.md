## Executive Summary

This study investigates the **indirect effects of fish chemical cues (skin mucus)** on the foraging behaviour of the facultative shredder isopod *[[Lirceus fontinalis]]*, and how fish-induced behavioural changes cascade to affect **leaf litter processing** in low-order streams. Rather than a direct predator-prey morphological study, the paper examines a trophic cascade: fish presence (via chemosensory cues) → reduced isopod activity → reduced leaf breakdown rates. Laboratory assays exposed isopods to mucus cues from five fish species differing in phylogenetic relatedness and feeding ecology, revealing differential behavioural responses strongest to predatory sunfish (*[[Lepomis]]*) cues and weakest to algivorous stoneroller (*[[Campostoma anomalum]]*) cues. Field data from a fishless headwater reach further demonstrated that isopod body size structure strongly influences leaf processing rates, suggesting that fish may indirectly regulate detrital processing in stream ecosystems both through behavioural suppression and through population size-structure effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Low-order (headwater) streams in the eastern United States; benthic stream environments where leaf litter accumulates and undergoes microbial/invertebrate-mediated breakdown.
- **Trophic role of fish**: Fish (both predatory sunfish and algivorous minnows) act as top-down modulators of benthic macroinvertebrate behaviour via chemical (mucus) cues, without necessarily requiring direct predation events.
- **Isopod role**: *[[Lirceus fontinalis]]* functions as a facultative shredder, contributing to leaf litter breakdown (a key detrital energy pathway in headwater stream food webs).
- **Indirect trophic cascade**: Fish presence → chemosensory detection by isopods → reduced isopod foraging/activity → reduced leaf disc breakdown rates — an example of a multi-trophic-level indirect effect analogous to cascades described for algivorous chironomids and algal biomass in the Eel River (Power, 1990).
- **Comparative context**: Other cited systems show variable outcomes — e.g., Reice (1991) found no fish effect on invertebrate abundance or leaf breakdown in Mount Sinai Creek, while Oberndorfer et al. (1984) demonstrated insect predators (*Megarcys signata*, *Rhyacophila* sp.) exerting similar indirect trophic effects in an alpine stream, indicating context-dependence of fish/predator cascades across stream systems.
- **Fishless reach comparison**: A field site lacking fish served as a natural experiment showing that isopod size structure (not just fish presence) governs leaf processing rates, highlighting an interaction between direct population demographic effects and indirect predator-cue effects.

## Key Findings

1. Isopods (*[[Lirceus fontinalis]]*) reduced their activity in response to waterborne skin mucus cues from fish, indicating a chemically-mediated, non-contact predator avoidance response.
2. Isopods were **most responsive** (greatest activity reduction) to mucus cues from predatory sunfish (*[[Lepomis]]*), and **least responsive** to mucus cues from algivorous stonerollers (*[[Campostoma anomalum]]*) — suggesting isopods can discriminate fish cues based on threat level/feeding ecology.
3. Exposure to chemical cues from confined green sunfish (*[[Lepomis cyanellus]]*) significantly reduced isopod-mediated **leaf disc breakdown rates** in laboratory trials, confirming a behaviourally-mediated indirect effect of fish on detrital processing.
4. In a fishless headwater stream reach, larger isopods (7–10 mm TL) processed leaf litter **4–5 times faster** than smaller isopods (≤5 mm TL), demonstrating that population size structure is a major direct determinant of leaf processing rates.
5. Overall, the study supports the hypothesis that fish can exert **indirect effects two or more trophic links removed** — influencing ecosystem-level processes like leaf litter breakdown — through both behavioural suppression of shredder activity and potential effects on shredder population size structure.
6. This adds to a mixed body of literature (e.g., Power 1990 on Eel River chironomid–algae cascades; Reice 1991 finding no cascade in Mount Sinai Creek) indicating that the strength and detectability of fish-mediated trophic cascades in stream ecosystems is variable and system-dependent.
