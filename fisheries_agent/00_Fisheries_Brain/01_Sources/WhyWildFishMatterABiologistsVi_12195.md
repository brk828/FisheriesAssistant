# Wild Fish Versus Hatchery Fish: A Case for Habitat and Natural Production

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 *Trout* magazine feature, the first part of a series, argues by [[Ray J. White]] that [[Wild Fish]] matter for biological, management and economic reasons, and that [[Hatchery Management]] and stocking have repeatedly failed to deliver long-term fish abundance while damaging wild populations. The author contends that protecting habitat and harvest, rather than building hatcheries, is the proper basis for fishery management. The article is an opinion and advocacy piece supported by literature citations and cited case studies; it reports no new original field sample sizes.

## Background & Description

- **Definition of wild fish:** A free-living fish hatched and reared in a stream, lake or sea from an egg spawned there by its mother. Fish raised with human help are considered progressively domesticated.
- **Native versus exotic:** A native species originates in a given locality; an exotic species comes from outside it. Stocked exotics can form wild non-native populations that harm native fish.
- **Adaptation framework:** Following [[Van R. Potter]], the author distinguishes evolutionary, physiological and behavioral adaptation, all of which underpin the value of wild fish.
- **Scope of the article:** Covers the three "Hs" of fishery management (habitat, harvest, hatchery), stocking types, adverse effects, agency policy shifts, and scientific critiques. It is part one of a series; the concluding article was promised for the next issue.
- **Companion material:** A reader-ad page promotes a bound 30-year anniversary set of *Trout* issues, and a reference list cites works including [[Hilborn 1991]], [[Nehlsen et Al. 1991]], [[Bachman 1984]] and [[Vincent 1987]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Old-growth habitat:** Pacific old-growth forests supply water and food, moderate stream temperature, hold streambeds together, trap spawning gravel, create pools and furnish cover for [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]].

**Community structure:** In natural waters, fish of each species and size group occupy distinct feeding positions and times. The author cites [[Peter Larkin]]: fish are "characterized by a place in which they feed rather than by a type of food." Social rank governs access to feeding positions. Example: a feeding aggregation of 15 to 18 inch [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] over deep water feeding on a stonefly hatch.

**Adaptation and temperature:** Physiological acclimation operates over seconds to seasons. Sudden extremes kill fish, usually those least able to adjust.

**Predator and prey interactions:** Hatchery releases can attract predators. In British Columbia, hatchery Chinook releases attracted dogfish sharks near river mouths, preying on weaker wild salmon runs.

**Nutrient cycling:** Pacific-drainage waters are often nutrient-poor. Returning salmon carcasses fertilize upstream habitat. Hatchery interception of spawners can reduce this flow.

**Water quality:** Hatchery effluents include disinfectants and feed-lot wastes, which can cause oxygen depletion downstream.

## Key Findings

**1. Hatchery production has repeatedly underperformed**
- [[Ray Hilborn]] (University of Washington) is quoted: artificial propagation in the Northwest "has been an abysmal failure" and poses a "substantial, and perhaps major, threat" to salmon.
- Oregon coho survival trends fell as hatchery systems expanded (Figure 1).
- British Columbia Chinook: about 10 times more smolts were released in the 1980s than in the mid-1970s, yet fewer hatchery Chinook were caught.
- Babine Lake sockeye (Skeena River): as artificial spawning channels increased young output, ocean survival per fish fell, and wild Skeena runs declined about 50 percent (Figure 2).

**2. Stocking statistics**
- Survival of stocked fish is low. In one Wisconsin study, 40 percent of yearling trout planted in spring did not survive to opening day, and less than 1 percent of hatchery brook trout survived a year after stocking.
- US "catchable" trout stocking rose 193 percent from 1947 to 1983 (55 percent from 1958 to 1983), while total "inland" salmonid stocking rose only 12 percent (Figure 3).
- Wild trout in excellent habitat lose about 95 percent of each generation within a year of fertilization.

**3. Adverse effects of hatchery fish on wild populations (summarized list of nine plus others)**
- Exotic introductions: brown and rainbow trout displaced brook trout in eastern North America; brook trout helped disintegrate Rocky Mountain cutthroat populations; rainbows hybridized out several cutthroat strains.
- Genetic dilution and loss of within-stock variation.
- Brood stock robbing of wild adults.
- Predation, competition and disruptive, aggressive behavior by hatchery fish.
- The "Pied Piper" effect, drawing young wild anadromous fish out to sea too early.
- Disease spread from crowding, which is several hundred times natural density. Norwegian fish farmers used 37.4 tons of antibiotics in 1990, twice the previous year.
- Harvest "pump-priming": stocking attracts anglers who then over-harvest weak wild stocks.
- A "false sense of security" that delays habitat and harvest reform.

**4. Stock status**
- Nehlsen, Williams and Lichatowich (1991) identified 107 native Pacific anadromous stocks as extinct and 214 as imperiled, with 102 imperiled stocks at high risk of extinction (lists admittedly incomplete).
- NMFS listed two stocks as endangered at writing. It declined to list lower Columbia wild coho, which appeared to have gone extinct.

**5. Management shifts cited as models**
- Canada's federal government abandoned salmon and trout hatcheries in the mid-1930s; Alberta ended stream trout stocking in the mid-1950s.
- [[Yellowstone National Park]] halted its 50-plus years of stocking in 1956.
- Michigan stopped stocking streams with natural trout reproduction in the mid-1960s; Montana ended stream stocking in the mid-1970s.
- Pennsylvania launched its wild trout program in 1983.

**6. Genetic and behavioral arguments**
- Each wild stock is genetically unique. Harrison Lake sockeye stocks use different spawning and rearing routes.
- Hatcheries warp genetics through interbreeding, drift, intentional and inadvertent selection, and removal of natural selection.
- Wild trout show dominance hierarchies. In a Pennsylvania stream study (Bachman), hatchery brown trout attacked more, wasted energy and disrupted wild trout feeding. In a Montana study (Vincent), trout populations declined after stocking and rose in years without stocking.

**7. Funding and policy**
- Hatcheries typically consumed 50 to 80 percent of fishery budgets over the last 100 years (Lichatowich). In Oregon, a third of the agency budget went to hatcheries.

**8. Author's recommendations**
- Eliminate much artificial propagation and stocking, make remaining programs less destructive, and prioritize habitat protection and restoration and harvest control.
- Require fish culturists to show reasonable evidence of safety before stocking.

**9. Public and professional momentum**
- Multiple 1991 conferences, including the Nanaimo symposium on interactions of enhanced and wild salmonids and a genetic conservation meeting at Idaho and Washington State universities.
- Coalitions such as the Save Our (Wild) Salmon group and the Yakima River Alliance formed to oppose hatchery expansion.
