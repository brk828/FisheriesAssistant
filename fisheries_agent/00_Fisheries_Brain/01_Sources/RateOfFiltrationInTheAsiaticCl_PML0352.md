# Filtration Rate of the Asiatic Clam, Corbicula fluminea

#malacology #marine-biology #bivalve #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by Joseph K. Buttner and Roy C. Heidinger, from the [[Cooperative Fisheries Research Laboratory]] and Department of Zoology at [[Southern Illinois University]], measured the filtration rate of the [[Asiatic Clam]] ([[Corbicula Fluminea]]) fed a monospecific green alga, [[Scenedesmus]] sp. The mean rate was 347 ml/hr/clam. The authors argue that earlier reports of very low rates (below 100 ml/hr/clam) were artifacts of poorly selected food suspensions and of [[Turbidimeter]]-based quantification. They conclude that the realistic filtration rate of *C. fluminea* lies between 300 and more than 800 ml/hr/clam.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula fluminea* (Müller, 1774) is an invasive freshwater bivalve, also known as *C. manilensis* and *C. leana* in older literature. Filtration rate had been studied by several groups: [[Prokopovich 1969]], [[Habel 1970]], [[Auerbach et al. 1977]], [[Haines 1979]], and [[Mattice 1979]]. Published values fell into two groups, those above 100 ml/hr/clam and those below it. The stated aims were to measure the true filtration rate and to set out considerations for future bivalve filtration studies.

**Methods overview:**
- **Test animals:** Ten clams weighing 0.83 to 7.40 g, taken from laboratory stocks maintained for more than two years.
- **Algal food:** Monospecific *Scenedesmus* sp. cultures from Carolina Biological Supply Company, grown in Bold's Basic Medium, Volvox Medium, and Soil-water Medium.
- **Experimental design:** Paired 250 ml beakers, each holding 200 ml of diluted culture. One beaker received a clam and the other served as a control. Runs lasted 1.5 to 3.0 hr at 21 to 24 °C under continuous aeration.
- **Algal concentration:** 3,314 to 17,742 plants/ml (the Methods section gives 3,414 at one point; the Abstract's 3,314 is used here).
- **Counting:** 0.2 ml aliquots were taken at the start, during, and at the end of each run and counted at 500x magnification. A control aliquot confirmed clam-induced reduction.
- **Calculation:** Mean rates per clam were computed with the formula of [[Jorgensen 1943]].
- **Weights:** Total weight, excised soft-tissue weight, and dried soft-tissue weight were measured to 0.01 g. Dried tissue was oven-dried at 37 °C for 36 hr.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Filtration rates in bivalves depend strongly on the food particles offered. Clams select particles by size, shape, specific gravity, and quality, and reject unacceptable ones (Jorgensen, 1966). Related species such as *Corbicula sandai* and *Sphaerium fluminense* feed mainly on diatoms and green algae. In contrast, filamentous algae, algae with thick gelatinous sheaths, and blue-green algae are poorly used by filter feeders, with blue-greens ingested and assimilated at reduced rates. Within the studied suspensions, the genera *Melosira* and *Scenedesmus* were positively selected, and studies using them recorded rates above 100 ml/hr/clam.

Turbidity is a poor proxy for particle concentration because many variables affect the conversion of turbidity units to suspended solids (Duchrow and Everhart, 1971). Habel (1970) observed 30 to 50% fluctuations in phytoplankton numbers at identical turbidity readings.

## Key Findings

**Filtration rates (n = 10 clams, 21 to 24 °C, *Scenedesmus* sp. at 3,314 to 17,742 plants/ml):**
- Mean filtration rate: **347 ml/hr/clam** (range 160 to 861 ml/hr/clam).
- Per unit weight: **177 ml/hr/g** total weight (range 34 to 839), **1,561 ml/hr/g** soft-tissue weight, and **20,499 ml/hr/g** dried soft-tissue weight.
- Mean algal concentration was reported as 2,866 plants/ml in the table footnote.
- Filtration rate was not strongly correlated with clam weight (r < 0.10).

**Comparison with earlier work:**
- The present rates matched the high values of [[Auerbach et al. 1977]] and [[Mattice 1979]].
- They contrasted sharply with the low rates reported by [[Prokopovich 1969]], [[Habel 1970]], and [[Haines 1979]].
- The low studies used poorly selected particles, including artificial soil suspensions, colonial green algae, and filamentous blue-greens. [[Prokopovich 1969]] and [[Haines 1979]] also reported considerable clam mortality, which the authors interpret as stress from unsuitable food.
- All three low-rate studies used a turbidimeter to quantify particles, which the authors judge insufficiently sensitive.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- The actual filtration rate of *C. fluminea* is estimated at **300 to more than 800 ml/hr/clam**.
- Future bivalve studies should use food suspensions known to be positively selected.
- Particle quantification should use methods other than turbidity.
- Filtration rate should be reported per unit weight (total, soft-tissue, or dried soft-tissue weight) per unit time, not only per clam.
