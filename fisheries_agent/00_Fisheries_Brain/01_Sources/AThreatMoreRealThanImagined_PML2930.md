# Wildlife Views, December 1988: Arizona Live Wildlife Regulations and the Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tiaroga-cobitis

## Executive Summary

This December 1988 issue of [[Wildlife Views]], published by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], is a magazine compilation rather than a research report. Its central theme is the proposed revision of the state's [[Live Wildlife Regulations]], which govern the possession of native and exotic animals. The issue argues that restrictions protect wildlife and public health, and it describes the regulatory history, enforcement problems, and federal and international laws that are not sufficient on their own. It also includes a species profile of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), an article on invasive species such as the [[Bullfrog]], accounts of wildlife smuggling, and pet-keeping guidance for legal species, followed by a hunting calendar and public meeting schedule.

## Background & Description

The issue is a periodical with several articles by Department staff. Key contributors include Ray Kohls (Law Enforcement Specialist), Kirk L. Young (Wildlife Assistant, Nongame Branch), Jim deVos (Research Branch Supervisor), Jim Bidte (Special Investigator), Terry B. Johnson (Nongame Branch Supervisor), and Randy Babb (Education Branch). The regulatory discussion centers on the revision of rules that were first enacted on or after July 1, 1973, and that the Department was required to review periodically under the Administrative Procedures Act.

The loach minnow profile describes a small minnow rarely exceeding about 2.75 inches, with a flattened, elongate body and dark, blotched pigmentation. Breeding males show bright red-orange coloration. The species is ecologically the western equivalent of the eastern darter, and it lives on the bottom of swift riffles because a reduced air bladder gives it negative buoyancy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Loach minnow.** The species is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. It was once widespread in the drainage but is now restricted to the upper Gila River in New Mexico, [[Aravaipa Creek]], and the Blue, San Francisco, and White Rivers in Arizona. Most biological data come from the Aravaipa Creek population, where it feeds mainly on riffle-dwelling aquatic insect larvae, particularly black flies and mayflies. Adhesive eggs are laid under rocks and guarded by males. Females first spawn in their second year, with clutches of roughly 250 to 1,200 eggs. Few individuals survive beyond their fourth year. Threats include stream-channel diversion, groundwater pumping, channelization, silt from mining and grazing, and non-native fishes such as the [[Red Shiner]].

**Invasive and introduced species.** The bullfrog was introduced to the West, first in California in the early 1900s, mainly as a source of frog legs. Stomach analysis from a Colorado River study near Yuma showed a broad diet including crayfish, wolf spiders, and cockroaches, with incidental prey such as muskrats, rattlesnakes, and fish. Department and Bureau of Reclamation surveys found bullfrogs at many historic [[Leopard Frog]] sites where native frogs had disappeared, providing circumstantial evidence of their role in the decline. A Department study found a strong negative correlation between bullfrog distribution and both leopard frog and [[Mexican Garter Snake]] distribution. Where bullfrogs were abundant on the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge, garter snake populations were dominated by large adults and most adults had damaged tails, apparently from failed bullfrog predation attempts.

Other examples cited include the [[Walking Catfish]] in Florida and the African clawed frog, both of which could establish if released in Arizona. The article also notes historical disease transfer, with mites from domestic sheep linked to bighorn sheep die-offs.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory rationale**
- Possession of native wildlife is generally prohibited or restricted, based on public health and safety and on the conservation of wild populations. Limited exceptions are granted for special purposes.
- The Department's proposed revision defines "restricted wildlife" as species that cannot be possessed without a special Department license. Listing criteria include competition with native wildlife, disease or parasite risk, habitat degradation, predation, danger to people, property damage, and nuisance.
- The pet trade is a major outlet for wildlife. About ten percent of animals sold are native to the United States. In the early 1970s, only about one in twenty wild animals destined for trade survived its first year in captivity.
- Roadside zoos, which housed animals in substandard conditions and returned dead animals to the wild or replaced them, prompted the original regulations.

**Species-specific concerns**
- Small rodents such as squirrels and chipmunks are hyperactive, bite readily, and may carry disease, including rabies.
- Most birds are protected under the federal [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]]. Parrots must be quarantined for 30 days before entry under rules in place since 1970, because of diseases such as [[Psittacosis]] and [[Newcastle Disease]].
- Alligators grew from about one foot to eight feet in roughly 15 years of captivity, leading to a landlord-forced removal.
- Federal rules require turtles to be at least four inches in diameter before sale. Some exotic snakes carry venom for which antivenom is unavailable in Arizona, so they are prohibited.
- Raccoons and skunks are described as poor pets, with rabies risk and unpredictable adult behavior.

**Enforcement and smuggling**
- Arizona's location makes it a route for smuggled reptiles and parrots from Mexico. One large shipment reportedly contained several thousand reptiles, about three-quarters of which were dead on arrival. Several large shipments were seized at the border in recent summers.
- The [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] had four special agents in Arizona, compared with more than 100 commissioned state Game and Fish officers.
- A Kingman man received Mohave County's first felony wildlife conviction for illegally obtaining and selling great horned owl chicks. He served 38 days in jail, paid a $787 fine, and received one year of probation.

**Legal framework**
- Arizona's game laws (ARS Title 17) authorize a five-member Commission to adopt rules governing live wildlife. Commission authority over live wildlife comes from ARS 17-306.
- The [[Lacey Act]] covers interstate and international trafficking in illegally taken wildlife, but prosecutions are difficult. An attempted case involving thick-billed parrots failed because the birds could only be charged as unlawful possession under state law.
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] regulates federally listed species, but it does not protect non-listed species, and it does not set care standards for reptiles, amphibians, fish, or birds.
- [[CITES]] regulates international trade in listed species but does not address captive-holding risks within Arizona.

**Conservation status and management**
- The loach minnow was federally listed as threatened in 1987 and appears on the Department's Threatened Native Wildlife list. Management priorities are to protect existing populations and habitat, identify threat factors, and remove them.
- Special-status reptiles and amphibians include the [[Desert Tortoise]] (protected as of January 1, 1988), the [[Gila Monster]], the [[Massasauga]] (no longer collectible from the wild without permits), and the [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (season reopened in 1988).

**Public process and other notes**
- Revised rules were to be presented for adoption at the Commission's January 19, 1989, meeting. Public meetings were scheduled in Sierra Vista, Sedona, Pinetop, Kingman, Flagstaff, Tucson, Yuma, Mesa, and Phoenix between January 4 and 7, 1989.
- The issue also includes pet-keeping articles on [[Kangaroo Rat]] and other rodents, snakes, lizards, geckos, amphibians, and invertebrates. These emphasize that wild animals need specialized diets, housing, humidity, and temperature, and that most do poorly in captivity.
- A hunting and fishing calendar lists key late-2088 dates, including the end of the 1988 license year and the start of the 1989 licensing year.
