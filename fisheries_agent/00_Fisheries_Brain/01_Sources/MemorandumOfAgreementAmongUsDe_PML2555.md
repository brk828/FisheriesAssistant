# Memorandum of Agreement for Stocking Captive-Reared Endangered Colorado River Fishes into Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) among the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[National Park Service]] (Glen Canyon National Recreation Area), and the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], with the [[Arizona Department of Game and Fish]] also involved. It sets conditions under which captive-reared populations of four endangered Colorado River fishes may be released into [[Lake Powell]], a reservoir managed as a sport fishery. The MOA was prompted by a surplus of captive-reared fish from the [[Upper Colorado Recovery Implementation Program]] (UCRIP) that the program could not house or use in its current recovery work. The agreement establishes stocking limits, tagging requirements, recapture data sharing, protections for sportfishing, and a termination process.

## Background & Description

**Parties and authority.** The MOA is made under Section 7(a)(1) of the [[Endangered Species Act]], which directs federal agencies to use their authorities to conserve listed species. Section 6(c) authorizes cooperative agreements with state agencies. Utah Code section 23-14.1 designates the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources as the wildlife authority for Utah, so an agreement with that Division was made for conservation of the four species. The [[Organic Act of 1916]] and the enabling legislation for [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] also direct management to protect endangered fish while providing recreational fishing.

**Target species.** The MOA covers four endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]:
- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), also called Colorado squawfish
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*)

**Lake Powell.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]], completed in 1963, created Lake Powell, which spans the Arizona-Utah border. The text describes the reservoir as encompassing nearly 2,000 miles of shoreline and over 180 miles in length, making it the second largest manmade reservoir in the United States. Filling occurred from 1953 until the early 1980s. The filling altered the free-flowing Colorado River and the habitat of the native ichthyofauna. A [[Sport Fishery]] developed as [[Nonnative Species]] such as largemouth bass, crappie, smallmouth bass, walleye, and striped bass proliferated, while native species declined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish decline.** Many capture records of native fishes, including bonytail, Colorado pikeminnow, and razorback sucker, were logged during the filling period, but native populations were displaced. Other projects in the basin, such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Fontenelle Dam]], [[Aspinall Unit]], and the [[Central Utah Project]], also fragmented and altered the historical habitat of the native fauna.

**Recovery programs.** Two programs address recovery of the four species:
- The UCRIP, established in 1989, aims to recover the four fishes in the mainstem Green and Colorado basins while allowing continued river development (depletion only) as long as recovery progress is sufficient.
- The [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (SJRIP), established in 1990, has similar goals for the San Juan River basin and its tributaries, including the Animas and La Plata Rivers.

Most funded activities occur in mainstem river habitats, including the Green River and its tributaries (Yampa, White, Duchesne, and San Rafael), the Colorado and Gunnison Rivers, and the mainstem San Juan River. Seasonal habitats form at the inflow areas of Lake Powell due to annual hydrology and Glen Canyon Dam operations.

**Inflow area studies.** Captures of endangered fish, especially Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker, were recorded in Lake Powell inflow areas from the late 1980s into the early 1990s. In the early 1980s, large numbers of adult Colorado pikeminnow were captured at [[Gypsum Canyon]] on the Colorado River. A [[Bio/West Study]] conducted for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] found that:
1. The inflow areas were dominated by nonnative species.
2. Adult and young-of-the-year endangered fish were captured annually, but habitats were ephemeral because of Glen Canyon Dam operations and annual inflow fluctuations.
3. Significant stranding mortality occurred when endangered fish habitat dried.
4. Available habitat was limited and varied seasonally.

Similar San Juan River inflow studies, funded by the Bureau of Reclamation for the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] under Section 7 consultation in 1989-1990, produced similar results. Adult razorback sucker were captured and recaptured in later years, but backwater habitats were even more limited, and only a few young-of-the-year were documented. The capture of larvae indicated a reproducing population of Colorado pikeminnow in the main river.

**Critical habitat.** In 1992-1993, the Service designated [[Critical Habitat]] for the four species. The inflow areas were designated as potential rearing habitats, although they are ephemeral and have high numbers of nonnative species. Lake Powell itself was not designated. The Service and managing agencies consider the lacustrine (lake) environment unsuitable for the endangered fish, so it offers no opportunity for survival and recovery.

## Key Findings

- **Surplus captive-reared fish.** The UCRIP's [[Captive Propagation Program]] produced more fish than needed, because attrition rates were unknown. Surplus fish strain limited broodstock and propagation facilities and operation and maintenance funds. The program sought a repository for these fish. Euthanasia was the last resort and was not preferred by the UCRIP or the Service.
- **Rationale for Lake Powell.** The UCRIP considers Lake Powell suitable because it lies within the Colorado River system and the species' historic range. Upstream or downstream escape from the lake is considered unlikely, and seasonal habitat offers some certainty of survival for stocked fish.
- **Stocking estimate.** Annual stocking numbers are difficult to predict. No more than 4,500 fish are estimated for the first year, and more fish are likely in year one than later because the facilities have held excess fish for several years.
- **Concerns.** The Division and the Arizona Department of Game and Fish are concerned about stocking endangered fish into a reservoir managed as a sport fishery. The MOA was written to set the conditions for release.
- **Stocking conditions (Section V).**
  - (a) All parties cooperate to reach recovery goals.
  - (b) Only captive-reared fish will be released into areas of Lake Powell that are presently considered critical habitat... as stated in the source; state and federal permits are required before release, and the stocking program must be coordinated with the Service, National Park Service, and Division.
  - (c) All fish are marked with a long-term identification tag approved by all parties. Recapture data are shared, and tag identification records are maintained by the Service in Grand Junction.
  - (d) Sportfish management will not be restricted because of stocking. Protocols for new introductions will be reviewed under the framework of the Glen Canyon NRA fish management plan.
  - (e) Proclamations will advise the sportfishing public of the potential to capture endangered fish, and anglers will be advised to release them as quickly as possible.
  - (f) Research activities must be consistent with the Fish Management Plan.
  - (g) Each year, the UCRIP will report propagation activities and make a written request to the Division and National Park Service indicating the number, size, and location of stocking.
  - (h) Persons found unlawfully and intentionally "taking" endangered fish may be prosecuted under Section 9 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Termination.** Any party may end the MOA with 30 days' written notice. A facilitated meeting will follow to determine the cause and resolution. Stocked fish will not be used as a basis to restrict future sportfish management if the MOA is terminated.
