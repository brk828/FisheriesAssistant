# Environmental Assessment: Management of the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rio-yaqui-fishes

## Executive Summary

This November 1985 Environmental Assessment (EA), prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) in Albuquerque, New Mexico, evaluates management options for the 2,308.9-acre [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR) in Cochise County, Arizona. The refuge was acquired primarily to protect six endangered, threatened, and proposed [[Rio Yaqui Fishes]]. The EA compares three alternatives: no action, maximum development, and a preferred alternative. The preferred alternative develops existing springs and wells for fish habitat, controls erosion in [[Black Draw]], restores abandoned farmland, thins mesquite, and adds modest public-use facilities. The plan runs for 10 years. The authors state that many biological and hydrological data gaps exist, so the proposed developments are experimental and depend on a pending [[Aquifer Study]].

## Background & Description

The refuge sits 16 miles east of Douglas, Arizona. The Service staffs it at a minimal "caretaker" level, with a Fish and Wildlife Biologist/Refuge Manager and seasonal employees. Administrative oversight moved from [[Bosque del Apache National Wildlife Refuge]] to the newly acquired [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] on October 1, 1985.

The refuge's aquatic features are its main value. Springs and wells have long supported:

- Longfin dace (not listed, but given special emphasis)
- Yaqui chub
- Beautiful shiner
- Yaqui topminnow

Historical records for Yaqui catfish, stoneroller, and sucker on the refuge are incomplete. The Service expects that suitable refuge habitats will allow these species to be reestablished, replacing populations that have been lost or that now survive only in Mexico. The document states that life history data are complete only for the topminnow. Management is therefore framed as "trial and error," with actions to be revised as aquifer data and monitoring results arrive.

The EA describes the refuge's history of intensive farming and pasturing before acquisition, followed by abandonment. It also notes a dense, nearly impenetrable [[Mesquite Bosque]] on the north end. A 1979 freeze top-killed much of the bosque, and resprouting created the current thicket.

The refuge is on the U.S./Mexico border, and its southern boundary lies 60 feet north of the border fence. Thirty-five prehistoric and historic archaeological sites have been recorded, and none are yet listed on the [[National Register of Historic Places]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate:** The refuge has a biseasonal southwestern climate. Mean annual precipitation is 12.4 inches. Maximum summer temperatures average above 95°F, and the winter mean minimum is 40°F. Spring drought, with high temperatures in May and June, is described as biologically more severe than fall drought.

**Water source:** The document attributes the artesian springs and wells to an 1887 earthquake. The quake caused a fault south of the refuge in Mexico, forming an impervious barrier and an artesian reservoir centered just above the border. This [[Artesian Aquifer]] extends north onto Stevens' Ranch and south into Mexico. The document calls water sources the most important features to protect.

**Vegetation (approximate acreages):**

| Vegetation type | Acres |
|---|---|
| [[Chihuahuan Desert Scrub]] | 1,689 |
| Irrigated pasture | 250 |
| Mesquite bosque | 250 |
| Riparian/aquatic | 120 |

Desert scrub is dominated by creosote bush, tarbush, and white-thorn acacia. Riparian areas are dominated by cottonwood and willows, but [[Cattail Invasion]] (Typha) has displaced considerable depth and surface area.

**Wildlife:** Mule deer (up to about 20) and javelina (5–10) use the refuge seasonally. A Douglas High School biology teacher compiled a list of 124 bird species from eight day trips between 1973 and 1978. Waterfowl, shorebirds, and marsh birds are most abundant in winter and migration. Migratory songbirds use the mesquite thickets.

**Regional pressures:**
- Downcutting in Black Draw has created an erosion scar, lowered the water table and associated springs, and allowed exotic fish to enter refuge waters.
- Geothermal exploration near the refuge could significantly affect the artesian aquifer. The Bureau of Land Management has received exploration permit applications, and [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] will be required.
- Dust and occasional smoke from the Douglas smelter affect air quality.

**Cultural resources:** Surveys by the [[Arizona State Museum]] documented Archaic sites (late Archaic about 1500–500 B.C. is most prevalent), Animas phase sites (about A.D. 1200–1300), Salado-period pueblos, and historic Slaughter Ranch features (1884 onward). The adjacent [[Johnson Historical Museum of the Southwest]] (JHMSW) owns 131 acres and holds an easement for fisheries management in House Pond.

## Key Findings

**Management alternatives compared:**

1. **Alternative 1, No Action:** Maintains caretaker status. Springs and wells stay undeveloped, exotic fish can invade Border Spring, House Pond, and Black Draw without intrusion barriers, and the refuge cannot reach target fish population levels. Hunting and woodcutting are not permitted. The EA concludes that the refuge would fail to achieve public goals for endangered fish.

2. **Alternative 2, Maximize Development:** Maximizes spring and well flows, adds new wells and pumps, creates extensive wetlands, acquires access east of Black Draw, and allows hunting. It would exceed fish population objectives and attract more visitors (an added 450 visits and 2,100 activity hours yearly from hunting). However, it would cost more per visitor, risk over-development, and could deplete the aquifer if pumping outpaces recharge.

3. **Alternative 3, Preferred:** Develops existing waters, limits land management, and adds moderate public-use facilities. Its key elements are:
   - Maximizing riffle, run, and pool habitats at each spring and well site.
   - Reopening previously shut-off springs and recreating ponds, cienegas, and stream runs from the early 1900s.
   - Gabion erosion control in Black Draw (healing will take far longer than a human lifetime).
   - Native grass and forb strip plantings on abandoned fields.
   - Selective mesquite cutting and stump treatment.
   - Eliminating grazing until range recovery.

**Fish outcomes:** The preferred alternative is expected to support at least 12 mixed-species populations of fishes at discrete, independent spring and well sites. Figure 1 gives projected sustained population numbers for each site, but the figure text is largely illegible in the source.

**Public use:**
- The interpretive and wildlife-observation program is expected to generate 3,200 visits and 5,960 activity hours annually.
- Other visitation is projected at 500 visits and 1,000 activity hours yearly.
- Up to 3,000 museum visitors per year may also visit the refuge.
- Facilities (entrance road, parking, overlook, restrooms, signs) will directly affect fewer than 5 acres.
- Hunting (quail, dove, rabbit) was withdrawn from the plan after public opposition. It will proceed only if suitable access is acquired.

**Water:** The aquifer study by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] is funded by the endangered species program for 3 years, beginning fiscal year 1985. The document states the study is the highest refuge planning priority. Developments may increase evaporation but reduce transpiration losses in dense cattail stands. Eliminating Black Draw downcutting should eventually raise the water table.

**Irreversible commitments:** The preferred alternative commits about 5 acres to the entrance road and shop building. Other sites can be restored. The main lasting cost is funding for development and maintenance.

**Public involvement:** The Service mailed comments requests to 560 contacts, received 26 letters or calls, and received 82 requests to remain on the mailing list. No public meetings were held. Most public comments opposed hunting, and the plan incorporated most other suggestions.

**Conclusion:** The EA treats all proposed developments as experimental until life history data are gathered and the aquifer study is complete. Plans will be revised as new information arrives.
