# Genetic Analysis of the Scalation Trait in Anatolian Toothcarps (Cyprinodontidae) by Testcross Experiments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by [[Wolfgang Villwock]] (Zoologisches Staatsinstitut und Museum Hamburg) reports testcross experiments on the scalation ("Beschuppung") of Anatolian toothcarps. Reduced scalation occurs in several endemic Anatolian [[Cyprinodontidae]] populations, and the author asks whether normal, complete scalation has a different genetic basis among populations. To test this, he crossed a strongly scale-reduced stock, [[Kosswigichthys Asquamatus]], as a constant "pick-up" (Aufleser) partner with 25 populations from at least five [[Aphanius]] species, yielding roughly 660 hybrids. The results support at least three genetically distinct systems for good scalation, which are interpreted as quantitative, polygenic differences. The paper also argues that scalation alone is unsuitable for systematics, though it can serve as one supporting character in phylogenetic analysis.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier work by [[Aksiray]] (1948, 1952), [[Kosswig]] (1953, 1956), [[Erni]] (1945), and [[Villwock]] (1958). Earlier authors had reported scale-reduction phenomena and had proposed "dominant" and "recessive" scalation genes, a view that Villwock re-examines.

- **Test stock:** *Kosswigichthys asquamatus* Sözer 1942 is described as extremely scale-reduced and assumed to be largely homozygous for this trait. It was used consistently as one parent so that differences among F1 hybrids could be attributed to the other parent's genotype.
- **Methods:** Crosses were made in 25-litre aquaria at the Hamburg institute under uniform freshwater conditions. Spawning substrates were thread algae and green perlon wool, and tanks were aerated and filtered through gravel and activated charcoal. Pair crosses were made, and reciprocal crosses were performed where possible. Kosswigichthys parents were reused across crosses, although some were worn out over time.
- **Scoring:** Scale counts were taken in the middle longitudinal row (MLR) and the transverse line (Ltr). Hybrids were also assigned to four general scalation types (symbol-coded in Figure 2).
- **Population groups:** The test populations were sorted into four groups:
  - **Group I:** Aci-Göl ("Anatolichthys") populations, including Gelif, Azmak, Tahtaköprü, Akpunar, Gemiş, and Gölcük-Gölü. Some have mostly normal scales, while Akpunar and Gölcük are obligately scale-reduced.
  - **Group II:** Burdur fishes (a panmictic macropopulation with mixed scalation types), Eğridir, and Konya/Çumra.
  - **Group III:** Normally scaled Central and Southwest Anatolian populations, plus the Kızılırmak populations (Samsun, İlıpunar).
  - **Group IV:** Non-endemic *Aphanius* species (*A. sophiae, A. cypris, A. iberus, A. fasciatus, A. dispar*, and *A. richardsoni*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Anatolian toothcarps live in isolated freshwater habitats, including the Aci-Göl and Tuz Gölü basins, the Burdur and Eğridir lakes, the Konya Plain on the western margin of the central Anatolian steppe, and Kızılırmak drainages. Villwock links scalation differences to population dynamics and to environmental history:

- Aci-Göl populations live in separate freshwater inflows, and the reduced individuals appear in most year classes, probably because of small population sizes and fluctuations between years.
- Under favorable hydrographic conditions, the scale-reduced Aci-Göl populations can form a single large macropopulation, which would prevent their long-term divergence.
- The author suggests that scale variation may reflect selective change of genotypes under unequal environmental conditions, though he does not settle this question. He considers it possible that the [[Hybrid-Swarm Hypothesis]] and a selection-based explanation both apply.
- The populations are considered to have arisen in the relatively short time since the [[Postglacial Period]], which limits how different their genetic systems could have become.

## Key Findings

1. **Testcross design:** Crossing 25 populations of at least five *Aphanius* species with *K. asquamatus* produced about 660 hybrids. Hybrid scale counts were compared to the parental counts reported in Table 2.

2. **Consonance of Konya/Çumra with Aci-Göl:** Hybrids from Konya and Çumra (Group II) closely resemble hybrids from the normally scaled Gelif and Azmak Aci-Göl populations in both mean scale counts and range. Reciprocal crosses also show that Konya and Aci-Göl populations are fertile in both sexes, whereas Çumra crosses with Burdur fish yield only fertile females. Villwock concludes that Konya/Çumra are more closely related to the Aci-Göl populations than to other normally scaled Anatolians.

3. **Three groups of genetic systems:** The author concludes that good scalation is controlled by at least three genetic systems:
   - **(a)** Aci-Göl populations (Gelif, Azmak, Karahdayık), together with Konya and Çumra, with the fewest scalation genes.
   - **(b)** The "A" populations (Eğridir, Söğüt-Gölü, Obruk, Cihanbeyli, Niğde), intermediate in gene number.
   - **(c)** The "B" populations (Maanderquelle, Ilgın, Samsun, İlıpunar), with the largest gene number.

4. **Polygenic basis:** The wide range of scale counts in hybrids from normally scaled parents is taken as evidence that good scalation is polyfactorial. Crossing disturbs the panmictic balance of each population, which can produce more extreme scale counts and, through recombination, scale reduction in later generations.

5. **Reinterpreting Aksiray (1952):** Aksiray had concluded that good scalation is "recessive" in Gelif and Azmak. Villwock argues instead that the Aci-Göl populations contain individuals with reduced scalation in nearly every year class, and that Aksiray's reduced hybrids came from chance crosses of carriers of weaker reductions. This avoids postulating different "dominant" and "recessive" gene systems in closely related populations.

6. **Systematic value:** Scalation alone is unsuitable for systematic classification, because identical scalation may hide different genotypes that only show up in crosses. Still, scalation correlates with other characters such as hybrid fertility or sterility, so it can be a supporting character in relationship analysis.

7. **Revised taxonomic conclusions:** Normally scaled endemic *Aphanius* populations represent at least two species-rank units:
   - **(a)** The Kızılırmak populations.
   - **(b)** The central Anatolian populations, with Konya and Çumra probably separable from the other central Anatolian populations.
   
   The Aci-Göl populations, despite large scalation differences, are treated as a single systematic unit within the *Aphanius* complex, because they are both natural members of the lake fauna and closely related to Konya and Çumra.

8. **Non-endemic species:** Hybrids of *K. asquamatus* with *A. cypris* show the most uniform body covering but not normal scale shape or size, so this similarity is not taken as a meaningful relationship. Because the non-endemic *Aphanius* species are probably genetically distinct from the Anatolian forms, similarities between their hybrids and Anatolian ones are regarded as largely accidental.

9. **Caveats:** The author stresses that sample sizes for several crosses are small and that some Kosswigichthys parents were exhausted over the course of the work. Quantitative experiments were still in progress at the time of writing, and the question of whether gene number differences, gene quality differences, or both explain the variation was left open.
