# Central Arizona Project Biological Opinion Implementation

#ichthyology #conservation-policy #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1994 memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) to the Bureau of Reclamation (BR) outlines the implementation strategy for two funding mechanisms — **recovery funding** and **nonnative species management funding** — mandated by the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) of the April 20, 1994 Biological Opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The document is not a primary biological/morphological study but a regulatory and administrative framework addressing jeopardy to four listed fish species in the [[Gila River Basin]] caused by the CAP water diversion project. The core conservation strategy relies on the concept of **mitigation** — using recovery actions against non-CAP threats to offset unalleviated CAP-related threats — implemented through the Service's existing recovery infrastructure rather than through a new BR-led program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: The four jeopardized fish species are primarily associated with the **[[Gila River Basin]]**, a tributary system of the Lower Colorado River. The [[Razorback Sucker]] additionally occupies habitats **outside** the Gila River basin within the broader **[[Lower Colorado River]]** system, per the draft "Lower Colorado River Native Fish Management Plan with Emphasis on Big River Fishes."
- **Threats identified**:
  - Habitat and flow alterations attributable to the **[[Central Arizona Project]]** (CAP water diversion).
  - **Nonnative species incursion**, explicitly identified as a major threat requiring a dedicated management fund. The RPA's underlying premise is that **fish barriers alone are insufficient** to prevent nonnative fish invasion without an accompanying active management program.
  - Non-CAP threats broadly, addressed via mitigation-based recovery actions to improve overall species status.
- **Interacting management framework**: Recovery efforts are coordinated through existing bodies including the **[[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]]** and the **[[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]]**, in cooperation with state wildlife agencies (AGFD, NMGF), federal land managers (BLM, NPS, Forest Service), tribal governments (White Mountain Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Tribe, Gila River Indian Community), and academic biologists.

## Key Findings
- **Four listed fish species** in the Gila River basin were found to be jeopardized by the CAP; three of the four had approved recovery plans at the time of writing, while the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** recovery plan was still in early preparation, with an interim draft management plan serving as guidance.
- The RPA established two BR-funded mechanisms under Service lead responsibility:
  1. **Recovery Fund** — implements existing recovery plans to achieve down-listing/de-listing, restricted to actions that measurably "remove the threat of jeopardy from CAP" and primarily confined geographically to the Gila River basin (with exceptions for razorback sucker given its wider range).
  2. **Nonnative Species Management Fund** — allocated approximately **50% to emergency contingency planning**, **35% to ongoing nonnative removal/management**, and **15% to supporting research**.
- Mitigation strategy: Because no technically or economically feasible method existed to fully alleviate CAP-driven threats, the Service adopted a mitigation approach — using recovery actions against unrelated (non-CAP) threats to offset CAP impacts and improve overall species status, a controversial but previously successful tactic for the Service.
- Administrative approach avoided creating duplicative infrastructure: rather than transferring recovery lead responsibility to BR (raising legal and administrative concerns, since species recovery is legally the Service's and States' responsibility), funding was channeled through the Service's **existing recovery program and infrastructure**.
- New process requirements were limited to: (1) fund transfer mechanisms, (2) BR review/approval of expenditures, and (3) annual Service progress reports to BR on jeopardy mitigation — reflecting BR's statutory responsibility for jeopardy removal despite delegating implementation to the Service.
- The nonnative species management fund required **entirely new coordination processes**, including formation of a multi-agency working group/committee (with possible administrative and biological subgroups) to develop and oversee a **contingency plan** for emergency response to nonnative fish invasions, formalized via cooperative agreement or MOU, and subject to NEPA-based public input.
- Funding execution was expected to follow the **Economy Act (31 U.S.C. 1535)**, generally through competitive contracting to Federal offices, State agencies, or outside entities based on best available expertise.
- Key referenced recovery infrastructure elements: recovery plans' **implementation schedules**, the December 9, 1991 **Regional Director's memorandum** establishing lead office responsibilities, and oversight by the Regional Office/Ecosystem Team with advisory input from Recovery Teams.
