# Opposition to Rainbow Smelt Introduction in Lake Powell

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #osmeridae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This letter from Dr. Stephen P. Vives, ichthyologist at Arizona State University, formally opposes a 1989 proposal by the Utah Department of Natural Resources to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]], Utah-Arizona. The letter argues that smelt introduction would jeopardize endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes including [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) through direct predation and indirect competition. Dr. Vives contends the proposal understates risks, overstates benefits, lacks sufficient justification, and contradicts biodiversity conservation principles endorsed by the [[National Science Foundation]] and [[American Association for the Advancement of Science]].

## Background & Description

The proposal under review, authored by A. Wayne Gustaveson and Bruce Bonebrake, sought to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] into [[Lake Powell]] to support the [[Striped Bass]] fishery. [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) are anadromous fish native to the Great Lakes and northeastern North America. The species exhibits high reproductive potential, with each female capable of producing large numbers of eggs. Adult smelt are known predators of fish and fish eggs across multiple habitat types.

Historical evidence from the [[Great Lakes]] region documents smelt population explosions in the late 1930s and early 1940s. Van Oosten (1940) documented smelt predation on diverse prey including smelt, [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Yellow Perch]], sculpin, burbot, [[Rock Bass]], coregonids, stickleback, and smelt eggs. Gordon (1961) demonstrated that for age group I and older smelt, fishes constitute an important dietary component. Smelt undergo diel vertical migrations that remain poorly understood, potentially crossing hypothesized temperature barriers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system contains three federally endangered fish species: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. These native cyprinids and catostomids face multiple threats to their survival and recovery. The proposal's target location, [[Lake Powell]], connects downstream to [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Havasu]], creating potential pathways for smelt dispersal throughout the basin.

Evidence from other regions demonstrates smelt cannot be contained once introduced. Rupp (1965) and Becker (1983) documented that smelt regularly move distances up to 15 miles during spawning runs, contradicting the proposal's citation of a 1935 study claiming smelt "seldom go upstream more than a quarter mile." Berst and Spangler (1973) documented the smelt explosion in [[Lake Huron]] and blamed smelt for over-utilizing the base of the production pyramid, noting corresponding declines in recruitment of [[Lake Trout]], whitefish, cisco, and chubs. Crowder (1980) concluded smelt were implicated in the reduction or disappearance of several fishes with pelagic life stages, with strong evidence linking smelt to declines in [[Emerald Shiner]] populations. Lyons (1987) reported large numbers of young-of-year [[Yellow Perch]] consumed by smelt even when available data did not place the species at the same depths.

## Key Findings

**Deficiencies in the Proposal:**
- No data provided demonstrating that primary productivity is underutilized in [[Lake Powell]]
- No bioenergetic model presented showing potential costs and benefits for [[Striped Bass]] and other community members
- Alternative management strategies for the striped bass fishery (e.g., slot limits) not evaluated
- Proposal understates potential problems and overstates potential benefits

**Ecological Risks Identified:**

1. **Uncontrollable Spread**: Historical stocking in other areas demonstrates smelt dispersal cannot be controlled; guaranteed bait-bucket releases due to easy collection during spawning runs

2. **Predation Impacts**: Smelt prey on fishes across multiple habitats (benthic, pelagic) and life stages; direct threat to endangered species through predation on juveniles and eggs

3. **Competition**: Indirect impacts through competition for resources shared by life stages of native fishes

4. **Behavioral Uncertainty**: Diel vertical migrations poorly understood, potentially allowing access to habitats assumed to be thermal barriers

5. **Basin-Wide Threat**: Smelt will spread downstream from [[Lake Powell]] into the [[Colorado River]] and Lakes [[Mead]], [[Mohave]], and [[Havasu]], impacting endangered species throughout the system

6. **Biodiversity Loss**: Introduction contrary to biodiversity conservation principles; threatens basin biodiversity

**Specific Concerns for Endangered Species:**
- Direct predation on [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]
- Indirect competition impacts
- Any impact by non-native smelt on these three endangered fishes deemed unacceptable
- Would make re-establishment of endangered species more difficult or impossible
- Regarding [[Lake Mohave]] [[Razorback Sucker]]: adding smelt as another potential problem makes determining and correcting recruitment failure more difficult or impossible

**Regional Precedents:**
- Wisconsin and Minnesota fishery biologists and ichthyologists would likely consider smelt introduction a mistake
- Particular concern about smelt spread into Minnesota boundary waters
- Contrast with North Dakota citation in proposal claiming smelt are "salvation" of Missouri River system reservoirs

**Recommendation**: Dr. Vives urged the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to oppose the introduction and prevent its implementation.
