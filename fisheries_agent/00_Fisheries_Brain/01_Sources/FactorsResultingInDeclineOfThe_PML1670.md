# Decline of the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

The endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) has declined sharply in distribution and abundance over recent decades. In the United States it now survives only in a few isolated localities in southern Arizona. The authors attribute the decline to habitat destruction and to the introduction of the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other non-native fishes. They conclude that predation on juvenile topminnows by introduced fishes in shallow, refuge-like waters is the most likely mechanism of replacement, while competition and abiotic change appear to play lesser roles. The study draws on field surveys conducted from August 1979 to July 1981, and the authors recommend continued monitoring, control of further introductions, and protection of remaining populations and refugia.

## Background & Description

The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] is a small poeciliid livebearer native to the Gila River system of Arizona and New Mexico and to northwestern Mexico. Two forms are recognised: *P. o. occidentalis* (the Gila topminnow) in the Gila River system, and *P. o. sonoriensis* in the Rio Yaqui basin, which extends north into Arizona. The northern form is currently widespread and abundant in Mexico but has been lost from much of its former US range.

The species is the northernmost member of the genus *Poeciliopsis*, whose range extends along the Pacific drainages of Central America south to Colombia. Its decline is set against a wider background in which arid western North America has a depauperate, largely endemic fish fauna. The [[Endangered Species Act]] listing reflects this status. The authors note that at least four western fish species and six other taxa were lost in the previous 40 years (OCR-garbled, likely subspecies), and that many North American fishes are listed as endangered or threatened.

Three hypotheses for decline are framed in the paper:
1. **Abiotic change:** habitat alteration, desiccation, flooding, and water chemistry changes linked to [[Arroyo Cutting]], channelisation, and groundwater pumping.
2. **Competition:** with introduced species for food, space, or spawning sites.
3. **Predation:** by introduced fishes on juvenile and adult topminnows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native habitats:** Ciénegas (riparian marshlands), springs and spring runs, and shallow vegetated waters. Arroyo cutting drained many of these, removing refuges from large native predators such as chubs (*Gila* spp.).
- **Introduced fishes:** The [[Mosquitofish]] was first recorded in Arizona in 1926 and rapidly replaced topminnows, often within a year or two of introduction. Other non-native species in the study systems included [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*).
- **Native associates:** [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) persisted with topminnows in the upper Santa Cruz River.
- **Physical disruption:** Floods breached an alluvial fan that had isolated springs near Bylas, allowing mosquitofish and red shiners access. Dry reaches and dams form barriers to native fish re-invasion.
- **Environmental tolerance:** Topminnows tolerate salinities from tap water to full seawater, temperatures from near freezing under ice to 36.7 °C, and one to two days of drying in warm mud. Moderate changes in oxygen, conductivity, alkalinity, pH, and ionic content had little effect provided some surface water remained.
- **Floods:** Moderate floods had no measurable impact. Extreme "100-year" floods eliminated introduced topminnow populations at [[Tule Creek]] and [[Aravaipa Creek]] where refugia were absent.

### Study Methods

All known US populations were visited one to ten times between August 1979 and July 1981. Sampling used 3 mm mesh dip nets, seines, and larger-mesh seines. Specimens were preserved, and live stock was taken to the laboratory. Environmental parameters including water temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductance, alkalinity, and ion concentrations were measured at most sites. Collections were made under Federal permits and deposited at Arizona State University.

## Key Findings

**Status and distribution**
- Currently survives in the US only in isolated localities in southern Arizona, and is listed as endangered by the US Department of the Interior (1980).
- Mexican populations of *P. o. sonoriensis* remain widespread and abundant in Sonora, though the effects of recent mosquitofish introductions there were not yet assessable.
- Localities where topminnows persist are mostly isolated springs or stream segments protected by barriers, many on private land.

**Replacement by mosquitofish (case studies)**
- **Sheehy Spring:** Upper reach free of mosquitofish after March 1977 and topminnows abundant. By December 1979 mosquitofish had invaded, and topminnows declined to near extinction by 1981.
- **House Pond, San Bernardino Ranch:** Topminnows recorded in 1943 but absent in 1964 after introduction of black crappie and black bullhead. Mosquitofish arrived in late 1979, and topminnows were not observed by September 1980 despite restocking in May 1980.
- **Bylas springs:** Mosquitofish invaded after floods breached the fan in winter 1978–79. In August 1980 mosquitofish outnumbered topminnows by at least 20 to 1 in Salt Creek. No topminnows were found in Salt Creek or one spring in October 1980, while the other springhead contained 62% of 147 fish sampled. No juveniles were observed in any system, and nearly all topminnows showed shredded fins. A new spring with 100–150 topminnows and no mosquitofish was found in June 1981 and remains secure.
- **Cocio Wash:** Topminnows were present in the mid-1970s but green sunfish invaded after flooding by March 1979. Only 11 topminnows were collected in June 1980, and fish of any species were absent after September 1980 flooding. Adults and juveniles reappeared in mid-August 1981.
- **Santa Cruz River:** Populations fluctuated greatly for at least 20 years, with topminnow numbers generally inversely related to introduced fish numbers. The species was considered extirpated from 1963 to 1974. Topminnows were in the hundreds in June 1981, but the reach dried two weeks later.
- **Sonoita Creek:** Topminnows coexisted with mosquitofish for at least seven years after 1969 but have not been collected there since 1976.
- **Redrock Canyon:** A newly discovered population has a patchy distribution due to severe flash flooding. Mosquitofish and largemouth bass occur in stock ponds at the headwaters but have not established in the stream.

**Mechanism of decline**
- **Competition (hypothesis 2) unlikely:** Dietary overlap is small, food is abundant, and the species have strongly different dentition. Mosquitofish have strong conical teeth suited to capturing moving prey. Topminnows have weaker, spatulate teeth suited to browsing vegetation and detritus. Schoenherr (1981, pers. comm.) argued that aggression by mosquitofish ("competition for space") could be important.
- **Predation (hypothesis 3) favoured:** Mosquitofish feed on juveniles, and chase and nip adults to the point of shredding fins, which can lead to infection and death. Female mosquitofish are dominant and aggressive, which increases topminnow mortality and reduces fecundity. Hubbs (1971) reported similar effects of introduced *Gambusia* on native *G. heterochir* in Texas.
- **Supporting literature:** Simberloff (1981) reported that of 71 extinctions caused by introduced species, 50 (70%) were attributed to predation, 11 (15.5%) to habitat alteration, and 3 (4.2%) to competitive exclusion.

**Abiotic factors**
- Desiccation, channelisation, impoundments, and lowered water tables have altered habitats, but many of these changes began long before major topminnow declines and had little effect when not accompanied by exotic introductions.
- Topminnows persist in undisturbed habitats allopatric with mosquitofish. The authors conclude that biotic interactions, rather than moderate abiotic change, are responsible for the decline.

**Other threats and vectors**
- Mosquitofish spread through northern Sonora and the Rio Yaqui basin, with records from San Bernardino Creek and artificial ponds in 1980–81.
- Private citizens transport mosquitofish and other fishes as bait or for sport, and the species has been used in mosquito control, despite the native topminnow also being an effective mosquito predator.
- Re-introductions of topminnows have largely failed.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The northern topminnow form (*P. o. occidentalis*) is considered unique and cannot simply be replaced by the Mexican form (*P. o. sonoriensis*) after local extinctions.
- Continued monitoring and maintenance of existing populations are essential.
- Searches for new refugia free of introduced fishes should be a priority.
- Preventing further introductions requires coordinated action by state and federal agencies, local citizens, and landowners.
