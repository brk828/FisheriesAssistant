# Inventory of the Fishes of Jordan Creek, Vermilion County, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report presents an intensive electrofishing inventory of the fish community of [[Jordan Creek]], a warm-water tributary of the [[Salt Fork Vermilion River]] in [[Vermilion County]], Illinois. The census ran from July 25 to September 5, 1950, across a 4-mile study section divided into eight divisions. The authors, working for the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] with the [[Illinois Department of Conservation]], recorded 40 species. Minnows dominated the catch by number, while the [[Hog Sucker]] was the heaviest species. The community was strongly structured by [[Stream Gradient]], shading, bottom material, and surrounding land use, which together produced a reversal of the usual upstream–downstream fish distribution. The [[Smallmouth Black Bass]], the species most sought by local anglers, was associated mainly with the hog sucker and the lower wooded reach.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Illinois stream fisheries had mostly been studied for species distribution in relation to habitat (e.g., [[Forbes and Richardson]] 1920; [[Thompson and Hunt]] 1930). Managing stream habitat to improve angling had received little attention. This inventory was a preliminary step toward developing small-stream fish management techniques. Most Illinois anglers fish lakes, and stream fishing was largely confined to a small group of people.

**Methods.** Crews worked each pool and riffle from the creek mouth upstream using electric shockers. These included hand-held electrodes and, later, a 21-foot electric seine powered by a 115-volt generator, which was faster and covered more area. Dip nets with quarter-inch mesh were used; the mesh set the minimum size collected. Game and pan fish were measured, scale-sampled, fin-clipped, and released. Other species were preserved and brought to the laboratory. The initial inventory comprised **52 separate collections**. In September–November 1950, repeat censuses of selected pools used numbered opercular tags. These repeat data are excluded from the core tables but added several species to the list.

**Study area.** Jordan Creek is 11 miles long and drains 10.6 square miles of glaciated land. The 4-mile study section was divided into eight divisions. Divisions 1–7 each measured 0.53 mile, and Division 8 measured 0.31 mile. No pool was split between divisions.

**Taxonomy.** Common and scientific names follow the [[American Fisheries Society]] list (1948), supplemented by [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1947) for some species. Table 1 lists the 40 species across families including Catostomidae (suckers), Cyprinidae (minnows), Ameiuridae (catfishes), Percidae (perches and darters), Centrarchidae (sunfishes), and single representatives of Esocidae and Cyprinodontidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage and landscape.** Jordan Creek drains a landscape shaped by the Illinoian and Wisconsin glaciers. The headwaters cross a flat, marshy area that was dredged to improve drainage. Downstream the creek flows north into rolling terrain that has not been dredged.

**Two contrasting habitats.** The study section splits naturally into two reaches of about equal length.

- **Lower, wooded area (divisions 1–4, first 2 miles).** The creek falls an average of 24 feet per mile and carries about 18.6 cubic feet per second at average water levels. Bedrock (siltstone, sandstone, shale) and gravel dominate the bottom. Hardwoods shade most of the banks, and land use is mainly permanent pasture. Soils are Vance silt loam, which erodes easily.
- **Upper, open area (divisions 5–8, last 2 miles).** The gradient averages 9.7 feet per mile, with about 12.9 cubic feet per second of flow. Long, quiet pools hold extensive sand and silt, and most of the stream is exposed to sunlight. Land use is intensive farming and pasture, on Catlin silt loam.

The usual pattern, with low gradient and long pools downstream and steep riffles upstream, is reversed here. The upper area has the features normally associated with larger streams. This reversal sharpens the separation of factors that control fish distribution.

**Table 10 gradient and shade by division** (as reported): gradient 34.0, 24.5, 20.8, 17.0, 8.5, 7.6, 9.4, and 13.3 feet per mile for divisions 1–8. Shading ranged from 75–85% in divisions 1–4 to 0% in division 8.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness and total catch.** The census recorded **40 species**. Minnows were the dominant family, comprising **75% of the total number and 38% of the total weight**. Sunfishes ranked second and suckers third by weight. Thirteen minnow species were present.

2. **Most abundant species.**
   - By number, the [[Stoneroller]] was most abundant (**9,830** individuals, 89.26 lb).
   - By weight, the [[Hog Sucker]] was heaviest (**2,398** individuals, **96.20 lb**).
   - The [[Longear Sunfish]] was the most abundant sunfish (**2,015** individuals, 85.14 lb).
   - The [[Bluntnose Minnow]] was the second most numerous minnow, and the [[Hornyhead Chub]] was second by weight among minnows.

3. **Sport fish.**
   - The smallmouth black bass totaled **369** individuals weighing **72.33 lb**. Sixty-seven percent of its weight came from the lower wooded area.
   - Its weight distribution correlated most strongly with that of the hog sucker (**r = 0.85**, significant at the 1% level). It correlated positively with the common shiner and hornyhead chub, and negatively with the [[Creek Chub]] (**r = −0.73**).
   - Pools with good bass populations had a high percentage of suckers and a low percentage of minnows.
   - The [[Largemouth Black Bass]] (41 individuals, 2.24 lb) occurred only in the upper open area, the reverse of the normal stream pattern.

4. **Habitat specialists.**
   - Rock bass, stonecat, brindled madtom, and blackside darter were confined to the lower wooded area.
   - Sand shiner (99% from the upper area), spotfin shiner, and starhead topminnow were confined to the upper area.
   - The sand shiner and [[Silverjaw Minnow]] had the highest recorded correlation (**r = 0.96**), consistent with shared preference for sand and silt bottoms.

5. **Darters.** Four darter species dominated: fantail (951), greenside (748), orangethroat (740), and rainbow (574). Rainbow and greenside favored larger, faster rapids in the lower area. Orangethroat and fantail favored smaller, slower riffles upstream. The orangethroat–fantail correlation was **r = 0.88**.

6. **Suckers.** The [[Golden Redhorse]] appeared in 79% of collections, and 95% of its specimens came from the open area. Almost all were young-of-the-year (only 27 of 1,024 were not). Quillback concentrated in the open area (164 of 167 specimens), and most came from division 8.

7. **Rare or transient species.** Warmouth (two specimens) appeared only in fall recheck censuses, likely from a quarry connected to the creek during high water. Spotted sucker, fathead minnow, and single specimens of carp, orangespotted sunfish, and logperch were treated as temporary residents. The grass pickerel (16 specimens, 14 from the open area) was the only pike-family species.

8. **Management relevance.** Habitat rather than a single species drove the community. The authors concluded that gradient, shading, bottom material, and land use influence distribution, although more specific variables such as velocity, pool size, or food supply probably determine it. The authors noted that bass and suckers co-occurred in deep, cover-rich pools, which suggests that pool structure is the key target for angling improvement.
