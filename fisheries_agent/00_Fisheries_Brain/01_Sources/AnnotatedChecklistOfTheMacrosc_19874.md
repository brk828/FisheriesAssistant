# Annotated Checklist of the Macroscopic Troglobites of Virginia With Notes on Their Geographic Distribution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #troglobites

*Note: This document concerns cave invertebrates and arachnids; the taxon tags above are retained from the required template, and the substantive taxa are the troglobitic invertebrates listed below.*

## Executive Summary

This checklist by John R. Holsinger, published in the *Bulletin of the National Speleological Society* (Vol. 25, Part 1, January 1963), catalogues the macroscopic troglobites of Virginia, the cave-obligate fauna that completes its life cycle in permanent darkness. Forty-one troglobitic species are recognized from Virginia: planarians (2), amphipods (4), isopods (3), millipeds (9), collembolans (4), beetles (10), pseudoscorpions (4) and spiders (5). At least fifteen further species are known but undescribed. The paper also discusses speciation, range patterns and the zoogeographic effects of Pleistocene glaciation. It is part of the [[Biological Survey of Virginia Caves]], a research project of the [[National Speleological Society]].

## Background & Description

Holsinger defines a troglobite as a species existing only in the cave environment, completing its life cycle in absolute darkness and never found on the surface unless washed out by flooding. Typical adaptations include loss or reduction of eyes, slender bodies, longer appendages, loss of pigmentation and intolerance of light and heat. Strict application of this definition excluded several cavernicoles that also occur epigean, such as [[Nesticus Pallidus]], *Tomocerus bidentatus* and *Phalangodes flavescens*. A grave record of *Pseudosinella argentea* was treated as a hypogean-like habitat.

The checklist builds on earlier national and state compilations by Nicholas (1960a, 1960b, 1960c) and Warren (1961, Florida). Major contributors include [[Thomas C. Barr Jr.]] (carabids and field collecting), Causey (millipeds), Hubricht (amphipods), Malcolm and Chamberlin (pseudoscorpions), and Steeves (asellid isopods).

Taxa covered include:
- **Planarians:** *Sphalloplana virginiana*, *Phagocata subterranea*
- **Amphipods:** *Crangonyx antennatus*, *Stygobromus mackini*, *Stygobromus spinosus*, *Synpleonia pizzinii*
- **Isopods:** *Asellus pricei*, *Caucasonethis henroti*, *Miktoniscus racovitzai*
- **Millipeds:** *Zygonopus* (3 species) and *Pseudotremia* (6 species) of the orders Chordeumida and Cleidogonidae
- **Collembolans:** *Pseudosinella argentea*, *P. orba*, *Sinella hoffmani*, *Arrhopalites ferrugineus*
- **Beetles:** [[Pseudanophthalmus]] (8 species, several subspecies), *Aphanotrechus virginicus*, *Arianops jeanneli*
- **Pseudoscorpions:** *Apochthonius coecus*, *Kleptochthonius gertschi*, *K. lutzi*, *Chitrella cavicola*
- **Spiders:** *Bathyphantes weyeri*, *Phanetta subterranea*, *Porrhomma cavernicolum*, *Nesticus carteri*, *N. tennesseensis*

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Holsinger proposes that isolation within caves and genetic change in cave populations drive troglobitic speciation, and that many cave species evolved from surface forms already partly preadapted to subterranean life. Groups such as anophthalmid beetles, pseudotremid millipeds and cave pseudoscorpions are restricted to very small areas, sometimes a single cave system. Linyphiid spiders and several collembolans have wide ranges across the southeastern United States. Aquatic troglobites are generally more widely dispersed than terrestrial ones, except among spiders and collembolans. Underground watercourses are less easily closed by silting, erosion and flowstone than dry passages, which may favor dispersal.

Habitat notes in the checklist describe troglobites in:
- Muddy pools and rimstone pools (*Sphalloplana virginiana* in Showalters Cave; *Phagocata subterranea* in Rock House Cave, where pools were contaminated by septic leakage)
- Decaying wood, leaf litter, raccoon feces and damp clay banks (pseudotremids, zygonopids, linyphiids)
- Cave streams and springs (asellids, amphipods)

Pleistocene glaciation is central to the biogeographic discussion. The Virginia limestone region, remote from glaciation, holds more than three times as many troglobitic species as the Pennsylvania limestone area, which lay near glacial ice. Of Pennsylvania's twelve true cave forms, all but three are aquatic (one milliped, two spiders). Several hypotheses are offered: aquatic forms survived glaciation, were extirpated and recolonized from unglaciated areas, or evolved since glacial recession roughly 10,000 to 15,000 years ago.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** 41 troglobitic species are recognized from Virginia, with at least 15 further undescribed species.
- **Undescribed material:** The author personally collected at least five and possibly six new forms, and ten or more others from earlier collectors await description.
- **Snails:** Blind, unpigmented bithyniid snails (seven tentatively assigned to *Fontigens*, plus an undescribed [[Lartetia]] from travertine pools in Skyline Caverns) occur in five cave streams and three springs.
- **Isopods:** An undescribed asellid, *Asellus* sp., is known from five localities in the upper Tennessee River drainage. A cirolanid from Madison Cave appears closer to the European *Sphaeromides raymondi* than to the Texas *Cirolanides texensis*.
- **Millipeds:** Three of six *Pseudotremia* species are known only from their type localities. Three *Zygonopus* species show less endemism. A new pseudotremid was found in Porters Cave.
- **Beetles:** Of eight *Pseudanophthalmus* species, one is known from a single cave and seven are restricted to a few closely related caves. *P. gracilis* and *P. punctatus* are sympatric in Clover Hollow and Tawneys Caves but occupy separate niches.
- **Pseudoscorpions:** Each of four recognized species has been collected from only one cave, and most cave collections yielded probable new species.
- **Range versus drainage:** Species ranges roughly follow drainage basins in Virginia, but not as a general rule. *Zygonopus weyeriensis* overlaps into the upper Potomac basin, coming within about ten miles of *Z. whitei* in Augusta County.
- **Widespread taxa:** Spiders (linyphiids, nesticids) and entomobryid collembolans are highly vagile and broadly distributed, with little speciation evident. Possible explanations include recent isolation and genetic drift.
- **Relict and transatlantic affinities:** Lartetia from Skyline Caverns is most closely related to a Rhine Valley cave form, and the Madison Cave cirolanid resembles a French species, which the author considers unusual for isolated caves.
- **Glaciation:** Holsinger concludes that aquatic troglobites were more resilient to glaciation, and notes the Pennsylvania aquatic fauna is disproportionately large.
- **Future work:** The author plans supplementary checklists as new data accumulate, with the most likely new discoveries in pseudoscorpions, pseudotremid millipeds, and carabid and pselaphid beetles.
