# Three Decades Near Cuatro Ciénegas, Mexico: Photographic Documentation and a Plea for Area Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) uses repeat photography to document ecological change in the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], a small, isolated desert valley in Coahuila, Mexico. Paired photographs from an August 1960 visit were compared with photographs of the same sites taken in 1988–89 and 1991. The author finds that habitats remote from humans changed little, while habitats directly affected by people changed substantially. Canalization, lowered water tables, surface mining and recreation degraded aquatic habitats. The basin nonetheless retains a rich, largely endemic biota, and the author argues strongly for its conservation as a natural laboratory and reserve.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The paper had three goals: (1) document observed change or lack of change in the basin, (2) argue for its protection, and (3) show the value of [[Rephotography]] for documenting environmental change over time.
- **Study area:** The basin is an intermontane valley of about 1,200 km² (roughly 30 × 40 km), bounded by limestone mountains and fed by at least 80 discrete springs. Its permanent surface water is unusual in the arid region.
- **Biota:** Biologists in the 1930s and later found more than 50 taxa in the basin, including plants, snails, crustaceans, scorpions, fishes and reptiles. Many are endemic or relict.
- **Methods:** The original 1960 photographs were taken by Charles B. Stone, and the repeat photographs were matched by trial and error using distant mountain peaks and nearby features. The author notes that the most important factor in rephotography is reoccupying the original vantage point. Surveys were funded largely by the author's own resources and by grants from Sigma Xi, the National Science Foundation and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting and human history**
- In 1960 the basin had about 2,500 residents, mostly in the town of Cuatro Ciénegas. By 1988–89 the town had more than 15,000 inhabitants.
- In 1960, firewood was the main cooking and heating fuel. By 1988–89 propane had largely replaced it, and electricity and piped water were available.
- Goat herds had nearly denuded areas near settlements in 1960. Goat numbers were greatly reduced by 1988–89.

**Aquatic habitats**
- Local fishermen exploited native fishes. Reeds (*carrizo*) were used for fishing poles and roofing.
- The [[Rio Cañon]] had a base flow of about 0.25 m³/s in the 1960s, and about 0.1 m³/s later. Pumping and population growth were blamed for the drop.
- [[Laguna Escobedo]] was the warmest constant-temperature spring found in the basin, at 34.4 °C.
- Canalization lowered water surfaces and water tables, leading to desiccation of marshes and invasion of terrestrial plants.

**Terrestrial habitats**
- Basin-floor vegetation was intact grassland. Mesquite increased near settlements.
- Commercial gypsum mining in the 1970s, at the western dunefield, substantially altered the dunes.

**Biodiversity comparisons**
- At least 64 native amphibians and reptiles are known from below 1,000 m in the basin. This is more than a third of the 170 Chihuahuan Desert herpetofauna specimens analyzed by Morafka (1977).
- Ash Meadows, Nevada (National Wildlife Refuge since 1988), supports five endemic fishes (one extinct) and nine molluscs.
- Dalhousie Springs, Australia (National Park since 1985), supports six fishes (four endemic) and six molluscs.

## Key Findings

1. **Change depends on human influence.** Paired photographs show ecological change in habitats directly influenced by humans, but essentially none in remote areas.

2. **Positive changes.** Propane replaced firewood, metal replaced wooden fenceposts, and livestock herds were reduced and fenced. These shifts had positive effects on local vegetation.

3. **Negative changes to water.** Electric pumps drawing water from subsurface aquifers, and the deepening and concrete lining of canals, lowered surface water levels and local water tables. Canalization dramatically reduced the area of springs and marshes. Only a few springs were destroyed outright, but many formerly permanent marshes are now dry or only seasonally wet.

4. **Biological consequences of canals.** Canals connect previously isolated habitats, allowing biota to intermingle and sometimes hybridize, and increasing the risk of exotic species invasion.

5. **Recreation.** Recreation increased after the highway was completed in the 1970s. It compacted and denuded lagoon banks and bottoms and increased debris. Fishing catches declined, probably from overharvest. Fishing is now prohibited in some areas as part of conservation efforts.

6. **Persistence of the ecosystem.** Two factors have protected the basin: terrestrial habitats have little potential for direct human use (saline soils, sparse vegetation, rugged mountains), and high total dissolved solids (magnesium sulfate and sodium chloride) make the water of limited use. Large-scale aquifer pumping has therefore not yet been applied.

7. **Exceptional endemism.** The basin supports 15 fishes (12 endemic), four endemic reptiles, six crustaceans, at least 10 molluscs, more than 20 endemic plants, and a number of insects and scorpions. No other similar-sized desert aquatic system known to the author is as rich.

8. **Conservation recommendation.** The author argues that the basin should not be preserved as a static, inviolate system. Instead, it should be managed as a natural laboratory and reserve, with water dynamics (springs, outflows, marshes and sumps) conserved as intact ecosystems. Other recommendations include permanently established access roads, concentrated and managed recreation areas, and continued Mexican government protection.

9. **Photographic record.** The 30-year record shows that the system changes slowly, if at all, unless directly influenced by people. It documents the persistence of most unique habitats and most indigenous flora and fauna.
