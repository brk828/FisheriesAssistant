## Executive Summary

This 1983 report by [[Kepner Jakle & Baucom 1983]] presents findings from a November 1982 aquatic survey of the [[Middle Gila River]], Arizona, contextualized against earlier assessments (Minckley 1972; Stephenson & Ohmart 1974; USBR 1976) that had characterized the river as biologically depauperate due to industrial pollution. The current study — covering 12 stations across 78 stream-km (48.5 mi) — found that water chemistry parameters (dissolved oxygen, pH, hardness, alkalinity, trace metals) now fall within acceptable state/federal standards for wildlife, fisheries, and agricultural use, suggesting the river had chemically recovered from its earlier "abiotic" state. However, both fish and [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] populations remained sparse, a condition attributed primarily to physical habitat degradation from suspended sediment loads and highly variable discharge rather than ongoing chemical pollution. Ten fish species were documented across the 1976–1982 survey period, with only two — [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] — being native. The dominant species shifted from desert sucker/channel catfish (1976) to [[Mosquitofish]] and longfin dace (1982), a change possibly reflecting sampling method differences rather than a true community shift.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Type:** The Middle Gila River is a desert lotic system historically dominated by homogeneous "run" habitat (95% per USBR 1976 classification) under normal mean discharge (459 cfs, n=64 yrs; USGS 1979). During the November 1982 survey, discharge was substantially lower (~25 cfs, no Coolidge Dam releases), producing better-developed riffle-run-pool heterogeneity — generally associated with higher-quality aquatic habitat.

**Water Chemistry (Table 2, selected stations):**
- Dissolved oxygen: consistently high (8.1–8.5 mg/l)
- pH: alkaline throughout
- Specific conductance: often >2,000 µmho/cm (precluded electrofishing use)
- Total hardness: >300 mg/l as CaCO₃, increasing upstream-to-downstream, driven by calcium cations
- Sulfate: strongly influenced by [[San Pedro River]] inflow (Station 7), nearly 3× higher than mainstem Gila (Station 7.5)
- Trace metals (Cu, Fe, Pb, Mn, Zn): all within sublethal/regulatory limits; manganese most prevalent; no mine-related contamination detected despite [[Mineral Creek]]'s history of heavy metal pollution (Rathbun 1975)
- Suspended sediment: San Pedro River mouth ~5× more concentrated than Gila River above the confluence

**Discharge (Table 3):** Gila River below Winkelman averaged 25.1 ± 1.9 cfs; San Pedro River contributed the majority of unregulated inflow (4.89 cfs at mouth vs. 0.11 cfs for Mineral Creek).

**Benthic Invertebrate Community:** 17 taxa across 10 orders identified via Surber sampling (n=27 quantitative samples). Combined density averaged 464.4 ± 626.8 individuals/m² and 3.6 ± 5.9 g/m² biomass — far lower than the 12,240 individuals/m² and 6.9 g/m² reported for nearby [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Bruns 1977), reinforcing the depauperate characterization. [[Baetidae]] (baetid mayfly nymphs) were the most abundant taxon overall. Standing crop (density) was highest in the San Pedro River, though biomass peaked in the Gila River mainstem. Mineral Creek showed the lowest density, biomass, and taxa richness of all stations. Diversity indices (H') were depressed in tributaries — San Pedro (H'=0.347, dominated 92.8% by *Baetis*) and Mineral Creek (H'=0.628, dominated 81.2% by [[Simuliidae]] black fly larvae) — indicating physically stressed or low-diversity substrate conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness increased from 8 (1976) to 10 (1981–1982)**, with [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) newly documented; this increase is attributed to greater sampling effort rather than true colonization.
- **Only two native species persist** in the study reach: longfin dace and desert sucker, consistent with Minckley's (1972) original assessment; all other species are non-native introductions (carp, channel catfish, yellow bullhead, red shiner, green sunfish, mosquitofish, threadfin shad, largemouth bass, fathead minnow).
- **Relative abundance shifted markedly**: 1976 dominants were desert sucker, channel catfish, longfin dace, and carp; by November 1982, mosquitofish, longfin dace, and desert sucker dominated (Table 8), possibly reflecting sampling gear bias (1976 relied heavily on fyke nets, which undersample small-bodied fish and juveniles).
- **Overall fish abundance remained very low**: 106 fish collected across 44 seine hauls (2.4 fish/haul average) and 34 fish across 11 kick-seine riffle samples (3.1 fish/sample) in the Gila mainstem — markedly lower than the 91 fish/haul recorded in [[Dripping Spring Wash]], a tributary.
- **Water chemistry no longer appears limiting**: all measured parameters met protected-use standards, contrasting with the "heavily polluted" characterization of Stephenson & Ohmart (1974).
- **Physical habitat degradation—not chemical pollution—is implicated as the primary constraint** on fish and invertebrate standing crops: high sediment loads reduce photosynthesis via turbidity, scour/abrade substrate limiting benthic colonization, and bury bottom structure creating homogeneous, low-diversity habitat.
- **Flow regime is a critical structuring factor**: normal high mean discharge (459 cfs) erases riffle-run-pool heterogeneity, while the anomalously low flow during the November 1982 survey (25 cfs, no dam releases) allowed better habitat structure to develop, though this did not translate to correspondingly higher standing fish crops within the survey window.
- **Trace metal contamination from Mineral Creek's historical mining legacy was not detected** at levels of ecological concern during this survey, despite prior documented heavy metal pollution issues (Rathbun 1975).
