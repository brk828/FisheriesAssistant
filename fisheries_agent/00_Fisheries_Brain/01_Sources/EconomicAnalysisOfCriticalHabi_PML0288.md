# Economic Analysis of Critical Habitat Designation for Four Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, a 1994 final economic study by [[David S. Brookshire]], [[Michael McKee]] and [[Gary Watts]] (University of New Mexico and Watts and Associates), estimates the incremental economic impacts of designating critical habitat for four endangered Colorado River fishes: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. Under the [[Endangered Species Act]], only the incremental impacts of critical habitat, beyond those of listing, must be quantified.

The study region covers seven states: Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. Input-output (I-O) models were built for each state and for the region, and a [[Computable General Equilibrium]] (CGE) model was built for national efficiency effects. The analysis covers 1995 to 2020, the period projected for recovery, and compares a "without fish" scenario with a "with fish" scenario.

Key results: the basin-wide present value (3% discount) of incremental output is a positive $129.40 million (1991$), and the national efficiency present value is $141.58 million. Impacts are uneven across states, from +$335.02 million in California to -$245.46 million in New Mexico. The percentage deviation from the without-fish baseline is very small (0.0003% for basin output).

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** The Act provides for designation of critical habitat necessary for the survival and recovery of listed species. Section 7(a)(2) requires federal agencies to ensure that their actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species or destroy or adversely modify critical habitat. Destruction or adverse modification is defined in 50 CFR 402.02 as an alteration that appreciably diminishes the value of critical habitat for survival and recovery.

**Listing history.**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were listed as endangered on March 11, 1967.
- [[Bonytail]] was listed as endangered on April 23, 1980.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] was proposed as threatened in 1978, the proposal was withdrawn in 1980, and it was listed as endangered in 1991.
- Critical habitat for all four species was proposed on January 29, 1993.

**Species and proposed habitat.**

| Species | Proposed reaches | Share of historical range designated |
|---|---|---|
| Razorback Sucker | 15 reaches | 49% |
| Colorado Squawfish | 6 reaches | 29% |
| Humpback Chub | 7 reaches | 28% |
| Bonytail | 7 reaches | 14% |

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is the largest of the four species. The [[Bonytail]] is the rarest native fish in the basin, and only a few old adults (40 years or more) remain, mainly in [[Lake Mohave]].

**Basin physical setting.** The [[Colorado River Basin]] drains about 112,000 square miles in the United States and about 2,000 square miles in Mexico. It is divided into Upper and Lower basins by the 1922 Colorado River Compact, with the dividing point at Lee's Ferry, Arizona. Of the 36 native fish species, 64% are found in no other North American river basin. Since 1905, 46 dams have been built, and irrigated land exceeds 2.1 million acres.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat alteration:** Dams and diversions have fragmented the river and blocked fish migration. Flow regimes have changed, altering water temperature and sediment loads. Contaminant releases from agriculture, loss of floodplain wetlands, and municipal and industrial activity have degraded water quality.
- **Water use:** Average consumption for 1976–1985 is summarized in the source's consumptive-use table (Upper Basin, Lower Basin and total acre-feet, with percentages).
- **Recovery linkage:** Recovery depends on Federal reservoir operations (flow requirements), nonflow actions along the river and its 100-year floodplain, and conservation of contaminant and passage conditions. Recovery Implementation Programs (RIPs) in the Upper Basin and San Juan River serve as reasonable and prudent alternatives to jeopardy.
- **Sector dependence:** Impacts fall on agriculture, hydroelectric generation, mining/oil/gas, recreation, municipal and industrial water use.

## Key Findings

1. **Basin-wide impacts are positive.** Present value of incremental output is +$129.40 million; earnings +$74.07 million; government revenue +$23.69 million. Annualized output is +$6.47 million.
2. **Impacts are geographically uneven.** California gains (output +$335.02 million; earnings +$57.60 million) because it diverts water at the lower end of critical habitat reaches. New Mexico loses (output -$245.46 million). Nevada earnings rise $67.99 million.
3. **Deviation from baseline is small.** Basin output deviates by only 0.0003%. State percentages range from -0.028% (New Mexico) to +0.0013% (California).
4. **Employment:** The basin gains about 60 jobs in 1995, rising to about 393 jobs in 2020. New Mexico loses about 2 jobs in 1995 and about 613 by 2020. California gains about 20 jobs in 1995 and about 1,162 by 2020.
5. **Sector results differ.** Other crops impacts range from +$174 million (California) to -$94.59 million (New Mexico), with a basin total of +$62.76 million. Livestock feed totals -$66.91 million for the basin. Recreation services total -$16.51 million. Electric power ranges from +$2.48 million (California) to -$36.88 million (Utah).
6. **National efficiency is positive but small.** Under Scenario A1 (idle construction and capital-equipment capacity, and irreplaceable basin recreation), real gross regional product rises $7.92 million in 1991$, with +710 jobs. The present value is $141.58 million (3%), with an annualized value of $7.08 million. Traditional direct-impact estimates give $54.02 million.
7. **Key uncertainty.** Results assume RIPs fail to show sufficient progress, so some water developments are foregone. This is an upper bound on economic effects. Successful RIPs could avoid many negative impacts. Impacts from projects already through Section 7 consultation are excluded.
8. **Methodological caveats.** National efficiency estimates assume distortion-free markets and no interregional resource flows. The authors state these assumptions are arguably violated, so the CGE framework is used to bound results.

**Conclusion (as stated by the authors):** Regional and national impacts are clearly positive, state-level impacts are unevenly distributed, and the percentage deviation from the without-fish scenario is small.
