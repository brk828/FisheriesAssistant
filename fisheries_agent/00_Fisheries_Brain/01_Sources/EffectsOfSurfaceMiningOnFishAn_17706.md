# Effects of Surface Mining on Fish and Wildlife in Appalachia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1968 special report from the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] assesses how [[Surface Mining]] for coal affects fish and wildlife across eight Appalachian States. It draws on field observations by a multi-agency [[Field Appraisal Team]] that toured mined areas in 1965–66. The report finds that surface mining has caused extensive damage: 832,605 acres are disturbed, and more than 5,000 miles of streams and 13,800 acres of impoundments have been seriously contaminated, largely by [[Acid Mine Drainage]]. Silt and sediment add further harm. The authors conclude that current [[Reclamation]] practice does not restore mined lands to minimum standards needed to protect fish and wildlife, and they recommend that water quality control become the primary objective of reclamation.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared under section 205 ("Mining Area Restoration") of the [[Appalachian Regional Development Act]] of 1965, which directs a national study of strip and surface mining. The first phase covered selected mining areas in Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Virginia, Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, Maryland, and Alabama. Because coal was the most important mineral recovered by surface mining in the region, the report focuses mainly on coal.

Two principal methods are described:

- **[[Contour Stripping]]:** A continuous cut follows a coal seam around a hillside or mountaintop. Each acre of coal recovered can disturb one to four additional acres. The result is a bench bounded by a [[Highwall]] on the inside and a ridge of [[Spoil Banks]] on the outside.
- **[[Area Stripping]]:** Used on flat to rolling terrain. Parallel ridges of spoil are left behind, and the final cut is usually left open, often contributing to pollution.

Surface mining in the region has a brief history, with the greatest expansion in the last 25 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance extent.** Table 1 reports 832,605 acres disturbed out of 227,362,280 total acres in the nine listed states (0.36 percent). West Virginia has the highest proportion (1.24 percent), followed by Pennsylvania (1.04 percent) and Ohio (0.68 percent). The report estimates that about 80 percent of the disturbance lies in Ohio, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia. West Virginia's coal field of 3,449,600 acres may see about 20 percent mined eventually.

**Isolation by highwalls.** Unscalable [[Highwall]]s cut off access to land for most wildlife. West Virginia alone has more than 411,000 acres isolated this way. Adding the 192,000 acres of recognized disturbance gives more than 603,000 acres. Topographic map estimates add about 700,000 acres elsewhere, for a total of roughly 1.5 million acres seriously affected in Appalachia.

**Acid pollution.** Mine drainage becomes acid through reactions among water, oxygen, and sulphur in [[Pyrite]] and related minerals. Table 2 (adapted from [[Kinney 1964]]) lists 5,132 miles of stream and 13,825 acres of impoundments affected, with Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Kentucky reporting the most. States estimated that 10 to 25 percent of acid pollution originates in strip mines, but the data do not separate deep-mine from surface sources. The report notes that [[Iron Hydroxide]] ("yellow boy") coats stream bottoms and destroys aquatic habitat.

**Silt and sediment.** Fine silt stays suspended and reduces light transmittance. Heavier particles blanket the stream bottom and smother aquatic life. Coal washery effluent can also cause abrasive gill injuries and, by blocking light, prevent the photosynthesis that supports invertebrates and fish.

**Terrestrial effects.** Contour mining denudes and pits land, and revegetation on steep spoil is slow. Wildlife populations declined in mined areas across several states.

## Key Findings

**Water quality thresholds for fish**
- The desirable pH range for fish in Appalachia is 6.7 to 8.6. Fish can survive briefly at pH 4.5, but pH below 6 is unfavorable.
- Rainbow trout died at pH 4.18 in soft water (Lloyd and Jordan, 1964).
- Trout eggs developed normally between pH 4 and 5 but died below pH 4 (Jones, 1964). Other authors report reproductive failure below pH 5.5.
- Neutralized acid water can become "hard" and increase iron precipitate. Extreme hardness can harm aquatic life nearly as much as acid itself (Virginia findings).

**State-level findings**
- **[[Alabama]]:** About 50,600 acres disturbed, less than 1 percent of accessible mineral acreage. No regulatory reclamation law existed at the time. Some acid pollution affects at least five streams.
- **[[Kentucky]]:** More than 48,000 acres of stripped land; about 4,000 new acres disturbed annually. About 580 miles of fishable streams are harmed by acid drainage, including an estimated 145 miles (25 percent) from strip mining. Wildlife has declined at least 25 percent in mined areas. New legislation passed in December 1965.
- **[[Maryland]]:** 2,200 acres disturbed, with about 400 new acres per year. An estimated 115 miles of fishable streams and 500 surface acres of water are polluted. The absence of a productive fishery is estimated to cost more than $100,000 a year. Maryland law does not treat mine acid as an industrial pollutant.
- **[[Ohio]]:** 179,256 acres affected, of which 137,503 are reclaimed under state law. About 392 stream miles are acid-polluted. Area mining there benefits from limestone buffering. About 690 acres of managed impoundments provide more than 10,000 man-days of fishing per year (14 man-days per acre).
- **[[Pennsylvania]]:** More than 2,900 miles of acid-destroyed streams. Disturbance exceeds 300,000 acres in the bituminous field, with 112,000 acres in the anthracite field. The state's 1965 law is described as the strictest in the nation. Average reclamation cost was $424 per acre under the state program and $744 under joint federal-state projects. Unreclaimed areas show at least a 15 percent wildlife decline.
- **[[Tennessee]]:** More than 25,000 strip-mined acres in 16 counties, growing by about 8,500 acres per year. Only 2,000 acres reclaimed. Fish habitat in more than 125 stream miles was destroyed, including former smallmouth bass and muskellunge waters.
- **[[Virginia]]:** About 30,000 acres disturbed in nine southwestern counties. Major stream systems have not been severely affected, largely because of calcareous material. Mining has reduced stream flow, and a state hatchery closed because of offsite mining effects.
- **[[West Virginia]]:** 4,689 miles of strip mines on 192,000 acres (conservatively reduced to 179,172). Kinney (1964) reports about 1,150 stream miles and 3,533 reservoir acres with acid pollution. An estimated 25 percent of acid discharge comes from strip mining, and up to 75 percent of water from abandoned mines may originate from surface mines.

**Geology and buffering**
- Limestone and calcareous overburden buffer acid in Ohio, Virginia, and parts of Tennessee.
- West Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Maryland are underlain by noncalcareous shale and sandstone, which offers little neutralizing capacity. Reclamation there requires more intensive and expensive treatment.

**Reclamation shortcomings**
- Most reclamation has focused on surface restoration and revegetation rather than water quality. In West Virginia, reforestation with black locust (about 90 percent of plantings) was often associated with erosion and siltation.
- Partly exposed coal from augering was often left uncovered, relying on natural slumpage that comparable areas do not reliably provide.
- Acid is formed only when iron-bearing materials, sulphur, air, and moisture coexist. Burying pyritic material under clean soil therefore prevents acid generation, a principle reflected in most state laws.

**Benefits**
- Reclaimed areas can support fish and wildlife. Table 4 summarizes one-time economic values for 30 selected reclamation projects covering 8,068 acres across Ohio, West Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Kentucky. The report notes these values are conservative.
- Unreclaimed strip mines generally support fewer fish and wildlife than before mining. Exceptions occur in gently rolling calcareous terrain, where shallow water tables create good-quality ponds.

**Recommendations**
1. Control all strip mining with adequate legislation that provides for the wise use of all natural resources.
2. Preplan all reclamation so that water quality control is the primary objective.
3. Fully consider fish and wildlife before extraction, and build protection and enhancement into reclamation plans.
4. Protect other natural resources, especially water, from harm caused by mineral extraction.
5. Restore all mined lands to a condition that supports their best use and respects the area's natural beauty.
