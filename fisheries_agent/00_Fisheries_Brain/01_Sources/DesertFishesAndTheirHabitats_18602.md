# Desert Fishes and Their Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

Edwin P. Pister, of the California Department of Fish and Game, presents a paper prepared for the American Fisheries Society and delivered at the 139th Meeting of the [[American Association for the Advancement of Science]] (December 29, 1972). The paper reviews the decline of endemic desert fishes in the Southwestern United States and northern Mexico, driven mainly by agricultural groundwater pumping, water diversion, and introduced predators and competitors. It describes the institutional response, including the [[Desert Fishes Council]] and the [[Pupfish Task Force]], and argues for habitat protection as the primary conservation strategy. The author states that the views are his own and not those of his agency.

## Background & Description

- **Scope of loss:** Over the 35 years before 1972, 3 species and 8 subspecies in 6 genera went extinct in California, Nevada, and Arizona. At least 50 further species and subspecies (26 genera) in 8 Great Basin states and northern Mexico were considered threatened, and 18 were listed as endangered by the Secretary of the Interior.
- **Pleistocene origins:** Large interconnected Pleistocene lakes once covered much of the [[Death Valley System]]. As the pluvial period ended, these waters receded to scattered springs and intermittent streams. Only the [[Owens River]], fed by the eastern Sierra Nevada, remained a substantial permanent stream.
- **Native fauna:** The fish fauna comprised killifishes (Cyprinodontidae), minnows (Cyprinidae), and suckers (Catostomidae). Killifishes diversified most, forming five *Cyprinodon* species plus subspecies and two *Empetrichthys* species (one with three subspecies). Isolated springs were likened to "islands of water in a sea of sand."
- **Water development:** The [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] (completed early 1900s) diverted Owens River water and drained marshes. Dams stopped the flooding that had created cyprinodont habitat, and game fishes were stocked, including [[Brown Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Mosquitofish]].
- **Hybridization:** Owens chub (*Gila bicolor* subsp.) hybridized with introduced bait minnows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Owens Valley:** The [[Owens Pupfish]] was reported abundant by Kennedy (1916), reduced to two locations by 1934, thought extinct after its 1948 description, and rediscovered in 1956. A 1969 Department of Fish and Game action prevented extinction when its only remaining habitat dried up. Only one genetically pure Owens chub population is known, below Long Valley Dam.
- **Ash Meadows and Amargosa Valley:** Falling water tables and exotic introductions drove the decline. *Empetrichthys merriami* went extinct, and two Pahrump Valley forms followed. Only the Pahrump killifish survives, in a few hundred individuals at [[Manse Spring]]. Two *Cyprinodon nevadensis* subspecies are feared extinct. At Jackrabbit Spring, a pump installed over the spring eliminated the fish population completely.
- **Devils Hole:** [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] lives in a cavern of roughly the smallest known range of any vertebrate. It depends on a limestone shelf, which provides spawning area and substrate for attached algae (its food source). Falling water levels expose the shelf and reduce the sunlit surface.
- **Ash Meadows uniqueness:** Only one comparable desert spring ecosystem is known in North America, the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico, which is also threatened by deep well pumping. Ash Meadows also has a unique flora (Beatley, 1971).
- **Extremophile traits:** One species tolerates salinities several times that of seawater. Another inhabits an artesian outflow ranging from freezing to 117 °F at the source. A population in Mexican Spring (Ash Meadows) numbers only 20 to 40 individuals in 80 gallons of water (40 to 95 °F).

## Key Findings

- **Preservation philosophy:** The Desert Fishes Council prioritizes retaining pristine native habitat and re-establishing fishes across original ranges. Transplants are recommended only when a species is on the verge of extinction, with care to avoid hybridization.
- **Habitat preservation benefits:** Protecting habitat (1) secures species continuity, (2) preserves evolutionary processes that transplants would interrupt, and (3) protects other, often unstudied, life forms in the habitat.
- **Devils Hole measures:** Fish were transferred to five refugia in California and Nevada. An artificial refugium below Hoover Dam was stocked, but reproductive success was not yet evaluated. Aquarists had failed to rear the species, which has never bred successfully outside Devils Hole. In 1970, a fiberglass spawning shelf and lights were installed.
- **Legal and legislative action:** The Department of Justice initiated pumping-restriction litigation in September 1971. A pre-injunction hearing was held in Las Vegas Federal District Court on July 3 and 5, 1972, with no decision yet. Senate Bill 2141 proposed a Desert Pupfish National Monument. The U.S. Geological Survey delineated an aquifer protection area, and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife was studying a National Wildlife Refuge at Ash Meadows.
- **Transplant results:** Pahrump killifish transplants to Corn Creek (1971) and Shoshone Ponds (1972) appeared promising, with reproduction reported at Corn Creek in 1972. [[Mohave Chub]] transplants established several new populations. The [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] (1969) protects four native Owens fishes.
- **Endangered count growth:** Endangered species rose from 2 (per Miller, 1967) to 7 by 1969 and 12 by 1972.
- **Conclusion:** Pister argues that interagency cooperation, grounded in people working toward common goals, enabled the program. He frames the Ash Meadows loss as a microcosm of global preservation-versus-development conflict and calls for guidelines transferable to similar problems elsewhere.
