# Fish Utilization of Springs and Ciénegas in the Arid Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review by Gary K. Meffe (Savannah River Ecology Laboratory) argues that small desert springs and [[Ciénegas]] (riparian marshlands), though a small fraction of surface water in the arid American Southwest, are critical to the evolution, persistence and endemism of native fishes. Using distribution records for the region's core fauna, the paper shows that roughly one-third of southwestern fish taxa depend on these wetlands, that wetland-dependence is far higher than in the mesic Southeast, and that most recent regional extinctions involved wetland fishes. The author concludes that continued loss of desert wetlands endangers the remaining fauna.

## Background & Description

Southwestern North America is extremely arid, and lakes are essentially absent as fish habitat, so aquatic life is confined to [[Desert Streams]], [[Desert Springs]] and ciénegas (Cole, 1963, 1968). Streams are unstable and subject to flash-flood scouring and intermittent drying. Springs offer constant flow and physicochemical conditions, though harsh chemistry (low pH, high free CO2, toxic or thermal water) can exclude some fishes. Ciénegas are saturated, organic marshes at low to moderate elevations (under 1000 m to about 2000 m) that are fed by permanent, scarcely fluctuating water and are generally sheltered from flood scouring. Their vegetation is dominated by monocots such as sedges (Eleocharis spp.) and rushes, with watercress (Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum) and water-penneywort (Hydrocotyle verticillata) locally common.

Desert springs range from creek-forming rheocrenes to quiet limnocrenes producing hundreds to thousands of liters per second, and from barely perceptible seeps to extensive marsh seepages. The fragmentation of aquatic habitat resembles terrestrial islands in an oceanic matrix, producing in-situ differentiation, monotypic genera, Pleistocene relict species and high endemism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation and endemism:** Each spring or ciénega can harbor a distinct biota, and the author stresses that even very small waters may be biologically unique.
- **Study area:** Fishes were assessed from the Rio Grande basin in Texas (upstream from and including the Devil's River), through New Mexico, Arizona, southeastern California, all of Nevada and western Utah, covering core Chihuahuan, Sonoran, Mojave and Great Basin deserts. Species sources included [[Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes]] (Lee et al., 1980) and other literature, expert consultation and field records.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas Basin:** An intermontane basin in the Chihuahuan Desert of Coahuila, Mexico, with high biological endemism (plants, crustaceans, molluscs, amphibians, reptiles and fishes), fed by springheads from a few centimeters to over 200 m in diameter and extensive subterranean flow (see [[Cuatro Ciénegas]]).
- **Human impacts:** Dewatering, damming and diversion of rivers, arroyo cutting from the late 19th century, and introduced predatory fishes have degraded or eliminated many desert wetlands (Hastings, 1959; Williams et al., 1985; Meffe, 1985).
- **Other taxa:** Desert springs also hold a diverse mollusc fauna with very high endemism, often restricted to single springheads (Hershler, 1984).

## Key Findings

- **Wetland dependence:** Of 81 core southwestern species, 24 (29.6%) are restricted to springs and ciénegas. Including subspecies (93 total), 35 (37.6%) are restricted. Another 13 species use these habitats without being restricted to them, giving 37 spring/ciénega species (45.7%). In total, 49 of 93 species and subspecies (52.7%) use these wetlands. The author summarizes this as about one-third of taxa being obligate wetland dwellers, mainly in Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae and Poeciliidae. Some spring-rich regions have nearly all ichthyofauna using springs, and the author cites up to 50% of the ichthyofauna depending largely or exclusively on wetlands.
- **Comparison with the Southeast:** In peninsular Florida and southern Georgia, only one of 31 species (3.2%), the [[Redeye Chub]] (Notropis harperi), is restricted to spring-influenced wetlands, and 9 of 31 (29.0%) use them. The mesic Southeast, with abundant rivers, lakes, floodplains and cave networks, shows lesser direct dependence. Fishes are therefore more wetland-dependent in the arid Southwest.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas fauna:** Of 16 native fish species, 8 are endemic and 4 more are recognizably differentiated at the subspecific level. Fifteen species occur in springs, marshes or playa lakes, and three are restricted to them. Only an undescribed endemic darter (Etheostoma sp.) lacks a spring-wetland association.
- **Extinctions:** At least 20 taxa (species and subspecies) went extinct in the Southwest during the 20th century, mostly in the last three decades. Sixteen were restricted to springs or spring-influenced wetlands, many endemic to one or a few localities. The author attributes all these extinctions to habitat alteration and/or displacement by introduced fishes. Isolated spring endemics are especially extinction-prone, since losing one population can eliminate the species.
- **Case study, Sonoran topminnow:** [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis) was described in 1941 as one of the commonest fishes in the Colorado River basin, but was federally listed as endangered in 1973. It now occurs at 13 localities, 9 of them springs or ciénegas. The remaining strongholds are spring-influenced wetlands.
- **Conservation conclusion:** Wetlands are critical for evolution and persistence of the region's fishes and serve as natural laboratories for evolutionary biology. Because human population growth and desert land changes continue, the author warns that further disruption of desert wetlands will extend the list of extinctions of native fishes and other wetland species.
