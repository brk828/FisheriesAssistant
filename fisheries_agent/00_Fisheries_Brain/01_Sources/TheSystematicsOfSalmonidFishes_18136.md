# Systematics of Salmonid Fishes of Recently Glaciated Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Robert J. Behnke]] (Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit, Colorado State University) examines the systematics of the subfamilies Salmoninae and Coregoninae in recently glaciated regions of the Holarctic. It was presented as part of a [[SCOL Symposium]] (Geneva Park, Ontario, July 1971) on the separate and joint effects of cultural eutrophication, fisheries exploitation and non-native introductions on salmonid communities of glaciated oligotrophic lakes. The central argument is that intraspecific genetic diversity in salmonids, expressed mainly through ecological and behavioral specialization rather than morphology, is essential for fisheries management, and that current binomial taxonomy poorly reflects evolutionary reality. Behnke argues that [[Homing Behavior]] sustains reproductive isolation among sympatric "sibling species", that postglacial communities are fragile, and that genetic diversity should be protected.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses two competing explanations for sympatric sibling species in glaciated lakes: [[Sympatric Speciation]], in which a single lake population splits without geographic isolation, and [[Allopatric Speciation]], in which divergence occurs in preglacial or glacial-refugium waters before postglacial contact. Behnke favors a middle path he terms [[Microgeographical Speciation]]: brief periods of allopatry (perhaps a few thousand years) caused by minor glacial advances or sea-level changes, followed by secondary contact and reinforcement of reproductive isolation through spawning-time and habitat differences.

The family is divided into three subfamilies (Salmoninae, Coregoninae, Thymallinae). Only the first two are treated; graylings lack specialized lacustrine forms. Taxonomic approaches discussed include the [[Biological Species Concept]], [[Polytypic Species]], and the [[Polytopic Subspecies]] concept, which Behnke proposes as a practical device for grouping independently derived but convergent populations. Diagnostic characters repeatedly used include [[Gillraker Count]], [[Pyloric Caeca]], vertebral number, and scale counts, though Behnke notes that gillraker number is labile and prone to convergence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Postglacial timescale:** Fishes in Holarctic lakes have inhabited these waters for roughly 8,000 to 40,000 years. Some sympatric pairs are estimated to have diverged within 5,000 to 10,000 years; Lake Ockesjön's introduced whitefish population is judged isolated for no more than 9,000 years.
- **Fragility:** Postglacial salmonid communities are described as "typically fragile and highly susceptible" to introductions, eutrophication and exploitation. Behnke states that many distinct genotypes of trout, char and whitefish were lost in the previous 50 years.
- **Introductions and extinctions:** The blueback char (*Salvelinus oquossa*) of the Rangely Lakes, Maine, declined after landlocked salmon and smelt were introduced; the last specimens were taken in 1904. Competition with smelt is argued to be the most significant factor. The silver char (*S. agassizii*) of Dublin Pond, New Hampshire, is considered extinct, attributed to introduced brook trout.
- **Hatchery effects:** Hatcheries can break down reproductive barriers between anadromous stocks through forced mixing, and mass hybridization occurred where hatchery rainbow trout were introduced into interior cutthroat waters.
- **Lahontan cutthroat trout:** Pyramid Lake populations were exterminated by blocking spawning access; stocked substitutes do not approach half the original maximum size (originally up to 20 kg).
- **Glacial and sea-level history:** Lake Taimyr (4,650 km²) was a Laptev Sea bay during the last transgression, and Behnke considers Taimyr char and whitefish to reflect both glacial-lake and marine entry routes.

## Key Findings

**Salvelinus (char)**
- The genus is taxonomically confused. Behnke recognizes three subgenera: *Cristivomer* (lake char), *Baione* (brook char) and *Salvelinus* (Arctic/Dolly Varden group).
- *Salvelinus profundus* (tiefseesaibling, Lake Constance) is elevated to full species; 19 specimens were examined and gillrakers are 20-25 versus 27-31 in the normalsaibling.
- *Salvelinus taimyricus* is recognized as a valid species from Lake Taimyr, sympatric with a large predaceous char (10 kg typical spawners, aged to 26 years). Its morphology is described as unique among salmonines.
- Behnke reviews Europe (Alpine, Swedish, Norwegian, Icelandic, British sympatric char), Siberia (Taimyr with 26-32 gillrakers, Lake Seg sympatric pair), Kamchatka and Japan, and arctic and eastern North America. He concludes that *S. malma* is a valid species and that the Brooks Range relict char (Angayukaksurak) may be preglacial *S. malma*.
- Relict *S. alpinus* populations in Maine, New Hampshire and Quebec (*S. oquossa*, *S. aureolus*, *S. marstoni*) are considered virtually identical. Electrophoretic work by [[Saunders and McKenzie]] (1971) shows only slight differences, consistent with 5,000-8,000 years of isolation.
- *Salvelinus agassizii* and the aurora trout (*S. timagamiensis*) are argued to belong to the *S. fontinalis* lineage. Sale and Qadri concluded the aurora trout should be a subspecies of *S. fontinalis*.

**Salvelinus namaycush and S. fontinalis**
- Lake char is described as a stable species with a broad northern distribution; siscowet of Lake Superior (150-180 m) is treated as a possibly reproductively isolated deepwater form.
- Brook char shows significant stock-level evolutionary change in growth, maturity and longevity (Cornell studies).

**Salmo**
- Atlantic salmon shows little major divergence; Payne et al. (1971) proposed subspecies on a single gene difference, which Behnke rejects as futile. Lake salmon are judged derived independently from anadromous stocks in postglacial times.
- *Salmothymus obtusirostris* shows no close affinity with *S. salar*.
- Brown trout lacustrine forms are independently derived in each lake. Lake Garda's carpione (*S. carpio*) is probably a double-invasion or sympatric case involving two forms of *S. trutta*.
- Western North American Salmo: rainbow and cutthroat trout are sympatric and reproductively isolated in coastal drainages, but hybridize in interior waters after introduction of rainbow trout. The Kamloops rainbow may derive partly from a golden trout lineage. Eagle Lake and Lake Tahoe "species" (*S. aquilarum*, *S. regalis*) are considered hatchery or hybrid artifacts.
- Seasonal races of steelhead (Capilano and Cowichan rivers) differ in run timing, vertebrae and gonad maturation, with little genetic exchange. Two rainbow trout populations in Kootenay Lake and two in Bothwell's Creek are treated as genetically distinct.
- The interior cutthroat trout of the upper Snake River has two forms (fine-spotted and large-spotted); Behnke asks what mechanisms maintain segregation in a continuous environment.

**Oncorhynchus nerka**
- Sockeye is described as ideally preadapted for lacustrine life. Virtually all freshwater kokanee are judged derived independently from anadromous populations since the last glaciation.
- Kokanee stocks introduced to Odell Lake, Oregon, maintain reproductive isolation. Behnke recommends a single species, *O. nerka*, while managing reproductively isolated stocks as distinct biological units.

**Coregonus and related whitefishes**
- Coregonines are the most abundant and ubiquitous fishes of recently glaciated lakes and support important commercial fisheries. The paper argues that *Coregonus* taxonomy has "advanced little since the time of Linnaeus".
- Behnke considers Coregonidae should not be elevated to family rank (against Vladykov), retaining Salmonidae with three subfamilies. He proposes coregonines originated no later than the early Miocene.
- *Prosopium*: three of six species are endemic to Bear Lake (Utah-Idaho). McCart (1970) found sympatric pygmy whitefish forms in three Alaskan lakes.
- *Stenodus leucichthys* (inconnu) is a derived predator; hybridization with humpback whitefish is documented. In one cross, 58% survival to fry from 51,000 eggs was achieved.
- *C. pidschian* and *C. muksun* are separated as species in sympatry across arctic Siberia (17-25 versus 45-70 gillrakers). Behnke elevates *C. pidschian* to species rank.
- *C. baunti* is treated as a full species (vertebrae 55-60; gillrakers 30-41).
- The Lake Baikal omul is elevated to species (*C. migratorius*) with three gillraker groups (modal 39, 43, 48).
- *C. clupeaformis* has sympatric dwarf and normal forms in Maine, and Lindsey et al. (1970) found Squanga Lake pairs share common affinities. Karyotype (arm number 108) and pyloric caeca (140-222) are proposed as additional markers.
- *C. canadensis* (Atlantic whitefish, Nova Scotia) lacks an official description and is noted for its terminal jaws and palatine and vomerine teeth.

**Management and nomenclature**
- Behnke states that a single species under the biological concept may contain many populations; excessive species naming would create "a proliferation of names until they became meaningless", while grouping by gillraker number risks polyphyletic species.
- He recommends that fisheries biologists develop a "breeder's handbook"-style shorthand for genetic life-history variability, and proposes polytopic subspecies for convergent, independently derived populations.
- He calls for protection of genetic diversity, citing the endangered status of several cutthroat subspecies (IUCN Red Data Book, 1969).

**General conclusions**
- Coregonine taxonomy must be resolved before sophisticated energy-flow models of lake ecosystems can be built, since such models require knowing whether the fauna has 1-2 or 5-6 reproductively isolated units.
- Better systematics would benefit both management and culture, with selected hybrid genotypes possibly suited to aquaculture.
- Behnke concludes that the most important task is to initiate studies that yield the information needed for an adequate taxonomy of Coregoninae and a better understanding of their evolution.
