# Effects of Predation by Nonnative Smallmouth Bass on Native Salmonid Prey: The Role of Predator and Prey Size

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a journal article (Transactions of the American Fisheries Society, 135:853-860, 2006) by Alexander L. Fritts and Todd N. Pearsons of the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife. It quantifies how nonnative [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) prey on juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and other salmonids in the lower [[Yakima River]]. The authors compare the size-based predation risk with that of the historically dominant native piscivore, [[Northern Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*), in the [[Columbia River]] basin. The study found that small smallmouth bass (150-199 mm FL) consumed the most salmonids, and that predation risk declined as predator size increased. This reverses the pattern documented for pikeminnow. The article is accompanied by a fragment of a separate paper on Chinook stock identification in Pacific Rim fisheries.

## Background & Description

**Study taxa**

- **Smallmouth Bass** (*Micropterus dolomieu*, Centrarchidae): a nonnative piscivore that had become the dominant fish predator of salmonids in the lower Yakima River.
- **Chinook Salmon** (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*, Salmonidae): valued prey. The study focuses on spring Chinook and ocean-type Chinook.
- **Northern Pikeminnow** (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*, Cyprinidae): historically the main fish piscivore on salmonids in the Columbia River and its tributaries, but now rarely captured in the lower Yakima River.

**Study area**

The Yakima River is a Columbia River tributary with a drainage area of approximately 15,900 km². The study covered the lower 68 km, divided into two sections: the "Benton" section (upper) and the "Vangie" section (lower). A low-head diversion dam separates the reaches.

**Objectives**

The authors aimed to:
1. Provide minimum, average, and maximum sizes of prey fish consumed by smallmouth bass.
2. Estimate per capita and population consumption of salmonids by size class of smallmouth bass.
3. Compare the predation risks posed by smallmouth bass and northern pikeminnow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator-prey size theory**

The introduction reviews the basis of size-based predation. Predator size generally correlates positively with the maximum prey size it can consume, and mouth gape limits this. Small predators may be more numerous, so their cumulative consumption can exceed that of larger predators. Foraging costs may also make very small prey unprofitable for large predators.

**Shift in dominant predator**

Northern pikeminnow were historically the main salmonid predators in the Columbia River system (Li et al. 1987; Poe et al. 1991). Smallmouth bass are now the dominant piscine predators of salmonids in the lower Yakima River (Fritts and Pearsons 2004). They are also apparently more numerous than pikeminnow in the John Day River (Shrader and Gray 1999). The two species differ in the age at which they become piscivorous and in body shape (mouth gape), which leads to different predation risks.

**Sampling methods (summary)**

- **Abundance:** Mark-recapture using boat electrofishing (Petersen-type estimates) during spring, 1998-2002, with no mark-recapture in 2001. Weekly CPUE-based abundance was partitioned into size classes using the mark-recapture proportions.
- **Age and growth:** Scales from smallmouth bass were collected in spring 1999-2001 (123, 73, and 100 fish, respectively). Lengths at age were back-calculated with the Fraser-Lee model, using a standard intercept of 35 mm.
- **Diet:** Pulsed gastric lavage was applied to a systematic subsample of smallmouth bass ≥150 mm FL, for a total of 4,135 predators. Prey were identified from diagnostic bones (see [[Diagnostic Bone Analysis]]).
- **Consumption:** Estimated from gastric evacuation time ([[Gastric Evacuation Rate]]; Rogers and Burley 1991) and a consumption equation (Ward et al. 1995). Daily totals were extrapolated to section and reach levels.
- **Statistics:** Linear regression with predator size as the independent variable, significance at P < 0.05; salmonid consumption data were log-transformed.

## Key Findings

**Consumption by predator size**

- Salmonid consumption was inversely related to smallmouth bass length (F₁,₃ = 33.82, P < 0.001).
- The 150-199 mm size group consumed the highest proportion of salmonids in 4 of 5 years, averaging 42.9% of total salmonid consumption over 1998-2002.
- The 200-249 mm group consumed 26.7% on average.
- Most salmonids were eaten by smallmouth bass smaller than 250 mm. Fish under 300 mm accounted for a mean of 83.6% over the five years.
- Smallmouth bass ≥100 mm FL... the proportion consuming salmonids ≥100 mm was only 3.6% of those with fish prey.

**Relative prey size**

- Relative prey length (prey length/predator length × 100) decreased with predator length (F₁,₃₁ = 97.57, P < 0.001; R² = 0.17).
- Relative prey length ranged from a minimum of 7.3% to a maximum of 56.6%, with a mean of 25.0%.
- Smallmouth bass consumed salmonids of 22-153 mm (mean 59 mm).

**Abundance and diet**

- Mean abundance of smallmouth bass decreased with increasing size (F₁,₅ = 15.66, P = 0.01). The 150-199 mm group had a mean estimated abundance of 1,586 fish, far above larger classes.
- Salmonids were less common in the guts of larger smallmouth bass (F₁,₅ = 18.99, P = 0.007).
- Larger smallmouth bass were less likely to contain any food items.

**Growth and maturity**

- Back-calculated mean fork lengths in the Results were 90 mm (age 1), 148 mm (age 2), 207 mm (age 3), 254 mm (age 4), and 301 mm (age 5).
- The Discussion gives different values for the same data (149 mm at age 2 and 311 mm at age 5), so the figures should be checked against the source table.
- Smallmouth bass become piscivorous at about 100-150 mm. Northern pikeminnow become piscivorous at about 200-250 mm (Poe et al. 1991; Vigg et al. 1991). Smallmouth bass therefore become piscivorous roughly 2-3 years earlier.

**Comparison with northern pikeminnow**

- Rieman and Beamesderfer (1990) found that intermediate and older pikeminnow (>400 mm) contributed over 50% of simulated predation in an unexploited population.
- Historically, the greatest predation threat to juvenile salmonids in the lower Yakima River came from pikeminnow >400 mm. Today, smallmouth bass <250 mm pose the greatest threat.
- Pikeminnow consumption of salmonids rises with predator size, while smallmouth bass consumption falls. The risk to different salmonid life-history types has therefore shifted with the establishment of smallmouth bass.
- The authors speculate that ocean-type Chinook salmon are now at greatest risk, whereas larger salmonids (spring Chinook, coho, steelhead) faced higher risk in the past.

**Limitations noted by the authors**

- Smallmouth bass <150 mm were not sampled, so the minimum size at which they eat salmonids is unknown. Based on the 25% mean relative prey length for 150-159 mm fish, a 100 mm smallmouth bass could theoretically consume a 25-35 mm fish, similar in size to newly emerged fall Chinook.
- The authors note that few studies have assessed size-based predation risk for nonnative predators, largely because abundance is hard to estimate.

**Conclusions**

The introduction of smallmouth bass has altered the size-based predation dynamics of the lower Yakima River. Predation on salmonids is concentrated in small smallmouth bass, which are both abundant and early piscivores. The authors argue that reducing predation by small fish may be the most effective way to reduce impacts on salmonids. The study also shows that introducing nonnative piscivores can have unanticipated negative consequences.

**Companion paper (Chinook stock identification)**

The accompanying abstract (Beacham and coauthors, received October 5, 2005; published online June 26, 2006) addresses microsatellite-based estimation of Chinook salmon stock composition in mixed-stock fisheries across the Pacific Rim. It reports that precision of stock-composition estimates depends on regional baseline data and that stock compositions vary among regions. The fragment does not give the full methods or numerical results.
