## Executive Summary
This 1999 editorial from the St. Louis Post-Dispatch discusses the ecological decline of the [[Missouri River]], driven by decades of channelization and dam operations, and its impact on native aquatic wildlife, most notably the [[Pallid Sturgeon]]. The piece is not a primary scientific study but a policy-focused commentary that references the endangered status of the species and emerging legislative efforts—led by Sen. Christopher S. Bond and Rep. Kenny Hulshof—to fund river restoration ($30 million program) and habitat mitigation. It also references a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers master plan for dam operations in Montana and South Dakota aimed at restoring more natural flow regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat requirements**: Sandbars, side channels, and slower-moving water are identified as critical habitat for reproduction of the [[Pallid Sturgeon]] and associated shorebird species.
- **Co-occurring endangered species**: [[Piping Plover]] and [[Least Tern]] are cited as shorebirds endangered by the same habitat degradation along the [[Missouri River]].
- **Habitat degradation**: Channelization over the "last half century" has eliminated natural habitat structures (sandbars, side channels, wetlands) necessary for wildlife reproduction.
- **Flow regime dependency**: Fast, uniform year-round water flow forces fish to expend excess energy on survival (e.g., maintaining position, foraging) rather than reproduction. Conservationists advocate restoring a more natural hydrograph—higher spring flows and lower summer flows—to reduce this energetic burden and support reproductive success.
- **Dam influence**: Six dams in Montana and South Dakota regulate flow; the Corps of Engineers has determined spring releases could be increased by up to one-third without causing downstream flooding, which would better mimic natural flood-pulse dynamics important for spawning cues and habitat formation.

## Key Findings
- The [[Missouri River]] was ranked the second-most-endangered river in the U.S. by American Rivers (1999 survey), largely due to channelization eliminating wildlife habitat.
- A proposed $30 million congressional program aims to restore aquatic wildlife habitat in the river, complementing an existing land-mitigation program allowing the Corps of Engineers to restore side channels and wetlands via land purchases from willing sellers.
- The Corps of Engineers' forthcoming master plan for six upstream dams may permit up to a one-third increase in spring water releases without causing downstream flooding, a change favorable for restoring natural flow patterns and native species reproduction.
- Urban sprawl is cited as the leading nationwide threat to rivers, with St. Louis County floodplain development (e.g., Chesterfield Valley) singled out as a poor management example that increases flood risk.
- Conservation advocates frame the upcoming 200th anniversary of the Lewis and Clark expedition (5 years from publication) as a symbolic deadline for achieving meaningful restoration of the [[Missouri River]] ecosystem toward a more historically natural state.
- The political framing is notable: restoration efforts are being led by Republican legislators (Bond, Hulshof) not traditionally aligned with environmental advocacy, suggesting bipartisan momentum for river restoration policy.
