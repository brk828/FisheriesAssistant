# North American Recent Soft-Shelled Turtles (Family Trionychidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trionychidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by Robert G. Webb (University of Kansas Publications, Museum of Natural History, Vol. 18, No. 10, pp. 429-611, published February 16, 1962) revises the Recent North American soft-shelled turtles of the genus [[Trionyx]]. The study examined 1849 soft-shelled turtles from many museum and private collections. It recognizes four species and eight subspecies, two of them (*T. spinifer pallidus* and *T. s. guadalupensis*) described as new. The work covers taxonomy, geographic variation, secondary sexual and ontogenetic variation, habitat, activity, feeding, reproduction, growth, mortality, parasites, economic importance, and evolutionary relationships. It is primarily a taxonomic and natural history revision, not an ecological field survey of fish or aquatic communities.

## Background & Description

The family [[Trionychidae]] comprises soft-shelled turtles, which lack horny epidermal shields, have a flattened, soft-skinned carapace and plastron, a tubate snout, and are aquatic and mainly carnivorous. Webb's central question was whether the degree of difference between closely related populations reflects how long drainage systems have been isolated. Historically, American softshells had been revised by Agassiz (1857), Siebenrock (1924), Stejneger (1944) and Neill (1951).

Key diagnostic characters used in the revision include:

- **Septal ridges**: present in [[Trionyx Ferox]] and [[Trionyx Spinifer]], absent in [[Trionyx Muticus]].
- **Marginal ridge**: present only in *T. ferox*.
- **Tubercles on the anterior carapace edge**: prominent in *T. ferox* and some *T. spinifer*, reduced or absent in *T. muticus* and *T. ater*.
- **Juvenal pattern**: black ocelli or spots, white dots, or dusky dots and streaks, varying by subspecies.
- **Skull features**: position of greatest skull width, opisthotic-exoccipital spur, and contact of the maxillaries above the premaxillaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Softshells occur in large rivers and streams with some current, and in large permanent lakes and ponds with soft mud or sand bottoms. They generally avoid temporary water. *T. ferox* occurs in all freshwater habitats but is chiefly lentic in the northern part of its range, where it is sympatric with [[Trionyx Spinifer Asper]]; the two are ecologically separated. *T. s. asper* is largely a running-water form in the southern range. *T. muticus* is mostly confined to rivers and streams, with few lentic records. *T. ater* is restricted to clear-water ponds in the Cuatro Ciénegas basin of Coahuila, Mexico. Several taxa tolerate brackish water, notably *T. ferox*, *T. s. pallidus* and *T. s. asper*.

**Geographic context.** The genus occurs across most of the eastern two-thirds of North America, drawn from the Mississippi drainage, the Great Plains, and the Gulf and Atlantic coastal drainages. It shows a clear east-west gradient in pattern and body proportions within *T. spinifer*. Webb suggests that the western sluggish, turbid, sandy streams and the eastern clear, swift waters may help explain the spinifer-hartwegi and pallidus-guadalupensis clines.

**Activity and feeding.** Softshells bask rarely, float at the surface, and bury themselves in sand or mud in shallow water, a habit used by turtle collectors ("noodeling"). They are mainly carnivorous, with crawfish and aquatic insects dominant in most diets. Webb notes that fish form only a small share of the diet, undermining the common fisherman belief that softshells harm fish stocks.

**Reproduction.** Males of *T. spinifer* from Lake Texoma become sexually mature at a plastral length of 9.0-10.0 cm. Females typically mature at about 20.0 cm plastron length, while *T. s. emoryi* from the Big Bend region matures at about 16.0 cm. Nesting occurs in sand, mostly in June and July in the northern range, and from late March in Florida for *T. ferox*. Clutches average about 20 eggs in northern populations, and Webb estimates about 10 eggs per clutch with two or three clutches per season in southern populations.

## Key Findings

- **Collection methods:** Hoop-nets were the most efficient method. In Lake Texoma, 156 turtles were caught in hoop-nets, 19 (12%) of them *T. spinifer*. Gill-net data showed 80-90% of turtles were softshells.
- **Taxonomic outcome:** Webb recognizes four species (*T. ferox*, *T. spinifer*, *T. muticus*, *T. ater*) and eight subspecies: *T. s. spinifer*, *hartwegi*, *asper*, *emoryi*, *guadalupensis* (new), *pallidus* (new); *T. m. muticus*, *calvatus*.
- **Size:** *T. ferox* is the largest species; *T. muticus* is the smallest. Maximum plastral lengths: males of *T. ferox* 26.0 cm (mean of ten largest 20.0 cm), females 34.0 cm (mean 27.9 cm); *T. spinifer* males 16.0 cm (mean 14.4 cm), females 31.0 cm (mean 28.0 cm); *T. muticus* males 14.0 cm (mean 12.3 cm), females 21.5 cm (mean 18.9 cm).
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males of *T. spinifer* have sandpapery carapaces from small tubercles. Males of *T. s. emoryi* from the Big Bend and Rio Conchos show orange coloration on the side of the head. Male tails elongate posteriorly to extend the cloaca beyond the carapace.
- **Geographic clines:** Within *T. spinifer*, tuberculation, snout and limb patterns, head proportions and carapace shape change gradually from the Mississippi valley westward to the Texas-Mexico region, with a sharp break between Colorado and Brazos drainages in the pallidus-guadalupensis cline.
- **Relationships:** *T. ferox* resembles Old World softshells most closely in large size, marginal ridge, and ridged tubercles; it is considered the most primitive North American form. *T. ater* is interpreted as a darkened, relict race of *T. s. emoryi*, though sympatry in one pond suggests species status.
- **Reproduction and growth:** Breckenridge's Minnesota data suggested age at maturity near 12 years for female *T. spinifer*; Webb argues the true figure is less, perhaps around 9 years, given slowing growth at maturity.
- **Mortality:** Predation on eggs by skunks, raccoons, foxes and crows accounts for most mortality. Humans take softshells as food and as perceived pests.
- **Parasites:** Trematodes including *Teloporia aspidonectes* were reported, along with leeches (*Placobdella parasitica*) on *T. muticus*.
- **Economic importance:** Softshells are eaten locally and sold in small quantities, with commercial value mainly in the southeastern United States. Webb says they are not a major fisheries problem.
- **Evolutionary note:** The genus is interpreted as having crossed into North America from the Old World, with *T. ferox* closest to ancestral forms, and [[Trionyx Spinifer]], [[Trionyx Muticus]] and [[Trionyx Ater]] as autochthonous North American lineages.
