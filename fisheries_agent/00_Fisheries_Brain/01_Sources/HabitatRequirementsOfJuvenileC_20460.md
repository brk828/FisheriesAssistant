# Habitat Requirements of Juvenile Colorado River Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service report (FWS/OBS-77/65, October 1977), prepared by Paul B. Holden of BIO/WEST, Inc., under the [[Biological Services Program]] and the Federal Interagency Energy/Environment Research and Development Program, examines the habitat use and movements of four rare fishes in the [[Green River]] of Utah. Sampling ran from Jensen to Green River, roughly 175 miles, in August and September 1977. The study focused on young [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered species. Its main aims were to define the physical and chemical habitat of rare fishes, locate spawning areas, track movements, identify associated species, and assess the relative value of river habitats. The central conclusion is that young squawfish concentrate in [[Backwaters]] with little or no current and silt bottoms, and that this preferred habitat may limit recruitment.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River basin supports two federally endangered fishes, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were rare and under consideration for listing. Recovery depends on two factors: adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and interactions with exotic fishes. The lower and middle Green River in Utah was identified as the least altered large-river habitat in the upper basin and as the area with reproducing squawfish populations. Adult squawfish elsewhere showed lacking recruitment.

The study's objectives were to:
1. Determine the physical and chemical parameters of habitats used by all sizes of rare fishes, with emphasis on young fish.
2. Determine whether spawning occurs in the study area and where.
3. Determine the movements of size (age) classes of rare fishes.
4. Determine the fish species associated with the study area.
5. Assess the relative importance of various river habitats to rare fishes.

Methods used seines, electrofishing, and a fry shovel, with water velocity, depth, temperature, and substrate measured at each habitat. Habitat was classed as backwater, eddy, run, or riffle. Habitat preferences were expressed as electivity curves, part of the [[Instream Flow Methodology]] developed by the Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group. Rare fish of more than about 100 mm total length were tagged with numbered plastic fingerling tags, and smaller juveniles were marked by anal fin clipping.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** River temperatures varied daily. Early morning temperatures were 3 to 5 °C cooler than late afternoon. Backwaters were the warmest areas, often 3 to 8 degrees above the main river in late afternoon. Morning temperatures at Jensen in August were usually 15-17 °C, and 19-21 °C downstream. In September, Desolation Canyon fell to a low of 10.5 °C. Water level fluctuated daily because of releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The fluctuation was 16 inches at Jensen and 3 to 4 inches in [[Desolation Canyon]]. Flows in 1977 appeared lower than normal, and the Yampa River was exceptionally low.

**Chemistry and turbidity.** Water chemistry changed little during the study, and all habitats were chemically similar, so fish could not select chemical ranges. Turbidity was highest after rainstorms. The watershed is highly erodible, and native fishes are adapted to sudden turbidity changes linked to flow increases.

**Habitat types and associated fauna.** Backwaters were nutrient-rich because of silt substrate and warm because of slow exchange. They also supported small crustaceans and aquatic insects that serve as food for young squawfish. Backwaters are classed as short-lived (changing with a 6-inch rise or fall) or long-lived (protected from minor level changes). Exotic species such as [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], and [[Fathead Minnow]] depended mainly on long-lived backwaters.

**Species assemblage.** Nineteen species totaling over 19,500 specimens were collected. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was overwhelmingly dominant, with 13,711 individuals. The [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) was the most abundant native species, with 918 individuals. Only two bonytail chubs were caught. Northern pike, previously reported, was not encountered.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and rare fish distribution**
- Over 330 fish samples were taken: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies, and 19 in riffles.
- Young-of-the-year squawfish (35-64 mm) were found only in the lower study area. Juveniles (65-200 mm) were concentrated at both ends, with fewer in the middle. Only six subadults were captured, three near Ouray.
- Six young-of-the-year razorback suckers were found in the middle study area, the first wild young razorbacks reported from the upper Colorado basin. Identification was tentative and based on lateral line scales (80-88).
- Desolation and Gray canyon chubs were treated as humpback chubs. One juvenile chub with 10-10 fin ray counts was tentatively identified as bonytail chub.

**Preferred habitat (electivity curves)**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish: backwaters with no current, firm silt bottom, and depth of 1 to 2 feet.
- Juvenile squawfish: backwaters with no current and silt bottom, less selective for depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm): variable velocities and depths, preferring sandy-bottomed eddies and runs to backwaters.
- Young-of-the-year razorbacks: eddies with about 0.2 fps velocity, 1.0 foot depth, and soft silt.
- Young-of-the-year humpback chubs: backwaters with no current, firm silt, and 2 feet maximum depth, but also eddies and runs at 0.4-0.8 fps. They were less selective than squawfish.
- Adult humpback chubs: primarily eddies and runs with sand bottoms. Some were taken at 2.5 fps and at 20 feet depth.
- No size class of squawfish or humpback chub selected temperature.

**Reliability of electivity curves** (criteria: excellent ≥200 measurements, good 50-200, fair <50)
- Young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub curves were rated good; the remaining curves were fair. None were rated excellent. Several years of data may be needed for seasonal juvenile and adult curves.

**Movement and mark-recapture**
- 20 juvenile squawfish were tagged, 13 more were anal fin clipped, and 4 subadults were tagged. One tagged juvenile was recaptured at its release site 4.5 hours later.
- In a single backwater, young-of-the-year squawfish catches over five samples were 4, 31, 29, 11, and 12. Marked fish numbered 4, 31, 28, 10, and 0. Recaptures were 0, 1, 1, and 1 in the last three samples. Results were inconclusive, though movement within the backwater is the likeliest explanation.
- Partial anal fin clipping was chosen over Bismark brown Y dye, which caused stress within 5 to 15 minutes.

**Spawning inference**
- Squawfish spawning in 1977 was apparently confined to Gray Canyon, probably near a backwater below Range Creek, since young were not found above that point in quantity.
- Razorback spawning was inferred near and just below Ouray, and humpback chub spawning in Desolation and Gray canyons.
- No young-of-the-year squawfish were found from Jensen to Ouray. Early-August temperatures near Jensen (15-20 °C) may have been below the 21 °C threshold cited for squawfish spawning.
- Low 1977 flows may have altered spawning. The report notes that spawning occurred only near Green River that year, compared with earlier reproduction from Ouray to Green River.

**Associated species and competition**
- Red shiners occurred in 100% of young-of-the-year squawfish samples (9 different areas), and in 95% of juvenile squawfish samples.
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were most often associated with red shiners, fathead minnows, juvenile carp, and young-of-the-year channel catfish.
- Young exotic species dominated young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub collections. Juvenile squawfish associated mainly with native species.
- The [[Smallmouth Bass]] adds a potential piscivore, though spring turbidity and river conditions were expected to limit its reproduction.

**Management implications**
- Pump intakes for flooding [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] ponds and irrigating croplands should avoid slow-water areas and backwaters. Placement in swift runs is recommended.
- Long-lived artificial backwaters should be avoided as a rare-fish enhancement technique, because they would also increase exotic populations. Short-lived backwaters may suffice for young-of-the-year squawfish.
- Low 1977 flows lengthened the life of long-lived backwaters, probably because high spring flows were nearly absent and backwaters formed earlier than normal.
- Future studies under normal flow regimes are needed to confirm whether low flows reduced spawning-rearing habitat.

**Principal conclusions**
- Backwaters are critical for young-of-the-year squawfish, and possibly for humpback chub larvae and juveniles.
- Young squawfish appear to move little from spawning sites, and preferred habitat concentrates juveniles.
- Preferred habitat for young fish may be a limiting factor for the rare fishes, particularly Colorado Squawfish.
