# Federal Listing of the Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) as an Endangered Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final rule, published in the *Federal Register* on October 23, 1991 (effective November 22, 1991), lists the [[Razorback Sucker]] as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The species persists in limited numbers across the [[Colorado River Basin]], with little evidence of natural recruitment in the past 30 years and a downward trend in adult numbers over the last 10 years. The Service determined that the species is in danger of extinction throughout all of its range, but found [[Critical Habitat]] not presently determinable. The document also compiles the species' biology, distribution, threats, regulatory context, public comments and responses, and a reference list, much of which concerns freshwater mussels (Unionidae) in the same region.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Abbott from a single mounted specimen from the Colorado River. Originally placed in the genus *Catostomus*; after further study it was assigned to its own genus, *Xyrauchen*, which is readily distinguished by the abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal keel behind the head and a large, terminal mouth. Adults can be confused with other sucker species, and juveniles may lack an obvious keel.
- **Size:** Cultured specimens as small as 85 mm (3.3 in.) have been recorded. Adults may exceed 3 kg (6 lbs) and 60 cm (2 ft) in length.
- **Historic range:** Once occurred over about 5,635 km (3,500 mi) of the basin, including Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming, and parts of Baja California Norte and Sonora, Mexico. Distribution has been reduced to about 1,205 km (750 mi) in the upper basin.
- **Historic abundance:** Dense aggregations were reported at Green River, Utah (1871), and residents near Clifton, Colorado, observed thousands during spring runoff in the 1930s and early 1940s. A commercial fishery existed in the lower basin in the early 1900s.
- **Food habits:** Adults in Lake Mohave had stomachs dominated by planktonic crustaceans, diatoms, filamentous algae and detritus (Marsh 1987). Larvae feed on zooplankton, with [[Bosmina]] and [[Copepoda]] selected or avoided in different studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current status and population**
- Approximately 60,000 adults were estimated in [[Lake Mohave]] (Marsh and Minckley, in press).
- Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 758 to 1,138 razorback suckers in the upper Green River.
- Most upper-basin fish occur in the Grand Valley; the number of adult captures there declined appreciably after 1975.
- Of 70 fish collected in 1981–83, ages were estimated at 24 to 44 years; 89% had hatched before or at the time of impoundment (McCarthy and Minckley 1987).

**Reproduction and spawning**
- Lower basin spawning occurs January through April, with water temperatures of 11.1–18.1°C (52.7–64.4°F).
- Upper basin ripe adults were observed in the Green River from April 20 to June 14 (1981–1989), with spawning temperatures of 9–17°C (48–63°F).
- In the Grand Valley, 40 of 42 running ripe adults were captured between May 24 and June 17 (Osmundson and Kaeding 1991).
- Spawning occurs over gravel bars cleaned by currents or wave action; several males accompany each female.

**Habitat use**
- Radio-tracked adults in the Green River (Tyus 1987a) were found at depths of 0.0–3.4 m (2.0–11.0 ft) and velocities of 0.1–0.6 m/s (0.33–2.0 ft/s) during non-breeding seasons.
- Valdez and Masslich (1989) found most of 17 tracked fish moved less than 5 km (3 mi) over winter.
- Seasonal depth selection in the Grand Valley ranged from 0.9–0.99 m in May–June to 1.83–2.16 m from November through April.

**Threats**
- **Habitat alteration (Factor A):** Flow regulation, dams, water depletion, the planned [[Central Utah Project]] (diverting 185,000 acre-ft from the Green River), and the Animas–La Plata and other reservoir projects. Storage of high spring flows reduced spring runoff by 28% in the Green River and 37% in the Colorado River.
- **Water temperature:** Temperatures below 15°C reduced survival, and egg mortality was high below 10°C (50°F). Stretches below Flaming Gorge Reservoir and Lake Powell are too cold for spawning.
- **Overutilization (Factor B):** Considered no longer a threat, as state laws now protect the species from harvest.
- **Disease and predation (Factor C):** No evidence that disease is a significant factor. Predation on eggs and larvae by carp, channel catfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass, sunfish and others is documented; nonnative predators such as walleye and northern pike have become prominent in the Green River.
- **Regulatory inadequacy (Factor D):** The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] is cooperative rather than regulatory and does not cover the San Juan River.
- **Other factors (Factor E):** Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was documented, but the Service judged the evidence suggestive rather than conclusive. Nonnative fish competition and introductions (e.g., [[Grass Carp]], tilapia, flathead catfish, rainbow smelt) are significant concerns.

## Key Findings

1. **Listing decision:** The razorback sucker is listed as endangered throughout its range. The Service rejected threatened status, stating that the species is currently in danger of extinction.
2. **Basis for listing:** Greatly reduced range; partitioning of its range by dams; extensive alteration of natural habitats through impoundment and altered flow and temperature regimes; apparent inability to recruit successfully in the wild; and introduction of nonnative fishes.
3. **Critical habitat:** Not determinable at this time, because the biological needs of the species are insufficiently known to identify the precise constituent elements essential to survival and recovery. The Service will reexamine prudence and determinability.
4. **Recruitment:** No significant recruitment has been documented in recent years; the population is composed primarily of old individuals.
5. **Public comment:** The proposed rule (May 22, 1990) drew 41 comments supporting listing, 24 neutral, and 9 opposed (per the document's tally, with a public hearing in Farmington, New Mexico, on August 14, 1990, attended by about 60 people, 18 of whom presented oral statements). Ten commenters supported critical habitat designation, and four opposed it.
6. **Responses to issues:** The Service addressed issues on the threatened-versus-endangered question, critical habitat benefits, river otter reintroduction (the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources concluded no significant impact on rare fishes), and the Two Forks project (which was consulted on for three other listed fishes, not for the razorback sucker).
7. **Regulatory action:** Amends 50 CFR 17.11(h) to add the razorback sucker to the List of Endangered and Threatened Wildlife, under the "Fishes" heading.
