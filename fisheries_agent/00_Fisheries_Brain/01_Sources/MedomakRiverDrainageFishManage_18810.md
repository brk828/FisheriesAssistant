# Medomak River Drainage Fish Management (1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multi-family-fish-assemblage

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by [[Robert E Foye]], Regional Fishery Biologist with the [[Maine Department Of Inland Fisheries And Game]], condenses biological survey findings and management recommendations for the [[Medomak River]] drainage in coastal Maine. The river is a 36-mile tributary of the Atlantic Ocean. The report covers warmwater and coldwater game fish, a formerly important [[Alewife]] run, dams and other obstructions to migration, beaver impacts, and sawmill and other pollution. It recommends making obstructions passable, managing deadwater reaches for warmwater fish, continuing spring trout stocking, and reducing sawmill pollution. It also questions the feasibility of restoring alewives.

## Background & Description

- **Study scope:** Biological studies began in 1941. Their purpose was to evaluate lakes and streams for species suitability, assess anadromous and commercial potential, locate pollution, and catalogue obstructions to fish migration. Lake inventories by [[Everhart]] et al. (1953) cover most ponds in the drainage.
- **Watershed:** The river rises as a spring brook in Liberty Township, Waldo County, and flows generally south through Waldo, Knox, and Lincoln Counties. It enters tidal water at Waldoboro. The drainage area is given as 74 square miles in the description and approximately 76 square miles in the summary. The river has a total fall of 530 feet.
- **Lakes:** Seven great ponds total about 1,077 acres. The largest is [[Washington Pond]] (581 acres). The others are [[Medomak Pond]] (228 acres), [[Crystal Pond]] (83 acres), [[Little Medomak Pond]] (83 acres), [[Kalers Pond]] (75 acres), [[Iron Pond]] (17 acres), and [[Johnson Pond]] (10 acres).
- **Fish assemblage:** Twenty species were recorded. Game fish include [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[White Perch]], [[Chain Pickerel]], and [[Yellow Perch]]. Other species include hornpout, smelt, alewife, eel, white sucker, minnows, lake chub, several shiners and dace, ninespine stickleback, and pumpkinseed sunfish. Distributions are detailed in the lake inventory reports.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat zones (main stem, upstream to downstream):**
- **Upper reaches:** Spring-fed, under 6 feet wide, with gravel, rubble, and ledge-boulder riffles and good overhead cover where not flowed. Beaver dams have created several large flowages.
- **Middle reaches (about 1.25 miles above the old Skidmore mill site to Medomak Pond):** A slow, meandering stream with sand, clay, silt, and profuse aquatic vegetation. Flow was estimated at 4 c.f.s. in August 1955. Temperatures ranged from 63°F to 72°F. Depths ranged from 8 inches to 4 feet.
- **Lower reaches:** Mostly deadwater, with some riffles and deep pools. Average width near tidewater is about 75 feet.

**Coldwater habitat:** Limited. Most is in the upper river above Route 105 in Washington, where summer flow of 1 to 2 c.f.s. (about 500 to 1,000 gpm) cannot support large trout populations. Several small cool tributaries also support brook trout.

**Warmwater habitat:** Abundant in the deadwater reaches and in five of the seven great ponds. Chain pickerel, smallmouth bass, and white perch are established in many deadwater areas.

**Obstructions:** Seven major main-stem obstructions block migration, along with several beaver dams. Most fishways are derelict. Examples include:
- **Obstruction #1:** Waldoboro mill dam, 7 ft head, with a dilapidated fishway. A natural falls about 100 ft long lies just above it.
- **Obstruction #3:** Mill dam about 5 miles upstream, over 200 ft long and 6 ft high, with an inoperable fishway.
- **Obstruction #4:** Sawmill dam, 420 ft long and 11 ft high, with a large mill pond above it. It blocks upstream migration at all water levels.
- **Obstruction #7:** South Liberty mill dam, 100 ft long and 10 ft high at spillway, with no fishway.

**Tributaries:**
- **[[Little Medomak Brook]]:** Outlet of Washington Pond, about 5 miles long. Four man-made obstructions and several beaver dams block it.
- **[[Pettingill Stream]]:** Dam and fishway built for duck marsh habitat. The fishway passes fish.
- **Kalers Pond outlet:** Nearly dry in summer. Beaver workings block migration both ways.
- **[[Hope Brook]]:** Outlet of Iron Pond. It provides brook trout spawning and nursery habitat, but past beaver dams limited trout access.

**Pollution:** Sources include sawmill wastes, a fish rearing station, septic overflow and household sewage, a canning factory, a poultry plant, and garage and dump oil wastes. Agricultural drainage is a major source of bacterial pollution. Water quality data come from the [[Water Improvement Commission]] surveys of 1953 and 1954. Waters are classed B1 or B2, except the lower river from 400 feet above the Route 1 bridge in Waldoboro to head of tide, which is class C. Sawmill wastes from North Waldoboro littered the river between North Waldoboro and Winslows Mills in 1955.

## Key Findings

1. **Alewife decline:** The river once supported an anadromous [[Alewife]] run. A 1896 production figure of 41,512 pounds is cited from [[Rounsefell And Stringer 1943]]. The run is now practically extinct because impassable dams block access to spawning ponds. Several hundred alewives were hauled to Medomak Pond in 1954. Washington Pond, nearly half the lake area, is blocked by seven man-made obstructions and beaver dams.
2. **Coldwater fisheries:** Natural brook trout fisheries exist in Iron Pond's outlet and in the upper river. A brown trout fishery in Kalers Pond relies on periodic stocking. Brook trout stocking in Little Medomak Pond did not establish a population. The brown trout fishery between Winslows Mills and Waldoboro Village showed no natural reproduction in 1955.
3. **Trout limitations:** Low summer flow, limited spawning and nursery habitat, obstructions, and old beaver flowages all reduce trout habitat. Sawmill wastes further restrict trout fishing in the lower river.
4. **Warmwater potential:** Deadwater reaches and five great ponds offer suitable warmwater habitat for chain pickerel, smallmouth bass, and white perch.
5. **Obstruction status:** Seven major obstructions block the main stem, with fishways at the first two dams derelict. The report's obstruction summary states four permanent man-made obstructions, three natural falls, and several beaver dams on the main stem, which does not fully match the seven-obstruction count given elsewhere.
6. **Pollution:** Pollution is relatively light except in the lower reaches, where sawmill, canning, poultry, garage, dump, and domestic sources have produced the class C designation.

**Management recommendations:**
1. Make upstream obstructions passable where possible. Consider removing the derelict South Liberty sawmill dam. Remove old beaver dams to restore trout migration, and keep the Iron Pond outlet free of obstructions.
2. Manage long deadwater reaches and warmwater ponds for warmwater fisheries, since they are unsuitable for coldwater management.
3. Continue spring stocking of brook trout in the lower river, after spring run-off, while it provides a suitable fishery.
4. Question the practicality of brown trout stocking given rearing costs and low creel returns. Conduct further creel studies.
5. Restore the alewife fishery by rebuilding fishways at major obstructions. A commercial run may be possible once fish reach Washington Pond, but feasibility is questionable given the many barriers.
6. Prevent sawdust and sawmill wastes from entering the river, with priority at North Waldoboro.
