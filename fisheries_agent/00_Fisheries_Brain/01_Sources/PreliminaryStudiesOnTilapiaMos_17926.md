# Preliminary Studies on Tilapia mossambica Peters Relative to Experimental Pond Culture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 thesis excerpt, prepared by H. D. Ketry of the [[Alabama Department of Conservation]] and submitted to the Alabama Polytechnic Institute, evaluates the exotic cichlid [[Tilapia Mossambica]] as a candidate for [[Farm Pond Stocking]] in the southeastern United States. In an 18-week concrete pond experiment, tilapia outgrew [[Bluegill]] under feeding, fertilization, and manuring regimes. However, cold-room trials showed that tilapia stop feeding near 60°F, begin dying at 52 to 58°F, and suffer 100 per cent mortality at 47 to 49°F. The author concluded that the species cannot survive winter conditions in most of the Southeast.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and origin:** Tilapia mossambica Peters is a member of the family [[Cichlidae]], a large family of over 600 species found mainly in tropical regions. The species is a native of east Africa (Atz, 1954).
- **Introductions:** The document lists introductions to Thailand, the Philippines, Haiti, and Formosa, plus Sumatra, Malaya, the British West Indies, Trinidad, North Borneo, West Borneo, East Borneo, Martinique, Pakistan, Ceylon, India, and the United States.
- **Habitat range:** Tilapia occur in both fresh and brackish waters. Vaas and Hofstede (1952) reported transfer from fresh water to brackish water of high salinity, "exceeding that of normal sea water," and noted that the transfer can be made in reverse.
- **Reproduction:** Tilapia is a true [[Mouth Brooding]] fish. Spawning reportedly begins at two or three months of age. The male clears a depression about 12 inches wide in soft bottom substrate, the female deposits eggs, and the male fertilizes them. The female carries eggs and young in her mouth for 10 to 14 days. Eggs incubate in 60 hours at 80 to 83°F. Mature females spawn at intervals of 30 to 40 days, and one male can fertilize the spawns of several females. Spawning frequency in a year is governed by environmental conditions (Chen, 1953).
- **Fecundity:** A spawner 8 centimeters long carried 100 to 150 eggs per spawning, compared with 200 to 250 eggs for a fish about 11 centimeters long (Chen, 1953). Villadolid and Acosta (undated) state that one pair of mature fish may produce 106,000 young in a year.
- **Diet (literature):** Vaas and Hofstede (1952) describe tilapia as primarily a vegetable feeder. Chen (1953) reports feeding on plankton, algae, decomposed vegetable matter, rice bran, soybean meal, chopped meat, and fish, but notes rejection of live food. Young fish feed mostly on diatoms, unicellular algae, and small Crustacea.
- **Perceived attributes:** Tilapia were described as fast-growing, rapidly reproducing, easy to feed and stock, of good food quality, helpful in controlling mosquito larvae, and useful for "manuring" pond waters (Villadolid and Acosta, undated). Hey (1947) reported a maximum weight of five pounds in South Africa, with an average of 2.5 pounds, and noted good sport fishing using earthworm bait. Schuster (1950) and Hofstede and Botke (1950) reported use of tilapia to clear filamentous algae and vegetation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Cold tolerance in prior introductions:** Tilapia died at temperatures below 46°F (Hey, 1953) and 57°F (Chen, 1953).
- **Auburn winter conditions:** Water temperatures recorded at Auburn over a 31-week period in 1951 to 1952 reached a minimum of 44°F. This temperature extended to a depth of seven feet in a one-acre pond.
- **Pond environment:** The concrete ponds drained into a stream affected by decomposition and by raw sewage and storm water from the city of Auburn. Precautions were taken against fish escape at outlets.
- **Gut and food web:** Tilapia have a very long, highly coiled intestine and no true stomach. The author noted that the food chain of tilapia is very short compared with that of the bluegill.
- **Water management:** Excessive manuring could cause fish deaths through oxygen depletion as manure decayed.

## Key Findings

**Objectives**
1. Determine in aquaria the minimum temperatures at which tilapia feed and survive.
2. Compare tilapia growth with [[Bluegill]] using feeding, fertilization, and manuring.
3. Determine some food habits of tilapia.

**Methods Overview**
- **Concrete ponds:** Eighteen circular concrete ponds, each 0.0025 acre in surface area and two feet deep, were stocked at the equivalent of 2,000 fish per acre. Water came from an unfertilized impoundment. Bluegills were seined from experimental ponds. Tilapia brood stock were shipped by air from the Steinhart Aquarium in San Francisco, California.
- **Feeding:** Fish were fed 6 per cent of body weight every other day with a standard trout food. Mixture 1 was used June 26 to August 20, and mixture 2 was used August 21 to October 30, 1954.
- **Fertilization:** 100 pounds of commercial 6-8-4 plus 10 pounds of ammonium nitrate per acre per application, at about two-week intervals.
- **Manuring:** Stable-grade cow manure at 4,000 pounds per acre per application, at about four-week intervals. Each pond received a total of 40 pounds of manure (16,000 pounds per acre) during the period.
- **Cold-room trials:** Aquaria contained 40 liters of continuously aerated water. Trial No. 1 started at 70°F and dropped about 7°F per week. Trial No. 2 dropped about 6°F per week for two weeks, then about 2°F per week, and included bluegills as controls. Bluegill aquaria were treated with one p.p.m. of acriflavin, a bactericide.
- **Food habits:** A dirt pond of 0.05 acre was used, managed on the same manuring schedule as the concrete ponds.

**Growth Results (18 weeks, June 26 to October 30, 1954)**

| Experiment | Species | Initial Length / Weight | Final Length / Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeding | Tilapia | 77 mm / 7.6 g | 169 mm / 91.6 g |
| Feeding | Bluegill | 75 mm / 7.2 g | 120 mm / 30.8 g |
| Fertilization | Tilapia | 73 mm / 7.3 g | 163 mm / 72.5 g |
| Fertilization | Bluegill | 73 mm / 6.6 g | 132 mm / 42.8 g |
| Manuring | Tilapia | 76 mm / 7.9 g | 162 mm / 74.1 g |
| Manuring | Bluegill | 76 mm / 7.2 g | 129 mm / 37.5 g |

- **Growth efficiency ratios (Abstract):** Tilapia showed 2.97 times greater growth than bluegill in feeding experiments, 1.69 times in fertilization, and 1.97 times in manuring.
- **Feeding behavior:** Tilapia utilized all food placed in feeding bowls, while periodic food accumulations occurred in bluegill bowls.
- **Seasonal note:** Observations at the Station indicated that bluegills grow most rapidly in spring, and that food consumed in late spring, summer, and early fall is transformed into sexual products. The author noted this period was largely outside bluegill's best growth season.

**Cold Tolerance Results**
- **Trial No. 1 (tilapia only, three weeks):** Tilapia measured 26 to 178 mm in total length. The first deaths occurred in small and intermediate groups at 52.5°F. Mortality rose until 100 per cent occurred at 47°F. Large fish showed loss of equilibrium, rolling and remaining inverted, with death in 24 to 48 hours. Feeding stopped at temperatures slightly above 60°F for large and intermediate fish, and at about 60°F or slightly less for small fish. Reduced movement appeared at 57°F.
- **Trial No. 2 (about seven weeks):** The temperature drop averaged 5.5°F per week for the first two weeks, then about 2°F per week. Distress seen in Trial 1 was not observed. One tilapia died unexplained early in the trial. The 100 per cent mortality of tilapia was corroborated in the field: when water temperatures dropped to 46°F, 100 per cent mortality occurred among tilapia in concrete and earthen ponds.
- **Summary of thresholds (Abstract):** Feeding ceased at approximately 60°F, deaths began at 52 to 58°F, and 100 per cent mortality occurred at 47 to 49°F.

**Food Habit Findings**
- **Diet:** Tilapia are primarily plankton feeders. Animal remains were in the minority. Dominant items were plankton algae, with fragments of filamentous algae also present. Debris and some animal fragments suggested bottom feeding, while plankton algae suggested feeding on suspended forms.
- **Gut specimens:** 18 fish were examined, ranging from 113 to 169 mm in length, with an average weight of 47.7 grams.
- **Phytoplankton abundance:**
  - Scenedesmus: abundant
  - Closterium and Staurastrum: common
  - Ankistrodesmus, Cosmarium, Volvocaceae (?), Micrasterias, Pediastrum, Tetradesmus (?): rare
  - Diatoms of many species: present in all specimens and extremely abundant
  - Unidentifiable phytoplankton: abundant in two specimens
- **Animal life:** Remnants of small Crustacea, bryozoan statoblasts, one small fish, insect larvae, and oligochetes. Debris composed much of the visible bulk.
- **Aquarium behavior:** Tilapia picked food from the bottom, cleaned sides and vegetation, suspended in water to ingest floating material, and strained surface food through the mouth. They ate larvae of Chaoborus and Chironomus and the branched alga Pithophora. Feeding was especially pronounced when zooplankton was introduced.

**Principal Conclusion**
- The author concluded that tilapia are more efficient than bluegill in growth under feeding, fertilization, and manuring, but that the species "cannot survive the winter conditions in most of the Southeast."
