# Seasonal Variations in the Hormone Content of the Pituitary Gland of the Perch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endocrinology #Percidae #Fundulidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by Swift and Pickford quantified seasonal fluctuations in pituitary hormone content of the [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*) using a sensitive *in vivo* bioassay in hypophysectomized [[Mummichog]] (*Fundulus heteroclitus*). Lyophilized perch pituitary extracts, collected at seven intervals across a full year from [[Lake Windermere]], were injected into hormone-deficient assay fish to measure growth-promoting, gonadotrophic, thyrotrophic, corticotrophic, and melanophore-stimulating activities. The central finding is that **growth hormone (GH) content in the perch pituitary undergoes a dramatic ~70-fold seasonal swing**, peaking in June (equivalent to 250 µg standard beef GH) and crashing to near-exhaustion in August (equivalent to only 3.5 µg). Critically, the peak GH accumulation in the gland **preceded** the perch's natural somatic growth peak by 4–6 weeks, whereas peak **gonadotrophin** content coincided precisely with the natural reproductive peak in April, with no lag. This work provides an early, detailed picture of coordinated but temporally distinct adenohypophysial (pituitary) hormone cycles tied to seasonal environmental and reproductive rhythms in a temperate teleost.

## Background & Description

- **Study species (hormone source):** [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*), a freshwater percid, sampled via gill-netting from [[Lake Windermere]] (Freshwater Biological Association, England) at seven timepoints (April 1961–February 1962: Feb, April, June, July, Aug, Sept, Nov).
- **Bioassay species:** [[Mummichog]] (*Fundulus heteroclitus*), a euryhaline estuarine fish, collected near New Haven, Connecticut, and maintained at the Bingham Oceanographic Laboratory (Yale University).
- **Rationale for species choice:** Perch were chosen because large samples were reliably available year-round from Windermere and because prior long-term ecological data on perch growth (Le Cren, 1951, 1958) and gonad cycles existed for comparison.
- **Core method:** Male *Fundulus* were [[Hypophysectomy|hypophysectomized]] (pituitary surgically removed) to eliminate endogenous pituitary hormone, then received intraperitoneal injections of lyophilized perch pituitary brei (100 µg/g body weight, thrice weekly for 6 weeks). Controls received either 0.6% saline or standard beef growth hormone (NIH-GH-B1, 20 µg/g).
- **Endpoints measured:** standard length, body weight, scale-width increments, gonadosomatic index, nuptial coloration, thyroid follicular cell height, interrenal (adrenocortical) cytology, hepatosomatic index, hematology, melanophore/melanocyte responses, and spawning reflex response.
- This is a classic **cross-species pituitary bioassay** design, exploiting the extreme hormone sensitivity and total endogenous depletion of hypophysectomized *Fundulus* to quantify heterologous (perch-derived) hormone potency.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Environmental driver:** The study was motivated by earlier work (Swift, 1961) showing that seasonal water temperature directly drives growth rate in [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), prompting investigation of whether pituitary hormone dynamics mediate this temperature-growth linkage in fish generally.
- **Windermere thermal regime:** Perch growth rate was indexed using "degree days" above 14°C (per Le Cren, 1958), which tracks the natural seasonal growth window of the lake population.
- **Temporal relationships uncovered:**
  - Peak pituitary **GH** content (June) preceded the natural somatic growth peak (~July) by 4–6 weeks — implying a lag between hormone accumulation and physiological growth response, possibly due to delayed hormone release or delayed tissue responsiveness.
  - Peak pituitary **gonadotrophin** content (April) coincided exactly with the natural spawning/gonad-maturation peak — no lag observed.
  - **Thyrotrophic** and **corticotrophic** activities followed a similar but lower-magnitude seasonal cycle, peaking in spring and depleted by July.
- **Proposed mechanism:** Rising day length in spring may trigger hormone synthesis before water temperature rises enough to permit full tissue-level responsiveness, suggesting two separable environmental thresholds (photoperiod for synthesis, temperature for target-tissue response).
- **Ecological interpretation:** The perch pituitary appears adapted to a **short, intense growing season**, rapidly building then fully depleting GH reserves — contrasted with the hypothesis that tropical fish with prolonged annual growth periods might show a flatter, less pulsed hormone cycle.
- **Comparative note:** Findings on *Fundulus* interrenal and thyroid tissue responsiveness draw on established sensitivity profiles from [[Pickford And Atz 1957|Pickford & Atz (1957)]], the foundational reference work on fish pituitary physiology.

## Key Findings

### Growth Hormone (Somatotropin) Cycle
- Growth-promoting potency of perch pituitary equivalent to **20–50 µg** standard beef GH (GH-B1) during autumn/winter ("resting level").
- Significant rise beginning **April**, peaking in **June** at potency equivalent to **250 µg GH-B1** (~10-fold increase over resting level).
- Sharp decline in **July**, reaching near-total exhaustion by **August** (equivalent to only **3.5 µg** GH-B1 — ~10-fold below resting level).
- Growth response was already statistically significant after just 2 weeks of injection (2.29% length increase for June sample vs. 1.70% for beef GH control).
- A **log dose-response line** was established using the beef GH control group, allowing perch pituitary potency to be expressed in standard GH-B1 equivalents.

### Scale Growth Correlate
- Scale-width increments closely paralleled body length increments.
- A minimum ~3% increase in body length was required before any measurable increase in scale width occurred; beyond this threshold, scale growth was proportional to somatic growth — a useful non-lethal proxy for growth rate validation.

### Gonadotrophin Cycle
- Gonadosomatic index (GSI) of assay fish peaked with the **April** perch pituitary sample, matching the natural Windermere perch spawning peak (late April; per [[Le Cren 1951]]).
- June sample nearly as effective as April; **July and August samples showed essential exhaustion** of gonadotrophin (not significantly different from saline controls).
- Significant recovery of gonadotrophin content observed by **September and November**, tracking the autumn/winter onset of gonad maturation.
- **No time lag** between peak pituitary gonadotrophin and peak natural reproductive activity (contrasts with the GH lag).

### Nuptial Coloration
- Closely tracked gonadal development; served as a secondary bioindicator of gonadotrophin activity.
- February sample gave erratic results due to a single aberrant fish with high GSI but low coloration score.

### Thyroid Activity
- Thyrotrophic activity followed a similar, but lower-magnitude, seasonal pattern to GH and gonadotrophin.
- Only the **April** sample produced statistically significant thyroid stimulation (cell height); June and September were significant only at the 10% level.
- **August sample was the only one definitively inactive** thyrotrophically.

### Interrenal (Adrenocortical) Tissue
- Perch pituitary preparations maintained interrenal cell layers near-normal across most seasons, with clearer stimulation from **February and April** samples.
- A minimum in stimulatory activity occurred in **July**, consistent with the general summer depletion pattern seen across hormone axes.

### Hematology
- Red blood cell count peaked in fish receiving **April** perch pituitary.
- White blood cell count was elevated in fall/winter samples and dropped to a minimum with **April** samples.

### Melanophore/Melanocyte & Neurohypophysial Activity
- All perch pituitary samples caused transient pallor (attributed to teleost **melanophore-concentrating hormone**, MCH).
- Dorsal darkening (prolactin-like activity indicator) and new melanocyte proliferation (attributed to **intermedin**/MSH) occurred in the majority of fish regardless of season; **only the August sample was deficient** in these effects.
- A prolactin-like hormone test (hypophysectomized red eft assay) gave negative results despite positive dorsal darkening responses, suggesting the darkening response may not be strictly prolactin-mediated in this system.
- All samples elicited the **spawning reflex response**, containing at minimum 1 Fundulus unit (~25 mU arginine vasotocin equivalent) per 100 µg, with no strong seasonal pattern detected in this neurohypophysial activity.

### Overall Pattern
- **Multiple pituitary hormone axes (GH, gonadotrophin, thyrotrophin, corticotrophin) show coordinated, synchronous seasonal cycling**, all peaking in spring/early summer and reaching minima in July–August, followed by gradual winter recovery.
- The GH cycle uniquely shows a **4–6 week lag** between peak gland content and peak natural growth, unlike the gonadotrophin axis, suggesting different release/response dynamics for somatotropic vs. gonadotropic regulation — potentially linked to a two-stage environmental trigger system (photoperiod for synthesis, temperature for tissue responsiveness).
