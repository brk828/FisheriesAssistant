# The Role of Hatcheries in the Management and Recovery of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-southwestern-fishes

## Executive Summary

This chapter, published in the proceedings of a 1985 American Fisheries Society symposium on fish culture in fisheries management, examines how artificial propagation in [[Hatchery]] facilities can help recover threatened and endangered fishes, mainly in the American Southwest. The authors, all fishery professionals with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), the U.S. Forest Service, and tribal and state agencies, describe the multi-species [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, a single-species cui-ui hatchery at Pyramid Lake, and an Apache trout program in Arizona. They conclude that hatchery propagation is politically and operationally feasible and can serve as a refugium, a research platform, and a source of fish for [[Re-Introduction]], but that its value depends on species-specific planning, careful documentation, genetic management, and the availability of suitable wild habitat.

## Background & Description

The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973 expanded federal involvement in protecting listed species. Its provisions covered habitat protection, prevention of federal actions that jeopardize listed species, prohibition of taking, and recovery through direct federal action and grants to states. Before the ESA, the Endangered Species Preservation Act (1966) and the Endangered Species Conservation Act (1969) had begun directing the Department of Interior to identify species facing extinction.

Southwestern fish faunas are heavily imperiled. The authors note that 81% of native Arizona fish species are classified or proposed as threatened or endangered, as are 42% of New Mexico's species, and that more than 35 southwestern species may need some form of artificial propagation to survive. More than 20 recovery plans for southwestern fishes had been completed by the time of writing, and nearly all included three goals: establishing a refuge for genetic stock, researching species-specific needs, and re-introducing locally extirpated populations.

Dexter National Fish Hatchery was built by the FWS in the mid-1930s as a warm-water facility producing [[Largemouth Bass]] and channel catfish for New Mexico. After about 40 years, federal warm-water rearing was centralized in Texas, and the state of New Mexico declined the facility. It was then considered for endangered-fish work and began receiving native southwestern species in November 1974, after a 1974 survey found overwhelming institutional support.

Site-selection criteria for an endangered-fish facility included the standard hatchery needs (water quality and quantity, space, access, security, and climate) plus two unique requirements: isolation from natural surface waters, to prevent escape of fish, parasites, or disease, and isolation of culture ponds from one another, because many species are closely related and could hybridize, making parental and hybrid phenotypes hard to tell apart.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native southwestern fishes have declined largely because of human activity. The authors attribute the current status of these fishes to habitat alteration, including dam construction, groundwater pumping, grazing, and introduction of non-native species, all of which have degraded or fragmented habitat.

Re-introduction proved politically contentious. Landowners and resource users worried that listing would curtail fishing, boating, minnow seining, swimming, grazing, irrigation, and energy development. As a result, none of the fishes held at Dexter had been re-introduced for several years, even though captive numbers far exceeded wild numbers for some species.

The Pyramid Lake system illustrates habitat-driven decline. [[Pyramid Lake]] in Nevada is a nutrient-rich, endorheic lake, 25 miles long, with a maximum depth of 339 ft and a mean depth of 164 ft. Diversions of its primary water source, the [[Truckee River]], began in 1905 for the Newland Reclamation Project. These diversions lowered the lake by about 75 feet, produced a delta that blocked spawning runs, and by 1938 led to the presumed extinction of the Pyramid Lake cutthroat trout. The [[Cui-ui]], an endemic lacustrine sucker, survived in reduced numbers, partly because of its longevity.

Environmental conditions at Dexter were carefully managed. The site sits at about 3,500 ft elevation in the Pecos River valley, uses well water, and has a constant temperature of about 64 °F. The facility includes 350-gallon tanks, a hatching battery, and earthen ponds. Water is drawn from a region that is slightly alkaline, and the water chemistry was noted as a factor in the facility's suitability.

## Key Findings

**Program at Dexter National Fish Hatchery**
- Thirteen native species, plus four additional *Gila* species, were held at Dexter as of July 1, 1982 (Table 1 lists the species, their federal status, and whether they were endangered, threatened, proposed, or candidate).
- Dexter's objectives were to maintain a viable captive gene pool, develop culture techniques, study biology, provide live fish and eggs to cooperators, exchange expertise, publish findings, and run a public information program.
- Dexter was the first multi-species endangered-fish facility of its kind in the United States. Its operating procedures emphasized isolation, separate ponds for species at risk of hybridization, disinfection of equipment, taxonomic verification of incoming fish, and parasite screening.
- Pupfish populations were stable, but live-bearers (Big Bend gambusia and Gila topminnow) experienced extreme annual fluctuations, with losses of up to 90% during winter.

**Re-introduction Milestones**
- The first re-introduction breakthrough occurred in 1980, when 35,000 Yaqui chub were stocked on the San Bernardino Ranch in southeastern Arizona.
- In 1981 the Arizona Game and Fish Department signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with the FWS to re-introduce razorback suckers into the Gila River and its tributaries in lieu of listing. The FWS agreed not to propose listing until restocking success could be judged, estimated conservatively at 10 years. By 1985, more than 6 million fry and 296,000 fingerling razorback suckers had been stocked in Arizona.
- In June 1982, more than 70 isolated aquatic habitats in Arizona were stocked with Gila topminnows under a Forest Service MOU. The authors estimated that the Gila topminnow effort may have been the most intensive recovery action attempted for an endangered species, with nearly 100 separate re-introductions completed by the time of writing.
- Between 1981 and 1984, Dexter personnel distributed 6,108,965 razorback sucker fry, 296,376 razorback fingerlings, and 116,638 Colorado squawfish fingerlings in Arizona and Colorado.

**Razorback Sucker**
- Aging from otoliths of Lake Mohave fish collected in 1980–81 gave ages of 24 to 43 years (1937 to 1957 year-classes). No documented recruitment has occurred since the mid-1950s.
- Hatchery-reared fish reached sexual maturity at six years and 14 to 16 inches total length. Female spawning is more closely related to size than age.
- Females spawned at 55 °F and were induced with chorionic gonadotropin (CG) at 100 IU per pound of body weight. Eggs hatched in about 96 to 144 hours at 70 °F, with peak hatching at 120 hours.
- In 1984, 70 three-year-old domestic brood fish averaged 2.8 pounds, with mean fecundity of 63,674 and egg viability of 61.1%. Twenty-five wild-caught fish averaged 7.76 pounds, with mean fecundity of 123,110 and egg viability of 28.8%.

**Colorado Squawfish**
- Historically the largest North American cyprinid, reaching up to six feet and nearly 100 pounds. Hatchery fish grow slowly: nine-year-old fish averaged 20 inches and 3 pounds in 1983.
- Females spawned in late May or early June at about 70 °F, within a one- to two-week window, and were induced with carp pituitary (2 mg per pound).
- In 1984, nine domestic females averaging 3.2 pounds produced 20,656 eggs per pound of body weight.

**Bonytail**
- Only 24 fish were collected in 11 years of extensive effort, and all were dead at the time of writing. Lake Mohave specimens were aged at 40 to 42 years.
- In May 1983, 24 females were spawned. Mean fecundity was 4,677, mean eggs per pound was 17,280, and egg viability averaged 67.5%.

**Woundfin**
- In 1984, 20 females were injected with carp pituitary, and 12 (60%) spawned successfully. Total fecundity was 2,067, and 1,163 swim-up fry were recovered. Natural spawning on a prepared gravel bed gave the greatest success.

**Chihuahua Chub and Other Chubs**
- The Chihuahua chub was thought to have been extinct for nearly 40 years until it was rediscovered. Only 10 fish could be captured after 1978 flooding, and these were brought to Dexter, where the stock has grown.
- Hybridization between bonytail, humpback chub, and roundtail chub in the upper Colorado River basin is a concern, and Dexter maintained these species for hybridization studies.

**Cui-ui Hatchery, Pyramid Lake**
- The tribal hatchery began operation in 1973 and was moved to a closed-system facility with biological filtration and ultraviolet sterilization by 1979.
- In 1982, 17.7 million eggs were taken and 10.9 million fry (61.6%) survived to swim-up.
- Fish were held at about 54.7 °F, which slowed Saprolegnia fungus. A back-up diesel generator protected the hatchery from frequent power outages.
- Gill-netting data show that catch-per-unit-effort was reduced by half between 1954–1958 and 1975–1977, reinforcing concern about population decline.
- Priorities for future work include rearing large numbers to fingerling size, evaluating survival of planted fish, and monitoring age composition.

**Apache Trout**
- Eighty-two Apache trout were collected from Ord Creek in 1963. In 1975 the species was down-listed to threatened, and 118 individuals were collected from Soldier Creek to found the present brood stock.
- Over 18 years of culture data, 69.7% of eggs failed to hatch, and only 38.7% of hatched fish survived for management or brood-stock use. Females (two to five years old) produced a mean of about 274 eggs. Fry were reluctant to accept artificial diets and were highly territorial, causing high mortality in intensive culture.
- Inbreeding risk was high because the initial stock came from a single headwater population, and the hatchery had only two raceways available for this species.

**Synthesis and Recommendations**
- Species differ in propagation ease. Razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish, Gila topminnow, and cui-ui are relatively easy to propagate, whereas woundfin and Apache trout have been difficult.
- Hatchery propagation is essential for species such as the cui-ui, bonytail, and Chihuahua chub, which survive only in a few locations. Other species, including the Gila trout, are better restored by wild-to-wild transfer.
- Genetic management is the most overlooked aspect of rare-species propagation. The authors recommend *Genetics and Conservation* (Schonewald-Cox et al. 1983) as essential reading.
- Hatcheries are only as useful as the wild habitats available for re-introduction. Experimental population rules and ecosystem-level management are key to success.
- Careful documentation of every step is needed so that other practitioners can build on successes and avoid repeating failures.
