# Summary Report: Fishery Management on the San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, 1965

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1965 annual summary, issued by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (Phoenix, Arizona) and dated January 29, 1966, reviews fishery management on the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe|San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]]. Management was carried out under a cooperative agreement among the San Carlos Apache Tribe, the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]], and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, which provided technical assistance and fish for stocking. The program covered 110 stock ponds, three streams, and one lake. Total fishing pressure rose from 12,670 man-days in 1964 to 13,153 man-days in 1965, even though fishing at [[Seneca Lake]] declined. The report also documents a state-record [[Bluegill]], a failed [[Tilapia Mossambica]] population, and summer water-quality conditions that made [[Seneca Lake]] unsuitable for [[Rainbow Trout]] during the warm season.

## Background & Description

The Reservation covers 1,643,939 acres. Nearly all of its waters are small impoundments built for livestock. The Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife stocked many of these [[Stock Tanks]], and the San Carlos Apache Tribe opened them to anglers on a permit basis. Because the tanks are small and sit on intermittent drainages, rainfall controls their water levels, and therefore controls the fishery.

Management divided the tanks into two groups:

- **Trout tanks:** Increased from 34 to 55 in 1965. Most newly added tanks were either newly surveyed or switched over from warm-water species. Stocking used mainly [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings.
- **Warm-water tanks:** Received less attention because little angling occurs there. Fish stocked here were mostly [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Bluegill]].

The report summarizes the two tank lists in tables (Table 1 for trout tanks, Table 2 for warm-water tanks). Table 1 lists about 60 trout tanks, and Table 2 lists about 70 warm-water tanks. Each entry gives surface area, the fish species present, and the number and kind of fish planted. The tables themselves are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rainfall and water levels.** Heavy rain in January 1965 filled most reservoirs, and this supply carried fish through the dry summer. Turbid run-off in early 1965 delayed fishing until late spring. Cool spring weather further suppressed angler interest. In December 1965, unusually heavy rain and snowmelt washed out several tanks, including Freezeout, Cienega, Geronimo, Deadman, and Turkey Roost. Four other tanks (Armstrong, Bone, Big Bonito, and Skeleton) were dropped from trout management because of competition from other fish, trout mortality, or inadequate water.

**Predator stocking.** The management strategy relies on predatory fish that cannot reproduce in the tanks, such as [[Channel Catfish]]. Predators reduce the existing stunted population, so the remaining fish have more food and grow larger. The approach works only if the predators do not reproduce and add to overpopulation.

**Stream conditions.** The Black, Salt, and San Carlos Rivers lie in rough terrain and are mostly inaccessible to anglers. [[Tilapia Mossambica]] stocked at Warm Springs on the San Carlos River in 1964 had built nests and appeared to be reproducing. Cold run-off from winter flooding in early 1965 killed them. The report describes the species as temperature-sensitive.

**Seneca Lake thermal and oxygen conditions.** [[Seneca Lake]] was constructed in 1963 and spilled for the first time in January 1965. It covers 27.5 surface acres and is run as a [[Put-And-Take Fishing]] trout fishery, with a small tribal concession. On July 21, 1965, the following temperatures were recorded:

| Depth | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Surface | 81°F |
| 9 ft | 74°F |
| 10 ft | 71°F |
| 12 ft | 67°F |
| 19 ft | 56°F |

Surface pH had risen to 9.5, and fishing was poor. On September 15, 1965, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was too low below eight feet to support trout. The report concludes that the surface was too warm and the depths too low in oxygen for [[Rainbow Trout]] to survive the summer. Trout losses were judged modest because catchable plants stopped after June 11, 1965. [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Golden Shiner]] survived in the warmer, oxygenated upper layers.

## Key Findings

**Fishing pressure and use**
- Total fishing pressure rose from 12,670 man-days (1964) to 13,153 man-days (1965), an increase of about 3.8%.
- Fishing at [[Seneca Lake]] fell from a figure the OCR renders as "T,oT?" man-days in 1964 to an estimated 6,000 man-days in 1965, while stock-tank fishing increased.

**Stocking totals (1965)**
- Trout tanks: 21,100 [[Rainbow Trout]] (mostly large fingerlings) and 400 [[Brook Trout]] fingerlings at Windy Bob Tank, an experiment. Seneca Lake received 14,500 catchable-size rainbow trout.
- Channel catfish: the trout-tank total is printed as "32?5" in the scanned text and is unclear. The value is not reproduced here.
- Warm-water tanks: 600 [[Channel Catfish]], 506 [[Black Crappie]], 1,740 [[Largemouth Bass]], and 1,000 rainbow trout.
- Streams: 964 largemouth bass in the San Carlos River and 7,000 [[Smallmouth Bass]] in the Black River.

**Notable catch**
- The Arizona state record [[Bluegill]] was taken from [[Soldiers Hole Tank]] by a rainbow-trout angler. It measured 14 1/8 inches long, 7 3/8 inches deep, and 15 3/4 inches in girth, and weighed 3 lb 5 oz. The report states it was about twice the weight of the previous state record. Other bluegills from the same tank also exceeded the old record.
- [[Channel Catfish]] at [[Seneca Lake]] averaged 9.78 inches by September 1965.

**Species interactions**
- [[Big Boni Tank]] was moved to the warm-water list late in the year after its largemouth bass spawned successfully. Young bass competed with rainbow trout for food.
- [[Golden Shiner]] populations in [[Seneca Lake]] were so large that they competed with trout for food. The report recommends initiating control measures.

**Management conclusions**
- Stock ponds are too small and too scattered to support a large recreational fishery.
- Significant growth in fishing pressure will require tribal construction of lakes designed for fishing, or making the [[Black River]] accessible to anglers.
- Campgrounds at [[Seneca Lake]] are needed to support the tribal concession there.
