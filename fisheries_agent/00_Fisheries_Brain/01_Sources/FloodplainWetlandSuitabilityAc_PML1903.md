# Floodplain Wetland Suitability, Access, and Use by Juvenile Razorback Suckers in the Middle Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Modde et al., 2001), evaluates whether a manipulated floodplain depression, [[Old Charley Wash]] on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], provides suitable nursery habitat for larval and juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. Data were collected in 1995 and 1996, high-water years. The wetland offered warmer temperatures, adequate dissolved oxygen, and dense zooplankton, while main-channel habitats did not. Larval access to floodplains, however, depended on river discharge and flow duration, which were reduced by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] was once common throughout much of the lower and upper Colorado River basins but has declined substantially. It is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1991). Adults are found mainly in low-gradient river reaches, while juveniles and subadults use off-channel floodplain depressions. Recruitment failure has been attributed to altered flow regimes, loss of floodplain inundation, and predation or competition by [[Nonnative Fishes]].

Little was known about the early life history needs of this species, and few links between those needs and floodplain habitat had been established before this study. The stated objectives were to (1) measure environmental variables (dissolved oxygen, temperature, depth, zooplankton density) in the study wetland, (2) determine larval access to floodplains by comparing larval drift timing with average bankfull flows, and (3) census the fishes occupying the wetland to determine whether razorback suckers used the site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Larval collections were made in the middle Green River between river kilometers 482 and 395. Old Charley Wash (also called Wood's Bottom) is a 147-ha floodplain depression. Only the 80-ha main unit was studied. Historically, water entered by gravity through an inlet canal when discharge reached 240 m³/s, and by flood flows over the natural levee at about 396 m³/s. The inlet was closed during this study, so all water arrived by overbank flooding. Dominant vegetation included smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), with bulrushes (*Scirpus* sp.) and cattails (*Typha* sp.) at the margins.

**Methods.** Maximum depth, subsurface temperature, dissolved oxygen, and zooplankton density were measured in spring and summer 1995 and 1996. Zooplankton were sampled with a 243-µm mesh net, and densities were calculated from the counted subsamples and the volume of water sampled. Larvae were collected with floating quarrefoil light traps in both main-channel and floodplain habitats, sampling from [[Spring Larval Drift]] until captures ceased. Fish from the wetland draining were identified, measured, and weighed.

**Hydrology.** Regulated flows below Flaming Gorge Dam (closed 1963) have sharply reduced both the magnitude and duration of floodplain inundation. The bankfull flow at Ouray is 575 m³/s.

## Key Findings

- **Physical habitat:** Wetland surface temperatures reached 26.2 °C (15 July 1995) and 26.2 °C maximum surface temperature in 1996 (as reported). Water was warmer in the wetland than the main channel throughout spring and summer. Dissolved oxygen was not below 4.0 mg/L in either year, and was rarely below 5.0 mg/L. Wetland depth declined to about 1 m by midsummer.
- **Food resources:** Peak zooplankton densities were 54.3 (1995) and 42.8 (1996), consisting mainly of cladocerans and copepods. Density was about 31 to 690 times higher in Old Charley Wash than in riverine backwaters reported by Mabey (1993).
- **Thermal optimum:** Temperatures were at or just above the temperature preferendum for hatchery-reared razorback suckers (22.9–24.8 °C), exceeding 25 °C in August 1995 and July 1996.
- **Larval access:** Larvae were collected in the river during 1993–1996, but the river did not connect to floodplains for long enough in most years. Access to floodplain wetlands was greatest in 1993 and 1996 (Table 1 of source). Larvae reached floodplains in 3 of 4 years, but access depended on the timing and duration of high flows, which were reduced by regulation.
- **Fish community:** Nonnative species dominated the wetland fish community, making up 91.2% of fish collected. Common carp and fathead minnow were the most abundant. Other common species included green sunfish, channel catfish, and red shiner.
- **Razorback sucker catches:** In 1995, 28 age-0 razorback suckers were collected (mean 94 mm total length, range 24–185 mm; mean 9.5 g, range 3–19 g). In 1996, 45 were collected (mean 65.9 mm, range 44–93 mm; mean 3.8 g, range 1–6 g). Two adults were collected in 1995 and none in 1996. Age-0 razorback suckers were the most abundant native fish in the wetland in both years.
- **Growth and survival:** Age-0 razorback suckers grew and survived despite the presence of nonnative predators and competitors. Survival was likely reduced by nonnative species such as black bullhead and green sunfish, but the study wetland was dry before spring flood flows, so residual impacts could not be assessed.
- **Conclusions:** Off-channel floodplain depressions appear to be important nursery habitats for larval and juvenile razorback suckers. Maintaining connectivity between floodplains and the river, through levee removal and adequate flood duration, is needed to allow larval access. Recovery depends on identifying and removing impediments to recruitment, and on sustaining the flows that allow larvae to reach these habitats.
