# Reversal of Swimming Direction by Rainbow Trout on Stopping a Current of Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This short laboratory study by D. W. M. Herbert (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, Stevenage) reports an unexpected behaviour in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*). Fish held in a circular current for months swam circuits of the tank in the **opposite** direction once the current was stopped. The reversal depended on vision: it did not occur in total darkness, and it was abolished by a few minutes of darkness after it had been established. [[Brown Trout]] parr from an upland beck did not show the response. The author describes the work as preliminary, because the subject could not be studied further at the Laboratory.

## Background & Description

The observations arose during an investigation of the survival of rainbow trout in suspensions of solid matter. Stirred aquaria were needed for that work, and the behaviour was noticed when the water's circular movement was stopped.

**Experimental apparatus**
- Square glass-walled [[Stirred Aquarium]], internal dimensions approximately 3 × 3 × 2 feet, holding 350 litres.
- A paddle revolved near the floor behind a false floor of plastic-covered expanded metal, producing a mainly circular ("[[Circular Current]]") motion.
- Current velocities, measured at 12 positions, ranged from 9.7 to 32.5 cm/s, with an average of 22.3 cm/s. The current was always anti-clockwise.

**Fish groups**
- **Control batch:** 25 yearling rainbow trout of an autumn-spawning strain, kept in the continuously stirred tank.
- **Second batch:** 20 one-year-old hatchery-reared rainbow trout parr, placed in the anti-clockwise current from 22 January 1962.
- **Brown trout:** parr, 11–15 cm long and 2–3 years old, from a fast-flowing beck with no connection to standing water before the sea. They were placed in the current about five days after capture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

In the wild, fish use compensatory movements to avoid being displaced by currents. The paper discusses two mechanisms:

- The [[Pseudo-rheotropic Response]], controlled by the [[Semicircular Canals]] of the labyrinth, which compensates for rotatory but not rectilinear displacement (Gray, 1937).
- The [[Optomotor Response]], which keeps objects at a constant position in the visual field whether the apparent motion is curved or straight (Lyon, 1905, 1907; Healey, 1957).

The author argues that the reversal probably depends on cessation of optomotor stimulation. Trout do not reverse in total darkness, which suggests that loss of semicircular canal stimulation is not the trigger. The brown trout result suggests that holding station against a largely rectilinear current in the wild does not by itself produce the reversing behaviour.

Development of the reversal in the second batch coincided with physiological changes. Loss of [[Parr Marks]] and the assumption of adult colouration were observed, along with [[Sexual Maturation]] of the gonads.

## Key Findings

**1. Reversal after current cessation (control batch)**
- After several months against the current, fish swam against it to hold position. When the paddles stopped, nearly all fish swam circuits in the opposite direction.
- In one series, all fish were swimming contralaterally within 14 minutes.
- A net anti-clockwise movement persisted for 5½ hours after the paddles stopped. Movement appeared random after about 7¼ hours.
- The increasing time per circuit was attributed both to slower swimming and to a growing tendency for individual fish to change direction temporarily.

**2. Developmental timing (second batch)**
- No reversal was detectable until the end of April 1962. The behaviour was well established by 5 June 1962.
- Onset coincided approximately with the loss of parr marks and the assumption of adult colouration.
- When killed on 18 July 1962, about one-third of females and one-third of males had maturing gonads. The female with the most developed gonads was usually the first to swim circuits when the current stopped.

**3. Brown trout comparison**
- Brown trout parr showed no tendency to reverse when the paddles were stopped at intervals over the following seven days.

**4. Direction fixed by experience**
- The reversal is unlikely to be a simple response to months of swimming in one direction. Seven weeks after the reflex developed in the second batch, the current was reversed. The fish held position against it normally and, after about two days, reversed direction again when the paddles stopped.
- After eight days in the reversed current, the original anti-clockwise current was restored. For a few hours the fish swam randomly. About three weeks later, they again swam in the direction they had held against the current, and this was repeated in later trials.
- The author concludes that early in development the fish take the opposite direction to the one they swam to hold station. Later, the direction becomes fixed and no longer depends on the current's sign.

**5. Role of vision**
- Under darkness, fish still held position head-on to the current, whether or not they could see.
- Reversal did not occur when the current stopped in total darkness. Light admitted a few seconds after the paddles stopped did not trigger circuits either.
- Abolition: if the tank was completely darkened for 5 minutes during circuit swimming, the reaction stopped and did not resume when light returned. Brief observer movement interrupted but did not abolish the reaction.
- Ability to see surroundings when the current stops is an essential component of the stimulus.

**6. Time-dependent visual appreciation**
- If the tank was darkened for 30 minutes, with light restored 15 minutes before the paddles stopped, the fish did not reverse.
- Reversal could be elicited again after the fish had swum against the current in light for between 1 hour 40 minutes and 3 hours 5 minutes. The author suggests that visual appreciation of surroundings is lost during darkness and must be regained over a few hours.
- Casual observations suggested faster reversal when more familiar landmarks were visible.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Observations were preliminary and not a full investigation.
- Sample sizes were small (25 and 20 fish), and some trial conditions are described only qualitatively.
- The author notes that the work could not be continued at the Laboratory.
