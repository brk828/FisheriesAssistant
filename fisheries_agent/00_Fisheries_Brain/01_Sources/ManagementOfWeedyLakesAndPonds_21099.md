# Grass Carp as a Biological Control Agent for Aquatic Vegetation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review, published in *Fisheries* (Vol. 2, No. 5, September–October 1977) by [[Roger Rottmann]], evaluates the use of [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) for biological control of excess aquatic vegetation in ponds and lakes. The paper finds broad agreement that grass carp effectively control macrophytes, and that they convert vegetation to fish flesh efficiently. Their wider use in the United States has been delayed by limited knowledge of their food habits and effects on water quality and native fish. The author concludes that grass carp can be a low-cost, effective tool when stocked at appropriate densities, provided that the goal is an optimum quantity and diversity of plants rather than total eradication, and that stocking should be controlled by permit in public and private waters.

The document also includes a segment of the American Fisheries Society (AFS) editorial and policy report, listing the Society's recent positions on aquatic resource issues.

## Background & Description

Excess aquatic vegetation can alter productivity, reduce oxygen stability, hinder fishing, boating, and swimming, and complicate fish culture. The author lists four management alternatives: mechanical, chemical, habitat manipulative, and biological control. Combinations of methods may be most appropriate.

The grass carp, also called the white amur, is among the largest members of the [[Minnow Family]] (Cyprinidae), reaching about 50 kg. It requires flowing water to spawn and does not reproduce in lakes and ponds. In Asian and European countries it is used in polyculture systems.

Key biological traits reported:
- **Early diet:** Fry under about 30 mm total length feed mainly on phytoplankton and invertebrates. Young fish may need animal protein until about 150 mm.
- **Adult diet:** Larger fish are herbivorous and prefer submerged and some floating plants, typically young and succulent ones. [[Hydrilla]] is described as an excellent food, attributed to its soft texture and high ash content.
- **Feeding selectivity:** Selectivity declines with size and rises or falls with temperature, age, and feeding history.
- **Growth:** Growth is rapid. Small fish doubled in weight about five times in three months (2.7 to 72.7 g in 88 days). In weedy ponds, fish grew from 0.6 kg (May 1973) to 3.8 kg (October 1973).
- **Longevity and maturity:** Grass carp mature in 5 to 9 years, according to Cross (1969).
- **Hardiness:** They tolerate water temperatures from 1 to over 32 °C and dissolved oxygen as low as 0.5 ppm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food web and detritus.** Submerged plants support fish and invertebrates and contribute to detritus pools. Emergent plants stabilize banks, support spawning and cover, and provide wildlife habitat. Grass carp consume little benthic invertebrate material. In one 3.6 ha lake, animal remains made up no more than 0.2% of stomach contents. Terrell and Fox (1975) found that fish did not become piscivorous when vegetation was absent.

**Consumption and conversion.** Under favorable conditions, grass carp can eat more than their body weight in plants per day. Duckweed-fed fish consumed about 150% of body weight daily. Feed conversion ratios for duckweed-fed fish ranged from 1.72 to 1.97. Digestibility of duckweed was reported at 67% (Van Dyke 1973). Grass carp cannot digest cellulose, so high-fiber vegetation is used less efficiently.

**Nutrients and water quality.** Rooted plants can absorb nutrients from mud, and much of the phosphorus is locked into sediments. Grass carp feces sink, so phosphorus is not released directly to the water column. Chemical control, by contrast, caused a 20-fold increase in total phosphorus and a 30-fold increase in available phosphorus eight days after treatment (Daniel 1972). Dense vegetation can raise pH to near-toxic levels and deplete night-time dissolved oxygen. Grass carp control lowered pH and increased total alkalinity, and feeding opened channels that improved mixing.

**Predation.** Small grass carp are vulnerable to predators. In one trial, 5,600 fry (48.2 mm) stocked into each of two 0.81 ha ponds yielded 1 fish recovered from the pond with established predators, versus 259 from the rotenoned pond (mortality 94.8%). Largemouth bass were more effective at capturing grass carp than golden shiners or bluegills in laboratory tests.

**Fish community.** Dense submerged vegetation can limit predation efficiency, creating unbalanced fish populations. Vegetation reduction may increase prey vulnerability and improve bass growth. Grass carp addition increased total fish production in one study by 270%.

## Key Findings

- **Effectiveness:** There is agreement in the literature that grass carp effectively control aquatic vegetation. Preferred plants are easiest to control, though others are also reduced.
- **Stocking size:** To avoid largemouth bass predation, grass carp should be larger than about 450 mm (17 inches) at stocking. Small fish (<200 mm) should be stocked when water exceeds 20 °C.
- **Stocking density:** Stocking should be limited to the amount needed to control nuisance growth. Densities of 70 to 233 carp/ha caused complete elimination of submerged plants in one summer, a level the author treats as excessive. Weithman (1975) found two large fish per 0.2 ha controlled most weeds, and the author suggests one fish per pond may be appropriate for small Midwest farm ponds.
- **Limnology:** Grass carp control does not appear to cause harmful limnological changes, and some water quality parameters may improve. Phytoplankton did not bloom excessively in grass carp ponds.
- **Fish production:** Grass carp did not significantly affect channel catfish production in weed-free ponds. Bluegill and golden shiner production increased where dense vegetation was controlled.
- **Economics:** Herbicide treatment costs $35 to more than $1,000 per acre per year. Grass carp are estimated to offer a major economic advantage, with benefits possibly lasting at least five years from one introduction.
- **Management limits:** Vegetation dynamics are hard to predict, so identical stocking rates may not give identical results. Emergent vegetation is less affected and may need spot chemical or mechanical control.
- **Policy:** The author recommends that stocking be controlled by permit and limited to drainage basins where grass carp are already present, because their impact on streams is difficult to predict.
- **Society business:** The AFS editorial lists 14 recent policy actions, including testimony on Sea Grant and deep-sea mining, Public Law 92-500, strip mine legislation, and Civil Service protection for state fisheries jobs. It also notes the 1977 Committee on Ecological Concerns.
