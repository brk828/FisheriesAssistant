# Biology of the Freshwater Shrimp Atyaephyra desmaresti: Feeding Rate and Assimilation Efficiency

#decapoda #freshwater-ecology #natantia

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study, the second part of a series on the biology of [[Atyaephyra Desmaresti]] in the [[River Douro]] (Portugal), measured the [[Feeding Rate]] and [[Assimilation Efficiency]] of the shrimp at 12 °C and 20 °C. Shrimp were fed [[Scenedesmus]] microalgae, [[Tetramin]] fish food, [[Lake Sediment]] ("mud") and mixtures of tetramin and mud. Assimilation efficiency was calculated with the [[Conover Ash Ratio Method]] from fecal analysis, and feeding rate was estimated from the uptake of ³²P-labeled foods. Assimilation efficiency did not differ significantly between temperatures, except for one mixed diet. Feeding rates were higher at 20 °C. The author also found that fecal pellets are surrounded by a [[Peritrophic Membrane]] and that [[Coprophagy]] may be important for the species. A [[Grazing Areas]] method was abandoned because the grazing patterns were too irregular.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** *Atyaephyra desmaresti* Millet, a freshwater decapod crustacean of the suborder Natantia. The document does not give its family.
- **Rationale:** Knowledge of feeding rate and assimilation efficiency on different diets was needed to maintain and rear the shrimp in the laboratory. The author noted that the species might be useful for [[Aquaculture]] because of its energetic value and protein content (Fidalgo, 1985).
- **Collection:** Shrimp were collected by hand net in the River Douro at Crestuma/Lever before the river was impounded, then transported to the institute.
- **Acclimation and fasting:** Shrimp were deprived of food for 24 hours before experiments to empty the gut.
- **Study context:** The work was part of a research programme of the Center of Ecology, funded by INIC (Instituto Nacional de Investigação Científica). Experiments were run at the Max Planck Institute in Plön, Germany.

### Experimental Diets

| Diet | Description |
|---|---|
| [[Microalgae]] | Monoculture of *Scenedesmus* sp. from chemostat culture |
| Tetramin | Aquarium fish food, about 49% protein, 36% carbohydrate, 6% lipid; about 5 cal/mg organic matter |
| Mud | Lake sediment from the mesotrophic [[Schöhsee]], sieved through 0.6 mm mesh; contains living and dead organic matter plus inorganic matter |
| Mixtures | 80/20, 50/50 and 20/80 tetramin/mud |

Experimental water came from the Schöhsee, filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane.

### Methods Overview

- **Assimilation efficiency:** Experiments lasted about 12 hours at 12 °C and 20 °C. Feces and diets were dried (2 h, 105 °C) and ashed (6 h, 550 °C) to determine organic matter fractions.
- **Feeding rate:** Shrimp were fed ³²P-labeled foods for 15–20 minutes. Gut passage time was about 20 minutes, so the author judged that short runs were needed to avoid defecation. Labeled counts were converted to µg of organic matter ingested. Body dry weight was estimated from total length with a regression from Fidalgo (1983).
- **Conversion assumption:** Organic matter was taken to be 82% of shrimp body mass (Fidalgo, 1985).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Coprophagy and energy cycling:** Shrimp re-ingest feces enclosed in peritrophic membranes, sometimes enriched with bacteria, protozoa and fungi. This allowed survival through long starvation periods, when shrimp also drew on their own reserves. The author suggested that coprophagy may be an important energy-transfer mechanism in freshwater ecosystems, although it can also spread parasites through food chains.
- **Moulting:** A decrease in food intake and fecal production was observed on the moulting day, suggesting that the [[Moulting Cycle]] and handling stress affect feeding dynamics.
- **Temperature sensitivity:** The author linked the higher fecal (E′) values at 12 °C to possibly lower digestive enzyme activity at low temperature, and/or to higher feeding rates at 20 °C.
- **Comparison with other taxa:** Results were compared with the copepod *Calanus hyperboreus* (Conover, 1966b), the amphipod *Calliopius laeviusculus* (Dagg, 1976), *Daphnia magna* (Schindler, 1968), *Palaemon lamarrei* (Katre & Reppy, 1977) and the gastropod [[Ancylus Fluviatilis]] (Streit, 1975).

## Key Findings

### 1. Assimilation efficiency

- The mean assimilation efficiency (U′) did not differ significantly between 12 °C and 20 °C (t-test, t = 0.38, P > 50%).
- A [[Mann-Whitney U Test]] showed the same result for all diets except the 80% tetramin + 20% mud diet.
- Mean U′ values at 12 °C (± SD):

| Food | U′ (%) |
|---|---|
| Microalgae | 97.56 ± 0.70 (n = 7) |
| Tetramin | 92.11 ± 0.48 (n = 4) |
| 80% tetramin + 20% mud | 55.12 ± 4.64 (n = 5) |
| 50% tetramin + 50% mud | 24.55 ± 8.06 (n = 6) |
| 20% tetramin + 80% mud | 4.11 ± 3.26 (n = 3) |
| Mud | < 0 |

- Mean U′ values at 20 °C (± SD):

| Food | U′ (%) |
|---|---|
| Microalgae | 98.13 ± 1.07 (n = 7) |
| Tetramin | 92.28 ± 3.31 (n = 11) |
| 80% tetramin + 20% mud | 75.97 ± 7.92 (n = 8) |
| 50% tetramin + 50% mud | 32.93 ± 12.70 (n = 6) |
| 20% tetramin + 80% mud | 9.37 ± 8.87 (n = 8) |
| Mud | < 0 |

- Utilization was significantly correlated with the organic matter content of the foods at both temperatures. Fitted curvilinear regressions are shown in Fig. 2.
- The Conover method was judged more suitable than the [[Gravimetric Method]] for this species, because feces are hard to recover in the presence of coprophagy.

### 2. Feeding rate

- Mean feeding rates of ³²P-labeled food (mean ± SD, Table 3):

| Food and temperature | µg/animal/h | µg/mg/h (weight-specific) | n |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microalgae, 12 °C | 0.18 ± 0.16 | 0.03 ± 0.02 | 43 |
| Microalgae, 20 °C | 3.70 ± 6.57 | 0.55 ± 0.83 | 55 |
| Tetramin, 20 °C | 66.73 ± 67.58 | 25.67 ± 27.17 | 47 |
| Mud, 20 °C | 67.56 ± 91.10 | 19.98 ± 26.02 | 45 |

- Feeding rates on labeled microalgae were higher at 20 °C than at 12 °C, but variability was high (Fig. 3).
- Tetramin and mud gave higher feeding rates than microalgae. The author attributed this to either selective ingestion of labeled microalgae in mixtures or inhomogeneous labeling of the foods. Both would bias the estimates.
- Feeding rate for mud and tetramin was measured only at 20 °C, because labeling those foods proved difficult.
- Weight-specific rates were preferred over per-animal rates because shrimp varied in size.

### 3. Grazing areas

- The method of measuring grazed spots on glass fiber filters was abandoned. The grazing areas were very irregular (Fig. 4), which the author attributed to food floating in the aquaria and to high shrimp activity. The method had been used successfully by Streit (1975) on *Ancylus fluviatilis*.

### 4. Peritrophic membranes and coprophagy

- Fecal pellets of *A. desmaresti* are surrounded by peritrophic membranes, as shown by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 5).
- Feces may therefore contain not only undigested food but also mucous and chitinous secretions, bacteria, protozoa and fungi. This complicates interpretation of fecal-based efficiency estimates.
- The author concluded that coprophagy may be important for the species.

### 5. Methodological limitations

- Incomplete or uneven labeling of foods and selective ingestion may over- or underestimate feeding rates.
- The 15–20 minute measurement period is short, and the author suggested it may add error and variability.
- The Conover method assumes that only the organic fraction of food is digested. It may overestimate utilization, because organic compounds can be lost to water through handling and excretion.

### 6. Conclusions and implications

- Assimilation efficiency is largely independent of temperature for this shrimp, but feeding rate appears higher at 20 °C.
- Microalgae were the most efficiently utilized food. Utilization declined as mud proportion increased.
- Results support the potential use of the shrimp in aquaculture and laboratory rearing, while the author stressed the need for longer experiments and better labeling.
