# The Life History of the Spottail Darter (*Etheostoma squamiceps*) in Big Creek, Illinois, and Ferguson Creek, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes report (No. 89, May 1974, by [[Lawrence M. Page]]) describes the life history of the [[Spottail Darter]] ([[Etheostoma Squamiceps]]), a small benthic percid of the subgenus [[Catonotus Subgenus]]. Field work ran from 6 October 1970 to 19 June 1973 in two streams: [[Big Creek]] (Illinois) and [[Ferguson Creek]] (Kentucky). The study examined 601 specimens from Big Creek and 430 from Ferguson Creek. The central finding is that the species depends strongly on [[Slab Rocks]]. Adults occupy slab pools in Big Creek and slab riffles in Ferguson Creek, and both populations use slab-rock cavities as nest sites. The species is short-lived (maximum about 3+ years), with males guarding nests and females producing up to 357 mature ova.

## Background & Description

The report is one of a planned series on the four *Catonotus* species occurring in southeastern Illinois and adjacent Kentucky. The others are [[Etheostoma Kennicotti]], [[Etheostoma Obeyense]], and a species treated separately. At the time, published ecological information on *Catonotus* was limited. The most studied member was [[Etheostoma Flabellare]], the fantail darter, which served as the main comparison for behavior and development.

*E. squamiceps* was described by Jordan (1877) from Russellville, Logan County, Kentucky. Its range includes southern Illinois, southwestern Indiana, western Kentucky, west-central Tennessee, and the Tennessee River drainages of Alabama and Mississippi. The species is absent from [[Coastal Plain Streams]] in extreme southern Illinois, which typically lack slab substrate.

Key morphological and reproductive traits:
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males had significantly longer anal and pelvic fins (P<0.01), longer pectoral fins (P<0.10), longer soft-dorsal fins, and deeper caudal peduncles (P<0.01) than females. Breeding males were boldly patterned, with a black head, vertical bars, and blackened fins. Breeding tubercles were absent.
- **Identification:** Compared with [[Etheostoma Kennicotti]] in a 24-specimen sample, *E. squamiceps* had more lateral-line scales (mean 50.5 vs. 42.6), more pored lateral-line scales (33.8 vs. 19.7), and fully scaled nape and belly. A single [[Hybridization]] specimen (*E. squamiceps* × *E. kennicotti*, 40 mm male, collected 1 October 1971) was intermediate in character.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams**
- **[[Big Creek]]** (Hardin County, Illinois) is a spring-fed direct tributary of the [[Ohio River]], in limestone of the Shawnee Hills. The substrate grades from slab rock and gravel upstream to sand downstream. The stream is forested and usually clear. Lewis (1957) reported the most abundant fishes as *Campostoma anomalum*, *Pimephales notatus*, *Notropis chrysocephalus*, and *Etheostoma caeruleum*.
- **[[Ferguson Creek]]** (Livingston County, Kentucky) is a short (about 3 miles) direct tributary of the lower [[Cumberland River]]. It consists mostly of shallow slab-rock pools and slab riffles, with a few deeper sand-bottomed pools. The most abundant fishes were *Pimephales notatus*, *Notropis umbratilis*, *Etheostoma obeyense*, *E. squamiceps*, and *Erimyzon oblongus*.

**Habitat types**
- [[Slab Pools]]: shallow (to about 300 mm), gravel with slab rocks 100–400 mm across. Often hot and stagnant in late summer in Big Creek.
- [[Slab Riffles]]: cool, swift-flowing, with slab rocks mainly 100–200 mm (Ferguson Creek riffles up to 150 mm).
- Non-slab pools: usually sand and gravel, seldom over 1 m deep in Big Creek and mostly sand-bottomed in Ferguson Creek.
- Gravel riffles (Big Creek): coarse gravel, where adults were seldom found.

Habitat use shows the species' reliance on slab rock. Its dichotomous preference (riffles in Ferguson Creek, pools in Big Creek) reflects where slab substrate was available. The authors concluded that slab rocks are more important to the species than temperature or current.

**Associated species**
- Other slab-rock spawners included [[Eurycea Bislineata]] (salamander), *E. kennicotti*, *E. obeyense*, and *P. notatus*. Nest sites were partly shared, and spawning was somewhat staggered to reduce competition.
- Potential predators included [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox americanus*) and *Lepomis cyanellus*.

## Key Findings

**Habitat and distribution**
- Adults in Big Creek strongly preferred slab pools in all seasons. Adults in Ferguson Creek were almost exclusively in slab riffles during nonspawning seasons. In April, adults of both populations concentrated in slab pools for spawning.
- Greatest densities: 9.6 darters/m² (all ages) in Ferguson Creek slab riffles in July; 2.0 darters/m² in Big Creek slab pools in April.
- Young (up to 1 year) dispersed downstream from Big Creek spawning areas and returned upstream to slab pools by 1 year. Migration in Ferguson Creek was an annual spring movement from riffles into pools for spawning, then back to riffles.

**Reproduction**
- **Spawning season:** late March through May, at water temperatures of 14–19 °C. Aquarium spawnings occurred at 19–25 °C. Eight laboratory spawnings were recorded, five observed.
- **Nests:** Males chose cavities under slab rocks, cleaned them by shaking their tails, and defended them against intruders. Eggs were laid on undersides of stones, up to 35 mm above the substrate. Nests were found in water up to 300 mm deep, though spawning reached 600 mm in the 1973 floods.
- **Egg counts in 20 nests:** 8, 90, 125, 125, 160, 200, 200, 200, 200, 250, 275, 300, 450, 475, 475, 500, 850, 1100, 1500, 1500; mean 449. Eggs averaged 1.8 mm in diameter. Several nests held eggs from more than one female.
- **Mature ova:** counted in 18 females, ranging from 28 to 357. Mature ova count was related to adjusted body weight (F = −27.12 + 83.88W, r = 0.845) and standard length (log F = −2.589 + 2.729 log L, r = 0.691). Ovary weight averaged 0.189 of adjusted body weight (range 0.091–0.287).
- **Spawning behavior:** Both sexes inverted; the female laid 2–5 eggs per inversion, and the male released sperm. Inversions lasted under 5 seconds, with 15 seconds to several minutes between them. Several females (at least three in one aquarium) spawned with one male, but only one spawned at a time.
- **Parental care:** Males alone guarded eggs and drove off some crayfish. A 63-mm male drove off a 75-mm *Orconectes kentuckiensis* but not a 90-mm *Cambarus laevis*. Females did not tend nests.
- **Territoriality:** Breeding males were strongly territorial; females and nonbreeding males were weakly so. The first male to spawn in an aquarium monopolized later spawnings.

**Development and growth**
- Incubation: 270–275 hours at 18–22 °C; 125–130 hours at 22–26 °C. One Big Creek nest needed at least 16 days at 14–18 °C.
- Hatchlings: 6.5 mm total length, with pectoral fins and lower jaw well developed. Fry left nests soon after hatching.
- Juveniles reached adult morphology at about 12 mm. The mottled pattern and lateral-line development were complete by 19 mm.
- Growth followed a decelerating curve. Males grew faster than females after year one; by year three, males were significantly larger (t = 3.78, df = 53 in Big Creek; t = 2.79, df = 27 in Ferguson Creek).
- Maximum size: 72 mm standard length (male, Ferguson Creek).
- Sexual maturity: females at about 1 year (earliest spawning at 40 mm); males at about 2 years, with males of 40 mm or more presumed mature.

**Demography and survival**
- Big Creek sample (n = 601): 55.7% ≤1 year, 34.6% 1–2 years, 9.2% 2–3 years, 0.5% >3 years.
- Ferguson Creek sample (n = 430): 72.1% ≤1 year, 20.5% 1–2 years, 6.7% 2–3 years, 0.7% >3 years.
- Sex ratio: 1.3 females:1 male in Big Creek and 1.9:1 in Ferguson Creek (total samples). Only the 3+ class had more males, suggesting greater male longevity. The authors suggested the skew may have a genetic basis and that a 50:50 ratio may have been selected against.
- Longevity: 3+ years maximum. The oldest specimen was a 3-year, 5-month-old female from Ferguson Creek.

**Diet**
- Stomachs: 173 from Big Creek and 67 from Ferguson Creek.
- Big Creek main prey: chironomid larvae, mayfly naiads, copepods, amphipods, isopods, ostracods, cladocerans.
- Ferguson Creek main prey: chironomids, caddisflies, mayflies, amphipods, isopods.
- Smaller darters ate mainly copepods, cladocerans, ostracods, and chironomids; larger darters ate amphipods, isopods, and chironomids (Big Creek).
- Chironomids peaked before spawning.

**Parasites and predation**
- Both populations were heavily infected with *Acanthocephalus jacksoni*: 194 worms from 240 darters. Mean infection was 0.91 worms per darter in Big Creek and 0.53 in Ferguson Creek, with up to 19 in a 69-mm male.
- Only ectoparasites found were two piscicolid leeches on two young darters in Big Creek (October).
- Predation: a 121-mm *Esox americanus* contained a 44-mm *E. squamiceps*. No other predation was observed.

**Summary of life history (Table 11)**
- Principal adult habitat: slab riffles (Ferguson Creek) and slab pools (Big Creek).
- Age at sexual maturity: 1 year (females); 2 years (males).
- Spawning: late March to May or early June, both sexes briefly inverted, underside of slab stone.
- Eggs: 1.8 mm, translucent, adhesive.
- Egg guarding: by males only.
- Sexual size: females about 85 mm; males about 40 mm.
