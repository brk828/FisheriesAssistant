# A Summary of Available Information on the Mooneye Family (Hiodontidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hiodontidae

## Executive Summary

This literature review, prepared by Eric W. Flohrschutz for Dr. W. L. Minckley, compiles available information on the family [[Hiodontidae]], which contains two North American freshwater species: the [[Mooneye]] (*Hiodon tergisus*) and the [[Goldeye]] (*Hiodon alosoides*). The review covers taxonomy, description, distribution, habitat, feeding, reproduction, growth, morphology, associated species, parasites, physiology, mortality, and behavior. Its central theme is habitat separation: the Goldeye tolerates turbid water, while the Mooneye is largely restricted to clearer eastern and mid-eastern waters. Much of the biological data for both species rests on [[Kennedy and Sprules 1967]] (Goldeye in Canada) and [[Trautman 1957]] (fishes of Ohio), with considerably less information available for the Mooneye.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The family includes two accepted species. A third name, *H. selenops*, was described by Jordan and Bean in 1877 but appears to be a geographic variant of *H. tergisus*. The genus was first described by [[Le Sueur]] in 1818 from two specimens of the Mooneye, which he named as separate species based on sexual dimorphism while noting that more study was needed. The Goldeye was first described by Rafinesque in 1819 as *Amphiodon alosoides* and was later renamed *Hyodon amphiodon* in 1820. Long-standing confusion arose from misidentifications, misprints (the genus is often spelled *Hyodon*), and inconsistent naming across more than a century of literature, which the review tabulates chronologically.

**General morphology.** Both species are deep-bodied and laterally compressed, resembling a shad. They have easily deciduous scales, pelvic axillary processes, adipose eyelids, and well-developed teeth.

**Diagnostic differences.**
- *Goldeye (per Cross, 1967):* Back nearly straight; dorsal fin with 9 or 10 rays; anal fin with 30–32 rays; lateral line scales 55–58; vertebrae 58–60; ventral keel present both before and after the pelvic fins; eye height less than half the postorbital head length. Coloration is green or gray along the back, silvery laterally, with a yellowish iris.
- *Mooneye:* Body at least half as deep as the head length in adults (deeper than the Goldeye); slightly arched back; dorsal fin with 11 or 12 rays; larger eye; 56–58 vertebrae; 8–9 branchiostegal rays; keel present only behind the pelvic fins.

**Sexual dimorphism.** In the Goldeye, adult males have the anterior half or two-thirds of the anal fin modified into a distinctive tube absent in females. In the Mooneye, males have a forked caudal fin, while females have a nearly straight or anteriorly concave posterior caudal margin.

**Size.** Goldeye are usually 15–17 inches long and weigh 1–2 pounds. The largest recorded by Trautman was 20 inches and 3 pounds 2 ounces. Mooneye average 11–15 inches and 12 ounces to 2 pounds. The largest recorded Mooneye (Trautman, 1957) was 17.5 inches and 2 pounds 7 ounces, a female from the Ohio River drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The Goldeye ranges from British Columbia and Alberta south to Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Mississippi, and from Montana and British Columbia east to Ohio, with an isolated pocket northwest of the Great Lakes. It is rare over much of its range but has no large areas where it has never been collected. The Mooneye ranges from southern Vermont and New Hampshire west to Alberta, and from Alabama and Mississippi north nearly to Hudson Bay. The two species overlap extensively but rarely coexist closely because of their habitat preferences.

**Habitat.** The Mooneye abounds in less turbid rivers of the northeast. Its numbers decreased drastically in the [[Ohio River]] as silt covered firm gravelly bottoms, yet it remained abundant in the clearer waters of Ohio and [[Lake Erie]]. The Goldeye prefers the more turbid waters to the west, north, and south, occurring in the lower Ohio, the Missouri, and muddy Manitoba streams. Although tolerant of clay turbidity, the Goldeye is absent from industrially polluted waters. Both species occur in still and moving water with little apparent preference. In impoundments, both species drop off in reproduction (Shields, 1958a,b), and the Mooneye has disappeared from at least one impoundment (Fitz, 1968).

**Physiology and environmental limits.** The only physiological study found addressed Goldeye metabolism. [[Hart 1968]] reported that swimming metabolic rate depends directly on oxygen supply, whereas resting metabolic rate is unaffected until oxygen reaches critical low levels. Carbon dioxide markedly limited respiration above a partial pressure of 50 mm of mercury. Combined partial pressures of carbon dioxide and oxygen between 20 and 40 mm of mercury limited Goldeye survival through winter.

**Mortality.** In exploited areas in Canada, total Goldeye mortality may reach 74% by ages 6–10. Natural mortality at [[Lake Clair]] was 42% at ages 8–10, 48% at age 11, 57% at age 12, 73% at age 13, and nearly 100% by age 14. At [[Lake Texoma]], on the species' southern edge, mortality was 74% at age four, 90% at age five, and close to 100% by age six. The review attributes the Texoma rates to the population's peripheral location rather than a normal life cycle.

## Key Findings

### Feeding and diet
- **Goldeye:** Diet is broad and reflects whatever is available at the surface. [[Coker 1929]] examined 29 fish from [[Keokuk Iowa]] on the Mississippi. Kennedy and Sprules recorded diet shifts across seasons at [[Lake Winnipegosis]], where December stomachs were dominated by empty fish and aquatic insects, while summer stomachs contained terrestrial insects and other fish.
- **Young-of-the-year:** Stomachs from young fish (examined by Arnason in 1948) included 25 from the [[Saskatchewan River]] Delta (1946) and four from Lake Clair (May 1947). Items were microcrustaceans, primarily *Daphnia* species, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*, *Bosmina*, and *Cyclops bicuspidatus*. Older fish also ate aquatic insects, invertebrates, and aquatic plants.
- **Unusual feeding events:** At Sandy Lake (September 29, 1953), nine Goldeye each contained one mouse. On July 5, 1947, 30 of 34 Goldeye contained pike, 26 exclusively. On July 15 of the same year, 23 of 30 contained pike exclusively. On July 28, 1945, eight Goldeye from Lake Winnipegosis contained *Hexagenia* adults, four exclusively. The authors concluded that Goldeye consume almost any available food that can be swallowed.
- **Mooneye:** Information is sparse. [[Forbes and Richardson]] listed insects, insect larvae, mollusks, and small minnows. [[Sibley 1929]] found Cladocera made up 6% of the contents of 106–146 mm specimens, with terrestrial insects the rest. Boesel's unpublished data from 17 fish in western Lake Erie showed that fish up to 39 mm fed on Entomostraca, while fish over 57 mm fed entirely on insects and amphipods taken from the surface. Coker (1929) found mayfly larvae, black-fly larvae and pupae, caddisfly larvae, dytiscid beetles, and water boatmen in twelve Mooneye from Keokuk.
- **Feeding mode:** Both species feed mainly at the air–water interface. Trautman noted that Mooneye often feed in swift water below dams, even when they are common in still water.

### Reproduction and life history
- **Goldeye spawning:** In Canada, spawning occurs from late May to the end of June, triggered by water temperatures of 50–55 °F, and continues for three to six weeks. Spawning in Montana is earlier (April–June, Hill 1960), and a ripe female was taken at Keokuk in late March (Coker, 1929), though the authors considered that record doubtful.
- **Mooneye spawning:** Ripe fish were taken at Keokuk in mid-March (Coker, 1929). Females measured 9.5–11 inches and contained eggs of about one-twelfth inch, suggesting similarity to the Goldeye, though further sampling is needed.
- **Fecundity and egg traits:** Goldeye females contained 5,000–25,000 eggs, each about one-twelfth inch in diameter. The eggs are semibuoyant, which fits the turbid habitat.
- **Maturity:** Goldeye males in a year class first mature at age four to six and last mature at age seven to nine. Females lag males by about one year. The sex ratio is approximately 1:1.
- **Annual spawning:** Female Goldeye appear to spawn annually, with only aberrant exceptions. Males were presumed to follow the same pattern, but milt is difficult to extract, so spawning status is hard to confirm.
- **Spawning behavior:** Specific behavior is unknown because of turbidity. The authors noted that the sexes must be in close proximity, since males yield only one drop of milt.
- **Morphology of gonads:** Goldeye females lack oviducts; eggs shed into the body cavity exit through the genital pore behind the anus. Male testes enlarge and become convoluted before spawning. No morphological study of the Mooneye was found.

### Growth
- **Goldeye:** Growth in Canada occurs from late May to September, with rapid growth through July. Growth lines (annuli) on scales allow age determination. First-year fish commonly reach 6.5 inches and 1.5 ounces, then gain several ounces and 1–1.5 inches per year through age 10–11. Females grow faster than males in older age classes.
- **Lake Texoma:** Goldeye there show stunting, with growth declining after two years and virtually stopping by age five. The authors attribute this to the peripheral location.
- **Maximum sizes:** Trautman recorded a Goldeye of 20 inches and 50 ounces; Coker recorded one of 18 inches and 3 pounds.
- **Mooneye:** Young-of-the-year in Ohio measured 4.5–6.5 inches. Adults there range from 11 to 15 inches and 12 to 32 ounces. The first-year growth of the Mooneye is probably slower than that of the Goldeye in the same area.

### Associated species
- **Predators (Goldeye):** The main predators in Canada are pike (*Esox lucius*), walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*), and sauger (*Stizostedion canadense*). Inconnu (*Stenodus leucichthys*) has minimal effect because its range barely overlaps. Avian and mammalian predation is suspected, with one recorded instance of cormorant (*Phalacrocorax auritus*) predation.
- **Competitors:** Eight species were found commonly in the same habitat as Goldeye, including lake whitefish, white sucker, northern redhorse, fathead chub, emerald shiner, spottail shiner, and yellow perch. Competition for space is the main concern, since competition for food is minimal in open water because Goldeye feed at the surface.
- **Winter feeding:** Fish in the Lake Winnipegosis December samples were mostly empty-stomached, leading the authors to suggest a non-feeding winter adaptation.

### Parasites
- **Goldeye (Canada):** Recorded parasites include *Bothriocephalus cuspidatus* (intestine), a *Bucephalus* species (body cavity), *Crepidostomum* and other trematodes, an ectoparasitic copepod (*Ergasilus* sp.), and *Paurorhynchus hiodontis* (Margolis, 1964). Lake Texoma Goldeye hosted *Bothriocephalus texomensis* and other parasites (Self, 1954).
- **Mooneye:** Parasites were reported from the St. Lawrence River, Black Lake (New York), and both ends of Lake Erie (Bangham and Hunter, 1939). *Paurorhynchus hiodontis* was first described from Mooneye in Lake Erie (Dickerman, 1954). Specimens under 150 mm were uninfested, while larger fish carried between one and thirty-six worms in the body cavity.

### Behavior
In Canadian lake samples, five-year-old Goldeye were strikingly absent, while in river samples both sexes were nearly twice as abundant at age five as at any other age. Sampling technique is unlikely to explain the gap, since mesh sizes of many types caught a wide size range. The authors suggest that five-year-olds may avoid nets through behavior, depth, or habitat choice.

### Research gaps identified
The review consistently notes that Mooneye information is far scarcer than Goldeye information. Gaps include Mooneye diet across size classes, spawning confirmation, morphology, physiology, and interspecific relationships. Several Goldeye questions also remain open, including the winter non-feeding hypothesis and the cause of the five-year-old gap.
