# Injury to Fish and Wildlife Species: Type B Technical Information Document

#ichthyology #marine-biology #natural-resource-damage

## Executive Summary

This June 1987 Type B Technical Information Document, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[University of Wyoming]] for the [[CERCLA 301 Project]] of the [[U.S. Department of the Interior]], provides technical guidance for assessing injury to fish and wildlife from releases of oil or hazardous substances. It supports the [[Type B Regulations]] at 43 CFR Part 11 but is not required to be followed to obtain the rebuttable presumption those regulations provide. The document reviews published literature on eight categories of biological response: death, disease, behavioral abnormalities, cancer, genetic mutations, physiological malfunctions, and physical deformations. For each, it applies four [[Biological Acceptance Criteria]] and recommends field and laboratory test methods where criteria are met.

The main conclusion is that only fish death (via fish kill counts, in situ bioassays and laboratory toxicity tests), fish fin erosion and certain cancers meet all or most criteria, while behavioral avoidance is the only behavioral response judged acceptable. Disease, genetic mutation, physiological and deformation responses are largely deemed unacceptable at present.

## Background & Description

The document was prepared under [[CERCLA]] Section 301(c), which requires regulations for assessing damages to natural resources. Section 301(c) calls for two sets of regulations: simplified [[Type A Regulations]] and alternative [[Type B Regulations]] for individual cases. The Secretary of the Interior holds this responsibility. The document distinguishes "damage" (money sought) from "injury" (a measurable adverse change in the chemical or physical quality, or viability, of a resource).

The Type B regulations define biological injury as a measurable change in an organism that adversely affects its ability to live in its natural environment. Eight general categories are recognized: death, disease, behavioral abnormalities, cancer, genetic mutations, physiological malfunctions (including reproduction), and physical deformations. Representative biological responses (for example, [[Eggshell Thinning]] in birds exposed to DDT) can demonstrate injury without measuring exposure levels, although exposure data may be needed to show the contamination pathway.

Four acceptance criteria apply to each response:
1. The response is often the result of exposure to oil or hazardous substances, and is not predominantly caused by other factors such as disturbance, nutrition, trauma or weather.
2. Exposure is known to cause the response, and the response has been documented in a natural ecosystem with correlation to exposure concentration.
3. Exposure is known to cause the response in controlled experiments, with environmentally realistic levels linked to field exposure. Laboratory-only responses are insufficient.
4. The measurement is practical to perform and produces scientifically valid, reproducible results with accepted statistical criteria.

The document is limited to fish and wildlife, though the criteria apply to all biological resources. The authors note that science evolves, so responses that currently fail may qualify later.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document places fish injury within [[Water Pollution Biology]]. Early investigations linked water quality to fish presence and absence, and laboratory toxicity tests became central. Historically, toxicity testing shifted between whole-effluent testing and single-chemical criteria, influenced by legislation such as the [[Toxic Substances Control Act]] (1976) and the 1977 amendments to the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act]].

Environmental stress is repeatedly implicated in fish disease. Many responses are stress-mediated, which complicates attribution to specific pollutants. Contaminated sediments, heated effluents, pulp and paper mill wastes, municipal discharges and mining waste sites feature throughout the cited case studies. Predatory and bottom-feeding species are particularly affected, and exposure can occur through food chains as well as direct contact.

The document repeatedly stresses that population-level effects, not individual responses alone, determine ecological significance, a point drawn from [[Warren 1971]].

## Key Findings

**Fish Death (Mortality)**
- Fish death is the most well-developed and validated fish injury measure. Three methods meet all acceptance criteria: [[Fish Kill Investigation]], [[In Situ Bioassay]] (caged fish) and [[Laboratory Toxicity Testing]].
- U.S. fish kill statistics have been compiled since 1960. One fish kill in the Back River, Maryland, killed 47 million fish. Unknown sources accounted for 24% of kills in 1977.
- The [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS 1982) guidelines are the most authoritative counting method. A one-time count underestimated the total: in the Barkley Lake rotenone study, the second-morning count yielded only 50% of fish found over the entire day and about 40% of fish found over three days.
- In situ bioassays are effective for defining the spatial extent of toxicity. Examples include Jackson (1960) on coho salmon and benzene hexachloride, Ziebell et al. (1970) in Port Angeles Harbor, and Kimerle et al. (1986) on 28 chemical plant effluents.
- Laboratory tests define LC50 and EC50 values, with [[Acute Toxicity Tests]] being the preferred approach for fish death. Partial-chronic (early life-stage) tests are cheaper estimators of [[Maximum Acceptable Toxicant Concentration]] (MATC) than full life-cycle tests. Within a factor of two agreement was found in most cases (McKim 1977, from over 60 full life-cycle tests).
- A tiered testing strategy from the [[U.S. EPA]] is recommended.
- The minimum performance standard for death is statistically higher mortality in affected areas than in controls.

**Fish Disease**
- Fin erosion (fin rot) is judged acceptable under the criteria. It has been documented in California, New York Bight, Florida, the Gulf of Mexico and Puget Sound. In New York Bight, 371 of 976 winter flounder (38%) at the apex had fin rot, compared with 26 of 1,357 (1.9%) from adjacent coastal areas.
- [[Ulceration]] of bacterial etiology is common but lacks unequivocal laboratory links to contaminants. [[Lymphocystis]] is associated with polluted waters but lacks laboratory confirmation. Microbial diseases and pollutant-parasite interactions are deemed not to meet the criteria.
- Minimum documentation for fin erosion requires a statistical difference in incidence between contaminated and control sites.

**Behavioral Abnormalities**
- Only [[Behavioral Avoidance]] meets all criteria. Locomotor activity, ventilation frequency, learning ability and swimming performance are rejected mainly because field monitoring is impractical and natural baselines are lacking.
- Laboratory avoidance studies include Hansen (1969) on sheepshead minnows, which avoided four of six pesticides, and DeGraeve (1982), which showed rainbow trout avoided phenol above a threshold of about 4.2 mg/L.
- Field protocol: confirm absence of fish where habitat suggests presence, then conduct laboratory avoidance tests, typically using a Y-maze apparatus, and calculate affected river reach.

**Cancer**
- Liver and skin cancers in bottom-feeding fish near polluted outfalls and sediments, such as brown and black bullhead catfish, English sole and white sucker, are linked to carcinogenic chemicals. Cancer prevalence increased with the age of surveyed fish.
- About 24 chemicals known to be carcinogenic in mammals have induced neoplasms in roughly 24 fish species in laboratory studies.
- Viral-induced neoplasms are documented in multiple taxa (see Table I), and must be distinguished from chemically induced neoplasms.
- Recommended methods include histopathology as the accepted technique, with 5-micron sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin, plus [[Trout Embryo Exposure System]] tests and [[Central Mudminnow]] tests with positive controls such as NMNG and aflatoxin B1.

**Genetic Mutations**
- Chromosome breakage and [[Sister Chromatid Exchange]] (SCE) assays in the Central Mudminnow (2n of 22 chromosomes) and Eastern Mudminnow show promise. For example, Klingerman (1979) found 50 to 100% SCE increases after mutagen exposure.
- Neither meets all criteria yet: validation for field use and sensitivity remains incomplete.

**Physiological Malfunctions**
- Only reproductive effects meet all criteria. Partial-chronic (early life-stage) tests with fish and chronic tests with cladocerans such as Daphnia magna are recommended. Norberg and Mount (1985) describe a 7-day fathead minnow subchronic test.
- Hematology, biochemistry, respiration, osmoregulation and endocrinology are judged unacceptable, mainly because they lack field verification and demonstrated links to population-level effects.

**Physical Deformations**
- Spinal deformities (lordosis, scoliosis, kyphosis) are well documented in laboratory studies with Kepone, toxaphene, PCB, malathion, lead, cadmium and zinc, as well as in field studies of Tomcod and sand bass.
- No response currently meets the criteria, because field detection is rare and sample sizes must be large. Field observation techniques need development.

**Overall Conclusions**
- The document provides standardized methods and minimum performance standards for fish death, fin erosion and cancer, with reproduction and avoidance as the only other accepted responses.
- Future validation of genetic and other responses may expand the acceptable set.
