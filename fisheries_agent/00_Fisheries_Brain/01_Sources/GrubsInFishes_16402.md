# Grubs in Fishes: Trematode Parasites of Wisconsin Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trematoda

## Executive Summary

This Wisconsin Conservation Department fishery biology bulletin, written by biologist Jacob H. Fischthal, answers common angler questions about the four main flesh- and organ-inhabiting "grubs" found in fishes across Wisconsin lakes and streams. It covers the [[Yellow Grub]] (*Clinostomum marginatum*), the [[Black Grub]] or black spot (various Strigeid larvae, including *Uvulifer ambloplitis* in [[Smallmouth Bass]]), the [[White Grub of Liver]] (*Posthodiplostomum minimum*), and the [[Eye Grub]] (genus *Diplostomum*). For each, the document describes the parasite, its multi-host life cycle, its apparent effect on fish, and whether it poses a risk to people. The central conclusions are that none of these grubs is known to threaten humans, that infected fish are generally edible when properly prepared, and that control depends on breaking the parasite life cycle rather than eliminating fish-eating birds.

## Background & Description

The bulletin addresses a practical concern: heavy infections with flesh-inhabiting parasites can lead to fish being classed as "unfit for food," and some Wisconsin fishing areas have declined as resorts because the catch was known to be "grubby." Anglers most often asked about two conditions, the white or yellow grub and the black grub or black spot. Five questions frame the discussion:

1. What is the nature of the grubby condition?
2. How does the fish become infected?
3. Are the grubs harmful to the fish?
4. Can man become infected by eating grubby fish?
5. Can the infection in fish be controlled?

### Yellow Grub (*Clinostomum marginatum*)

- A trematode (fluke) whose larvae form small whitish or yellowish cysts in muscle and just beneath the skin, especially at the base of fins and tail, producing wart-like bulges that anglers call "grubby."
- In heavy infections, cysts may also occur in the abdominal wall, general musculature, membranes of the head, throat, and gills, and gill filaments.
- Living worms are approximately 1/4 inch long by 1/20 inch wide, opaque, brilliant white or yellow, and flattened.
- Occurs principally in [[Yellow Perch]], but also reported in [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], sunfish, [[Northern Pike]], pickerel, minnows, and darters.

### Black Grub or Black Spot (Strigeid larvae)

- Appears as a small black spot in the skin about 1/25 inch in diameter, found mainly in or just beneath the skin.
- Occurs in most warm-water fishes and also in trout.
- The cyst has two walls: a thin inner wall laid down by the worm and a thicker outer wall deposited by the fish. The black color comes from pigment the fish deposits around the cyst.

### White Grub of Liver (*Posthodiplostomum minimum*)

- Described as one of the most widespread and economically important larval fluke parasites.
- Grubs lie in thin-walled, large, loose-fitting cysts in the liver, the normal site of infection; the liver is sometimes almost completely destroyed.

### Eye Grub (genus *Diplostomum*)

- Small, flattened, slightly elongate flukes found in the humors or lens of the eye, without a cyst or capsule.
- Larvae of different species are not readily distinguished from each other in the eye.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Life Cycles and Hosts

All four parasites follow complex, multi-host life cycles that depend on water, snails, and fish-eating birds:

- **Yellow grub:** Adult worms live in the mouth, under the tongue, and in the throat and esophagus of fish-eating birds. Eggs shed into the water hatch within a few hours into miracidia, which penetrate a particular snail species. After several generations in the snail, cercariae emerge in spring and summer, penetrate fish, and become metacercariae. A heron that eats the fish digests the grubs out, and the cycle repeats. - **Black grub:** Adult worms in the intestine of fish-eating birds (a [[Kingfisher]] is shown in Figure 2) pass eggs in droppings. Eggs that reach water hatch into miracidia, which infect a snail (*[[Physa Gyrina]]* is the primary host in the figure for the white grub of liver cycle; the black grub cycle follows the same general pattern). Cercariae penetrate beneath scales or into fins, and encysting takes 22+ days per Figure 2. The document notes that [[Common Sunfish]], [[Rock Bass]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] serve as intermediate hosts in the white grub of liver diagram.
- **White grub of liver:** Adult worms mature in the intestine of the [[Great Blue Heron]]. Eggs leave in droppings and develop in water before hatching into miracidia that penetrate snails. Cercariae penetrate fish beneath the scales, then migrate to the liver and associated organs. Eggs are noted to hatch in about 3 weeks.
- **Eye grub:** Involves three hosts. Adult worms mature in fish-eating birds. Eggs need two to three weeks in water to hatch into miracidia, which penetrate a snail. Fork-tailed cercariae then penetrate fish and localize in the eye. Development takes about six weeks after penetration of the fish. Being eaten by the bird host releases the grubs to mature.

### Host Specificity and Distribution

- The white grub of liver is most common in [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and [[Rock Bass]]. It is found occasionally in black basses, minnows, and darters, and is rare in [[Walleye]], perch, and bullheads.
- Eye grubs have been found in nearly all fish species, including suckers, perch, walleye, sunfishes, [[Northern Pike]], bass, crappies, rock bass, darters, [[Brook Trout]] (trout), and burbot.
- Several bird species may serve as carriers, which shapes the control strategy.

### Effects on Fish

- **Yellow grub:** Light infections show little apparent detrimental effect. Activity studies found heavily infected fish swim more slowly, making them easier for fish-eating birds to catch. Heavy infections would undoubtedly reduce growth.
- **Black grub:** Bass fry usually die within three days after exposure to large numbers of cercariae. Small and medium fish withstand large infections, and fish can tolerate heavier infections when they build up over time. Experimental infections with black grubs caused a statistically significant loss of weight in a group of four-inch smallmouth bass; infections reached up to 708 grubs per fish.
- **White grub of liver:** Natural infections heavily invaded the liver, kidneys, spleen, reproductive organs, body membranes, and heart membranes. Experimental infections caused fish death.
- **Eye grub:** Fish experimentally exposed to large numbers of cercariae died rapidly. Heavy invasion can cause red patches, "popeye" bulging (from fluid accumulation in the eyeball), and white lenses. Blind fish cannot feed, become emaciated, and die. A few dozen worms in the lens may not cloud the eye in a fish of suitable size.

### Human Health and Edibility

- The yellow grub poses no danger to people, even if swallowed alive. Many fish are rejected annually because of prejudice against eating parasitized fish.
- The black grub's infection of people is described as "very remote." Skinning removes most grubs because nearly all cysts are in the skin, and normal cooking destroys all grubs. Black-spotted fish are "quite edible."
- The white grub of liver cannot develop in humans, and because it does not occur in the flesh, there is no danger from eating the fish. Normal cooking destroys any grubs that cling to body membranes.
- Fish with eye grubs are edible because the parasite occurs only in the eyes, which are not used in preparing fish for the table.

### Control Strategy

- Control of the yellow and black grubs is similar and depends on breaking the life-cycle chain. Studies on whether control is practical were in progress.
- Control of the white grub of liver and eye grubs is similarly based on breaking the life cycle. Studies on control of the black grub were underway and could be applied to other grubs if practical.
- Extermination of fish-eating birds is neither feasible nor desirable, since several species may be carriers.

## Key Findings

- **Four main grub types** affect Wisconsin fishes: the [[Yellow Grub]], [[Black Grub]] (black spot), [[White Grub of Liver]], and [[Eye Grub]]. Each is a fluke whose life cycle requires a fish-eating bird, a snail, and water.
- **Human risk is minimal.** The document states there is no danger of yellow grub or white grub of liver infecting people, and the risk from black grubs is "very remote."
- **Edibility is generally preserved.** Black-spotted fish are "quite edible" after skinning and normal cooking; eye-grub fish are edible because the parasite is confined to the eyes.
- **Harm varies by parasite.** The yellow grub has little apparent effect in light infections. The black grub caused a statistically significant weight loss in four-inch smallmouth bass, with infections up to 708 grubs per fish. The white grub of liver and eye grubs can kill fish, especially under experimental conditions.
- **Bass fry are especially vulnerable** to black grub cercariae, usually dying within three days of heavy exposure.
- **Control focuses on the life cycle.** Breaking the parasite's life-cycle chain is the recommended approach. Eliminating fish-eating birds is rejected because several species may be carriers and total extermination is neither feasible nor desirable.
- **Knowledge gaps remain.** The document states that sufficient information is lacking on the harm done by the yellow grub, that accurate information on black grub harm is limited, and that control practicality was still under study.
