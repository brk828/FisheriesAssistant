# Age and Growth of Striped Bass in the Thames River Estuary, Connecticut

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] (University of Connecticut, Department of Natural Resources Conservation) estimates the age and growth of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in the [[Thames River Estuary]], Connecticut. Ages were determined by [[Scale Analysis]] of fish collected in 1972 and 1973, and back-calculated fork lengths were derived with the [[Fraser-Lee Back-Calculation]] approach. Growth in the estuary was similar to that reported from other New England, Maryland and Delaware populations, and there has been little change in New England striped bass growth over the previous 40 years. The authors present the results as a baseline for detecting future changes as coastal areas are developed.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The striped bass is a widely distributed anadromous marine species that supports substantial sport fisheries on both the Atlantic and Pacific coasts of North America. Its recreational and economic importance has generated extensive life history research.
- **Previous New England work:** Earlier age and growth studies include those of [[Merriman]] (1941), [[Fitzpatrick and Cookson]] (1958) and [[Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries]] investigations, plus [[Mansueti and Beardsley]] (1967) and [[Pearson]] (1938) for other regions.
- **Study rationale:** The work supplies current growth information for comparison with earlier studies as coastal habitats become increasingly developed by humans.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The Thames River estuary forms at Norwich, eastern Connecticut, where the [[Shetucket River]] and [[Yantic River]] join the [[Quinebaug River]]. It flows about 26 km south to [[Long Island Sound]] at New London.
- **Physical conditions:** A saltwater wedge lies about 1 m below the surface in the upper 3 km of the estuary, except during high spring discharge. Salinities are generally above 10 ppt below 3 m.
- **Biological community:** The estuary supports resident freshwater species and transient marine fishes. Striped bass are present year-round, with peak numbers in spring.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were collected from October 1972 through November 1973 in the upper 3 km of the estuary using nylon gill nets, seines, and hook and line. Fork length was measured to the nearest millimetre, and scale samples were removed following [[Merriman]] (1941).
- **Scale analysis:** Scale impressions were made on cellulose-acetate slides with a roller press, enlarged with a microprojector and interpreted using Merriman's criteria. Distances from the focus to each annulus and to the scale margin were measured for the two clearest scales per sample.
- **Back-calculation:** Data were pooled with those of [[Whitworth et al.]] (1975) to increase sample size. Fork length at annulus formation was back-calculated using a body-scale proportional formula with k = 1.1 cm, the fork length at scale formation.

## Key Findings

- **Growth in the first six years:** Back-calculated mean fork lengths at the end of the first six years of life were reported as 12.5, 23.1, 31.8 and 38.1 cm for the first four years. The fifth and sixth values are not clearly legible in the scan (roughly 44.7 and 51.3 cm, uncertain).
- **Maximum growth:** Greatest growth occurred during the first year of life (12.5 cm). Annual increments were 8.7 to 10.6 cm in each of the following five years.
- **Sample size:** The grand-average back-calculated length table covers 14 fish across age classes (e.g., 93, 83, 76, 61, 51, 43, 35 and 22 fish in successive age groups of the table, as legible in the scan).
- **Bias in capture lengths:** Mean fork length at capture exceeded back-calculated lengths for all ages because most fish were collected late in the growing season but before annulus formation.
- **Comparison with other regions:** Thames River growth was similar to that in New England (Merriman 1941), Maryland (Pearson 1938; Mansueti 1961) and Delaware ([[Bason]] 1971). Lengths matched those of Massachusetts bass ([[Fitzpatrick and Cookson]] 1958), with differences likely due to sampling time.
- **Comparison with Quebec:** Growth was faster than in the [[Saint Lawrence River]] population in Quebec ([[Massé and Beaulieu]] 1967). Quebec bass are an indigenous population rather than part of the coastal migratory stock, and their slower growth is probably due to colder water and a shorter growing season.
- **Comparison with North Carolina:** [[Roanoke River]] bass (North Carolina), also an indigenous population, grew to greater lengths at the end of each year than New England fish ([[Trent and Hassler]] 1968), reflecting warmer waters and a longer growing season.
- **Temporal stability:** There has been little change in New England striped bass growth over the last 40 years.
- **Management implication:** Coastal development for power generation, industry, recreation, navigation and offshore resource use may alter coastal water environments and nursery areas. Continued monitoring of fisheries resources is needed to recognize population changes and to take action that minimizes potential impacts.
