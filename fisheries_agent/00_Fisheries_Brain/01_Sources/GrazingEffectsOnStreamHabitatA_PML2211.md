# Grazing Effects on Stream Habitat and Fishes: Research Design Considerations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1987 manuscript by [[John N. Rinne]] (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Arizona State University) with a reviewer's letter dated 17 November 1987. The manuscript reports a 4-year case-history study (1982–1985) of [[Grazing Effects]] on a montane stream, the [[Rio de las Vacas]] in New Mexico, where cattle had been excluded for about 10 years. Bank vegetation and stability improved and substrate fines were somewhat reduced, but fish populations were not measurably affected. The author argues that this and similar [[Riparian Ecosystems]] grazing studies share three design flaws: lack of [[Pretreatment Data]], poor consideration of [[Fisheries Management]] principles, and [[Linear Positioning]] of treatments along a stream. The reviewer judged the paper well written and informative but poorly organized, because original data and a critical literature review were combined in a way that undermined both. The reviewer recommended restructuring the paper so the Vacas results are presented in the Results section and the critique is placed in the Discussion, and encouraged publication.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The Rio de las Vacas is a third-order montane stream in the [[Santa Fe National Forest]], draining the San Pedro Parks Wilderness Area. The study area lies at an elevation given as 2,160 m (OCR-garbled in the source). Two exclosures, each about 1 km long and 50 m wide (about 10 ha total), were established in 1972 and 1975. Study sections 1, 4 and 5 fall in one exclosure, and sections 2, 3 and 6 in the other. Private land separates the exclosures from a downstream grazed reach on National Forest land. The Cuba Community Allotment is classed as roughly 50% unsuitable, 40% suitable for grazing, 5% private and 5% riparian. The historical grazing season ran from 1 June to 31 October, averaging 2,688 animal unit months (AUM) for the allotment.

**Fish community.** Five fish species occur in the Vacas. Three are native: [[Rio Grande Sucker]], [[Rio Grande Chub]] and [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]. [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] are introduced periodically, and the rainbow trout hybridizes with the native cutthroat. Hybridization and intergeneric competition have greatly reduced pure native cutthroat numbers in this and other streams.

**Manuscript and letter.** The manuscript was written as a critique of grazing research design, with the Vacas study as its central example. The reviewer's letter addresses the structure of the paper, not the field data themselves.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Total alkalinity was consistently and significantly higher in the lower grazed reaches in all seasons (P < 0.01; df = 39). Nutrient content, total hardness, and seasonal temperatures were similar between areas.
- **Riparian vegetation:** Stream bank vegetation and overhanging vegetation were markedly greater in the ungrazed reach. The reported values are 82% for stream bank vegetation and 17.2% for overhanging vegetation (OCR-ambiguous).
- **Bank stability:** Banks were totally stable within exclosures, compared with 54% instability in the downstream grazed area.
- **Temperature:** In April and June 1985, water temperatures ranged 0–14 °C in the upper ungrazed area and 2–22 °C in the lower grazed area. The author concluded that the vegetation recovery did not significantly change the thermal regime, although slightly warmer water in the grazed reach may favor nonsalmonids.
- **Substrate permeability:** Median filtration rates were 11.6 mL/s in the grazed area and 19.1 mL/s in the ungrazed area, with wide ranges (9–40 mL/s within exclosures). Median intragravel flow was 31.1 cm/h ungrazed and 17.5 cm/h grazed (range 0–131 cm/h). Neither difference was significant.
- **Woody debris and cover:** Streamside trees and shrubs provide cover and thermal buffering. Woody debris is important to fish and invertebrate habitat in the Pacific Northwest, eastern waters, and arid Southwest streams.
- **Hypothesis tested:** Cattle grazing had no effect on riparian or stream habitat or on fishes in the Vacas.

## Key Findings

**Study results**

- Exclosure of cattle for about 10 years produced clear improvements in stream bank vegetation and stability. Substrate fines were somewhat reduced, but fish populations were unaffected.
- The reported results were "not the anticipated ones," and the author says they add to the ambiguity surrounding grazing effects on fishes and stream habitat.

**Fish populations and sampling**

- Fish density was estimated once a year during summer low flow (June–July) using a three-pass removal method in 50 m blocked sections, with backpack [[Electrofishing]].
- Salmonid density did not differ significantly between the exclosures and the grazed area, and total fish density did not differ either.
- In the grazed downstream section, mean fish numbers per 50 m were 225 (range 133–256). In the upper ungrazed area, the mean was 91 (range 73–126). Private grazed sections F and G averaged 86 (range 62–104). The similarity of estimates on private grazed land and National Forest ungrazed land suggests that section location may have influenced population estimates as much as grazing did.
- Nonsalmonids made up about 75–95% of the fish community in 1982 and about 91–92% of fish numbers in later years.

**Research design problems identified by the author**

1. **Lack of pretreatment data.** Fencing was installed in the early-to-mid 1970s, before any study, so baseline conditions cannot be reconstructed. A "frame of reference" approach, using monitoring before treatment, is proposed as more feasible than strict control–treatment designs in altered systems. See [[Reference Frame]].
2. **Poor consideration of fishery management.** Stocking and angling confounded the trout data. About 9,000 catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked between 1982 and 1983, and 800 [[Brown Trout]] were stocked in September 1982. Estimated angler effort in 1983 was roughly 9,000 fisherman days, with an estimated catch of about 22,000 trout. The author argues that relating trout populations to grazing is futile under these conditions. Interspecific interactions were also ignored; brown trout may have contributed to the decline of Rio Grande chub.
3. **Linear positioning of treatments.** Treatments were placed along one stream, so the upstream and downstream reaches were affected by different management and natural factors. The author describes this as the most serious problem in the design. [[Water Quality]] data were especially affected, as alkalinity and specific conductance were higher in the lower grazed reaches over distances of only several kilometers.

**Recommendations for future research**

- Use [[Watershed Approach]] studies with long-term (10+ year) monitoring and pretreatment data, rather than short linear designs.
- Impose a selected grazing strategy on a whole watershed where possible.
- Account for fish movement and turnover; the author observed marked-fish movements suggesting substantial exchange between study areas.
- Consider [[Put-and-Take Stocking]] and angling effects explicitly when interpreting trout data.
- Note that cattle prefer lush near-stream areas, so per-allotment AUM figures may be misleading. Fencing riparian areas may be uneconomical, so behavioral management should be considered.

**Reviewer's assessment (letter, 17 November 1987)**

- The manuscript is clear and informative, but the combination of original Vacas data with a critical review is a "bad marriage." The critique undermines the original results and leaves the reader asking why the study was published at all.
- Three options were offered: (a) present only the Vacas results and drop the comparative critique; (b) rewrite the paper as a review of grazing studies, with the Vacas work reduced; or (c) reorganize so that the Vacas results form the body of the paper and the comparative critique goes into the Discussion. The reviewer preferred option (c) and believed it reflects the author's original intent.
- The reviewer thinks the work can be rescued through restructuring and expects publication if the revision is completed.
