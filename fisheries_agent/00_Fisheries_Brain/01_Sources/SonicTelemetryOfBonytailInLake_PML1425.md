# Sonic Telemetry of Bonytail in Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (Biological Resources Division) describes a 1996 sonic telemetry study of the endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a large-river cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Ten fish (five of each sex) were implanted with sonic tags in May 1996 and tracked through August 1996 in [[Lake Mohave]], which holds the largest remaining wild population. Tagged fish moved up to 42 km to 56 km (depending on individual), wild-caught fish tended to move downlake into the lower [[Reservoir Canyons]], and cove-reared fish tended to move upstream. The study was intended to improve monitoring of reintroductions and to guide the collection of broodstock.

## Background & Description

Bonytail is one of the native "big-river" fishes of the Colorado River system, which has been transformed by dams, impoundments, channelization, and the introduction of non-native fishes. Bonytail now persists only in Lake Mohave and [[Lake Havasu]], and in few other places; reproduction in the wild is not known to be successful, and recruitment is a recognized problem for big-river fishes, including [[Razorback Sucker]]. The species is federally listed as endangered, and the report characterizes it as a critically imperiled species.

Fish used in the study were a mix of wild-caught individuals and cove-reared fish. Cove-reared fish were offspring of F1 adults (Lake Mohave stock) spawned at a hatchery; wild fish were captured by trammel net near Bill Gayes and Dandy coves (NV) and Arrowhead Cove (AZ). Tagged fish were 35.5 to 46.5 cm long.

**Tagging methods**
- Sonic tags (Sonotronics, Tucson; MT-95-2) were about 8 mm long x 32 mm diameter, weighed 5 g, and were encased in inert ABS plastic.
- Tags were surgically implanted under anaesthesia (tricaine methanesulfonate, MS-222) through a 10–12 mm abdominal incision; a passive integrated transponder (PIT) tag was inserted with each sonic tag. Surgery took under 5 minutes from anaesthesia to fresh water.
- Tags operated at 72 or 74 kHz, with pulse interval extended to 1.7 s to extend nominal battery life from 47 to 90 days.

**Tracking methods**
- Tracking used a USR-SW receiver and DH-2 hydrophone; detection range was typically over 500 m and approached 1000 m under ideal conditions.
- Routine monitoring used 71 listening stations (waypoints) at roughly 1.6 km intervals, covering approximately 85% of the reservoir surface. Locations were determined by triangulation with a GPS unit.
- The reservoir was searched at least weekly, with additional "cove surveys" in June.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir was divided into five zones: Black Canyon, Chalk Cliff/Basin, Castle Cliff Basin, Cottonwood Basin, and Lower Reservoir Canyons. Lake surface elevation varied by slightly more than a metre (about 1.1 m) over the study period. Mean releases from Hoover Dam were about 500 m³/s (17,675 cfs), and releases from Davis Dam averaged about 7,980 cfs (range 351 to 572 m³/s), per [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] records.

Summertime surface temperatures in the lower Reservoir Canyons reached about 25 °C and possibly 30 °C. These canyon waters are relatively deep, steep-walled, and the deepest water anywhere in the lake, which the author links to the fish's downlake movements.

Tagged bonytail were found near shore in 76% of contacts. When time of day and shore orientation were considered together, wild-caught fish spent most daylight hours in deeper open water and most nighttime and crepuscular hours in shallow water along shore.

## Key Findings

- **Movement distances:** Four of five wild-caught fish moved downlake about 5–6 km (reported as up to 35.3 km linear displacement in individual records) and remained in the lower Reservoir Canyons area; the other wild-caught fish moved about 14 km and stayed within a few km of their release sites.
- **Cove-reared fish:** Two cove-reared fish moved upstream; one moved about 55 km upstream and 75 km total cumulative distance, while another stayed near its release site.
- **Maximum displacement:** Individual linear displacements ranged from about 4 km to 55 km; total cumulative point-to-point distances ranged from about 4 km to 75 km.
- **Post-release contact:** Two fish (both cove-reared) were never contacted after release; possible causes include immediate mortality, tag failure, or removal by avian or mammalian predators, and the data could not distinguish these.
- **Survival:** All fish survived anaesthesia and surgery and swam vigorously upon release.
- **Habitat use:** Fish preferred shallow shoreline water in the majority of contacts, with diel shifts between open water (daytime) and shore (night).
- **Capture implications:** Tagged fish were used to direct net sets near the Arrowhead Cove area, where two wild-caught tagged fish were recaptured, and four unmarked adult bonytail were captured. The authors note that previous collections were concentrated in upper Cottonwood Basin, and that lower Reservoir Canyon sampling should be augmented.
- **Management relevance:** The authors argue that telemetry can improve monitoring of reintroduced fish, help locate fish that are otherwise hard to find, and support the acquisition of broodstock for a species they describe as critically imperiled.
