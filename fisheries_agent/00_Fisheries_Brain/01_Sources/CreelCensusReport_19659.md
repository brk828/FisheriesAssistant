# 1954 Colorado Creel Census Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Colorado Department Of Game And Fish]] 1954 [[Creel Census]] report, issued in June 1955, summarizes angler catch and effort on Colorado trout and warm-water waters. Wardens checked anglers' creels and licenses, and the results were projected to statewide totals using a [[Random Survey]] method. Total license sales reached a record 387,708. The overall catch per man hour fell to 0.74, the lowest in five years, while the warm-water catch rate rose slightly. Rainbow trout dominated both the angler catch and the hatchery stocking program.

## Background & Description

The report is a fisheries management tool. Creel checks record the hours fished, fish released, species in the creel and average length. Warden reports go to the Denver office, where they are compiled by water body and combined with random survey data to estimate the annual state catch. The estimates guide seasons, bag limits and stocking schedules.

Survey methods changed over time:

- **1945–1946:** A personal interview survey of 18 questions, conducted with the National Opinion Research Center, Denver.
- **1949 and 1950:** Revisions using a postcard questionnaire. All 1954 figures use the 1950 revision, and the previous four years were recalculated to match.

The authors acknowledge that the random survey is subject to bias, but state that the figures are reliable as trend indicators.

The report has two parts. Part One covers trout waters: streams, lakes, stocking and non-resident anglers. Part Two covers warm-water lakes and streams. An appendix documents the projection formulas, and a closing page presents a conservation pledge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report presents the fishery as a managed system in which hatcheries and natural reproduction both contribute fish.

- **Hatchery dependence:** Nearly 10,000,000 trout were planted in 1954, but fewer than 50 per cent reached an angler's creel. The authors estimate that over 8,000,000 fish taken were "furnished by nature."
- **Rainbow emphasis:** Rainbow trout are easier to hatch and raise, so they make up most of the stocking program.
- **Natural-production measures:** The authors list pollution control, watershed protection, spawning-area protection and rough-fish control as ways to support natural reproduction. Gains from natural production would also free hatchery fish for heavily fished waters.
- **Warm-water behavior:** Species such as [[Black Bass]], [[Walleye]] and [[White Bass]] are harder to catch because they do not concentrate predictably. [[Channel Catfish]] feed mainly at night and spread over wide areas.
- **Rough fish:** Many thousands of suckers and carp are taken each year, but these are not counted in creel reports.

## Key Findings

### Statewide Summary

- License sales for 1954 rose by 2,539 over 1953 to 387,708, the largest number ever sold in one year.
- Creels were checked for 50,104 trout fishermen and 21,062 warm-water fishermen.
- The catch per man hour (CPMH) for trout streams and lakes combined fell to 0.74, the lowest in five years.
- CPMH was 0.87 on trout streams and 0.65 on trout lakes.
- The projected trout catch was 13,576,167: 4,534,282 from lakes and 9,041,885 from streams. This is a drop of 513,315 from the 1953 catch of 14,089,482.
- The warm-water CPMH was 0.97, up from 0.96 in 1953. The projected warm-water catch was 2,130,737, an increase of 24,423.

### Trout Streams

- Wardens checked 21,552 anglers on 375 streams and stream sections. Those anglers had fished 64,640 hours, an average of 3.00 hours each.
- CPMH was 0.87, about one fish per 70 minutes of effort.
- Table 4 lists 56,164 trout checked:

### Trout Lakes

- Wardens contacted 28,552 anglers on 207 lakes and reservoirs. Those anglers had fished 105,875 hours, an average of 3.71 hours each.
- CPMH was 0.65, about one fish per 93 minutes.
- Rainbow made up 86 per cent of the lake catch, followed by brook trout (9 per cent), native trout (3 per cent) and brown trout (2 per cent).
- Table 5 lists 69,168 trout checked, including 59,224 rainbow. Smaller numbers of [[Mackinaw Trout]] (2) and [[Kokanee Salmon]] (147) were also recorded.

### Cold-Water Species Mix and Trends

- Across the whole cold-water catch, rainbow made up 75 per cent, brook 16 per cent, brown 6 per cent and native 3 per cent.
- Lake and combined CPMH were 0.74 in both 1953 and 1954, according to the warden data.
- The projected trout catch peaked in 1952 at 16,475,329.
- Rainbow numbers and their share of checks increased steadily over the five years reviewed.
- Fishing pressure rose by about 87,000 licenses since 1950, and total fishing hours also increased.

### Stocking

- Of all trout stocked in Colorado waters in 1954, 6,352,508 (62.5 per cent) were [[Rainbow Trout]].
- Stocking also included 464,190 [[Kokanee Salmon]] under three inches and 74 [[Grayling]] three to six inches long, both in only a few lakes.
- The stocking program placed more trout under three inches on certain lakes.
- Stocking records are available to the public on request.

### Non-Resident Anglers

- Non-residents made up 9.0 per cent of anglers checked: 10.9 per cent on streams and 7.5 per cent on lakes.
- Non-residents caught 8.7 per cent of all fish checked.
- Their CPMH was 0.98 on streams, 0.64 on lakes and 0.80 on the two types combined.
- Non-residents provided 34.5 per cent of total fishing license revenue.

### Warm-Water Lakes and Streams

- Wardens checked 21,062 anglers who had fished 59,219 hours, an average of 2.6 hours each.
- CPMH was 0.97, about one fish per 62 minutes.
- The survey covered 56 lakes (over 40,000 acres) and nine streams.
- Table 9 lists 57,042 fish checked:

| Species | Share of catch |

| [[Yellow Perch]] | 57.49% |
| [[Crappie]] | 12.41% |

| [[Black Bass]] | 0.94% |

- Over 80 per cent of warm-water anglers used still fishing.
- Yellow perch, bullheads, crappie, rainbow trout and sunfish dominated the catch. Black bass, channel catfish, walleye and white bass contributed little.
- Warm-water CPMH from 1950 to 1954 was 0.76, 0.65, 0.91, 0.96 and 0.97.

### Four Reservoirs Versus Other Waters

- Two Buttes, North Sterling, Jumbo and Bonny reservoirs were compared with the other 52 lakes and nine streams.
- The four reservoirs had 12,095 anglers who caught 44,139 fish. Their CPMH was 1.15, about one fish per 53 minutes.
- Other warm waters had a CPMH of 0.61, about one fish per 100 minutes.
- North Sterling had the highest CPMH of the four reservoirs (1.48).

### Warm-Water Stocking, 1954

- Over 5,000,000 walleye fry were stocked, with excellent early survival reported.
- About 150,000 [[Largemouth Bass]] fry were received from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- Channel catfish fry came from Kansas (100,000) and Oklahoma (100,000) and were reared in Colorado ponds before release.
- Missouri provided 800 [[Smallmouth Bass]] fingerlings, to be held until they could spawn.

### Appendix Estimates

- The random survey found that 15.94 per cent (44,866) of resident combination licensees did not fish.
- The survey also found that 4.65 per cent (4,364) of non-resident 5-day permittees did not fish.
- These non-fishers were excluded from the calculations.
