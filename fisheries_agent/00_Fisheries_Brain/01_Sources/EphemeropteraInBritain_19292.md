# Ephemeroptera in Britain

#freshwater-biology #ephemeroptera #entomology

## Executive Summary

This reprinted paper, presented at the XI International Congress of Entomology (Vienna, 1960), reviews the mayflies (Ephemeroptera) of Britain. It records 47 species from the British list, notes that several are rare or of doubtful status, and argues that the timing of emergence and the distribution of mayflies are controlled largely by water and air temperature. The paper also documents how a septic-tank overflow in [[Ford Wood Beck]] appears to have altered the stream fauna over a decade. The author, T. T. Macan, emphasises that field temperature records are a necessary base for later laboratory experiments.

## Background & Description

The paper covers the Ephemeroptera of Britain, with reference to the wider European literature. Forty-seven species have been recorded in Britain, and a reference list of taxonomic works (keys and nymph descriptions) is appended. Keys and nymph descriptions are cited for many genera, including [[Baetis Rhodani]] and related Baetis species, Ecdyonurus, Heptagenia, Rhithrogena, Caenidae, Leptophlebiidae and Siphlonuridae.

Several species are noted as rare or doubtful:

- **[[Arthroplea Congener]]**: its British status is somewhat doubtful, as it rests on one specimen taken about 40 years earlier.
- **Ephemera lineata, Potamanthus luteus, Brachycercus harrisella, Baetis atrebatinus**: rare species of what pass for large rivers in Britain. Their range may have shrunk through human river use.
- **Rhithrogena haarupi**: possibly also rare in this habitat.
- **Heptagenia fuscogrisea**: common in limestone lakes in Ireland but rare elsewhere in the British Isles. It is the only ecdyonurid the author has found clinging to flat leaves.
- **Paraleptophlebia tumida**: known from only two chalk streams that dry up in summer.
- **[[Ameletus Inopinatus]]**: a mountain and arctic species, abundant in streams above 300–400 m, though also found in lakes at sea level in northern Scotland.

Six species are characteristic of small stony streams in Britain and on the continent: [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]], Ecdyonurus torrentis or venosus, [[Heptagenia Lateralis]], Baetis rhodani, Baetis pumilus and Ephemerella ignita. Surveys by Illies (1952) and Dittmar (1955) support this pattern. Species richness increases as current slackens and stream size increases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and emergence.** The author argues that once a life history is known, the next question is what triggers each event. In three cool summers (1953, 1954, 1956), [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] adults were still emerging in an emergence cage in late August. In four other years, emergence ended when the average air temperature, which water temperature follows with some lag, reached 16–17 °C and stayed there for at least a week. Nymphs that have not yet emerged may be killed by lethal heat. Lethal limits depend on both temperature and duration of exposure. [[Thermal Tolerance]] data from Whitney (1939) gave a lethal range of 22.4–24.7 °C, but the author considers the critical level to be lower just before emergence.

**Egg versus nymph resistance.** Eggs are thought to tolerate higher temperatures than nymphs. Comparing Harker's stream with the author's stream supports this. In Harker's stream, emergence continued into July and overlapped with the new generation. In the author's stream, emergence was mostly in June or late May, nymphs of the next generation never appeared before August, and the two year-classes never overlapped. The author links the later emergence and earlier hatching in Harker's stream to its lower temperature.

**Lake and stream differences.** [[Heptagenia Lateralis]] resembles Rhithrogena in life history but does not grow during the coldest winter months, and its upper lethal temperature is lower. In 1959, Heptagenia was numerous where stream maxima were 18 °C or less, and scarce or absent where they reached 18 °C or more, except at one station just above a densely populated cool zone. It also lives on the stony shores of [[Windermere]], which is several degrees warmer than 18 °C. There, the lake warmed slowly and did not reach 18 °C until late June, after nymphs would have emerged.

**Downstream movement.** [[Baetis Rhodani]] nymphs move downstream before emerging. In [[Ford Wood Beck]], six stations were sampled, and emergence traps ran at [[Outgate]] near the source and at station 1 near the mouth for seven years. Nymphs were always more abundant at station 1. Emerging adults were rare at Outgate: 20 were caught in 1955, a year of unusual abundance for all species, and only 4 in the other six years combined. Station 1 was consistently 2–3 °C warmer than Outgate.

**Temperature records.** Outgate temperatures were logged by thermograph over several years, which showed how hard it is to summarise stream temperature briefly. Pleskot described the stream as "maximum meist 16 bis 17 °C." In 1959, the maximum at station 1 was 19–20.5 °C and reached 22 °C in August. Pleskot's Schwechat stream reached above 20 °C. In that stream, a month-and-a-half gap separates the summer and overwintering generations of B. rhodani, which the author attributes to high temperature. In Ford Wood Beck the two generations overlap.

**Pollution effects.** Water supply to Outgate was installed in the early 1950s, and nearly all houses were connected by 1953. The resulting overflow from one septic tank into the stream appears to have changed the fauna. [[Polycelis Felina]], a predatory flatworm, increased markedly and is thought to exploit the extra organic matter, bacteria, fungi and ciliates. Simulium and net-spinning Trichoptera also increased. Ecdyonurus torrentis vanished from emergence catches in 1959, and both B. rhodani and Rhithrogena semicolorata declined after peaks in 1953–1955. Rhithrogena, the most abundant species in 1953, was not caught in 1960. Ephemerella ignita seemed unaffected. The author rejects a direct toxin, because that would have caused an earlier and more abrupt decline. Species that live on stones declined more than those in gravel, such as B. pumilus and the Plecoptera.

**[[Habrophlebia Fusca]]** is the only species that increased, becoming abundant at Outgate. The author now thinks food supply, not current speed, limits it.

## Key Findings

- Forty-seven mayfly species are recorded from Britain. Several are rare, and the status of Arthroplea congener is doubtful.
- Emergence in [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] ends when air and water temperatures reach 16–17 °C and stay there for about a week. Cool summers prolong emergence into late August.
- Eggs appear more heat-resistant than nymphs. Nymph mortality from high temperature is likely, but the lethal level depends on both temperature and exposure time.
- Life-history differences between streams (Macan's and Harker's) are linked to temperature. The stream with cooler temperatures had later emergence and earlier hatching.
- [[Heptagenia Lateralis]] is limited to cooler streams (18 °C or less in the 1959 survey), and it was absent from warmer stations.
- [[Baetis Rhodani]] nymphs move downstream before emergence, so emergence trap catches at the source are much smaller than at the mouth.
- Field temperature records (thermograph data and Table 1 hour counts) are considered an essential base for laboratory tests of critical temperatures.
- A septic-tank overflow in Ford Wood Beck is thought to have boosted [[Polycelis Felina]] and other organisms, causing declines in stone-dwelling mayflies. Food supply, not toxins, is considered the cause.
- [[Habrophlebia Fusca]] increased in abundance, suggesting that food supply rather than current limits it.
