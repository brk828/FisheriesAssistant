# Illustrated Manual of Massachusetts Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1969 manual, written by Paul S. Mugford and published by the [[Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game]] (Publication No. 4064), is a field identification guide to the freshwater fishes of Massachusetts. It illustrates more than seventy species, provides dichotomous keys to families and species, and explains how to count fin rays, scales and gill rakers. It also gives angling hints and presents the agency's fisheries management philosophy, including [[Fish Conservation Fundamentals]] and the [[Massachusetts Fisheries Policy]]. Its central message is that stocking and harvest should be managed according to the productive capacity of each water body, not by reflex.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and audience.** The manual was written as an aid for Commonwealth fisheries staff and was later extended to students, naturalists and anglers. The author notes that no single existing reference covered the full freshwater fauna of the state, and that identification was often difficult.

**Sources.** Much of the content comes from unpublished Division survey files (Westboro field headquarters). The bibliography lists a long series of regional fish guides, Division project reports and life-history compendia, including works on [[Brook Trout]] (Bridges, 1958), [[White Perch]] (Thoits, 1958) and [[Chain Pickerel]] (Wich, 1958).

**Identification system.** Each key couplet offers two choices, (a) or (b), leading to a family and then to a species. Counting conventions are specified for:
- Dorsal and anal fin rays, including rudimentary rays in pikes, salmonids and catfishes
- Paired fin rays
- Lateral line scales
- Branchiostegal rays and gill rakers

Each species account lists resemblances, structure, color, distribution, size and habits. Figures are line drawings.

**Taxonomic coverage.** The keys and species accounts span the following groups:
- Lampreys and sturgeons
- Eels, pipefishes, sticklebacks and catfishes
- Smelts, salmonids and herrings
- Suckers and minnows (about 16 shiners and dace-type species)
- Pikes, silversides, killifishes and sculpins
- Perches, darters and sunfishes, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]]

A separate section covers rare species and possibles, such as [[Burbot]], [[Muskellunge]] and [[Coho Salmon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Resource base.** The manual reports 157,588 acres of inland waters and annual rainfall averaging nearly four feet. The standing crop is over 12 million pounds of fish. Annual losses from fishing, disease, predation, pollution and other causes exceed 6 million pounds, but these losses are replaced by growth and recruitment.

**Access limits.** Nearly half of Massachusetts waters are unavailable to the public: over 41,000 acres of private ponds, 20,000 acres of domestic water supplies and over 11,000 acres of mostly privately owned streams. In total, 73,000 acres are closed to angling.

**Human pressure.** The state population exceeded five million and was projected to exceed ten million within thirty-five years. The Division estimated about 750,000 freshwater anglers, including over one-half million unlicensed youngsters, with about 1.5 million expected by the year 2000.

**Native and introduced fauna.** Only the [[Brook Trout]] is native to the state among trouts. [[Brown Trout]] (introduced), [[Rainbow Trout]] (introduced), [[Lake Trout]] (introduced in [[Quabbin Reservoir]]) and [[Kokanee]] (recently introduced in Onota Lake, Pittsfield) are stocked or established. Many warm-water species, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Carp]], were introduced, and the manual notes that introductions have sometimes been successful and sometimes not.

**Brackish and coastal species.** Anadromous and estuarine species are covered, including [[Sea Lamprey]], [[Atlantic Sturgeon]], [[American Shad]] and [[Alewife]]. Shad spawn in the [[Connecticut River]] from April to May.

## Key Findings

**Management philosophy**
- Fish production is limited by water fertility. Adding fish to a water body that already has a population increases competition, stunts growth and lowers yield. Removing fish can open a "virgin" habitat for restocking.
- Fish cannot be stockpiled. Closing a pond to fishing does not produce large fish; size reflects environment more than age. In most cases the problem is too many fish, not too few.
- Trout fishing in Massachusetts depends almost wholly on hatchery rearing and [[Put-And-Take Stocking]]. Natural trout reproduction is said to satisfy probably less than five percent of anglers.
- Warm-water fish are prolific. Reported egg counts per adult are 10,000–25,000 (bass), 10,000–50,000 (bluegill), 20,000–125,000 (crappie) and up to 2,000,000 (carp). The manual states that past stocking of millions of warm-water fish produced no improvement in creel returns.
- Stocking is warranted when a population has been removed, in new ponds, and after fish kills from winter oxygen deficiency, pollution or other causes.

**Growth data (Table 1 and text)**
- Growth is highly variable. Some trout stocked as 3-inch fingerlings reached 15 inches within twelve months in reclaimed ponds.
- White perch averaged 3 years to reach 8 inches, and some took 7 years to reach only 4 inches.
- Table 1 gives mean length and weight at the end of each growing season for several species. The authors caution that individual fish may depart from these averages.

**Survey example.** On a 150-acre pond, fry of largemouth bass, bluegill, rock bass and pumpkinseed exceeded 500,000 per acre, while the pond supported only a few hundred adults per acre.

**Species-specific angling and status notes**
- [[Chain Pickerel]] is one of the most heavily harvested species in the state and is taken year-round.
- [[Brown Bullhead]] is abundant and has no bag limit. [[Channel Catfish]] is migratory and prefers larger rivers, with the Connecticut River as its stronghold.
- [[Lake Trout]] has a minimum legal length of 18 inches and a daily bag of two fish.
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] is extremely rare; landlocked populations are likely extinct except in Quabbin Reservoir.
- [[White Perch]] is underharvested and has no bag limit.
- Several species are listed as rare or of uncertain status, including [[Northern Pike]], [[Redfin Pickerel]] and the Muskellunge.
