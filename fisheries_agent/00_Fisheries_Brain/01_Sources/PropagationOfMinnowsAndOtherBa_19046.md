# Propagation of Minnows and Other Bait Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service circular, prepared by J. R. Dobie, O. L. Meehean, and G. N. Washburn for the Tri-State Fishery Conference, is a practical guide to raising minnows and other bait species in ponds as a commercial venture. It was motivated by a shortage of wild bait minnows, tightening seining regulations, and rising angler demand in the northern states. The bulletin covers pond siting, construction and outlets, species selection, fertilization and artificial feeding, stocking with adults, eggs and fry, control of weeds, predators and disease, handling and holding tanks, and profiles of about 15 important bait species. It also summarizes small-scale culture of bait invertebrates such as earthworms, crayfish, crickets and insect larvae.

## Background & Description

The bulletin was assembled by a Tri-State Minnow Committee formed at the second annual Tri-State Fishery Conference in 1946. Contributors included agency biologists from Wisconsin, Minnesota, Michigan and the Fish and Wildlife Service. Its stated purpose is to give bait dealers a guide to rearing their own supply rather than relying on free-lance seining.

The text is organized into:

- **Pond design:** water supply, dams, drainage, outlets and catch basins, and natural ponds.
- **Species selection:** a list of 12 criteria, such as growth rate, seining tolerance, prolificacy and keeping quality.
- **Operation:** fertilization, feeding, stocking with brood fish, eggs and fry, and aquatic vegetation control.
- **Species accounts:** the 15 principal bait fishes, each with description, habitat, spawning, and food.
- **Invertebrate baits:** earthworms, crayfish, crickets, leeches and insect larvae, including [[Hellgrammite]], [[Caddis Fly Larvae]], [[May Fly Nymph]] and [[European Corn Borer]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Dependable, moderately hard, slightly alkaline water is preferred. Springs and artesian wells are the best sources. Surface supplies carry turbidity, silt, predators and dissolved gases such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide. Excess turbidity restricts light and reduces food production.

**Pond productivity.** Natural fertility depends largely on water hardness. Medium-hard to hard water produces more fish per acre than soft water. Winter oxygen depletion under ice and snow is a major hazard in shallow natural ponds, which should be at least 6 feet deep (15 feet in the Lake States, where freeze-out is a risk).

**Predators and competitors.** Herons, kingfishers, water snakes, turtles, muskrats, back-swimmers, water tigers, predatory fish and crayfish all reduce yields. Controls include oil films on the water, fencing, rotenone treatment and drainage.

**Parasites and disease.** Key concerns are fungus (*Saprolegnia parasitica*), fin rot, black grub (*Neascus*), the tapeworm *Ligula intestinalis* and the copepod *Lernaea*.

## Key Findings

- **Pond requirements:** four essentials are a dependable water supply, soil that holds water, independent pond units, and complete drainability. A drainage area of about 5 acres per acre-foot of pond water is recommended in high-rainfall regions, and more in drier areas such as southeastern Wisconsin and Minnesota.
- **Pond size and depth:** ponds of 1/4 to 1 acre are ideal. Propagation ponds should be 5 to 6 feet deep at the outlet, and winter holding ponds 10 to 12 feet deep in northern states. Dams should carry 2 to 3 feet of freeboard, and dam slopes are typically 3:1 upstream and 2:1 downstream.
- **Productivity:** fertilized or artificially fed ponds can yield far more fish per acre than natural ponds. The text cites more than 200,000 carp per acre in US ponds and over 200,000 fat-headed minnows per acre (328 pounds) in other states.
- **Fertilization:** barnyard manure at 400 to 1,000 pounds per acre, applied 2 weeks before stocking, is recommended. Commercial fertilizer at about 300 pounds per acre per season is applied every 2 weeks. A rule of thumb is to keep water coloured so a hand is not visible at 1 foot.
- **Artificial feeding:** feed only what fish consume in about 2 hours. Artificially fed ponds can support at least twice as many fish per acre. Populations over 2,000 pounds per acre need constant flow for aeration.
- **Stocking rates for adults:** recommended rates range from 500 to 5,000 adults per acre, depending on species and source (for example, 1,500 fat-headed minnows per acre per Raney, 1941).
- **Egg and fry stocking:** for suckers, natural-feeding ponds take 40,000 to 500,000 eyed eggs per acre, or 25,000 to 100,000 advanced fry. Artificially fed or fertilized ponds take 500,000 to 1,000,000 eggs or 100,000 to 300,000 fry per acre. For creek chub, the rates are 40,000 to 80,000 fry in natural ponds and 100,000 to 200,000 fry in fertilized or fed ponds.
- **Sucker survival:** Michigan studies found eyed-egg stocking returned no more than about 10 percent saleable bait (1 to 25 percent), while advanced fry returned about 50 percent.
- **Creek chub yields:** about 800 fry per female were expected in good raceway operations. Ohio returns were about 400 and 450 saleable fish per female in 1941 and 1942. Ponds could produce 100,000 to 200,000 saleable fish per acre per year with artificial feeding, but fewer than 50,000 on natural food.
- **Sex ratio:** a 50:50 brood-stock ratio is preferred, though more females are satisfactory for fat-headed minnows.
- **Pond yield example:** a Minnesota 5-acre golden shiner pond stocked at 70 per acre yielded 85 pounds per acre. A 1-acre pond stocked at 200 per acre yielded 245 pounds per acre. The 5-acre pond was understocked and produced the same number of young per female (about 650).
- **Loss causes:** losses are greatest during handling and holding. Main causes include crushing in nets, rapid temperature change, inadequate aeration, overcrowding, poor tank sanitation and fungus.
- **Temperature and oxygen limits:** non-aerated tanks should stay at 65° F or lower. Carrying tanks need at least 3 ppm dissolved oxygen, and minnows should not experience changes greater than 10° F without gradual tempering. Tempering requires 20 minutes per 10° change.
- **Hardening:** a 24-hour cold-water (50 to 60° F) conditioning period improves transport survival.
- **Disease treatment:** malachite green at 1:15,000 for 10 seconds is effective against fungus. Copper sulphate at 1:2,000 for 1 to 2 minutes treats fin rot, and formalin at 1:4,000 for 1 hour is an alternative. Sodium hypochlorite at 1:10,000 is used for tank sterilization.
- **Predator control:** rotenone at 1.32 pounds of 6 percent powder per acre-foot (about 0.5 ppm) is recommended where ponds cannot be drained.
- **Species profiles:** the 15 featured species are northern creek chub, northern pearl dace, horny-headed chub, river chub, western black-nosed dace, fine-scaled dace, northern and southern red-bellied dace, western golden shiner, lake emerald shiner, northern common shiner, spotfin shiner, brassy minnow, fat-headed minnow, blunt-nosed minnow, central stone-roller, common sucker, northern black bullhead, stone catfish and western mud minnow.
- **Preferred Lake States species:** suckers, creek chub, red-bellied dace, golden shiner, fat-headed minnow and blunt-nosed minnow.
- **Creek chub diet:** a study of 37 stomachs from the East and Midwest recorded insects at 51.3 percent and surface drift at 26.0 percent.
- **Golden shiner growth:** the species may reach a maximum length of 10 inches. Individuals have lived up to 8 years.
- **Invertebrate baits:** earthworms take about 3 months to reach bait size and 6 months to mature. Crickets in a 24-inch can can produce 400 crickets in 3 months. Crayfish from Ohio ponds reached about 2-3/8 inches by October 30.
- **Regulatory context:** the bulletin notes an impending legislative limit on minnow seining and states that the wild supply is being depleted.
