# Life History Strategies of Four Small-Size Fishes in the Suez Canal, Egypt

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae #cyprinodontidae #atherinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by M. M. Fouda (Zoology Department, Al-Azhar University, Cairo; published in the *Journal of Fish Biology*, 1995, 46: 687–702) examines the life histories of four small fishes on the sandy shores of the [[Suez Canal]]. Two species are of [[Red Sea]] origin ([[Silhouettea aegyptia]], [[Aphanius dispar]]) and two are [[Mediterranean Sea]] species ([[Pomatoschistus marmoratus]], [[Atherina boyeri]]). Over a ten-year sampling effort, the study tests how species of different origin share or partition resources after meeting in the canal. The principal conclusions are that salinity limits the distribution of Mediterranean species, that populations are dominated by a single age group with lifespans of 2 years or less, that the warm-water and temperate species spawn in different seasons, and that dietary and reproductive separation reduces competition.

## Background & Description

The Suez Canal has served as a corridor for fish migration since the early twentieth century. Tillier (1902) recorded 78 species in the canal, and later workers including Norman (1927), Chabanaud (1934), Tortonese (1947, 1952), Steinitz & Ben-Tuvia (1972) and El-Etreby (1986) documented further immigration. By the time of this study, 51 Red Sea species had invaded the eastern Mediterranean through the canal, a process known as [[Lessepsian Migration]].

The four focal species are small, short-lived fishes:

- **[[Silhouettea aegyptia]]** (Red Sea goby), a Red Sea gobiid.
- **[[Pomatoschistus marmoratus]]** (marbled goby), a Mediterranean gobiid.
- **[[Aphanius dispar]]** (Arabian toothcarp), a Red Sea cyprinodontid tolerant of high salinity.
- **[[Atherina boyeri]]** (big-scale sand smelt), a Mediterranean atherinid.

A fifth species, *Aphanius fasciatus* (a Mediterranean cyprinodontid), is discussed alongside *A. dispar*. It was restricted to the northern canal and hybridized with *A. dispar*. Earlier authors (Tortonese, 1947, 1952) reported hybridization between these two species, but Villwock (1987) could not confirm it.

The study asks three questions: do species of different origin share or compete for resources, do they retain their original biology, and what environmental factors limit their distribution and colonization.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** The canal is about 195 km long and passes through a series of lagoons. Key sites include:

- [[Lake Manzallah]]: approximately 1670 km², with a maximum depth rarely above 1 m. Bottom substrates are mud, sand, or bivalve shell.
- [[Lake Timsah]]: about 15 km long, with lagoons up to 3 m deep. Its substrate is mostly sand.
- [[Bitter Lakes]]: 36 km long, up to 13 km wide, and up to 15 m deep. Substrates range from rocky to muddy sand, often covered by algae and seagrasses. The Bitter Lakes act as a high-salinity barrier (about 44‰) and behave as a littoral lagoon of the Red Sea.

**Salinity.** Salinity is the main environmental variable in the study. It fluctuates seasonally and regionally:

- Lake Manzallah: 13.7‰ in May, rising to 38‰ in August, falling to winter lows of 6.7‰.
- Lake Timsah: 7.8‰ to 41.6‰, with strong stratification (fresh to brackish surface water, saline bottom layer).
- Bitter Lakes: about 44‰. Salinity has declined from 47–48‰ over the previous 20 years (Soliman et al., 1988).

**Temperature.** Water temperature reaches about 30 °C in June–September and falls to 12–16 °C in January–February.

**Sampling.** Between 1980 and 1985, fish were sampled at irregular intervals at Port Said, Port Fouad, El-Ballaha, Qantara, Ismailia, Deversoir, Fayid and Kabrit. From August 1985 to July 1988, monthly samples were collected at two sites on Lake Timsah and at Deversoir. Fish were taken at midday in water less than 1 m deep using a 3 × 1–2 m [[Seine Netting]] seine of 1 mm mesh. Each visit used four to five hauls of 15–20 min covering about 100 m² of shore. Totals were 31 samples from Timsah and Bitter Lakes, 11 from Manzallah and 13 from Port Fouad. Temperature, salinity, invertebrates, algae and seagrasses were also recorded.

## Key Findings

**1. Species assemblage.**
- 35 fish species were collected from sandy shores: 19 of Red Sea origin, 15 Mediterranean, and one Nile species (*Tilapia zillii*, freshwater).
- All species except *A. fasciatus* occurred in Timsah Lake, and 28 occurred in the Bitter Lake.
- Abundant species included *S. aegyptia*, *P. marmoratus*, *A. dispar*, *A. boyeri* and *T. zillii*. Many others were rare or common only seasonally.
- Juvenile commercial species (mugilids, sparids, moronids) were present, but adults rarely exceeded 10 cm.

**2. Distribution and salinity.**
- *S. aegyptia* and *A. dispar* were distributed throughout the canal system and reached the Bardawil lagoon.
- *A. fasciatus* was restricted to the northern canal.
- *P. marmoratus* and *A. boyeri* had not spread south beyond the Bitter Lakes.
- Salinity is the main limiting factor for Mediterranean species.

**3. Relative abundance.**
- Four focal species totalled 4846 specimens in Timsah Lake (in roughly equal proportions) and 2435 in the Bitter Lake.
- In the Bitter Lake, *A. dispar* made up 61%, *A. boyeri* 30%, *S. aegyptia* 7.0% and *P. marmoratus* 2%.
- *S. aegyptia* dominated Timsah Lake in 1986 (74.6%), but *P. marmoratus* dominated in 1987 and 1988.
- At Lake Manzallah, *A. fasciatus* (600 fish) outnumbered *A. dispar* (187) and hybrids (103). At Port Fouad, *A. dispar* (5917) far outnumbered *A. fasciatus* (23) and hybrids (365).

**4. Age and growth.**
- Ages were estimated from size-frequency distributions, because scales and otoliths showed no reliable annuli except in some older *A. boyeri*.
- Each population was dominated by a single age group. Lifespans did not exceed 2 years.
- Young *S. aegyptia* appeared in July–August, and young *P. marmoratus* in March–April. The previous year class disappeared by January (*S. aegyptia*) and by August–September (*P. marmoratus*).
- Oldest *S. aegyptia* were estimated at 17–21 months; oldest *P. marmoratus* at 17–20 months.
- *P. marmoratus* and *A. boyeri* grew rapidly in their first spring. *S. aegyptia* and *A. dispar* grew rapidly in their first summer and early autumn. Growth nearly stopped in late autumn and winter.

**5. Diet.** The four species were almost exclusively bottom feeders, although diet composition differed:
- *S. aegyptia* and *P. marmoratus* fed mainly on harpacticoid copepods, followed by polychaetes and invertebrate eggs.
- *A. dispar* fed on diatoms, blue-green algae, crustaceans, polychaetes and invertebrate eggs, and took much sand.
- *A. boyeri* concentrated on planktonic copepods, with amphipods and mysids as well.
- Indices of relative importance (IRI) for copepods were highest for *S. aegyptia* (IRI 8902) and *P. marmoratus* (4870).
- Consumption of harpacticoids rose with body size in *S. aegyptia* but fell in *P. marmoratus*. Polychaetes became more important in larger *P. marmoratus*.
- Diet varied seasonally (P < 0.01 for harpacticoids and polychaetes in *S. aegyptia* and *P. marmoratus*).

**6. Reproduction.**
- *S. aegyptia* spawns in summer and *P. marmoratus* in autumn–winter (Fouda et al., 1993).
- Male *A. dispar* and *A. boyeri* mature at smaller sizes than females. The smallest mature male *A. dispar* was 26 mm SL (collected 26 April), versus 34 mm for the smallest mature female. The smallest mature male *A. boyeri* was 22 mm SL, and the smallest female 27 mm SL (February).
- Age at first maturity for both *A. dispar* and *A. boyeri* was probably under 1 year.
- Female *A. dispar* bred from March to September, with peak [[Gonadosomatic Index]] of 19.4 in May. Male GSI peaked at 2.5 in April.
- Female *A. boyeri* bred from January to the end of March, with peak GSI of 5.3 in February. Male GSI peaked at 4.4 (February) and 4.5 (March).
- *A. dispar* oocytes showed several size modes from summer to September, indicating continuous spawning.

**7. Fecundity.**
- *A. dispar* fecundity ranged from 21 to 367 ripe eggs (mean 113; n = 45).
- *A. boyeri* fecundity ranged from 12 to 68 (mean 38; n = 58).
- [[Fecundity]] relationships were curvilinear against standard length, body weight and ovary weight. For *A. dispar*, log F = 0.066 + 2.67 log L (r = 0.77). For *A. boyeri*, log F = 0.0154 + 1.369 log L (r = 0.85).

**8. Community and niche partitioning.**
- The study interprets the coexistence of these four species as niche partitioning. Warm-water species spawn in summer, temperate species in autumn–winter. Diet diversity further separates them ([[Niche Partitioning]]).
- Competition was judged to be minimal, given abundant food. Competition would be most likely in autumn, when *P. marmoratus* ate few harpacticoids and polychaetes, but at that time it switched to other food items.
- Seasonal abundance changes were attributed to recruitment, mortality and movement. Juveniles move to deeper water (>1 m) in late autumn and return to shallows in early spring.

**9. Conclusions.** Salinity is the major factor limiting fish distribution in the canal. Resident species such as *S. aegyptia*, *P. marmoratus*, *A. dispar* and *A. boyeri* show that the lakes are suitable habitats, not just passageways for migrants. Their small size, opportunistic feeding, short lifespans and extended breeding seasons with repeated spawning help them exploit canal shore habitats ([[Euryhaline Species]]).
