# Carp, River Carpsucker, Smallmouth Buffalo and Bigmouth Buffalo in Lewis and Clark Lake, Missouri River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service research report (Research Report 69, 1966) by Charles H. Walburg and William R. Nelson examines age composition, growth, length-weight relation, reproduction, year-class strength and food habits of four large-bodied fishes in [[Lewis and Clark Lake]], a [[Missouri River]] reservoir impounded in July 1955. Data were collected from 1962 to 1964. All four species grew slowly, which the authors attributed to low standing crops of plankton and bottom fauna caused by excessive turbidity and rapid water exchange. Strong year classes appeared in the two years after impoundment, but reproduction has been poor since then, most probably because water levels did not rise during the spawning period.

## Background & Description

The study was part of the North Central Reservoir Investigations program, which examines how [[Water Management Operations]] and other environmental factors affect main stem Missouri River reservoir fish populations. Studies began on Lewis and Clark Lake in 1962 and were extended to [[Oahe Reservoir]] and [[Big Bend Reservoir]] (Lake Sharpe) in 1964.

The four species studied:

- **Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*), one of the two most abundant fishes in the reservoir.
- **River carpsucker** (*Carpiodes carpio*), the other most abundant fish.
- **Smallmouth buffalo** (*Ictiobus bubalus*) and **bigmouth buffalo** (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), both much less abundant. None was sport-caught or commercially fished.

The reservoir is the smallest and southernmost of six main stem [[Missouri River Mainstem Reservoirs]] built by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]]:

- Maximum surface area 28,000 acres; length 21.2 miles; average width 2.1 miles.
- Maximum depth 58 ft; average depth 16 ft; shoreline 50 miles; storage 450,000 acre-feet.
- Lake level held between 1,204 and 1,208 ft above mean sea level; weekly fluctuations rarely exceed 1 ft.
- Bottom mostly silt, with isolated sand, gravel and rubble. The [[Niobrara River]] (mean flow 1,750 c.f.s.) deposits sand and silt at the upper end.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water exchange:** The entire storage is passed through the dam every 8 to 10 days during navigation season (March to November); in winter, exchange is possible every 30 days.
- **Temperature and chemistry:** Water rarely exceeds 28° C (82° F); no thermocline. Summer dissolved oxygen usually exceeds 80 percent saturation. Total alkalinity 140 to 177 p.p.m.; pH 7.8 to 8.2; total hardness 240 to 290 p.p.m.; conductivity 650 to 840 micromhos at 25° C.
- **Turbidity:** Summer turbidity ranged from 25 to 675 p.p.m., lowest near the dam (mean 80 p.p.m.) and highest in the headwaters (mean 180 p.p.m.). Secchi visibility ranged from 8 in. (mean 7 in.) in the upper reservoir to 38 in. (mean 24 in.) near the dam. Wind-driven wave action and Niobrara inflow drive turbidity.
- **Plankton and benthos:** Zooplankton averaged fewer than two organisms per liter (July to September 1964). Bottom fauna averaged 11 kg/ha wet weight (July to October 1962), dominated by [[Hexagenia]] mayflies, which rose to 29 kg/ha by 1964.
- **Missouri River reach:** The 50-mile section below the reservoir to [[Fort Randall Dam]] carries 17,000 to 40,000 c.f.s. (average about 25,000 c.f.s.) from March to November.

### Methods (summary)

- Sampling April to October 1962 to 1964 using gill nets, frame nets, trawls, bag seines, electroshocking and trap nets.
- Ages were read from scales using an [[Eberbach Projector]]; growth was back-calculated by the [[Fraser-Lee Method]] (Fraser, 1916), with [[Hile Method]] used for mean growth deviation.
- Stomach contents were analyzed microscopically, with volume determined by [[Centrifuged Volume]] comparison.

## Key Findings

### Carp
- Sample: 3,136 fish measured in 1963 (23 to 614 mm); 911 aged, ages from young-of-the-year to age group XIII.
- Body-scale relation (1,639 fish): L = 18.4 + 1.59R (R = anterior scale radius, mm × 27).
- Growth declined steadily after impoundment until 1962, when it improved for the first time. Calculated length at age VII was 60 mm less than in preimpoundment year classes.
- Length-weight (grouped, 1,090 fish): log W = −4.40758 + 2.79858 log L.
- Maturity: males at age IV, females at age V; all males and 95 percent of females mature by age VIII.
- Spawning occurred June 15 to July 15, mostly mid-June, at 18.3° to 23.9° C.
- Diet (267 stomachs): organic detritus 61 percent by volume, insects 19 percent, microcrustaceans 10 percent, phytoplankton 9 percent. Young-of-the-year fed more on phytoplankton and copepods.

### River Carpsucker
- Sample: 982 fish aged (16 to 496 mm in 1963; ages young-of-the-year to XII).
- Body-scale relation (1,488 fish): L = 25.80 + 1.955R.
- Growth was slower than in [[Oahe Reservoir]], [[Fort Randall Reservoir]], [[Grand Lake]] and [[Lake Texoma]].
- Length-weight (grouped, 487 fish): log W = −4.72625 + 2.91662 log L.
- Maturity: some at age IV, more than half at age V, all by age VIII.
- Spawning June 18 to July 12 at 21° to 24° C; not directly observed in this study.
- Diet (168 stomachs): organic detritus 69 percent, zooplankton 14 percent, phytoplankton 3 percent, insects 3 percent. Sand and silt presence indicates bottom feeding.

### Smallmouth Buffalo
- Sample: 1964 collections; 86 percent of fish from age groups VI to VIII (year classes 1956 to 1958).
- Growth slower than in Oahe, Fort Randall, Grand Lake and [[Watts Bar Reservoir]].
- Length-weight (139 fish): log W = −4.94471 + 3.02043 log L.
- Males matured earlier and smaller than females; some males at age VII, all by IX; females at X to XI.
- Maturity appears governed by size rather than age.
- Spawning probably May to June at 16.7° to 21.1° C.
- Diet (271 adults): zooplankton 55 percent by volume, phytoplankton 31 percent, insects 8 percent.

### Bigmouth Buffalo
- Sample: 1964 collections, five age groups; most fish from age groups VII and VIII (year classes 1956 and 1957).
- Length-weight (187 fish): log W = −5.55997 + 3.27604 log L.
- Maturity: some females (25 percent) at age VIII; most (67 percent) at age IX. Maturation was much slower than in Saskatchewan or Alabama reports.
- Year-class composition in 1964: 1955 (1.6 percent), 1956 (66.0 percent), 1957 (25.4 percent), 1958 (1.0 percent), 1964 (6.0 percent).
- Spawning first observed May 20, 1964 (ripe female); water 16.7° to 20.0° C.
- Diet: zooplankton 100 percent of volume in young (261 stomachs) and adults (125 fish); copepods and cladocerans dominated.

### Discussion and Conclusions
- Growth of all four species was slower than in comparable reservoirs, especially [[Fort Randall Reservoir]] and Oahe Reservoir, which are deeper and clearer. Carp grew slowest and ate more plant material and detritus, which have low nutritive value.
- The authors concluded that limited food supply, caused by high turbidity and rapid water exchange, produced slow growth.
- Strong year classes followed impoundment in 1955 to 1957; year classes after 1958 were weak.
- Successful reproduction appears to depend on rising water levels during the spawning period. Levels were stable or falling in the June-August period after 1957, while the early years of flooding of lowlands and grass areas coincided with strong year classes.
- Oahe data supported the flooding effect: 92 percent of carp and 96 percent of bigmouth buffalo in fall 1963 trap catches came from the 1962 year class, the year water rose 18 ft.
- The primary factor limiting reproduction was probably lack of water-level rise during spawning. Management recommendations were not explicitly stated beyond maintaining flooding during spawning.
