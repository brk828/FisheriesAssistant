# Status of the Genus Gila in Grand Canyon National Park

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #native-fish #dam-impacts

## Executive Summary

This final report documents the status of three native chub species (genus *Gila*) in [[Grand Canyon National Park]] during 1977-1979: the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), and [[Colorado Chub]] (*G. robusta*). The [[Humpback Chub]] persists in localized populations concentrated near the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence (River Mile 61.5), while the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Colorado Chub]] are considered extinct or exceedingly rare in the Grand Canyon. The study provides critical baseline data on distribution, age and growth, relative density, reproduction, and parasitism of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], documenting threats from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, water fluctuations, and the parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinaceae*.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* is represented by seven species and four subspecies in Arizona. Three species historically occurred in the [[Grand Canyon]] region:

**[[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)**: First collected in [[Glen Canyon]] in 1960 prior to dam impoundment. Collections by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] at Glen Canyon Damsite (1961-1963) and below the dam (1966) represent the only modern records. Currently considered extinct or exceedingly rare in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Listed in Group IV (species of special interest due to limited distribution) by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]].

**[[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)**: Historically occurred throughout the mainstream [[Colorado River]]. Earliest record is non-fossilized remains from 4,000-year-old flood deposits in [[Stanton's Cave]], approximately 30 miles downstream from [[Lee Ferry]]. Single specimens taken by anglers from [[Bright Angel Creek]] and [[Phantom Creek]] (1942, 1944). One specimen from [[Spencer Creek]] confluence (1955). Currently considered extinct or exceedingly rare in Grand Canyon, though persists in [[Lake Mohave]]. Proposed for federal endangered status; listed in Group II (species in danger of elimination from Arizona) by Arizona.

**[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: Endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Not reported before the 1940s due to restriction to inaccessible canyon areas. Type locality is [[Bright Angel Creek]] (River Mile 87.5) based on one specimen taken by angler in 1942. Historical remains found in riverine deposits at [[Stanton's Cave]]. Collected in [[Little Colorado River]] in early 1900s by Kolb brothers who used them for food.

The [[Humpback Chub]] is distinguished by its distinctive dorsal hump and specialized morphology adapted to swift canyon waters. Age determination uses the opercle method, which shows regular annuli increase correlated with total length. A highly significant correlation (r = 0.956, p < 0.01) exists between total length and primary opercle radius, described by the equation O = -0.734 + 0.055 × L.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**: [[Humpback Chub]] populations are concentrated in swift-water canyon habitats, particularly near tributary confluences. The [[Little Colorado River]] confluence (River Mile 61.5) serves as the major spawning locality and nursery area for the species in the lower [[Colorado River]] system. Other concentration areas include Tiger Wash (River Mile 27.5).

**Distribution**: Current distribution (1977-1979) extends from River Mile 16 to River Mile 194, with most frequent encounters above the [[Little Colorado River]] mouth. The species occurs sporadically in localized areas. One specimen collected 13 km upstream in the [[Little Colorado River]] (1977) represents the furthest upstream tributary record.

**Food Habits**: Analysis of three juvenile specimens revealed principal food items consist of benthic insect larvae (Chironomidae, Ceratopogonidae, Dolichopodidae) and organic detritus, indicating benthic foraging behavior on or near substrate.

**Reproduction**: Spawning activities were not directly observed. Reproductive behavior inferred from related species ([[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Colorado Chub]]). Young-of-the-year first appeared in June sampling at approximately 30 mm total length.

**Dam Impacts**: Construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has profoundly altered the aquatic ecosystem:
- Changed historical flow regimes and water temperatures
- Destroyed backwater areas and normal water fluctuations
- Blocked spawning runs
- Maintained water temperatures below spawning requirements
- Created daily water fluctuations from hydroelectric operations (highest releases during summer peak power demand)

The [[Paria River]] at its confluence illustrates extreme impacts: 35 m wide and 2 m deep at high water, reduced hours later to a mud flat with 1 m wide, 4 cm deep channel. These fluctuations expose shoreline daily, reduce cover for smaller fishes, increase predation susceptibility, and expose juveniles to colder mainstream water and stronger currents prematurely.

**Parasitism**: The external copepod *Lernaea cyprinaceae* represents a significant threat. First encountered October 1978 on [[Humpback Chub]] from [[Little Colorado River]] with very high infestation rates. Geographic origin uncertain; possible sources include upper reaches of [[Kanab Creek]] and [[Little Colorado River]], transport via runoff, movement from [[Lake Mead]] via parasitized carp, or from [[Lee Ferry]] via infested hatchery trout. The thermophilic nature of *L. cyprinaceae* restricts successful reproduction to relatively warm habitats like the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Kanab Creek]].

## Key Findings

**Age and Growth**: Sample of 10 specimens represented four age classes (IV, V, VI, IX). Largest specimen was 9 years old, 380 mm total length. Growth patterns:
- Year 1: Rapid growth, mean 86.0 mm (range 80.5-92.2 mm)
- Year 2: Slowed to mean 39.7 mm increment
- Years 3-6: Fluctuated between 46.6 mm (year 3) and 30.4 mm (year 5)
- Years 7-9: Steady decline to 18.6 mm (year 9)

Young-of-the-year reached approximately 80-90 mm by May of first year. Two size peaks occurred in July-August sampling: 170 mm and 330 mm, corresponding to mean lengths of 3-year (172.1 mm) and 8-year (328.7 mm) fish.

**Relative Density**: Catch per unit effort varied by location and method:

*Mainstream trammel nets*: 0.5 fish/hour (f/h) in winter to 2 f/h in summer

*Little Colorado River confluence*: Present during each trip, largest numbers in spring and summer
- Electrofishing: Generally unsuccessful, maximum 5 f/h recorded twice
- Drifting gill nets: Sporadic success, 5.3 f/h recorded; individual drifts occasionally captured over 20 individuals
- Angling: 4.3 f/h, demonstrating susceptibility to angling
- Gill net sets: 35.2 f/h (most productive method)
- Standard trammel nets: Up to 70 f/h

*Seining collections* (young-of-the-year and juveniles):
- Spring: 721 fish/hectare (f/ha) at 0.1 km upstream station
- Summer: 2,473 f/ha at 0.1 km station; 223 f/ha at confluence; 2,300-6,364 f/ha at 8 km upstream
- Fall: 990 f/ha at 0.1 km station

**Distribution Records**: Table 1 summarizes 40+ collecting localities from archaeological remains (4,000 years B.P.) through October 1979. Key findings:
- Furthest upstream specimen since 1970: River Mile 16
- Range extended downstream to River Mile 194 (86 miles beyond previous records)
- One yearling (70 mm total length) taken at [[Granite Rapid]] (April 1979) represents first juvenile from mainstream since 1970

**Parasitism Incidence**: *Lernaea cyprinaceae* infestation on [[Humpback Chub]] in [[Little Colorado River]]:
- October 1978: 53.8% of 65 fish examined (1-5 parasites per fish)
- April 1979: 7.4% of 27 fish examined (1 parasite per fish)
- July 1979: 2.3% of 87 fish examined (1 parasite per fish)
- October 1979: 5.5% of 29 fish examined (1-2 parasites per fish)

Attachment locations (October 1978): pectoral fins (31.4%), pelvic fins (31.4%), dorsal fins (23.5%), body wall (7.8%), anal fin (3.9%), opercle (2.0%). High occurrence on juveniles in the [[Little Colorado River]] nursery area is of particular concern.

**Threats and Management Implications**:
1. Daily water fluctuations from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations expose spawning areas, destroy eggs and fry, prevent spawner access to preferred areas, and destroy nursery areas
2. Dam construction in [[Little Colorado River]] would be "devastating to the humpback chub population in Grand Canyon, leading to its extinction"
3. Parasitism by *L. cyprinaceae* may induce stress within the population, potentially resulting in further population decline
4. The species currently persists at low levels; continued existence depends on efforts of administrative agencies
5. "The alternative, extinction of the humpback chub within the State of Arizona, is unacceptable"

**Conservation Status**: The [[Humpback Chub]] is federally listed as endangered. The [[Bonytail Chub]] is proposed for endangered status. Both species demonstrate the vulnerability of native fishes to ecosystem modifications from dam construction, altered flow regimes, and introduction of exotic biota. The [[Little Colorado River]] represents critical habitat as the major spawning and nursery area for [[Humpback Chub]] in the lower [[Colorado River]] system.
