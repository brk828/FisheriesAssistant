## Executive Summary

This 1974 report to the American Smelting and Refining Corporation evaluates the physico-chemical and biological condition of [[Cocio Wash]], Pima County, Arizona — a desert stream system receiving seepage water from tailings at the Silverbell Mine — with special focus on a population of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*). Researchers W.L. Minckley and George O. Constantz sampled four stations (north seepage, south seepage, junction of seepages, and the main Cocio Wash habitat) over 24-hour diel cycles, measuring dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, and heavy metal concentrations. While the main topminnow habitat supported an apparently healthy, reproducing fish and invertebrate community, the tailings seepage points—particularly the south seepage directly at the base of the tailings slope—showed signs of chemical oxygen demand, near-sterile biological conditions, and elevated heavy metals (notably lead and copper) exceeding known tolerance limits for some fish species. The authors conclude that although no acute biological damage was detected during the study period, the tailings seepage represents a potential long-term threat to the persistence of this fish population, one of only 5–6 known natural populations of the species remaining at the time.

## Background & Description

- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): a small, live-bearing poeciliid fish.
- Observed pathology: **separation of the epithelial layers of the interradial membranes of the caudal fin** in some adult topminnows at the south seepage sampling site — a condition the authors have previously associated with waters of high hydrogen ion concentration (low/acidic pH).
- Note: the measured pH of 5.5 at this site was considered by the authors insufficiently low, based on their prior experience, to fully explain this fin damage, leaving the causal mechanism somewhat unresolved.
- No detailed morphometric or meristic data on the topminnow population are provided in this report; the focus is on habitat condition rather than individual fish morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary habitat**: Perennial reach of [[Cocio Wash]] downstream of mine seepage inputs, supporting a "normal biological community."
- **Riparian vegetation**: dense stands of [[Tamarix Pentandra]] (tamarisk/saltcedar), *Salix* sp. (willows), and *Typha angustifolia* (cattails).
- **Co-occurring aquatic fauna**:
  - [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) — a characteristic Sonoran Desert fish species co-occurring with the topminnow.
  - Larval and adult [[Rana Pipiens]] (leopard frogs).
  - Aquatic insect larvae, especially Chironomidae (true flies), immediately downstream of the south seepage origin.
  - Numerous birds and desert mammals utilizing the wash as a water source.
- **Threats to niche**:
  - Historical primary driver of Gila topminnow decline across its range: introduction of the predaceous, competitive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) (see Schoenherr 1974).
  - Secondary/contributing factors: habitat drying, overgrazing, siltation, and spring capping (Minckley 1969, 1973; Minckley & Deacon 1968).
  - At Cocio Wash specifically: tailings seepage from the Silverbell Mine introduces low dissolved oxygen, elevated temperature, chemical oxygen demand, iron precipitates, and elevated heavy metal concentrations (lead, copper) into portions of the wash system.
  - Surface waters for ~11 miles downstream of the main Cocio Wash sampling station were found to be biologically sterile, most likely drying seasonally in summer.
- **Population context**: At the time of the report, only 5–6 natural populations of Gila topminnow were known to persist; few reintroduction attempts had succeeded (Minckley 1969). The species was listed as endangered both nationally (1972) and internationally (Miller 1969, 1972).

## Key Findings

- **Dissolved oxygen (DO) patterns** varied strongly by station:
  - *North seepage*: Minimal diel DO fluctuation (6–7 mg/L), indicating a relatively sterile biological system, possibly due to chemical influence rather than biological (photosynthetic/respiratory) control.
  - *South seepage*: Consistently very low DO with little diel variation, indicating strong **chemical oxygen demand** within the tailings material itself; accompanied by relatively high, stable temperatures and a flocculent iron precipitate characteristic of de-oxygenated water where dissolved iron oxidizes upon reaching aerobic conditions. Discharge estimated at ~0.3 cubic feet per second.
  - *Junction of north/south seepages*: An anomalous DO curve nearly inverse to typical biologically-controlled systems, suggesting strong chemical oxygen demand active in the presence of light; a whitish flocculent precipitate on the substrate suggested an undetected chemical shift, likely in pH. Possible unaccounted-for inflow (e.g., underflow from northwest) may explain elevated heavy metal readings at this site.
  - *Main Cocio Wash habitat*: Displayed a **normal, biologically-controlled diel DO curve**, consistent with a healthy, functioning aquatic ecosystem supporting fish and invertebrate life.
- **Temperature**: Followed a normal diel cycle at the main habitat and north seepage sampling point (supporting the aeration/distance hypothesis for the north seepage), but showed little fluctuation and elevated baseline values at the south seepage, attributed to ongoing chemical reactions within the tailings.
- **Heavy metals and water chemistry** (Table 1, comparing to literature-derived lethal limits for various fish species and Salt River tailings-lake data):
  - **Lead and copper** concentrations at the main Cocio Wash habitat were found to already exceed tolerance levels documented for *some* fish species (not necessarily Gila topminnow, whose specific tolerances remain undetermined).
  - Elevated concentrations of several ions/elements at the downstream main habitat station were attributed to **evapotranspirative concentration**, not toxic seepage inputs per se.
  - Mercury was detected only sporadically and at trace levels (near the 0.001 mg/L detection limit) in seepage and junction samples.
  - pH was recorded as low as 5.5 at the south seepage site.
- **Biological impact assessment**: Despite chemical stressors, "little evidence of biological damage was detected" during the study period at the main topminnow habitat. However, the authors caution that:
  - Long-term genetic damage or selection for metal/ion-tolerant individuals is possible but unstudied.
  - Bioaccumulation/biomagnification of measured elements through the food web was not assessed due to time and budget constraints.
  - The tailings seepage from the Silverbell Mine "may be a long-term danger" to animal populations in Cocio Wash even though acute toxicity thresholds had not yet been reached at the time of sampling.
- **Downstream conditions**: Surface waters approximately 11 miles below the main Cocio Wash station were found to be sterile, likely due to seasonal drying rather than continued chemical contamination.
