# Sonic Tracking of Striped Bass in the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 study by Koo and Wilson, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, used [[Sonic Telemetry]] to follow five adult [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) through the [[Chesapeake and Delaware Canal]] in April and May 1971. Transmitters were placed in the stomachs of the fish, and movements were tracked for up to about 4 days and nights. The study reports that tags were retained, signal reception was reliable, and the fish moved in a "[[Rest-and-Go Movement]]" pattern with long pauses. Spawning-stage differences and nocturnal activity were noted. The authors state that five fish are too few to support firm conclusions, so the results are presented as preliminary observations.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The canal links [[Delaware Bay]] and [[Chesapeake Bay]], about 41.8 km (26 miles) long. Its eastern half is fully artificial, and the rest is dredged from natural waterways. The canal was built in 1824–1829 with a width of 11 m and depth of 3 m. It was converted to a sea-level canal from 1921 to 1927, enlarged to 76.2 m by 8.2 m by 1938, and enlargement to 137.2 m by 10.7 m began in 1958 and was about 80% complete at the time of writing. Tidal flow is generally eastward toward Delaware. Salinity increases from the Chesapeake end to the Delaware end and is stratified vertically, with higher salinity near the bottom. See [[Salinity Gradient]] and [[Tidal Flow]].

**Species.** The striped bass is anadromous, spawning in fresh water and feeding in salt water. The authors describe it as the most important fish in Chesapeake Bay, both commercially and recreationally. Earlier conventional tagging suggested that Chesapeake-origin fish follow a coastwise migration, and [[Nichols and Miller]] (1967) proposed that Potomac River fish leave the bay through the canal.

**Tagging method.** Tags were [[Smith-Root SR-69B Transmitter]] units measuring 65 × 14 mm, weighing 12.0 g in water, powered by six 1.4 V mercury batteries (maximum life 14 days in the authors' tests). Transmission was at 74 kHz ±1.5 kHz with 0.5–8.0 pulses per second. Signals were received with a unidirectional [[Smith-Root SR-70H Hydrophone]] connected to a TA-60 receiver. The maximum tracking range was 1.6 km (1 mile), with a detection angle under 2° at that distance. Tags were inserted into the stomach rather than fastened externally, following a comparison with [[Shad Migration]] and [[Chinook Salmon]] studies. Holding trials with dummy tags showed no shedding in seven adult bass over 2 weeks, with no stomach irritation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Movement and behavior.** Fish moved in bursts separated by long rests, often in response to tidal change. Some fish drifted with the tide, while others held position against currents of up to about three knots. The authors found no clear, consistent response to current direction.
- **Diel activity.** The spent female was more active at night. Both canal banks are lit by [[Mercury Vapor Lighting]] at roughly 500-foot intervals, and she often paused for 10–15 minutes at each light, which the authors suggest may reflect feeding on light-attracted prey. They caution that nocturnal activity may be an artifact of the lighting.
- **Ship traffic.** Ships using the canal mostly at night briefly interrupted signal reception. Fish did not appear disturbed and signals were typically recovered afterward. See [[Ship Traffic]].
- **Habitat use.** The authors conclude that the canal functions as a migration corridor in both directions, as a resting area, and possibly as a feeding area.

## Key Findings

**Study design and sample**
- Five adult striped bass were tracked: three prespawning (two ripe males, one ripening female) and two postspawning (spent females).
- Fork lengths ranged from 470 to 908 mm.
- Tracking was continuous for up to about 4 days and nights, using boats with [[Radar Navigation]] for positioning in darkness.

**Tracking results (Table 1)**

| 2 | 908 | Female, ripening | 11 h 13 min | Drifted 16 km east in 6.5 h; entered the Delaware River and tracked 2.2 km south |
| 3 | 525 | Male, ripe | 8 h 20 min | Held overnight, then followed a path similar to Exp. 2; recaptured 10 days later at the western entrance |
| 4 | 825 | Female, spent | 4 h 22 min | Traveled 12 km; signal lost twice and regained; then lost permanently |

**Notable observations**
- **Experiments 2 and 3:** Both fish came from the same gillnet haul. Although Exp. 3 was held one day before release, the two fish followed nearly identical paths, timing, and direction. Exp. 3 paused for about 2 hours after release, while the tide was still flowing west.
- **Experiment 3 recapture:** The tagged male was caught again 10 days later at the western entrance of the canal, showing that it had moved back through the full canal length, likely to spawn.
- **Experiment 1 caveat:** The fish was caught at the western entrance but released about 21 km inside the canal, so the authors do not consider its movement necessarily natural.
- **Experiment 5 loss:** The signal was recovered a day later at nearly the same spot, but a receiver failure ended tracking. Two days of searching found nothing, and the authors believe the fish left the canal.

**Interpretations and conclusions**
- Striped bass use the canal as a two-way route for spawning migration, and also as a resting and possibly feeding area.
- Prespawning fish moved more consistently than postspawning fish, which lingered in the canal and rested longer, especially during the day.
- The stomach-tagging method was judged satisfactory: no signal loss from body-wall attenuation was observed, and no tag shedding occurred. The authors suggest the difference from Hallock et al.'s findings on chinook salmon may relate to transducer shape (tubular here, flat disc in the salmon tag). Tag-to-stomach size ratio is likely important for shedding.
- The authors note that five fish are insufficient for firm behavioral or migratory conclusions.
