# Respiratory Function of Gills in Ambystoma punctatum Larvae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 experimental study by Boell, Greenfield, and Hille, published in *Developmental Biology*, tests whether the external gills of [[Spotted Salamander]] (*Ambystoma punctatum*) larvae are essential for respiration. Using [[Gill Removal Surgery]] and [[Warburg Manometry]] on larvae of different developmental stages, the authors found that gill-less larvae consume oxygen at rates indistinguishable from normal larvae in water saturated with 21%, 15% and 10% oxygen. Gill-less larvae only showed reduced respiration at 5% oxygen, where they were often paralysed or died. The authors conclude that gills are not essential for normal oxygen uptake under ordinary conditions, and that hemoglobin has limited significance in oxygen transport during early larval development.

## Background & Description

The study addresses an apparent paradox: salamander larval gills are structurally well suited to respiration, yet earlier work suggested they can be dispensed with. Prior research established that larvae reared in low-oxygen water develop much larger gills, with [[Bond 1960 Gill Response Study|Bond (1960)]] reporting roughly a threefold increase in gill surface across three salamander species. Earlier experiments by [[Harrison 1921 Gill Development Study|Harrison (1921)]] and [[Severinghaus 1930 Gill Development Study|Severinghaus (1930)]] showed that *A. punctatum* and *A. tigrinum* larvae developed essentially normally after experimental interference with gill development. Gill-less larvae were also reported to match normal larvae in swimming performance.

Study animals were eggs of *A. punctatum* collected near New Haven, reared in fresh spring water at about 23°C and fed on *Daphnia* or enchytraeid worms. Observations covered roughly two months from stage 38, when gills are proportionately largest. Respiration was measured in Warburg manometers at 25°C, with each animal serving as its own control.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work treats oxygen availability as the key environmental variable. Experimental media were air-saturated water and gas mixtures of 50%, 21%, 15%, 10% and 5% oxygen, with 100% oxygen used in one comparison. Authors note that 5% oxygen is at or near the lower limit of tolerance for *A. punctatum* larvae, and that Bond (1960) reported the same minimum for *A. opaca* and *A. jeffersonianum*. Long-term exposure to pure oxygen or 50% oxygen caused gills to shrivel and thicken.

The authors contrast amphibian larvae with organisms that have blood respiratory pigments, many of which regulate oxygen consumption across a wide range of oxygen concentrations. They also compare the larval blood with that of [[Bullfrog Tadpole]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) and [[Leopard Frog Tadpole]] (*Rana pipiens*), which show low hemoglobin loading tensions and oxygen-independent respiration down to low oxygen levels. Blood-free or hemoglobin-deficient animals such as [[Icefish]] (Chaenichthyidae), hagfish and *Xenopus* are cited as evidence that oxygen transport can be sustained without hemoglobin.

## Key Findings

- **Gills are not essential for respiration under normal conditions.** Gill removal at stage 46 had no measurable effect on oxygen consumption, either immediately or after recovery. Across four comparison pairs in air-saturated water, gill-less rates were 92–105% of control rates (for example, 3.5 vs 3.5 µl/larva/h; 20.0 vs 20.8 µl/larva/h).
- **Respiration is maintained at 21%, 15% and 10% oxygen.** In the dose-response curve (Fig. 2), gill-less and normal larvae showed essentially identical oxygen consumption across this range. At 10% oxygen, normal and gill-less larvae respired at 89% and 87% of their air values, respectively.
- **Gill-less larvae are impaired at 5% oxygen.** Gill-less larvae respired at 39% of the air value, compared with 81% for normal larvae. Gill-less animals were paralysed, had gas-filled stomachs, floated upside down, and several were dead the next morning.
- **Sample sizes in the oxygen series:** normal larvae were measured in 8 (100% O₂), 11 (50%), 15 (21%), 71 (10%) and 16 (5%) observations; gill-less larvae in 0, 4 (50%), 27 (21%), 33 (10%) and 16 (5%) observations.
- **Hemoglobin contribution is limited in early development.** Carbon monoxide exposure (20% CO, 20% O₂, 60% N₂) did not affect respiration from stage 38 to about stage 43. Above stage 43, respiration was increasingly depressed, suggesting that hemoglobin begins to take over oxygen transport around that stage. Before then, oxygen is likely carried mainly in solution.
- **Gill functions beyond respiration are plausible.** The authors suggest gills may be involved in ion uptake and excretion of inorganic materials or metabolic products, but these functions were not tested.
- **Overall conclusion:** gills may contribute to respiratory exchange but are not essential for normal oxygen uptake under ordinary circumstances, and hemoglobin has limited significance for oxygen transport in these larvae.
