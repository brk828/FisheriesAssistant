# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes, Fort Apache and San Carlos Indian Reservations, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This 1934 field survey by the [[Bureau of Fisheries]], authored by M. J. Madsen (Temporary Assistant Biologist), assessed the physical, chemical, and biological conditions of the streams and lakes on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] and [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in east-central Arizona. The survey was conducted August 3 to 14, 1934, and covered all permanent and important waters in both reservations. Its purpose was to provide data for choosing the species, size, and number of fish to stock, and to identify where habitat changes could increase fish production. The report is explicitly a condition survey, not a complete study of any water body.

The main conclusions are that the streams are generally unproductive for aquatic plants and invertebrate food, that trout are the principal game fish, and that stocking is needed to maintain fishing. The 1934 drought had dried much of the stream network, and the superintendents suspended fishing and stocking that year. The report recommends a stocking-replacement system and provides a stocking table.

## Background & Description

**Survey party and timing.** The party comprised M. J. Madsen (biologist in charge), Paul Klingebiel and Harry G. Moseley (assistant biologists), and W. M. Hudspeth (camp attendant). The party was supported by the Arizona State Fish and Game Commission, which provided deputy game agents and boats, and by the Indian Service, which supplied maps.

**Study area.** The reservations cover roughly 4,150 square miles (OCR-ambiguous figure) and contain all the headwaters of the [[San Carlos River]] and about 80 percent of the headwaters of the [[Salt River]]. Elevations range from 2,415 feet at [[Coolidge Lake]] (Coolidge Reservoir) to 8,000 feet at the Mogollon Rim. Except for the northeast portion of Fort Apache, the area is semi-desert.

**Survey objectives.** The four stated aims were to:
1. Determine biological, chemical, and physical conditions of streams and lakes.
2. Use these data to select the species, size, and number of fish best suited to each water.
3. Demonstrate where and how alterations of the environment could increase production.
4. Identify special problems needing further study.

**Methods.** Field work used the following approaches:
- **Physical:** widths and depths measured with rule and steel tape. Altitude and gradient came from USGS and Indian Service maps. Lake areas were calculated from maps. Depths were measured with cotton lines, and lake temperatures with a Negretti-Zambra reversing thermometer. Turbidity was measured with a 10 cm white disc.
- **Chemical:** pH by colorimetric tubes with bromthymol blue; free carbon dioxide by phenolphthalein titration against N/4 sodium hydroxide; dissolved oxygen by the [[Winkler Method]] (modified); alkalinity by titration with 0.02 N sulfuric acid.
- **Biological:** fish sampled with gill nets, seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling, and observation. Aquatic plants were rated as dense, thin, or scarce. [[Bottom Fauna]] were sampled with a square-foot net in streams and an Ekman dredge in lakes. Plankton was sampled with an 11 cm net of #20 bolting silk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Streams in the [[White River]] drainage are steep in their upper reaches and sluggish in their lower sections. Temperatures reached about 70°F in the White River (partly illegible in the OCR). Waters above the junction of the East Fork and the main White River were suitable for trout. Below that point, the water was too warm for trout and suited only to warm-water fishes. Riffle and pool relations were good on a few small streams and poor on others. Larger streams had generally good riffles and fair pools.

**Chemical conditions.** Water chemistry in the White River watershed was very uniform. Waters were only slightly alkaline and had low carbon dioxide concentrations. Dissolved oxygen was a main variable in Coolidge Lake, where it was highest at the inlet and lowest in quiet, motionless areas, indicating stagnation. Chemical results appear in a summary table.

**Aquatic plants.** The rapid flow of most streams naturally inhibits aquatic plant growth. Only small amounts of algae were present. Dense vegetation occurred only in a few small, non-flowing spring-fed areas. Most streams are unlikely to produce abundant aquatic vegetation.

**Bottom fauna and fish food.** Food counts were grouped into three grades:
- Grade I (best): over 200 animals per square foot.
- Grade II (average): 100 to 200 per square foot.
- Grade III (poorest): under 100 per square foot.

The average across all streams was about 200 organisms per square foot. Counts ranged from 14 at Diamond Creek to a maximum of about 600 (OCR-garbled) at a White River station. [[Caddisfly Larvae]] averaged 91 per station and [[Mayfly Nymphs]] averaged 61. Other organisms were scarce. Small headwater streams generally held more food than the larger lower White River and East Fork reaches. Terrestrial insects that fell into the water were also an important summer food for trout and bass, though they were not quantified.

**Lake conditions.** Lake bottom samples yielded only one or two organisms per square foot. Plankton was scarce. Fluctuating water levels limit the development of [[Aquatic Vegetation]] in lakes.

**Drought of 1934.** The drought dried over two-thirds of the streams in 1934, leaving only the upper reaches of the White River drainage unchanged. Superintendents considered the streams dangerously low, so fishing was discontinued during 1934 and no stocking or improvement work was done.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and fisheries**
- The game fishes are [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], and a few [[Brown Trout]]. Rainbow trout were most numerous and were displacing other trout species.
- [[Suckers]] and [[Minnows]] were very numerous, but no evidence showed they were used as food by game fish.
- Natural trout spawning was rated only "fair" because of low water conditions.
- Natural enemies, diseases, and parasites did not appear to damage the fish of these streams.

**Blue Lake**
- A spring-fed lake of about 2 acres with no outside drainage and an underground outlet.
- Held a good stock of rainbow trout averaging three-quarters of a pound.
- Recommendation: stock annually with fish of suitable size and reduce the bag limit to ten fish per day.

**Coolidge Lake**
- The only fishing water on the White Mountain San Carlos Reservation, formed by damming the Gila River just below its junction with the San Carlos River.
- Covered about 8 square miles at the time of the survey. Records show it had never been filled to capacity.
- Suitable only for warm-water fishes.
- Fish sampled by gill nets included [[Black Bass]] in poor condition due to heavy infestation with a [[Nematode Parasite]]. Other fish were in good condition.
- The report states the parasite is specific to fish, is not known to infect humans, and is not harmful to human consumers. The authors recommended against transferring bass from infected to uninfected waters.

**Stocking recommendations**
- The report recommends that fish removed by anglers be replaced with fish from nearby hatcheries.
- [[Table 3]] lists species, numbers, and frequency for each stream section. The table contains about a dozen stream sections and two lakes, mostly stocked with rainbow trout. Annual stocking numbers range from about 260 to 11,500 fish, with some entries stocked every five years.
- Stocking factors include water temperature, type of bottom, food supply, pH, dissolved oxygen, fishing pressure, previous stocking success, and natural spawning.

**Habitat improvement**
- Natural materials for improvement structures are abundant on all streams, and little labor would be needed.
- The [[Bureau of Fisheries]] was expected to assist if the Indian Service undertook improvements.
- Because the streams lie within the reservations and are subject to Indian Service regulation, the report states that wholesale improvement by other agencies could not be suggested.

**Unresolved problems**
- The life cycle of the black bass parasite is incompletely known, and the report could not recommend control measures beyond prohibiting bass transfers.
