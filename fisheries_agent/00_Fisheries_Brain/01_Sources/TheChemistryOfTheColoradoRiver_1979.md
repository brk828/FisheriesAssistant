# The Chemistry of the Colorado River and its Tributaries in Marble and Grand Canyons

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #hydrochemistry #[[Colorado River Basin]]

## Executive Summary

This paper (Kubly & Cole, 1979) is **not a fish-specific study** but a foundational hydrochemical survey of the [[Colorado River]] mainstem and its tributary springs and creeks through [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]], conducted across six raft-supported field trips in 1975–1976. The authors characterize ionic composition, total dissolved solids (TDS), turbidity, and light transmittance of the post-dam Colorado River and classify 20+ tributaries into chemical water types based on the geology of their source aquifers (chiefly Paleozoic limestones — Kaibab, Redwall, Muav, and Supai/Coconino formations). Because water chemistry, clarity, temperature, and turbidity are primary drivers of aquatic habitat suitability, this data set is directly relevant to understanding the post-[[Glen Canyon Dam]] environment experienced by native Colorado River fishes (e.g., [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) and their invertebrate/algal food resources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper defines the **abiotic habitat template** for the Colorado River ecosystem in this reach, which is essential context for any species inhabiting it:

- **Mainstem regime (post-dam, Lee's Ferry):** Regulated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (15 miles upstream), producing near-constant discharge with diurnal (tidal-like) fluctuations rather than the large seasonal swings of the pre-dam river. Annual temperature range 7–10°C; pH 7.4–7.9; free CO₂ >100% saturation; undersaturated with respect to calcite.
- **Water clarity:** Mean vertical light transmittance 57.8–75.4%/meter at Lee's Ferry — a marked post-dam phenomenon (Lake Powell traps sediment), contrasting with historically turbid pre-dam conditions.
- **Ionic character:** Mainstem cation order Ca²⁺ ≈ Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺; anion order SO₄²⁻ > HCO₃⁻ > Cl⁻ — a "sulfato-carbonate" type, distinct from world-river averages (Ca²⁺-HCO₃⁻ dominated per Livingstone 1963). TDS at Lee's Ferry averaged 605 mg/L, ~5× the world river mean.
- **Tributary types and habitat contribution:**
  - **Dilute dolomitic springs** (Vasey's Paradise, Redwall Moss Spring, Clear Creek, [[Bright Angel Creek]], Shinumo Creek, Stone Creek, Tapeats Creek, Deer Creek) — Ca-Mg bicarbonate waters emerging from Kaibab/Redwall/Muav limestone karst; among the most dilute waters in the system, likely providing cool, clear, stable refugial habitat.
  - **Impure dolomitic waters** (Havasu Creek/Spring, Lava Warm Spring, Three Springs, Spencer Creek, Emory Falls) — higher in Mg, SO₄, Cl, Na; Havasu Creek shows dramatic downstream calcite precipitation (Ca/Mg ratio drops from >2.0 at source to 0.65 at mouth), producing the travertine formations and characteristic blue-green pools associated with [[Havasu Creek]] habitat.
  - **Sodium-bicarbonate waters** (Diamond Creek, Travertine Falls) — chemically unusual "soda" type waters, oversaturated with calcite.
  - **Sulfate waters** ([[Paria River]], Kanab Creek, Elves Chasm) — gypsum-influenced, from Jurassic/Triassic sediment drainages; Paria River is the largest sediment/nutrient contributor (highest N, high P) and dominates turbidity pulses during floods.
  - **Saline sodium-chloride waters** (Pumpkin Spring, [[Blue Spring]]) — most concentrated waters in the system; Blue Spring is the single largest point-source contributor of dissolved solids in the region, discharging from Redwall limestone but sourced from deep Coconino/De Chelly sandstone aquifers with Supai salt beds.
- **Turbidity/nutrient dynamics:** The [[Little Colorado River]] (entering at Mile 61.5) causes a marked rise in mainstem TDS and, during floods, reduces light transmission to <0.001%, an opacity persisting into upper Lake Mead — a significant driver of light/turbidity habitat heterogeneity for downstream biota.

## Key Findings

- Mainstem Colorado River chemistry at Lee's Ferry has become chemically and thermally stabilized since Glen Canyon Dam closure, replacing pre-dam seasonal variability with diurnal fluctuation — a regime shift with direct implications for native fish adapted to the historic warm, turbid, seasonally variable river.
- Combined average tributary discharge between Lee's Ferry and Lake Mead (~750 cfs) constitutes less than 10% of mainstem discharge, meaning tributary chemical influence on the mainstem is generally diluting rather than concentrating, except at high-solute point sources.
- Tributary chemistry is strongly predictable from bedrock geology: dolomitic karst springs (Kaibab/Redwall/Muav limestones) yield dilute Ca-Mg-HCO₃ waters, while deeper Supai/Coconino/Tapeats aquifers and evaporite-bearing formations yield saline Na-Cl or sulfate waters (Blue Spring, Pumpkin Spring, Kanab Creek, Paria River).
- [[Blue Spring]] is identified as the largest single point-source of dissolved solids entering the Colorado River in this reach, with salinity heavily influenced by dilution from Little Colorado River discharge.
- [[Havasu Creek]] provides a clear natural laboratory for CaCO₃ precipitation dynamics (travertine formation), with measurable downstream shifts in Ca/Mg and CO₃/Cl ratios between spring source and confluence with the Colorado.
- The [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Paria River]] are identified as the dominant natural sources of sediment/turbidity and nutrients (N, P) entering the regulated, now-clear mainstem, sustaining episodic turbid conditions important to native fish life history (e.g., spawning cues, predator avoidance) despite dam-induced clarity upstream.
- Silica content varies tributary to tributary (highest in Diamond Creek and Havasu Creek, ~14.5 mg/L), likely reflecting sandstone (Supai) versus limestone aquifer sources, with possible implications for diatom/algal productivity supporting the aquatic food web.
