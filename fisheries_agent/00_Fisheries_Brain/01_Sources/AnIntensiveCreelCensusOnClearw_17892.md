# Intensive Creel Census on Clearwater Lake, Missouri, 1949–1952

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 paper reports an intensive creel census of [[Clearwater Lake]], a flood-control reservoir on the [[Black River]] in southeastern Missouri, during its first four years of impoundment (1949–1952). The study compared species composition, fishing effort, catch rates and harvest across years and seasons, and tracked the shift from a stream to a lake fishery. Catch per hour declined from 0.65 fish/hour in 1949 to 0.34 in 1952, while fishing effort and total harvest rose through 1951 before falling in 1952. Crappies replaced smallmouth bass and other stream species as dominant catch components.

## Background & Description

Clearwater Lake was built and operated by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] for flood control. The dam was completed in 1948 and the lake filled in June of that year. At conservation pool it covers 1,650 acres with 27 miles of shoreline; at flood pool it exceeds 10,000 acres with about 172 miles of shoreline. Impounded water extends more than six miles up the Black River and about half that distance up Logan Creek.

The census was initiated in 1949 under [[Paul H. Eschmeyer]] and conducted by the [[Missouri Conservation Commission]]. Objectives included measuring catch composition, annual angler yield, seasonal changes in fishing quality, stream-to-lake shifts in species composition, and fishing pressure. The census ran during the black bass season in 1949 (May 28–November 30) and 1950 (May 30–November 30), and covered year-round fishing from 1951 onward, after the year-round season took effect on January 1, 1951.

**Methods (summarized):**
- Creel clerk counted anglers on a pre-arranged schedule and interviewed returning anglers for date, party size, hours fished, method, bait, catch by species and number, estimated lengths, and residence.
- Work days were eight hours, five and one-half days per week, with start times rotated on a fixed schedule.
- Fishing intensity was measured by boat counts at maximum speed between the lake's extremes. Daily fisherman turnover was computed from the census day length and average trip duration, a departure from the doubled-boat-count method of [[Eschmeyer 1942]] and [[Tarzwell and Miller 1942]].
- Catch weights were estimated from length-weight relationships from other studies on the reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The census documents a transition from a stream fishery to a reservoir fishery. The upper Black River was a noted [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] stream, where smallmouth averaged 30% of the 1946–1950 catch. After impoundment, smallmouth fell from 5.3% of the catch in 1949 to 0.5% in 1952, and longear sunfish and rock bass also nearly disappeared from the creel. Suckers (golden redhorse, black redhorse, hog sucker) were abundant in the lake per Commission population studies but were scarcely sought by anglers; only eight were recorded in the creel.

Bullheads, which are not classed as game fish in Missouri, became important: they rose from 1.4% of the upper Black River creel (1946–1950) to 6.6–14.6% of the lake catch. Green sunfish fell from 8.8% (1949) to 1.5% (1950), with only two reported in 1951.

Seasonal patterns were tied to temperature and season openings. Crappie fishing peaked in spring and fall, suggesting better success in cool weather. Bluegill fishing was consistently strongest in summer. Largemouth bass harvest concentrated in the opening days of the season in 1949 and in spring after year-round fishing began.

## Key Findings

**Fishing success and effort**
- An estimated 95,175 fisherman days were expended over four years; 11,387 creel records (12% of trips) were obtained.
- Catch per hour: 0.65 (1949), 0.50 (1950), 0.49 (1951), 0.34 (1952). Catch per trip: 2.7, 2.6, 2.5, 1.9.
- Shore anglers were 10.4% of fishermen and had above-average success, but mostly caught pan fishes and bullheads. Artificial-bait anglers accounted for 17% of hours yet half of their catch was black bass.
- Average daily fishermen counts: 30 (1949), 41 (1950), 42 (1951), 32 (1952). Fishing intensity was greatest in summer in all years except 1952, when spring dominated.
- Fishing hours per acre: 44.1 (1949), 60.1 (1950), 111.2 (1951), 79.0 (1952).

**Species composition (percent of catch, 1949 → 1952)**
- [[Crappie]]: 13.4% → 28.7% → 51.5% → 45.4%, the largest gain of any species.
- [[Bluegill]]: 23.4% → 29.2% → 25.2% → 28.8%, the least variable of the principal species.
- [[Largemouth Black Bass]]: 25.5% → 24.6% → 7.5% → 10.4%. Average length rose from 9.5 in (1949) to 12.4 in (1952).
- Bullheads: 6.6% → 11.6% → 14.6% → 12.2%.
- Nineteen species were recorded in total; the four above dominated the creel.

**Harvest estimates**
- Total catch: 47,125 (1949), 51,775 (1950), 96,625 (1951), 45,450 (1952).
- Fish per acre: 28.6, 31.4, 58.6, 27.5.
- Estimated weight of catch: 11,300 lb (6.8 lb/acre, 1949); 17,200 lb (10.4, 1950); 51,600 lb (31.3, 1951); 25,200 lb (15.2, 1952). Yield in 1952 was lower than in 1949 by number but more than doubled per acre by weight, reflecting larger fish.

**Seasonality**
- Summer produced 62% of the 1949 catch and about 78% of the 1949 and 1950 harvest combined in summer. In 1951 and 1952 (year-round census), spring produced 43% and 49% of the catch respectively, and 50% and 55% of the year's catch.
- Winter catch was negligible (0.2% of the catch in 1951 and 1952).

**Management implications and discussion**
- The steady four-year decline in catch rate did not match the expected post-impoundment pattern of good early fishing followed by decline (noted in discussion by Mr. Kathrein).
- Opening the year-round season extended fishing pressure but did not reverse the summer decline in catch rate.
- The author attributes the 1951 harvest increase to higher fishing intensity and growing populations of certain species, and the 1952 drop to lower success and reduced effort.
