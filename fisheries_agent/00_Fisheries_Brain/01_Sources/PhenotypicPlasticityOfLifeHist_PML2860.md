# Life-History Plasticity of Sexual and Clonal Poeciliopsis Under Resource Stress

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Stephen C. Weeks]] (*Oecologia*, 1993, 93: 307–314) tests [[Life-History Theory]] predictions on how livebearing fish allocate resources to growth, survival and reproduction under high versus low density. Three strains of [[Poeciliopsis]] were compared: one sexual strain (*P. monacha*) and two hybridogenetic clonal strains (ML/VII and ML/VIII). Fish were raised for 70 days in a crossed design of three strains and two density treatments (high: 24 fish per tank; low: 12 fish per tank).

Density did not affect survival but reduced growth, egg production and the proportion of females reaching maturity. Egg size was unaffected by density. Strains differed in reproductive timing and investment: the sexual strain matured earlier, produced more eggs per unit body size, and had smaller eggs than one clonal strain. The author interprets these differences in light of contrasting upstream and downstream habitats.

## Background & Description

**Theoretical framework.** Life-history theory holds that organisms allocate resources among maintenance, growth, reproduction and storage to maximize fitness. Environmental heterogeneity within a lifespan should favor phenotypic plasticity, while heterogeneity across generations should favor genetic variability. Reproductive timing and investment are expected to respond to trade-offs between current and future reproduction. Poor-environment predictions include larger investment per offspring, though these predictions are not always supported.

**Study organisms.** The clonal strains (ML/VII and ML/VIII) are diploid hybridogenetic fish derived from a *P. monacha* female and a *P. lucida* male. During oogenesis the *P. lucida* genome is excluded, so the clones carry essentially only the *P. monacha* genome from the mother, with diploidy restored after fertilization by a *P. lucida* male. The sexual strain (*P. monacha*) was naturally inseminated by *P. monacha* males in large rearing tanks. Clones were artificially inseminated with sperm from isogenic *P. lucida* males (strain SOS-a), a design intended to remove variation within clonal lines.

**Experimental design.** Juveniles aged 3–21 days were raised in common conditions for a homogenizing period, then assigned to treatments. A total of 108 juveniles per strain were weighed and divided between two density treatments across two blocks of three replicates each. Each strain–density combination was replicated in 18 experimental aquaria (75 L glass tanks with standpipes, sand substrate, and three substrate types). Fish were fed brine shrimp, chironomid larvae and tubifex worms, and were sacrificed after 10 weeks. Eggs were classified as atretic, mature or immature, and mature egg volume was used as an index of energy investment.

**Analysis.** Data were analyzed with SAS General Linear Models (univariate ANOVA and ANCOVA). Strain comparisons of growth and reproduction used females only.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Density and resource stress.** High density simulates resource stress. Populations of these fish show strong seasonal fluctuations in resource abundance and population size, so phenotypically plastic investment strategies are expected.
- **Habitat contrasts.** Upstream *P. monacha* habitats are described as resource-poor and harsher than the downstream habitats of the two clones (Thibault 1974a; Thibault and Schultz 1978; Schenck and Vrijenhoek 1986). Upstream habitats also contain roughly 40-fold more predaceous aquatic insects (notably *Buenoa arizonis*), which may increase size-selective predation on small *Poeciliopsis*.
- **Cannibalism.** Cannibalistic tendency in *P. monacha* and its associated clones is reported as significantly lower in downstream habitats.
- **Coexistence.** The sexual and clonal forms coexist in natural streams, and differences in their life histories may reflect different selective pressures in their native habitats.

## Key Findings

**Survival**
- Survival was high in all strains at both densities, ranging from 58% to 100%.
- Density had no effect on survival (F₁,₂₀ = 0.05, NS).
- *P. monacha* and ML/VII had higher survivorship than ML/VIII (F₁₂,₂₀₁ = 7.28, P = 0.003).

**Growth**
- Growth was about 70% greater on average at low than at high density in all three strains.
- Growth was reduced nearly twofold at high density.
- No among-strain differences in overall growth were detected.
- Initial weight differed among strains (*P. monacha* > ML/VII > ML/VIII), but these differences were not apparent at the end of the experiment.
- Growth data were non-normal (leptokurtic), so reported P-values for growth are considered conservative.

**Reproductive timing**
- The proportion of sexually mature females was significantly lower at high density in all strains (F₁,₁₀₇ = 39.03; P < 0.0001).
- *P. monacha* had a greater proportion of mature females than either clone across densities (F₂,₁₀₇ = 56.50; P < 0.0001).
- The density-related decline in maturity was steeper in *P. monacha* (F₁₂,₁₀₇ = 6.54; P < 0.004).
- End weight did not account for maturity differences, indicating maturity was not merely a reflection of size.

**Fecundity**
- Eggs per reproductive female were reduced at high density in all three strains.
- Egg production was reduced about 25% at high density.
- Once body size was controlled (ANCOVA), *P. monacha* produced more eggs per unit weight than either clone (F₂,₉₆ = 3.78, P = 0.0349).

**Egg size**
- Egg size was not affected by density in any strain.
- Egg size differed among strains; ML/VIII eggs were reported as larger than ML/VII eggs (P < 0.05), and the text suggests a difference of about 16%. This figure is partly garbled in the OCR, so treat the exact value with caution.
- The author notes that these differences are consistent with differences in starting size and with earlier reports of differences between ML/VIII and *P. monacha*.

**Interpretation**
- Within-strain responses to density were largely similar across the three strains and did not match the prediction that resource-stressed fish should invest more in current reproduction.
- The clear life-history differences were among strains rather than within strains. *P. monacha* matured earlier, invested more per unit body size, and produced more eggs, which the author links to its harsher upstream environment, where shorter lifespans favor early reproduction (Williams 1966; Schaffer 1974).
- Increased size-selective predation in upstream habitats is proposed to favor greater investment per offspring, which is consistent with the larger eggs of ML/VIII relative to *P. monacha* in some comparisons.
- Limitations: adult size at maturity was not measured, and the author states that a fuller test of adaptive plasticity would require further experiments and direct field data on age-specific survival.
- The decreased age at maturity seen with greater resource stress in *P. monacha* conflicts with some models (Stearns and Koella 1986), which the author suggests may reflect constraints or a need to revise predictions.
