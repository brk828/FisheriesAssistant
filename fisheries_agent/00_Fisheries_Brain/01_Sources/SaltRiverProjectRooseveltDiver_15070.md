# Roosevelt Diversion Dam Removal: Biological Assessment of Fishery and Eagle Impacts

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae #conservation-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment evaluates potential impacts of removing the [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] on the [[Salt River]], Arizona. Electrofishing surveys conducted March 15-16, 1988 documented stark differences in fish assemblages above versus below the dam. Above the dam, 230 fish (23,680 gm total biomass) were collected in 125 minutes, dominated by small-bodied species: [[Fathead Minnow]] (45.22%), [[Green Sunfish]] (31.30%), and [[Red Shiner]] (9.57%). Below the dam, 262 fish (262,141 gm biomass—over 10× greater) were collected in 105 minutes, dominated by large-bodied species: [[Common Carp]] (47.31%), [[Channel Catfish]] (32.31%), and [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (10.77%). Only one native fish was captured: a single [[Razorback Sucker]] (part of experimental reintroduction). The dam currently functions as a critical barrier preventing upstream movement of large piscivorous non-native fishes from [[Roosevelt Lake]]. Resource agencies unanimously opposed dam removal, citing threats to native fish populations, reintroduction efforts for endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and uncertain impacts on eight breeding pairs of [[Bald Eagle]] (one-third of Arizona's population) nesting upstream.

## Background & Description

The [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] was completed in 1906 to generate hydroelectric power for construction of [[Roosevelt Dam]]. Hydroelectric operations ceased in 1955 as uneconomical. Water diversions continue from the north abutment to three downstream users. An unintended consequence of construction was blocking fish movement from [[Roosevelt Lake]] to upper [[Salt River]] reaches.

**Historical Context**: Previous surveys (AGFD and FWS 1986) of the upper Salt River from State Road 60 to Globe-Young bridge documented fish populations with 43% native and 57% non-native representatives. [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) was most abundant (27%), followed by [[Channel Catfish]] (19%). Combined native suckers ([[Gila Sucker]] and [[Mountain Sucker]] *Pantosteus clarki*) comprised 36% versus 27% for non-native catfishes ([[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] *Pylodictis olivaris*).

**Species Biology**: [[Flathead Catfish]], native to midwestern U.S. large rivers and lakes, were introduced to the [[Gila River]] system before 1950 and again 1968-1975 into Salt, Verde and San Francisco Rivers. Young flatheads occupy shallow reaches; older fish prefer covered habitats (cutbanks, root masses). Adults are exclusively piscivorous. [[Channel Catfish]] are more omnivorous (algae, insects, fish) but documented preying on native fishes. Spawning occurs in spring (May-June). [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*) were introduced in 1918 by U.S. Bureau of Fisheries; consume mainly planktonic crustaceans and algae; inhabit deeper reservoir reaches. Field data showed spawning migration into Salt River by adult males beginning mid-March, with females spawning April-May.

**Predation Studies**: Juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]]s reintroduced into [[Gila River]] below Clifton, Arizona were heavily preyed upon by channel and flathead catfish (Marsh and Brooks, unpublished). Predation by catfishes increased during warmer water temperatures; predator size positively correlated with razorback prey size. Channel catfish predation decreased at lower temperatures due to reduced feeding activity; flathead catfish predation remained constant under both temperature regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study Methods**: Fishes were sampled March 15-16, 1988 in 500-meter reaches above and below the dam using a Coffelt VVP-15 electroshocker mounted on 18-foot Domar inflatable raft. Electrical fields: 25% pulse width, 60 cycles/second, 6-9 amperes, 300-600 volts. Seine hauls (20×4 ft×1/4 in. net) taken in connected backwaters above dam. Fish were weighed (nearest gram), measured total length (nearest mm), tagged with numbered Floy tags, and returned to water. Equal effort expended in similar habitat types both reaches.

**Habitat Characteristics**: Habitat sampled primarily "run" type. Large connected backwater immediately above diversion. Below dam, river velocity increased with more "eddy" habitat. Both reaches had similar cover: cutbanks with *Phragmites* sp. growth. Seine hauls in backwaters above dam yielded red shiners and fathead minnows in silt/sand substrates with heavy macrophyte growth.

**Current Distribution**: Recent AGFD electroshocking of Upper Salt indicated almost complete displacement of native fish species by non-natives below [[Gleason Flat]], approximately 34 river miles upstream. Native fishes may have temporarily relocated due to spawning. Upper Salt River surveys show native species absent below Gleason Flat, replaced by large biomass of carp and catfish.

**Sport Fishery**: Spring concentrations of channel and flathead catfish below diversion dam well known. Anglers crowd shores "shoulder to shoulder" during peak run, with several thousand people fishing and 400-500 vehicles/day parking near dam. [[Flathead Catfish]] are underexploited resource in Arizona. Angler survey data (AGFD 1987) showed catch per hour (CFPH) increased from 0.25 in March/April to 0.51 in May/June, then dropped to 0.07 in July—supporting documented spawning movements from [[Roosevelt Lake]].

**Bald Eagle Populations**: Eight breeding pairs of [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), a federally-listed endangered species, nest upstream of dam—approximately one-third of Arizona's breeding population. Primary diet consists of fish; other prey (snakes, small mammals, waterfowl) taken occasionally. Limited data for [[Pinal Creek]] pair (Biosystems Analysis 1987) showed extensive foraging in Salt River arm of [[Roosevelt Lake]] upstream to diversion dam. Eagles used waterfowl over lake in winter; shifted to live fish and fish carrion almost exclusively through summer, foraging regularly in reach above reservoir and below dam. Eagles forage on fish within definite size/weight limits: 141-313 mm, 263-636 gm (Haywood and Ohmart 1980). Skulls of large flathead catfish found in [[Redmond Flat]] eagle nest upstream.

## Key Findings

**Fish Community Composition Above Dam** (500 m upstream, March 15, 1988):
- Total catch: 230 fish, 23,680 gm biomass
- Shocking effort: 125 minutes
- Catch per unit effort: 1.84 fish/minute
- Average weight: 542 gm; average length: 128 mm
- Eight species collected, including one [[Razorback Sucker]] (experimental reintroduction)
- Dominant species: [[Fathead Minnow]] 45.22% (n=104), [[Green Sunfish]] 31.30% (n=72), [[Red Shiner]] 9.57% (n=22), [[Common Carp]] 7.83% (n=18)
- Other species: [[Channel Catfish]] 3.48% (n=8, 190 mm, 420 gm), [[Yellow Bullhead]] 1.74% (n=4), [[Flathead Catfish]] 0.43% (n=1, 426 mm, 812 gm), [[Razorback Sucker]] 0.43% (n=1, 246 mm, 196 gm)

**Fish Community Composition Below Dam** (500 m downstream, March 16, 1988):
- Total catch: 262 fish, 262,141 gm biomass (10× greater than above)
- Shocking effort: 105 minutes
- Catch per unit effort: 2.48 fish/minute
- Average weight: 1,498 gm; average length: 396 mm
- Nine species of introduced fishes
- Three species found only below dam: [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]]
- Dominant species: [[Common Carp]] 47.31% (n=123, 419 mm, 975 gm), [[Channel Catfish]] 32.31% (n=84, 335 mm, 411 gm), [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] 10.77% (n=28, 572 mm, 2,834 gm)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] 6.15% (n=16, 305 mm, 890 gm), [[Flathead Catfish]] 1.54% (n=4, 569 mm, 3,390 gm)
- Small-bodied species rare: [[Green Sunfish]] 0.77% (n=2), [[Fathead Minnow]] 0.38% (n=1), [[Red Shiner]] 0.38% (n=1)

**Spawning Evidence**: Approximately one-third of [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] collected were males running with milt, indicating active spawning migration from [[Roosevelt Lake]].

**Barrier Function**: Ichthyofauna dominated by introduced species (11 species collected; only one native individual). Overwhelming difference in species assemblages and biomass between reaches. Sheer size of individual fish below dam (average 1,498 gm vs. 542 gm above) suggests upstream populations could sustain severe predation impacts if downstream fish gained access. Preliminary swimming speed data: [[Common Carp]] maximum 8.2 ft/sec versus calculated dam crest velocity during 2-year high flow event (20,000 cfs) of 10.78 fps—indicating carp would be stressed negotiating floodwaters. Previous high flow events have not resulted in widespread colonization of Upper Salt by lake fishes.

**Agency Consensus**: March 17, 1988 workshop at dam site with Salt River Project, consulting firms (Biosystems Analysis Inc., SWCA Inc.), and resource agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Arizona Game and Fish Department). Consensus among agency representatives: [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] currently serves important biological function as barrier to upstream movement of non-native fishes. This or equally effective fish barrier should be maintained. None of resource agencies favored dam removal proposal.

**Potential Adverse Impacts on Native Fishes**: [[Roosevelt Lake]] acts as reservoir of non-native fishes expected to invade tributary streams to spawn and colonize riverine habitats if barrier removed. Reach below dam well known as concentration point for [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]], especially during spring—both notably piscivorous. Concentrations stop at diversion dam except possibly during exceptionally high flow. [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] not known to exist at all in reaches above dam. Great disparity in biomass (over 10× greater below) and fish size presents greatest potential for adverse impacts. Large numbers of large fish moving upstream from [[Roosevelt Lake]] would reasonably affect existing conditions in canyon sections where native populations most stable and important. Changes in species composition, size class distributions, and relative densities possible. Historically, invasions of non-native species into Arizona river systems detrimental to native species. Canyon-bound reaches of undammed streams offer "largest and most persistent populations of native fishes that remain in Arizona."

**Reintroduction Program Threats**: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), federally-listed endangered species, formerly inhabited Salt River system; last adult taken from Salt River above [[Roosevelt Lake]] in 1937. [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is candidate for federal listing. Reintroduction efforts still in early stages; evaluation of success/failure not expected until early 1990s when evidence of successful reproduction and recruitment first available. Predation of razorback suckers documented in [[Gila River]] (Marsh and Brooks, unpublished); same pattern likely in Upper Salt if large numbers of piscivores pulsed upstream. Reintroduced [[Colorado Squawfish]] populations also expected to suffer from introductions of aggressive non-native predators. Salt River Project cooperated with Arizona Game and Fish Department in reintroduction efforts but not liable for management actions adversely affecting these efforts, as introductions made as Experimental, Non-essential populations under Section 7(a)(4) of Endangered Species Act.

**Sport Fishery Impacts**: Dam removal could result in both positive and negative impacts. Spring concentrations of catfish below dam support intensive fishery. Removing dam would likely disperse spring concentrations, dispersing fishermen and their impacts on public lands but possibly reducing ease of catching catfish, leading to reduction in overall harvest and quality of recreational experience. Dispersing large fish population could create wilderness fishery upstream of Route 288 bridge. Anecdotal evidence suggests wilderness fishery already exists on Upper Salt above [[Coon Creek]] where local fishermen report taking sizable (5-18 pound) flathead and channel catfish. [[Salt River Canyon]] already popular for whitewater rafting and kayaking; dramatic increase in large fish could add fishing dimension to wilderness experience. Fishermen tend to remove largest individuals which would become largest predators of native and reintroduced species upstream; large adults also have greatest reproductive capacity. Control of large catfish population has implications for [[Roosevelt Lake]] fishery quality, as these fishes feed on young of highly sought species (crappie, largemouth bass, smallmouth bass) and other game/non-game fish.

**Bald Eagle Impact Uncertainty**: Insufficient data to make clear determination of potential adverse or beneficial impacts on upstream [[Bald Eagle]]s. Possibilities for improving proximity and abundance of forage base exist alongside possibilities for losses of available forage during critical nesting periods. [[Pinal Creek]] pair (5 miles above dam) used [[Roosevelt Lake]] for foraging more in winter than summer, taking live or pirated waterfowl over lake in winter plus fish carrion. Foraging moved consistently eastward toward nest site as season progressed. Use of waterfowl ended with winter season; eagles used live fish and fish carrion almost exclusively through summer. Eagles may be attracted by concentrations of fish attempting to ascend river to spawn. Carrion incidental to sport fishing could be made available to eagles over longer river reach if dam removed, closer to Pinal and other upstream nests—potential benefit. If eagles benefit from concentration of large fish below dam and it is removed, birds would either adapt quickly or suffer reduced nest productivity. Increased biomass from [[Roosevelt Lake]] could become available to eagles while passing through or colonizing shallows above dam—new abundance and closer proximity of prey resources could increase nest productivity. Reduction in species diversity caused by depredations by large piscivores could adversely affect eagles if wide spectrum of species spawning in shallow water at different times was suddenly narrowed, causing loss in forage availability at critical breeding season points. Too little known about spawning phenology in tributaries or forage base in canyon sections to accurately predict effects. Reduction in native fishes (probably more resistant than introduced fishes to flooding effects) could leave voids in eagle food supply in flood years.
