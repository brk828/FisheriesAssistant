# Productivity of a Herbivorous Pupfish Population (*Cyprinodon nevadensis*) in a Warm Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 study by [[Robert J. Naiman]] (Arizona State University) quantifies energy flow through an herbivorous [[Amargosa Pupfish]] population living in the outflow of a thermal artesian well, [[Tecopa Bore]], in the Mohave Desert near Death Valley, California. Water emerges at 47.5 °C and cools 8–12 °C before leaving the 300 m study area. The population's annual production was 118.6–119 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, roughly ten times the values reported for carnivorous fishes in natural waters. The fish ingested about 1941 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, equal to 17.5% of annual net primary production, and assimilated only a small fraction, giving a gross growth efficiency of about 6.1%. The high production is attributed to a long growing season, abundant algal food, a large standing crop, and a short life cycle, rather than to faster individual growth.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Amargosa pupfish, *Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae* (the text also cites the desert pupfish, *Cyprinodon macularius*, for comparison). Mosquitofish, *Gambusia affinis*, comprised less than 5% of the fish population and was the only other fish species present.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic omnivore in general, but at Tecopa Bore it fed mainly on algal-detritus mixtures because invertebrates were scarce. The gut had no discernible stomach.
- **Thermal optimum:** Laboratory work by [[Kinne (1960)]] found maximal growth at 30 °C for desert pupfish. At Tecopa Bore the whole population lived above 25 °C, with most fish between 28 and 34 °C.
- **Study area:** A marsh-fed stream 3–6 m wide, under 10 cm deep, with discharge of 2–3 to 6–7 m³ min⁻¹. Algal mats (Oscillatoria, Spirulina, Anabaena, diatoms), bacteria, fungi and detritus covered much of the bed. The invertebrate fauna (ostracods, copepods, moss beetles, chironomid and tabanid larvae) was sparse and restricted to cooler areas.
- **Motivation:** Herbivorous fishes are understudied relative to carnivorous species, yet are an important protein source in parts of Africa and Asia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal ecosystem:** A year-round growing season fixes large amounts of energy in primary producers, and the trophic structure is simple (few algal species, essentially one herbivore).
- **Spatial occupancy:** Pupfish occupied 23.7% to 53.9% of the study area (334 m² in August 1973 to 785 m² in April 1973), averaging 583 m² (41%, SD 109 m²). They concentrated in the cooler lower half of the stream.
- **Movement:** Two-way minnow traps set in five months yielded small, roughly equal catches moving upstream and downstream, so the study did not adjust production for immigration or emigration.
- **Energy budget context:** Annual net primary production was 11,065 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ ([[Naiman (1974)]]). Direct algal feeding was estimated at 30.3 kcal m⁻² day⁻¹.
- **Comparative production:** Temperate single-species standing waters produce 2–15 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹; highest recorded bluegill-sunfish ponds yielded 18.1; brown trout streams up to 18; multispecies River Thames fish about 45. Intensively cultured Israeli ponds produced 100–300 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ and Asian polyculture 200–400 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹.

## Key Findings

- **Density and biomass:** Mean density was 89 individuals m⁻² (range 13–196). Biomass ranged from 7 to 42 kcal m⁻². Mean annual standing crop was about 23.6–24 kcal m⁻², turning over roughly five times a year.
- **Growth rates:** Juveniles (under 15 mm SL) grew fastest, with a mean of 9.5% day⁻¹. Young adults (15–30 mm) averaged 1.11% day⁻¹, and large adults (over 30 mm) averaged 0.84% day⁻¹. Size-class differences were highly significant (P<0.01); seasonal differences were not statistically significant.
- **Monthly production:** Highest in autumn (up to 22 kcal m⁻² in September) and lowest in July and August (about 3 kcal m⁻²). Production was 14–22 kcal m⁻² from September to November.
- **Annual production:** 118.6 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, equal to 155.4 g wet weight, 29.3 g dry weight and 20.0 g AFDW m⁻². This is 1.07% of energy fixed by primary producers and 6.1% of food ingested.
- **Ingestion:** About 1941 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, or 17.5% of annual net primary production. Individual ingestion reached about 30% of body energy per day, compared with 1–6% for carnivorous fishes. Gut passage time averaged 280 min (SE 18.6, n = 41), the same at 30 and 34 °C. Food turnover ranged from 2.35 (December) to 3.52 (June) times per day.
- **Diet composition:** Algal-detritus made up about 85% of gut volume, invertebrates 4%, Ruppia 2%, and 9% was empty. Of 218 guts, 99% contained algal-detritus and 22% contained invertebrates. Invertebrates occurred in 29% of fish under 15 mm.
- **Gross growth efficiency:** Averaged about 6.1% (range 3.0% in June to 14.2% in September). Roughly 30.5% of ingested food went to respiration and maintenance, and 36.6% was assimilated, according to the author's estimate.
- **Comparison with invertebrates:** Analogous detritivorous and herbivorous invertebrates eat 10–50% of body energy daily, which the author cites as support for the high ingestion rates.
- **Comparison with Kinne (1960):** Gross conversion efficiency for desert pupfish at 30 °C was 1.3% and at 35 °C 0.8%. Estimated percent of body calories eaten was about 21%.
- **Conclusion:** Production was high because of an extended growing season, high algal productivity, and a large standing crop, not because of rapid individual growth. Growth rates were similar to those of other warm-water fishes.
