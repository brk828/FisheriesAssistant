## Executive Summary

This document is an organizational overview of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], a nationwide interagency body founded in 1970 to address the imminent extinction of multiple desert-endemic fish species in the southwestern United States. Rather than presenting primary species data, the source outlines the Council's founding mandate, its geographic scope across the North American desert (Basin and Range Province), and its 1974 restructuring into regional committees to improve conservation efficiency. The Council operated in close relation to the incoming [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] and convened an Annual Symposium as its central forum for status reporting and research dissemination.

## Background & Description

- **Organization**: Desert Fishes Council, headquartered at 407 West Line Street, Bishop, California 93514.
- **Founded**: 1970, in direct response to accelerating habitat degradation across southwestern desert drainages that threatened several fish species with extinction.
- **Mandate**: Preservation of "America's desert fishes," broadly defined as any endemic fish taxon (species, subspecies, or race) inhabiting drainages of the North American desert (Basin and Range Province), plus any other areas deemed pertinent.
- **Taxonomic scope**: Explicitly includes:
  - [[Whitefishes]] (e.g., Bear Lake, Idaho–Utah populations)
  - [[Trouts]]
  - [[Sculpins]]
  - Endangered big-river fishes, notably the [[Colorado Squawfish]]
- **Restructuring event**: At the Fifth Annual Symposium (Arizona State University, Tempe, November 13–15, 1973), the Council resolved to delegate regional affairs to local committees for greater administrative efficiency.
- **First committee meeting**: The [[Death Valley System Committee]] convened in January 1974, marking the operational start of the new committee structure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Council's area of concern spans the **North American desert (Basin and Range Province)**, an inland, non-oceanic system of isolated and interconnected desert drainages and endorheic basins. The designated regional committee areas illustrate the geographic and hydrological complexity of desert fish habitats:

- [[Bonneville Basin]]
- [[Interbasin]] (including [[White River]])
- [[Death Valley System]]
- [[Oregon Lakes]]
- [[Lahontan Basin]]
- [[Sonoran Desert]] – Mexico
- [[Sonoran Desert]] – U.S. (including [[Gila River]])
- [[Chihuahuan Desert]] – U.S. (New Mexico and Texas)
- [[Chihuahuan Desert]] – Mexico
- [[Chihuahuan Desert]] – Isolated Basins
- [[Lower Colorado River]]
- [[Upper Colorado River]]

These regions represent a mix of isolated relict lake/spring systems (e.g., Death Valley, Bonneville, Lahontan) and large interconnected river systems (e.g., Colorado River, Gila River), reflecting the diverse habitat degradation pressures affecting desert-endemic fish populations—including water diversion, groundwater pumping, and general habitat loss driving several species toward extinction.

## Key Findings

- The Desert Fishes Council was founded in **1970** specifically because habitat degradation had brought several desert fish species to the brink of extinction.
- The Council's defined scope is broad, covering not just narrowly "desert" fishes but also cold-water taxa such as trouts, sculpins, and whitefishes where they occur within Basin and Range drainages.
- A major structural reform in **1973–1974** decentralized Council operations into **twelve regional committees**, improving liaison capacity with governmental agencies across a vast and hydrologically fragmented geographic scope.
- The committees serve as a **liaison mechanism** between the Council and governmental agencies, assisting directly with desert fish preservation efforts.
- The Council reports it had already been **instrumental in saving at least one endangered fish species from extinction** by 1974, with progress underway on others (species not specified in this excerpt).
- The Council anticipated an expanded role coinciding with implementation of the **Endangered Species Act of 1973**.
- The **Annual Symposium** functions as the Council's central knowledge-exchange venue, integrating academic, agency, and private stakeholders around preservation planning, species status updates, and new research.
