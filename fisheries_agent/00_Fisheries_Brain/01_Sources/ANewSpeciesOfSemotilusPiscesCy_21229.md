# A New Species of *Semotilus* (Pisces: Cyprinidae) from the Carolinas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 taxonomic paper by [[Franklin F. Snelson Jr.]] and [[Royal D. Suttkus]] describes [[Semotilus Lumbee]] (Sandhills Chub), a new cyprinid endemic to the Sandhills region of North and South Carolina. The species is known from the Cape Fear, Lumber (Little Peedee), and Yadkin (Peedee) river systems. Its closest relative is judged to be [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]], based on tuberculation, habitat, and distribution. The two species are segregated by the [[Fall Line]]: *S. lumbee* lives in Coastal Plain streams immediately below it, while *S. atromaculatus* is largely a Piedmont species. The authors conclude that the species' long-term survival depends on the central populations in the upper [[Lumber River]] system, which face threats from urban expansion, sewage infrastructure, and proposed water withdrawals.

## Background & Description

The fish was first collected on 30 March 1949 by [[Edward C. Raney]] and students from Cornell University during a collecting trip in the North Carolina Coastal Plain. Raney recognized it as atypical in coloration and body shape. A second expedition in 1951 yielded more material, but the study was postponed, so the status of the fish was never assessed in the literature. Raney asked Snelson to complete the work in 1974. The paper was published in the *Bulletin of the Alabama Museum of Natural History* (No. 3, 1978).

**Diagnostic characters** (from the authors' summary):
- Nine principal dorsal fin rays (most other North American cyprinids have eight).
- Anterior basidorsal (dorsal-origin) pigment spot usually absent or diffuse.
- Different body form and morphometrics (more robust body, shorter head, anterior dorsal fin).
- Larger scales.
- Fewer cephalic lateral line pores.

**Holotype:** TU 101095 (formerly NCSM 7090), a tuberculate adult male, 151.2 mm standard length (SL), collected from a tributary of [[Aberdeen Creek]] in Moore County, North Carolina, on 8 May 1975 (W. M. Palmer, A. L. Braswell, and J. E. Cooper). Paratypes were drawn from several series (roughly 10–15 specimens each, plus the former NCSM 4139 series), with SL ranging from about 27.4 to 139.1 mm.

**Tuberculation:** Breeding males develop large, horn-like tubercles on the head (postorbital, preorbital, and snout), on the body scales of the caudal peduncle, and on the pectoral and caudal fin rays. Females are nontuberculate. Tubercles are most developed in males collected from late March to mid-May. In *S. atromaculatus*, the head tubercles are more uniform in size and the caudal fin is non-tuberculate.

**Coloration:** Juveniles show a broad, dark midlateral stripe that fades with growth. Most adults over 70 mm SL show typical adult pigmentation. Breeding males have pink or orange tones on the head, ventral body, and fins.

**Sexual dimorphism and allometry:** Males differ from females in caudal peduncle depth and interorbital width, and have longer anal and pectoral fins. *S. atromaculatus* is more strongly dimorphic. Both sexes of both species show strong negative allometry in eye size.

**Etymology:** The name refers to the Lumbee, Indigenous people of uncertain origin who inhabit the Lumber River area, mainly in Robeson County, North Carolina.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Streams are typically small to moderate creeks (2–4 m wide) with clear water that may be darkly stained. Bottoms are mostly sand, with gravel or rubble in some reaches, and silt in pools and protected areas. Current is moderate, faster than most other Coastal Plain streams. Pools reach about 1.5 m deep. The only record from a large river is a single young fish from the [[Lynches River]] in South Carolina, possibly a waif from a tributary population.

**Geology and landscape:** The range lies within the [[Carolina Sandhills]], a segment of the Fall Line hills at 60–150 m elevation. The hills are blanketed with loose sand and support pine–turkey oak and scrub oak vegetation. The surficial sands belong largely to the [[Pinehurst Formation]], which overlies Cretaceous strata (the Middendorf/Tuscaloosa formation). Most of the Sandhills are thought not to have been inundated by the ocean since the late Eocene.

**Sympatry and segregation:** The two congeners are only rarely sympatric.
- *S. atromaculatus* is absent from the Lumber River, where *S. lumbee* is most abundant, but is widespread in Piedmont portions of the adjacent Yadkin and Cape Fear drainages.
- The two species were collected together twice in the Big Mountain Creek system (Yadkin tributary, Richmond County).
- In the Cape Fear drainage, both occur in the Little River system in Moore County, but have not been taken together.

**Associated species:** The distribution of *S. lumbee* nearly matches that of another Carolina endemic darter, [[Etheostoma Mariae]], which is also limited to the Sandhills of the Lumber and Peedee systems, but has not been found in the Cape Fear drainage.

**Related species:** The authors reject a close relationship with [[Semotilus Corporalis]], which occurs in large creeks and rivers, is separated by a distributional gap, and has numerous small head tubercles in breeding males.

## Key Findings

**Material examined:** Forty collections containing 566 specimens were examined. Most came from the Lumber/Peedee system, especially Drowning Creek and its tributaries, Aberdeen Creek, and Sandy Run. The collections include several Cape Fear and Yadkin samples and two South Carolina localities.

**Distribution:**
- Most records come from the Drowning Creek system (Lumber River headwaters) in southeastern Moore County and northeastern Richmond County, North Carolina.
- The species also occupies Big Mountain Creek, a westward-flowing Yadkin tributary, and streams in the Cape Fear drainage (Cumberland, Hoke, and Moore counties).
- Single collections were made from two Peedee tributaries in Chesterfield and Kershaw counties, South Carolina, about 80 km southwest of the North Carolina core range.

**Meristic counts (Tables 1–2):**
- Dorsal fin rays: 9 in 92 of 95 additional *S. lumbee* specimens; 8 in 3. Most *S. atromaculatus* have 8 rays.
- Lateral line scales: mean 47.1 (n = 55) in *S. lumbee* vs. about 51.2 (n = 104) in *S. atromaculatus*.
- Cephalic lateral line: the POM canal is interrupted in 98.2% of *S. lumbee* vs. 85.7% of *S. atromaculatus*. The ST canal is interrupted in 34.6% vs. 99.0%.
- Anterior dorsolateral scale rows: mean 24.3 in *S. lumbee* vs. 28.8 in *S. atromaculatus*.

**Vertebrae (Table 3):** *S. atromaculatus* has, on average, one more precaudal and one fewer caudal vertebra than *S. lumbee*, which places its dorsal fin farther posteriorly.

**Morphometrics (Table 4):** *S. lumbee* has a shorter head, a more anterior dorsal fin, and larger fins. Its snout is more pointed, and its mouth is smaller and less oblique. Its barbel has a tapering tip, whereas that of *S. atromaculatus* is bluntly triangular. The authors note that *S. lumbee* reaches a larger adult size than most central Atlantic *S. atromaculatus* populations.

**Geographic variation:** Gulf coast populations (nominal *thoreauianus*) have larger scales, but still have 8 dorsal rays and the typical *S. atromaculatus* body form. They are clearly distinct from *S. lumbee*.

**Conservation status:**
- The authors judge the species' long-term survival to be in jeopardy. Populations outside the upper Lumber River system are small and scattered, so the species' survival depends on the central populations.
- Aberdeen Creek, the type locality and near the center of abundance, has deteriorated with the expansion of Aberdeen, Pinehurst, Pine Bluff, and Southern Pines.
- A sewer interceptor line is under construction along Aberdeen Creek. A second line is under consideration. A sewage treatment plant at Addor will discharge upstream of the Drowning Creek confluence, with an initial capacity of 6.7 million gallons per day (MGD).
- A proposed regional water plant on Drowning Creek has been at least temporarily averted.
- During a 1968 drought lasting over six weeks, Drowning Creek at U.S. Hwy 1 had a seven-day low flow of 15.7 MGD, about 10% of average flow.
- No comprehensive environmental impact review of these projects has been conducted.

**Official status:** North Carolina lists *S. lumbee* as "special concern." South Carolina lists it as "threatened." The authors recommend close monitoring of the Lumber River headwaters.

**Principal conclusion:** The species is a distinct, endemic Sandhills cyprinid. Its conservation depends on protecting its core habitat in the upper Lumber River system.
