# Fishes of Eagle Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990s survey, based on a [[Eagle Creek Fish Survey (1987)]] and earlier collections dating to 1950, documents the fish assemblage of Eagle Creek, a tributary of the [[Gila River]] in southeastern Arizona. The authors record at least 20 fish species, nine of them native (including a reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]]) and 11 non-native. The study focuses on the long-running question of the original Eagle Creek trout, the apparent disappearance of [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] from the system, and the role of a diversion dam in shaping upstream and downstream fish communities. Native fishes dominated upstream of the dam, while non-native species dominated below it. The authors recommend frequent monitoring to detect non-native establishment above the dam early.

## Background & Description

Eagle Creek is a desert stream in Graham and Greenlee counties, Arizona, with headwaters in mixed-conifer and oak woodland and a lower course through desert terrain. Its flow is strongly seasonal, with peak flows typically occurring in July and August and high-flow events often producing floods that scour the channel. A 5.0 m vertical-fall [[Diversion Dam]] (Phelps Dodge) acts as a fish barrier during all but major flood periods.

The authors divided the creek into five reaches: headwater streams above Honeymoon Campground; Honeymoon to Sheep Wash; Sheep Wash to P-Bar Ranch; P-Bar Ranch to the diversion dam (10.6 km); and the diversion dam to the Gila River (21.2 km). Only the four downstream reaches were sampled in 1987. Methods included backpack and bank-mounted [[Electrofishing]], gill and trammel nets, seines, and angling. Because sampling spanned nearly four decades, used varied techniques, and concentrated at road access points, the authors caution that abundance and distribution comparisons are tentative.

The historic [[Native Trout Identity]] question is central. Early workers referred Eagle Creek trout to Gila trout (O. gilae), but Miller (1972) later described the Apache trout (O. apache). Subsequent genetic work (allozymes and mitochondrial DNA) by Papoulias et al. and Dowling and Childs found that the Chitty Creek population had been supplanted by introduced rainbow trout, so the original population's identity likely cannot be confirmed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish communities in Eagle Creek reflect the arid American Southwest, where [[Flood Disturbance]] and the species' capacity to recolonize shape persistence. Native fishes (e.g., longfin dace and Sonora sucker) withstand natural flooding, while non-native fishes are displaced downstream during floods. Floods that pass the diversion dam can carry native fish downstream, where they cannot return. The authors note that predation by large non-native catfishes and [[Largemouth Bass]] threatens native suckers and minnows. Introduced species such as red shiner, mosquitofish, and fathead minnow are linked to baitfish releases, pond escapes and floods. The [[Gila River]] basin and the wider region have experienced documented declines of native fishes linked to non-native competition and predation.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** At least 20 fish kinds are recorded from Eagle Creek: nine native species (including reintroduced razorback sucker) and 11 non-native forms.
- **1950 baseline:** [[Miller (1950)]] collected only native species, including longfin dace, roundtail chub, speckled dace, desert sucker, and Sonora sucker, but not spikedace or a native trout.
- **Original trout identity unresolved:** Records variously assign the native trout to Gila trout, Apache trout, or hybrid populations. The authors conclude the original genome has likely been replaced by rainbow trout, so the question may remain unanswered.
- **Spikedace:** Not collected in 1987 and not recorded since 1950. The authors conclude an undetected population must have been present in 1950, since neighboring suitable habitat appeared to be unused and the species was not historically known from the upper Gila mainstream or upstream Salt River tributaries.
- **Loach minnow:** Not found since the 1950 record, although apparently suitable habitat was sampled intensively. The authors suspect it persists in low numbers.
- **1987 survey (13-18 July):** Native fishes comprised about 91% to 98% of specimens in the three upstream reaches. Native fishes were nearly 91% of specimens in the upper reaches, while non-native species dominated below the diversion dam, where introduced species made up 76.6% of 418 specimens.
- **Reach-level diversity:** Native species richness was 5 to 6 per reach among the four upper reaches, but declined sharply near Honeymoon, where only the [[Roundtail Chub]] (a problematic *Gila intermedia*-like form) was found.
- **Razorback sucker:** Two subadults (211 and 223 mm SL) were captured above the diversion dam in August 1986, and 10 individuals (47-72 mm SL) were taken below Sheep Wash in July 1987. Fish had been stocked upstream from the Willow Creek mouth, with 93,750 individuals (about 60 mm SL) stocked on 2 June 1987.
- **Non-native species:** Rainbow trout (sport fishing) occurred from Honeymoon to Sheep Wash. Red shiner, fathead minnow, yellow bullhead, channel catfish and smallmouth bass were found between Sheep Wash and the diversion dam. Diversity of non-native forms doubled below the dam, including common carp, flathead catfish, mosquitofish, and largemouth bass.
- **Smallmouth bass origin:** Its presence is considered an enigma; the authors believe it was transferred from the Black River by pumping via Willow Creek.
- **Red shiner:** Comprised more than half the fish taken below the diversion dam and is implicated in spikedace declines in the Gila River basin.
- **Diversion dam effects:** The dam blocks upstream fish movement, so native populations above it persist, but non-native fish can spread upstream through unintentional or intentional transfers. The authors describe the current native fauna as essentially intact but vulnerable.
- **Conservation recommendations:** The authors urge a common management goal for Chitty Creek (preserving either Apache or Gila trout), frequent monitoring of Eagle Creek to detect non-native expansion above the dam early, and development of conservation strategies for the native fauna.
