# Biological Evaluation of Grow-Out Ponds for Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub at the Lake Havasu City Wastewater Treatment Plant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Biological Evaluation]], prepared by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (Lake Havasu Field Office) for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and dated 20 August 1996, assesses the effects of using two tertiary treatment ponds at the [[Lake Havasu City Wastewater Treatment Plant]] to grow juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) to more than 250 mm total length (TL) before release into [[Lake Havasu]]. Both species are federally listed as endangered. The project is a joint venture between the City of Lake Havasu and the [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Project]] (LHFIP).

The authors conclude that the action **may affect, but is not likely to adversely affect**, the two species or their habitat. They also find no direct or indirect effects on bonytail chub critical habitat. The ponds are expected to increase the number of fish reaching the lake, and the reintroduced population is projected to persist for 40 to 50 years, roughly the species' life span.

## Background & Description

**Project area.** The two ponds sit on the treatment-plant site, locally called "the Island." Each covers 0.25 to 0.35 surface acres, is about 2 m deep, and is rectangular with uniformly sloped sides. They are lined with PVC and covered with gravel. Inflow is steady at about 2 cubic feet per second (cfs). In summer, pond water irrigates a nearby golf course. In winter, overflow goes to adjacent holding ponds to evaporate or infiltrate.

**Operations and security.** The ponds are used on alternating years so one can be dried for aquatic macrophyte control. Continuous 24-hour aeration keeps dissolved oxygen high, with saturation approaching or exceeding 100% throughout the water column. The site is enclosed by a 10-foot chain-link fence topped with barbed wire and is staffed 24 hours a day. Treatment staff have previously removed large goldfish (*Carassius auratus*, over 300 mm TL) from both ponds, which indicates the effluent is biologically productive.

**Proposed implementation.**
- Any resident fish are removed while each pond is drained before stocking.
- Inlets and outlets are screened to prevent fish movement, and netting covers the ponds to deter avian predators.
- For 1996–97, the grow-out area is 0.35 surface acres.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, through the Parker Fishery Resource Office, supplies about 500 juvenile razorback suckers.
- Bonytail chub are supplied in a separate batch at approximately 50 to 150 mm TL; the count is illegible in the scanned source. Fish are to be obtained from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] or [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].
- Stocking is planned for fall, with harvest for release by June.
- Growth and survival are monitored monthly. BLM monitors dissolved oxygen, water temperature, pH, total dissolved solids, total alkalinity, nitrite, nitrate, orthophosphate, and unionized ammonia on a biweekly basis, in addition to the plant's own effluent monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Listed species in the area.** The document lists these federally threatened or endangered animals as possibly occurring in the general area: razorback sucker, bonytail chub, [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Brown Pelican]], and [[Peregrine Falcon]]. No listed plant species are known from the project area.

**Critical habitat.** On 21 March 1994, Lake Havasu was designated critical habitat for the bonytail chub up to full pool elevation (450 ft MSL). No critical habitat for any listed species lies within the project area.

**Other wildlife.** The evaluation finds no suitable habitat for bald eagles, peregrine falcons, Yuma clapper rails, [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], or brown pelicans at the treatment plant. Surveys by the BLM for Yuma clapper rails are conducted annually in the lake, and rails have been found at several sites. No southwestern willow flycatchers were detected in a June 1996 survey. Biologists at the Havasu National Wildlife Refuge recorded three flycatchers at Mesquite Bay, about four miles north of the site. Bald eagles and peregrine falcons are recorded mainly through incidental observation.

**Water quality (July 1996 pond monitoring).** The document states that all parameters were within tolerable ranges for warmwater fish growth and survival, with one exception. Reported effluent values were monitored from January through July 1996 in 1–2 sampling events per parameter; the source does not give a summary of compliance thresholds beyond this statement.

**Effluent contaminants.** Volatile organic, base/neutral, pesticide, and PCB analyses of the effluent (January 1996) reported most compounds as not detected (ND). Parasite and enteric virus tests were negative for *Ascaris lumbricoides* and enteric viruses. Radiochemical analysis was also submitted, reported in picocuries per liter.

**Historical status of the razorback sucker.** The species once occurred throughout the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. Dams and impoundments permanently altered its habitat, and backwaters were lost. Razorback suckers are now rare or sporadic, and the species has essentially disappeared from Lake Havasu and the lower river.
- Minckley (1979) reported that trammel and tow-net surveys in Lake Havasu from 1976 to 1982 captured no razorback suckers.
- Five adults from Lake Havasu were found in the Central Arizona Project Granite Reef Aqueduct sampling in 1986.
- Tow-net surveys in 1985 and 1986 found larvae comprising 0.56% of 6,617 collected larvae.
- No significant natural recruitment in the lower basin has been documented since 1963.

**Historical status of the bonytail chub.** The bonytail once ranged throughout the mainstream Colorado River and its major tributaries. It was rare by 1942 and is now described as functionally extinct, with only a few individuals remaining. A senile remnant population persists in [[Lake Mohave]]. No successful recruitment has occurred since 1954, based on the age of wild fish captured.

**Angler interaction.** Lake Havasu receives an estimated 43,000 angler use days per year. Only two razorback suckers are known to have been taken by anglers (Ulmer and Anderson 1985). Information signs and an outreach program are planned to ask anglers to release any bonytail or razorback sucker they catch unharmed.

## Key Findings

1. **Reintroduction background.** Since 1993, the LHFIP has released about 1,000 sub-adult razorback suckers and about 150 bonytail chub (250–350 mm TL) into Lake Havasu. A bonytail chub was recaptured in 1996 in the Bill Williams River arm. Parker trammel netting in 1996 captured five of the released razorback suckers (as read from the scanned text).

2. **Historical absence from the site.** The ponds are constructed, so they were never historically used by either species.

3. **Expected benefit.** The ponds would add rearing space and increase the number of fish released, which would enlarge wild populations in Lake Havasu. The project also offers a chance for local public involvement in recovery.

4. **Expected mortality.** Mortality is unknown but expected. Predator netting, pond aeration, and inlet/outlet screening are intended to reduce losses.

5. **Angler risk.** Grown-out fish released into the lake could increase accidental take by anglers. Signage and outreach are the planned mitigation.

6. **Effect determination.** The proposed rearing and release "may affect but is not likely to adversely affect" razorback sucker and bonytail chub or their habitat. The authors find no direct or indirect effects on bonytail chub critical habitat. Expanded rearing is expected to result in larger wild populations that could persist for 40 to 50 years, approximately the species' life spans.

7. **Consultation.** The evaluation lists individuals contacted, including USFWS biologists at the Parker Fishery Resource Office and Havasu National Wildlife Refuge, BLM fisheries and wildlife staff, Anglers United, and Lake Havasu City public works and wastewater personnel. Principal references include [[Minckley]] (1973, 1979, 1983, 1991), [[Marsh]] and Minckley (1989), [[Ulmer]] and Anderson (1985), and [[USEPA]] (1976) water-quality criteria.
