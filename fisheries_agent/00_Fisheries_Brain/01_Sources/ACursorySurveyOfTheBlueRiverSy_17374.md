# A Cursory Survey of the Blue River System of Nebraska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This brief 1931 Bureau of Fisheries Economic Circular (No. 73), by H. L. Canfield and A. H. Wiebe, reports a cursory survey of the [[Blue River System]] in Nebraska, carried out in July 1930. The survey aimed to determine which game fish species could profitably be introduced into the river system. Because time, personnel and equipment were severely limited, the authors describe the work as very incomplete, but consider the system broadly representative of streams in the region. The survey measured pH, [[Methyl Orange Alkalinity]], [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and temperature, seined for fish, and noted aquatic organisms. The authors concluded that water quality was largely suitable for fish, that [[Hydroelectric Dams]] were the most serious habitat problem, and that [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] should be planted.

## Background & Description

The streams are typical of agricultural (cultivated) watersheds. Apart from short stretches near their heads, they are turbid and silt-laden, with bottoms largely covered by silt washed from ploughed fields. Gravelly reaches occur where current is swift. Stream width varies greatly, and power dams modify it along many reaches.

Survey sites included:

- **[[Big Blue River]]:** Stromsburg, Surprise, Ulysses, Staplehurst, Seward, Milford, Crete, Wilber, Dewitt, Beatrice, Blue Springs and Barneston.
- **[[Little Blue River]]:** Fairbury and Hebron.
- **[[Sandy Creek]]:** Alexandria, a largely spring-fed tributary of the Little Blue.
- **[[West Fork Big Blue River]]:** Stockham, McGool Junction and Beaver Crossing.
- **[[Beaver Creek]]:** near York, a tributary of the West Fork.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry**

- **pH:** Big Blue values ranged from about 7.5 (Seward, Milford, Wilber, Barneston, Crete below city) to 8.2 at Stromsburg near the river's head. The Little Blue at Fairbury reached 8.3. Sandy Creek read 7.4 and 7.5 above and below a dam. The West Fork read 7.5 to 7.9. The lowest value came from Beaver Creek at York, where pollution was suspected. The scanned figure is partly illegible but is below neutral. All values were alkaline and tolerable for common warm-water fishes.
- **Methyl orange alkalinity** (a rough measure of hardness): Big Blue 200–280 ppm; Little Blue 205 ppm; West Fork 210 and 240 ppm; Beaver Creek at York 310 ppm, the maximum. The authors note that hard waters may support more zooplankton, an important food for small fish. Tests at Seward indicated that carbonic acid is the principal acid in the Big Blue.
- **[[Dissolved Oxygen]]:** the authors treat this as the most reliable indicator of fish suitability. Big Blue values ranged from 13.3 ppm at Stromsburg down to 5.33 ppm below the Crete sewer outlet and below Wilber. Little Blue at Fairbury read 9.33 ppm. Sandy Creek read 7.20 and 9.06 ppm. West Fork ranged from 6.4 to 8.26 ppm. Beaver Creek below York read 1.06 ppm, the only value below the minimum required by most fishes.
- **Temperature:** surface and bottom readings in the summer table fell roughly between 65°F and 80°F.

**Pollution.** Sewage from towns, gas-works wastes at Beatrice and garage wastes contributed to local oxygen depletion. The authors judged pollution a minor factor overall, citing high oxygen, the absence of sludge worms, and abundant [[May-fly Nymphs]]. Natural gas was replacing gas-works effluent, and a possible end to sewage discharge at Crete was being discussed. Beaver Creek below York was the clear exception.

**Dams.** There are 12 or 13 power dams on the Big Blue between Surprise and Barneston, 3 on the Little Blue between Hebron and Steele City, and 3 on the West Fork between Beaver Crossing and its mouth. No [[Fishways]] were maintained, so upstream migration was blocked. Operation through mill races caused water-level fluctuations of up to 2 feet in 24 hours. Dams also created deeper reservoirs that favoured larger fish.

**Silting.** Heavy rain carries large quantities of soil from plowed fields into the streams, producing turbidity and silt deposits that may interfere with spawning.

## Key Findings

**Fish community**

- The survey took few game fish in its seine hauls. Minnows (chiefly [[Blackhead Minnow]]), crayfish, bullheads and small carp were common.
- Food fishes taken in the system were channel catfish, bullheads, carp, white crappie, green sunfish and buffalo. There is evidence that these reproduce in the stream or in lagoons and sloughs.
- Yellow perch apparently reproduce in lagoons or sloughs. Yellow pike are taken occasionally at Crete but show no evidence of present reproduction.
- No bass, black crappie or bluegill were taken or reported by anglers. Green sunfish were the only sunfish present. Black crappie had been planted by the State but were not propagating.
- Residents said the Big Blue was once a prime pike stream. Only a few old pike remained, and reintroduction was not recommended because pike and pickerel are highly predaceous.

**Recommendations**

1. Create more permanent backwaters to serve as spawning areas for largemouth bass, white crappie, bluegill and, based on results at Fairport, Iowa, smallmouth bass. The new ponds at Crete (about 20 to 30 acres, being built for natural ice and connected to the river) were identified as a good bass opportunity.
2. Require dam operators to maintain a more constant water level.
3. Establish fishways, if practical, so fish can migrate upstream.
4. Plant largemouth black bass, smallmouth black bass and bluegill. Water temperature, oxygen, alkalinity and habitat were judged suitable for these species.

**Trout.** Water in places, notably spring-fed [[Sandy Creek]] at Alexandria, was suitable for some trout. However, high-water influxes of warmer water made natural reproduction doubtful. Large stocked fingerlings might survive to provide fishing. [[Brown Trout]] were suggested as the only species likely to reproduce, since they spawn in fall and tolerate summer warmth. Turkey Creek, suggested by residents, was not visited.

**Overall conclusion.** Water quality across the system was generally adequate for fish. Dams, sudden water-level changes, the lack of fishways, sewage at local points and silt were the main limiting factors. The authors judged the introduction of black bass and bluegill to be a reasonable venture.
