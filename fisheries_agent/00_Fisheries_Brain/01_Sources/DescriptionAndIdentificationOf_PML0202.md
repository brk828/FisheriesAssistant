# Larval Catostomid Identification in the Gila River Drainage: Draft Report and Expert Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1989 draft report by Kevin R. Bestgen with a formal review letter from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies, dated 4 November 1989) addressed to Dean A. Hendrickson of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Nongame Branch. The draft provides diagnostic criteria and original illustrations for identifying early life stages of three catostomids of the [[Gila River Drainage]]: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*). The work supports the [[Razorback Sucker]] restoration program, which requires reliable larval identification.

The reviewer judged the draft to need careful revision. The main objections were the omission of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Rio Grande Sucker]], missing sample sizes, weak integration of figures and keys, and an inadequate introduction and methods section.

## Background & Description

**Study species**
- ***Xyrauchen texanus* (Razorback Sucker):** An endemic Colorado River basin species, formerly widespread and abundant. It declined through habitat alteration, water development, land use, and non-native fish introductions. In the Gila basin it was severely reduced by 1950 and extirpated shortly afterward (Minckley 1973, 1983). It is listed as endangered by Arizona and proposed for federal listing.
- ***Catostomus insignis* (Sonora Sucker) and *Pantosteus clarki* (Desert Sucker):** Both are native to the Gila basin and are the only catostomids other than the razorback sucker that currently occur sympatrically with it there. Their larval taxonomy was described as incomplete, and diagnostic criteria were limited to certain life stages.
- ***Catostomus latipinnis* (Flannelmouth Sucker):** Rare or possibly extirpated in the basin. The reviewer noted it may become the focus of a future reintroduction program.
- ***Pantosteus plebeius* (Rio Grande Sucker):** Introduced to a limited area of the upper Gila drainage in New Mexico and collected once in eastern Arizona.

**Development stages (after Snyder and Muth)**
- **Protolarva:** the period after hatching until distinct fin rays appear.
- **Flexion mesolarva:** an incomplete adult complement of caudal fin rays.
- **Post-flexion mesolarva:** a full adult caudal complement.
- **Metalarva:** full adult complements of principal rays in the caudal, dorsal, and anal fins.
- **Juvenile:** the point at which the finfold is completely absorbed.

Stage transitions were assessed from quantitative and qualitative analysis of many specimens. The author notes that food, temperature, and light can shift pigmentation and size at transition, so aberrant specimens need cautious interpretation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Specimen sources:** Sonora and desert sucker eggs were fertilized from fish captured in the upper Gila River, New Mexico, cultured in aquaria, and preserved at intervals. Field specimens came from the Verde and Salt rivers, the Gila drainage and tributaries, and [[Aravaipa Creek]] in Arizona, and from the Gila and San Francisco rivers in New Mexico.
- **Razorback sucker specimens:** Cultured fish came from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (1986 and 1987 stocks) and from wild Colorado River adults near Grand Junction, Colorado. Some were previously stocked fish provided by J.E. Brooks (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service).
- **Hatchery effects:** Razorback specimens were from hatcheries and were likely well fed, which may have influenced body shape. Pigmentation differences between cultured and wild fish appeared negligible.
- **Stocking status:** A program to stock hatchery-reared razorback suckers began in 1981. The reviewer stated there is no evidence that wild or reintroduced razorbacks have survived to reproductive size in the basin for at least 40 years, and larval stockings were discontinued some time ago. Predation by ictalurid catfishes was identified as a deterrent to reestablishment (Marsh and Brooks 1989).
- **Environmental influences on development:** Temperature affected hatching time. Eggs held at 15°C for *P. clarki* hatched in 12–15 days (P.R. Turner, personal communication). Water temperature, flow, and turbidity were noted as possible sources of variable growth.

## Key Findings

**Diagnostic and identification results**
- The draft includes a dichotomous key with accompanying illustrations (Figs. 1–16), species accounts, and life-stage comparisons.
- **Protolarvae:** Pigmentation pattern is the most diagnostic feature. Unpigmented protolarvae under 9.5 mm SL are attributed to *X. texanus*. Unpigmented protolarvae and early mesolarvae between 9.5 and 14 mm SL are attributed to *C. insignis*.
- **Hatching size:** *P. clarki* hatches with observable pigment (30–50×) and is slightly smaller than *C. insignis*. *X. texanus* is much smaller and less robust.
- **Dorsal melanophores:** *X. texanus* has sparse dorsal melanophores of uneven size, with a few large stellate cells. *P. clarki* has small, profuse dorsal melanophores. *C. insignis* shows three parallel dorsal rows with a "tractor tread" pattern.
- **Mesolarvae:** Gut pigmentation and ventral patterns separate the species. *P. clarki* has darkened gut and a pre-vent pigment line.
- **Metalarvae and juveniles:** Mouth position, lower-lip cleft, and lip notching differ among species. *X. texanus* has a terminal mouth with an oblique lower jaw. *P. clarki* has notched lips and a shallow median cleft.
- **Meristics:** Dorsal fin rays in *X. texanus* are 13–16. Other species have fewer than 13.
- **Size-at-event data:** Developmental events occur at different sizes in each species, making them strong discriminators. Figure captions give example stages: *P. clarki* protolarva 11.5 mm SL, post-flexion mesolarva 15.8 mm, metalarva 20.2 mm, and juvenile 30.0 mm. *C. insignis* protolarva 14.3 mm, post-flexion mesolarva 17.9 mm, metalarva 21.5 mm, and juvenile 30.2 mm.

**Sample sizes**
- The number of specimens examined per species is not legible in the scanned draft. The review letter states that no sample sizes are given in the text or tables, and that a sample of 4 specimens for *C. insignis* raises concern.

**Reviewer critique (Marsh, 1989)**
- **Scope:** Omitting [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Rio Grande Sucker]] seriously limits the document's utility. The limitations should be stated clearly at the start of the report.
- **Geographic bias:** Most *Catostomus* and *Pantosteus* specimens came from New Mexico, not Arizona.
- **Introduction:** It should begin with an overview of the catostomid fauna of the Gila basin and clearly state the rationale, including the absence of adult razorbacks for 40 years. The reviewer asked why Minckley and Gustafson (1983) was not cited, as it describes razorback development.
- **Methods:** Needed expansion, including how novice collectors can identify larvae as catostomids, collection locations, dates, methods, and culture conditions.
- **Results:** Figures should be referenced throughout the text and grouped by stage so that all three species can be compared on the same page. Quantitative lengths should replace generic terms in the keys.
- **Tables:** Tables 1 and 2 lack sample sizes and collection data. Some cited sources (Smith 1987, Snyder 1989) are missing from the literature list.
- **References:** Four cited works (Brooks 1987, Hamman 1985, Johnson and Rinne 1982, and Minckley and Gustafson 1982) do not appear in the text.
- **Culture conditions:** Not provided for razorback sucker.

**Recommendations**
- Revise the draft to address the listed omissions, add sample sizes and collection data, integrate figures and keys, and clarify the rationale for the work.
- Have experts in larval taxonomy review the revised draft before finalization.
