# Chemical and Biological Patterns in the Lower Colorado River System

#limnology #plankton #zooplankton

## Executive Summary

This Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science paper by Everett, Carlson, and Qasim (University of Arizona, Tucson) reports a preliminary survey of the [[Lower Colorado River]] and its reservoirs, sampled March 20–26, 1970. The authors link changes in plankton communities to water chemistry, temperature, light, and dissolved oxygen along the [[Colorado River]] and its dam-controlled reservoirs. The main pattern is a downstream decline in zooplankton, especially [[Daphnia]] and [[Calanoid Copepods]], alongside a shift toward blue-green algae and benthic diatoms. The authors suggest that low concentrations of essential [[Micronutrients]], such as copper and possibly cobalt, may contribute to these changes. They describe the results as preliminary and speculative, and they call for a whole-system research program.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** The authors state that the river's salinity had risen by nearly 240 ppm, mainly from agricultural activity and salt loading. Changes in water quality were expected to affect the frequency, quantity, and biomass of plankton, which in turn affect fish and invertebrate food supplies.
- **Motivation:** Earlier studies showed a distinct shift in plankton communities, with large populations of [[Blue-Green Algae]] and [[Benthic Diatoms]] and a decline in healthy consumer populations. The authors note that the [[Hoover Dam]] reservoir system strongly affects downstream water temperature and chemistry.
- **Study area:** The survey covered the main stem of the Lower Colorado River and its reservoirs, including [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Havasu]]. Sampling sites included South Cove, Temple Bar, Bonelli Landing, Echo Bay, Willow Beach, [[Las Vegas Wash]], [[Kingman Wash]], Lake Havasu, Ehrenberg, and Yuma. Figure 1 maps the locations, and Figure 2 shows sites on Lake Mead.
- **Approach:** The team combined standard limnological methods with plankton counts, water chemistry, and bacteriological and oxygen measurements. They describe the work as a system-level study rather than one based only on isolated observations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Plankton community structure.** Seven indicator zooplankton taxa were identified and quantified at the sampling sites. Table 1 gives qualitative plankton abundance for three lakes, including cladocerans, copepods, rotifers, diatoms, and blue-green algae. Table 2 gives counts per cubic metre across sites, including Cyclopoida, Calanoida, nauplii, [[Daphnia]], and [[Asplanchna]].

**Key ecological relationships reported:**
- Filamentous blue-green algae are poor food for zooplankton. The authors say they create a "dead end" at the primary food-chain level.
- The authors invoke a basic ecological principle: a species dominates where conditions are most favourable. Water quality changes along the river therefore create a range of conditions and associated dominance patterns.
- Reproductive temperature is critical for most organisms, even though adults tolerate temperature fluctuations. The reservoir outlet depth therefore helps determine the fauna and flora downstream.

**Physical and chemical conditions:**
- **Temperature:** Surface temperature varied by about 10 degrees between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Hoover Dam, showing the effect of the dam-reservoir system.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Stratification was noted in Lake Powell, Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, and Lake Havasu. Available dissolved oxygen was more than 8 ppm, which the authors consider too high to allow major anaerobic conditions during a summer thermocline.
- **Biochemical oxygen demand:** BOD did not indicate surface problems from organic loading. The highest value, 4 ppm, came from Lake Mohave. The authors caution that bottom waters may still experience anaerobic conditions from organic fallout.
- **Light:** [[Solar Radiation Penetration]] was generally poor. The 5% light level was between roughly 10 and 18 m (partly illegible in the scan), possibly due to high turbidity. Transmittance tended to decrease across Lake Mead.
- **Major ions:** Calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, and chloride increased downstream, which the authors attribute to loading and concentration effects. [[Sulfate]] increased significantly across the lake, and the authors link this to ion exchange at sediment surfaces.
- **Nutrients:** [[Nitrate]] concentrations showed a positive trend across Lake Mead. [[Phosphate]] concentrations varied considerably downstream. The authors say more data on the rate, type, and biomass of primary producers are needed to interpret these fluctuations.

**Micronutrients (Table 3).** Eight micronutrients were measured. Key results:
- Copper declined in Lake Mead and was undetectable below Willow Beach.
- [[Cobalt]] was not detected by standard [[Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy]] in all samples. The authors plan better analytical methods and bioassays.
- [[Manganese]] concentrations appeared to increase from Lake Powell to Lake Havasu, possibly due to downstream loading.
- Boron, fluoride, zinc, silicate, and iron were present in sufficient amounts that they were unlikely to limit primary productivity.

## Key Findings

1. **Zooplankton decline:** The [[Daphnia]] population in Lake Mead fell from about 134,000 per m² to about 1,000 per m². A separate series in the text also reports a fall from about 175,000 per m² to complete extinction at Hoover Dam (the taxon is not explicit in the scanned text). Calanoid copepods fell from about 118,000 per m² at South Cove to about 12,000 at Kingman Wash.
2. **Seven indicator species:** The authors used seven indicator zooplankton species to track changes, showing a marked decline in zooplankton across Lake Mead and a similar pattern further downstream.
3. **Algal shift:** Blue-green algae and benthic diatoms increased, while preferred zooplankton food declined. This points to a food-chain imbalance.
4. **Micronutrient limitation:** Low copper and possibly cobalt levels may reduce primary productivity and could shift plankton communities. The authors emphasise that these links are speculative.
5. **Water quality is spatially variable:** Dissolved oxygen, light, temperature, major ions, and nutrients all show clear downstream or reservoir-to-reservoir patterns.
6. **Bacteriology:** Colony counts below Hoover Dam were high, but the nonspecific nutrient agar used cannot identify contamination sources. The authors plan follow-up tests for [[Fecal Coliform Bacteria]].
7. **Conclusions and future work:** The authors state that their results are a preliminary basis for discussing abiotic-biotic interactions in the Colorado River system. They recommend better analytical methods, more work on primary productivity, and continued research on the whole system.
