# Effects of Artificial Propagation and Weather on Recruitment in the Lake Ontario Whitefish Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonus

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by W. J. Christie, from the Ontario Department of Lands and Forests, tests whether hatchery [[Fry Planting]] of [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*) in [[Lake Ontario]] increased the commercial catch. Using long-term catch statistics, an [[Alternate-Year Planting Experiment]] (1944–1954), and scale-based [[Year-Class Strength]] analysis, the author found no significant relationship between fry planting levels and later catches. Year-class strength was instead associated with air temperatures during spawning and hatching. The study also links the catch's instability to rising fishing pressure, a shorter age structure in the catch, and a continuing decline in spawning stock, and it raises the possibility of over-exploitation.

## Background & Description

*Coregonus clupeaformis* (Mitchill) is the Lake Whitefish, the target species of the [[Gill-Net Fishery]] studied here. Canadian waters of Lake Ontario received hatchery whitefish fry in nearly all years from 1882 to 1954. Commercial catches varied widely from year to year, and did not track the size of preceding plantings. Peak catches in the 1920s were followed by a steep, multi-year decline. Concern over this decline led to a study of the effectiveness of fry plantings, starting in 1944.

The [[Glenora Provincial Fish Hatchery]], on the [[Bay of Quinte]] near [[Picton Ontario]], was the only source of whitefish fry for Lake Ontario for some years. Its operations were placed on an alternate-year schedule. The hatchery produced fry in the even-numbered years 1944 through 1954 and was inoperative in the odd-numbered years 1945 through 1953. No plantings have been made since 1954.

Picton is the main clearing centre for the Lake Ontario commercial fishery, which made it a convenient place to examine the catch. Investigators have been stationed there each summer since 1944 to sample the commercial whitefish catch for effects of the alternate planting routine.

A preliminary report (Lapworth, 1956) covered data from 1944 through 1951. It examined long-term catch statistics for the Bay of Quinte against preceding levels of hatchery support. Scale-based age determination showed that five-year-old fish (five completed annuli) dominated the samples, contributing an average of 47% of annual scale samples. For 1924 through 1951, Lapworth found no correlation between the number of fry planted in a given year and the catch five years later, when those fish would have been recruited.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishery and species interactions.** The disappearance of [[Lake Trout]] and [[Ciscoes]] from the commercial catch increased fishing pressure on whitefish. The author attributes the rise in fishing efficiency and intensity during the study period to this shift. These changes were likely responsible for lowering the average age of whitefish in the catch by almost one year. The catch became restricted to essentially one year-class in recent years.

**Age structure and spawning stock.** Most whitefish were caught before their first spawning. A decline in spawning stock was almost continuous throughout the study period. Year-to-year fluctuations in the catch grew both because of the younger age structure and because year-class strength varied more.

**Climate and recruitment.** The study tested a link between weather and recruitment. Air temperatures at the times of spawning and hatching correlated significantly with the strength of the year-classes produced. Cold Novembers followed by warm Aprils were associated with larger year-classes. The opposite combinations were associated with weaker broods. The author suggests this climatic pattern may explain the periodicity in the catch statistics.

**Stock–recruitment dynamics.** Year-classes produced in favourable years tended to remain strong down to quite low stock levels. This pattern resembles [[Ricker Type C Reproduction Curve]]. It differs mainly in that the limiting diagonal for minimum reproduction falls below the replacement diagonal. The author suggests that whitefish may need more than one spawning to achieve stock replacement.

## Key Findings

1. **No detectable hatchery contribution.** Long-term statistics showed no significant relationship between fry planting levels and ensuing catch levels. The alternate-year experiment (even-numbered years 1944–1954) also failed to show a detectable contribution of hatchery fish to the fishery.

2. **Apparent support effect judged coincidental.** Year-classes that received hatchery support averaged larger than unsupported ones. The author attributes this to a phase match with the alternate-year periodicity that characterized the catch statistics across the whole series. That periodicity was also present in the six years before the alternate-year plantings began.

3. **Progeny per parent unaffected.** Estimates of spawning stock were calculated. Progeny-per-parent ratios were not significantly affected by fry plantings.

4. **Weather explains year-class variation.** Air temperatures at spawning and hatching were significantly correlated with year-class strength. Cold Novembers followed by warm Aprils favoured large year-classes, and the reverse favoured weak ones. This was offered as a possible explanation for the periodicity.

5. **Fishery shifts altered the catch.** Removal of [[Lake Trout]] and [[Ciscoes]] from the catch increased pressure on whitefish. Rising efficiency and intensity of fishing lowered the average age of catch by almost one year. The annual catch became restricted to one year-class in recent years.

6. **Declining spawning stock.** Spawning stock declined almost continuously during the study period. Most fish were caught before first spawning, which likely contributed to the decline.

7. **Growing catch instability.** Catch fluctuations increased because of the reduced average age and greater variation in year-class strength.

8. **Stock–recruitment pattern.** Favourable-year year-classes persisted at low stock levels, resembling a [[Ricker Type C Reproduction Curve]]. The limiting diagonal lies below the replacement diagonal, suggesting that whitefish may require more than one spawning to replace the stock.

9. **Over-exploitation concern.** The author concludes that the present instability of the catch, together with the potentially serious effect of any sustained break in the climatic rhythm, points to over-exploitation of the stock.
