# Gila Trout Habitat Decline and Conservation Controversy

## Executive Summary
This document is a 1994 letter to the editor from the Greater Gila Biodiversity Project, responding to a newspaper article on the petition to designate critical habitat for the [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*). The letter disputes official framing of the causes of species decline, arguing that livestock grazing, historic loss of beaver populations, decades of fire suppression, and introduction of non-native trout have combined to severely degrade Gila trout habitat in Arizona and New Mexico. While not a peer-reviewed scientific source, it contains reported population and habitat statistics relevant to the conservation status of the species as of 1994.

## Background & Description
No morphological data (body measurements, coloration, meristics, or anatomical descriptions) are provided in this source. The text is a policy/conservation letter rather than a biological description of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Cold, high-elevation streams and associated wetland habitats within the [[Gila River]] drainage in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Key habitat threats identified:**
  - [[Livestock Grazing]] impacts on wetland/riparian habitat
  - Near-elimination of [[Beaver]] populations (loss of beaver-modified wetland habitat)
  - Decades of [[Fire Suppression]] in Southwestern forests, altering watershed hydrology and fire regimes
  - Introduction of non-native trout (implied [[Rainbow Trout]]) causing [[Hybridization]] and competitive displacement
- **Watershed occupancy:** Six of the watersheds currently occupied by Gila trout are grazed by livestock; unoccupied watersheds considered critical for species recovery in Arizona and New Mexico reportedly lack protection from overgrazing by the [[U.S. Forest Service]].
- **Case example:** The Main Diamond Creek population (Diamond Bar Allotment, [[Gila National Forest]]) — once regarded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as the "most stable and secure population of Gila trout" — was extirpated by a fire-suppression-induced wildfire followed by grazing-induced flooding.
- **Broader ecosystem linkage:** The letter frames healthy Gila trout watersheds as coupled to downstream human water quality and flood risk reduction, indicating an ecosystem-services perspective connecting fish habitat health to human community outcomes.

## Key Findings
- **Primary historic driver of decline:** Introduction of non-native trout species into habitat formerly occupied by Gila trout is cited as the most important historic cause of population decline.
- **Current primary threat (per Fish and Wildlife Service official):** [[Hybridization]] with non-native trout, rather than grazing or habitat degradation, is identified as the main current problem — though the letter's authors dispute this framing as incomplete.
- **Population trend data:**
  - Number of Gila trout populations increased from 5 (1967) to 12 (as of reporting, attributed to recovery efforts).
  - However, since 1989: 4 of the 12 populations have been eliminated, and 3 additional populations were reduced by 95–98%.
  - The Main Diamond Creek population loss alone eliminated an estimated one-half of all wild Gila trout.
- **Management discrepancy:** Forest Service officials stated grazing occurs on only "two or three streams" with extant Gila trout populations; the letter counters that six occupied watersheds are grazed, and additional unoccupied recovery-critical watersheds remain unprotected from overgrazing.
- **Conservation metric emphasis:** Both the Forest Service (Jerry Stefferud) and the letter's author agree that number of streams and stream-miles occupied are more meaningful conservation metrics than raw population counts — though the letter argues these metrics have also declined sharply since 1989.
- **Overall conclusion:** The letter argues for a multi-factor model of decline (grazing, beaver loss, fire suppression, non-native introductions/hybridization) rather than a single-cause explanation, and calls for expanded critical habitat protections, including in currently unoccupied but recovery-critical watersheds.
