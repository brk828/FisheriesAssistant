# Stream Insects in Algonquin Park, Ontario: Cage-Trap Emergence Study (1938–1941)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-insects #Ephemeroptera #Plecoptera #Trichoptera

## Executive Summary

This University of Toronto Studies monograph (Biological Series No. 56, Ontario Fisheries Research Laboratory Publication No. 69, 1947) by Wm. M. Sprules reports a four-year ecological investigation (1938–1941) of stream insects in the Mud Creek and Madawaska River system of [[Algonquin Park]], Ontario. Using the [[Cage-Trap Method]] to sample insect emergence from unit areas of stream bed, the study examined diurnal, seasonal and annual emergence patterns and the effects of freshets, desiccation, beaver-dam flooding, bottom type, distance from source and water temperature. The work is framed as a contribution to stream management, since fish food supply depends on the benthic insect fauna.

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by the scarcity of stream invertebrate research with frequent, regular sampling, and by the limitations of bottom-sampling devices (hand-dip, [[Surber Sampler]], [[Needham Square-Foot Trap]], Ekman dredge). The cage-trap method, introduced by [[F. P. Ide]] in 1937, measures the turnover of bottom fauna rather than a standing population and was found to yield higher values than bottom sampling.

Fieldwork ran during summer months of 1938–1941 at roughly ten stations, with collections made daily (sometimes hourly or at four-hour intervals in diurnal series). Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera were identified to species; Chironomidae, Simuliidae and a "Miscellaneous" group (Empidae, Ceratopogonidae, Tipulidae, Hemerobiidae, Coenagrionidae) were counted at family level. Physical and chemical conditions were recorded, including temperature (continuous at station 3 by a Negretti and Zambra recorder), flow, depth, colour, dissolved oxygen, and pH.

The study area lies on the Precambrian shield in the mixed conifer-hardwood forest. Mud Creek drains about 6 square miles, falling from 1,400 ft to Galeairy Lake at 1,281 ft, and drains into the [[Ottawa River]] via the Madawaska. Waters were brown and acidic (pH 6.1–6.7 at stations in summer 1938).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions (summer 1938, Table 1):**
- Bottom type: rubble at stations 1–4; muck at 5; gravel at 6; sand at 7.
- Dissolved oxygen 6.3–7.6 cc/l; saturation 91–99%.
- Flow 0.1–2.3 ft/s; volume 0.1–288 cu. ft/s (station 4, the Madawaska River).
- Colour 32–115 (station 3 highest, attributed to meadow runoff).

**Temperature:** Station 3 averaged 62.0 °F over the study. Stations 2 and 3a averaged 6.1 °F and 1.7 °F lower; station 4 averaged 5.4 °F higher. The maximum recorded was 85.0 °F at station 3 (16 August 1938). Accumulated heat above 32 °F from 1 June to 13 September was 3,470 (1938), 3,450 (1939), 3,240 (1940) and 3,470 (1941) day-degrees, indicating little annual variation in total heat budget.

**Disturbances studied:**
- *Freshet (25 May 1940):* A beaver dam at the outlet of [[Castalia Lake]] failed, sending a flood down Mud Creek for about 7 days.
- *Intermittent flow (1938):* Station 1 dried on 17 July 1938 after a beaver pond supply failed.
- *Beaver dam (end of June 1939):* A dam converted the rapids at station 3 into a deep, silted pool.

**Setting:** Stream type ranged from small, shaded headwater riffles (Mud Creek) to a large, sunlit boulder rapids in the [[Madawaska River]] below the Whitney dam. Dominant bottom vegetation included aquatic mosses (*Fontinalis dalecarlica*) and sedges (*Carex rostrata*).

## Key Findings

**1. Cage-trap sampling bias.** Hourly or four-hourly collections over 24 h yielded roughly double the insects of a single daily collection (more than double in some cases). Most of the gain came from small Chironomidae and Baetinae; the authors concluded results are useful for comparison but too low for absolute estimates, and no correction factor was established.

**2. Diurnal emergence.** Emergence was low from midnight to before sunset and rose sharply between 7 and 9 p.m., peaking at 11 p.m. Around 60% of daily emergence occurred between 8 and 12 p.m. Each species emerged at a consistent time of day (e.g., *Pseudocloeon virile* at 4–5 p.m.; *Stenonema heterotarsale* at 10–11 p.m.). The authors inferred that the final stimulus for emergence is decreasing light intensity.

**3. Freshet effects.** Emergence from stations 6 and 7 fell by 41.0% and 51.9% in 1940 compared with 1939, against a 5.6% change at the deep, protected station 5. Free-roving forms (Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera, Chironomidae) declined most; attached larvae and Simuliidae were little affected.

**4. Intermittent flow and desiccation (station 1).** In 1938, 5,017 insects emerged (79.8% before 17 July). In 1939, 4,899 emerged, with 66.8% before 17 July. Fixed-habit groups (Simuliidae and many Trichoptera) were eliminated; mobile Ephemeroptera and Plecoptera likely migrated laterally with receding water. Species richness at station 1 (26 Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera species) was lower than at permanently flowing stations 2 (37) and 3a (48).

**5. Beaver dam flooding.** Conversion of riffle to pool reduced Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera and Trichoptera, while Chironomidae rose from 31% of the catch in 1938 to 84% in 1940. Of the Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Plecoptera, 33 species were eliminated or reduced, 15 showed no change, and 10 new species appeared. Rapid-water specialists were lost immediately; quiet-water species took longer to establish.

**6. Bottom type.** Mean ratios of emergence relative to sand (= 1.0) were: rapid rubble 4.6; rubble (pool) 3.3; gravel 2.1; muck 1.8. Biomass ratios were 20.7, 3.6, 1.5 and 1.0 for rubble, gravel, muck and sand. Species diversity (Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera) was 54 species on rubble, 38 on gravel, 35 on muck and 30 on sand. Chironomidae dominated sand (83.9%) and muck (74.8%); Simuliidae were 21.4% of gravel and 10.8% of rubble emergence.

**7. Surface area and productivity.** The author proposed that the number of insects in an area depends mainly on the utilizable surface area of bottom particles exposed to water, supported by Wene and Wickliff (1940), where rubble baskets in sand held about 3 to 5 times more insects than surrounding sand.

**8. Distance from source.** Total emergence at stations 2, 3a and 3 was similar (approximately 5,400 to 7,200 insects in the periods compared), but station 4 yielded 31,505, reflecting its much larger rubble surface area. Species number increased downstream for Ephemeroptera (10 at station 2 to 28 at station 4) and Trichoptera (15 to 30), while Plecoptera decreased (12 to 7). Plecoptera were relatively more important near the source; Chloroperlidae rose from 16.7% of species at station 2 to 57.1% at station 4.

**9. Temperature and species change.** The total number of species changing between successive stations was directly correlated with average summer temperature. Examples: 9 species changed over 4.4 °F between stations 2 and 3a; 12 species over 5.4 °F between stations 3 and 4. Plecoptera appear largely cold-tolerant, while Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera increase in warmer water. Station 1, though close to the source, showed a warm-water fauna (temperature-limited distribution).

**10. Seasonal emergence.** Species emerged in the same order each year. Timing varied with pre-emergence water temperature: a warm spring advanced emergence and shortened it. *Ameletus ludens* emerged from 29 May 1939 (25-day period) but began five days later in 1940. Some Trichoptera (e.g., *Hydropsyche morosa*, *Cheumatopsyche campyla*) showed two emergence peaks at station 4, possibly two generations per season.

**11. Conclusions.** Quantitative and qualitative distribution of insects results from interacting factors. Temperature, bottom configuration and flow rate are of fundamental importance, and the author stressed that findings from one drainage area cannot be assumed for another.
