# Roots versus Shoots in Nutrient Uptake by Aquatic Macrophytes in Flowing Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-botany #potamogetonaceae #freshwater-ecology

> **Note:** This source concerns aquatic vascular plant (macrophyte) nutrient ecology rather than fish taxa directly. The note has been structured to reflect the actual study organism — [[Potamogeton Crispus]] — within a riverine ecosystem context relevant to aquatic habitat science.

## Executive Summary

Chambers, Prepas, Bothwell, and Hamilton (1989) conducted a factorial transplant experiment in the [[South Saskatchewan River]] to resolve a long-standing debate regarding whether aquatic macrophytes in flowing waters acquire nutrients primarily from **root uptake (sediments)** or **shoot uptake (open water column)**. Using [[Potamogeton Crispus]] transplanted into pails of high- or low-nutrient sediment, placed at sites with contrasting open-water nutrient concentrations (driven by the Saskatoon sewage treatment plant outfall), the authors demonstrated that **sediment nutrient content — not water column chemistry — was the dominant driver of plant biomass, shoot density, and tissue nutrient concentration**. This supports the hypothesis that roots are the primary nutrient acquisition organ for rooted macrophytes even in continuously flowing, well-mixed river systems, challenging prior assumptions that riverine macrophytes rely mainly on open-water nutrient uptake through shoots.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** *Potamogeton crispus* L. (curly-leaf pondweed), a submersed rooted aquatic angiosperm.
- **Plant structure examined:** shoot, root, and rhizome biomass measured separately (dried at 110°C to constant weight).
- **Propagule used in experiment:** intact rhizomes bearing 2–3 stems (each <8 cm long), collected June 28, 1987.
- **Growth response metrics:**
  - Shoot density (stems per pail)
  - Total, shoot, root, and rhizome biomass (g dry weight)
  - Tissue phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N) concentrations, and N/P ratios
- **Key morphological/growth findings:**
  - Shoot biomass in low-nutrient sediment: ~19 g dry weight·m⁻²
  - Shoot biomass in high-nutrient sediment: ~86 g dry weight·m⁻² (>4× greater)
  - Biomass and shoot density consistently greater (P < 0.01) in high- vs. low-nutrient sediment treatments regardless of open-water nutrient regime.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Submersed rooted macrophyte community colonizing riverbed sediments in a large, regulated, seventh-order prairie river (max. depth 2 m; experimental depth 0.6 m).
- **River system:** [[South Saskatchewan River]] → joins [[North Saskatchewan River]] → [[Saskatchewan River]] → Cedar Lake → [[Lake Winnipeg]] → Nelson River → Hudson Bay.
- **Anthropogenic nutrient gradient:** Site located 22 km downstream of the Saskatoon sewage treatment plant, exploiting a natural West-bank (high-nutrient, sewage-plume-influenced) vs. East-bank (low-nutrient) gradient due to limited cross-channel mixing.
- **Sediment characteristics:**
  - Upstream (low-nutrient) sediments: coarser, sandier, lower organic content, water content, and nutrient concentration.
  - Downstream (high-nutrient) sediments: finer particles, higher organic matter (2.40% vs. 0.48%), higher water content (89.0% vs. 11.3%).
- **Environmental co-variables controlled/monitored:** temperature (16–26°C, no bank difference), current velocity (22.9 → 11.9 cm·s⁻¹ over season, attributed to plant biomass blocking flow), and underwater PAR attenuation (weak but significant difference between banks: East 0.79 m⁻¹ vs. West 1.04 m⁻¹) — none considered growth-limiting at 0.6 m depth.
- **Relevance to eutrophication management:** Findings inform aquatic weed control and nutrient loading policy for prairie rivers subject to sewage, agricultural, and industrial nutrient inputs plus flow regulation (e.g., via the [[Gardiner Dam]]).
- **Comparative ecological context:** Contrasts with earlier lentic (lake) studies (e.g., [[Carignan And Kalff 1980]], Best and Mantai 1978) showing sediment-derived P dominance, and with prior lotic (flowing water) studies (Owens and Edwards 1961; Ladle and Casey 1971; Kern-Hanson and Dawson 1981) that failed to find open-water nutrient–biomass correlations — this study provides the mechanistic sediment-based explanation for that pattern.

## Key Findings

1. **Sediment nutrient content, not open-water chemistry, controlled plant growth.** Total, shoot, root, and rhizome biomass and shoot density were highly dependent on sediment nutrient type (P < 0.001) with no significant response to water-column P/N levels (P > 0.25) and no significant sediment × water interaction (P > 0.75).

2. **Tissue nutrient concentrations were primarily sediment-driven.** Tissue N and P concentrations were significantly greater (P < 0.0001) in plants grown in high- vs. low-nutrient sediment.

3. **Weak secondary effect of open water on tissue P and N/P ratio.** In low-nutrient sediment only, plants exposed to nutrient-enriched (West bank) water had higher tissue P (0.01 < P < 0.05) and lower N/P ratios (0.0001 < P < 0.001) than those exposed to low-nutrient (East bank) water — indicating a minor supplementary role for water-column uptake, but only when sediment nutrients were limiting.

4. **No open-water effect detected in high-nutrient sediment**, suggesting that once sediment nutrient supply is sufficient, water column P/N concentration becomes irrelevant to tissue nutrient status.

5. **Water chemistry gradient confirmed:** West bank (sewage-influenced) consistently had higher TP, TDP, SRP, and TDN than East bank across all sampling dates; physicochemical parameters (pH, conductivity, alkalinity, hardness, TDS, major ions) also differed but to a much lesser degree than nutrient concentrations.

6. **Sediment quality contrast:** Downstream sediments were substantially more nutrient-rich (602 µg·g⁻¹ TP; 712 µg·g⁻¹ total organic N) than upstream sediments (258 µg·g⁻¹ TP; 109 µg·g⁻¹ total organic N), consistent with ranges reported for other Canadian prairie rivers.

7. **Ecological conclusion:** Roots are an important, and in this system the dominant, site of nutrient uptake for [[Potamogeton Crispus]] in flowing waters — extending conclusions previously established for lentic macrophytes (e.g., *Myriophyllum spicatum*, per Nichols and Keeney 1976; Best and Mantai 1978; Barko and Smart 1980, 1981; Smith and Adams 1986) to lotic (riverine) systems.

8. **Management implication:** Because higher current velocities are associated with coarser, less nutrient-rich, less densely packed river-bed sediments, the relative importance of sediment-bound vs. river-water nutrients for root uptake likely varies with flow regime and particle size — a critical consideration for predicting the effectiveness of reduced nutrient loading (e.g., from sewage treatment) as an aquatic weed control strategy in regulated prairie rivers.

9. **Unresolved question:** The study could not distinguish whether root-acquired nutrients originate from a sediment-bound pool versus river water percolating through coarse sediment interstitial spaces (porewater exchange), a distinction with direct implications for eutrophication management.
