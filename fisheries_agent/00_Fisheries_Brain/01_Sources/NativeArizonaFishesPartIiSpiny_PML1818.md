# Wildlife Views, Nov-Dec 1969: Native Arizona Spiny-Rayed Minnows and Related Wildlife Articles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This bi-monthly issue of [[Wildlife Views]], published by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], covers native fishes, winter survival, a bird-rich canyon, wildlife habitat theory, a backwater dredging project, and reader correspondence. The lead feature is Part II of W. L. Minckley's series on the [[Spiny-Rayed Minnows]] (tribe Plagopterini), which describes four small Arizona cyprinids that are restricted to streams of the [[Colorado River]] system and several of which are declining. The remaining articles address cold-weather survival, the [[Madera Canyon]] bird habitat, the five essentials of wildlife habitat, and the [[Blythe Backwater A-7]] habitat project.

## Background & Description

- **Author and publisher:** Dr. W. L. Minckley, Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]], Tempe. The magazine is funded by hunting and fishing license sales and federal excise taxes on ammunition, firearms and fishing tackle, not by state general funds.
- **Taxonomic group:** The spiny-rayed minnows are a distinctive tribe found only in the middle and lower Colorado River drainage. Seven kinds are known (six species, one with two subspecies). Two of these, in Nevada, have become extinct in recent years.
- **Diagnostic features:** The group is named for modified fin supports. The dorsal fin has double, spine-like rays at its leading edge, with one ray fitting into a groove in the next. The pelvic fins also carry spine-like rays, a feature the author says is not found in any other minnow. All four Arizona species are silvery, active, and stream-dwelling rather than lake-dwelling.

### The Four Arizona Species

- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** The most distinctive species, with a smooth, scaleless body, flat belly, and strong expanded fins suited to swift, sandy-bottomed channels. It survives only in the lower [[Virgin River]], the last Arizona stream (and the last in the world) that supports it. It once occurred in the [[Gila River]] upstream to Safford, but became extinct soon after settlement. Only three historic records are known, and the last Gila system specimen was collected in 1894. Its name means "wound fin" (dorsal spine) and "most silver" (body color). Its biology is little known.
- **[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*):** Restricted to the Gila River basin. It formerly lived throughout much of the drainage upstream from the Salt-Gila junction, but in the Verde River basin it has been replaced by the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). It is marked by tiny black spots on silvery sides and brownish mottling. It inhabits slow, shallow pools and spawns in spring over sand and gravel.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*):** Restricted to the Little Colorado River basin along the Mogollon Rim. Its dorsal spines do not reach the fin tip and end in soft, unmodified tissue.
- **[[Virgin River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*):** Inhabits springs and smaller creeks of the Virgin River system. It is common in many tributary creeks in Arizona, Nevada and Utah. Its biology has not been studied.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preferences:** Spikedace favor slow, shallow pools and spawn over sand and gravel. Both spinedaces prefer creek pools and deeper, quieter water. The Little Colorado Spinedace feeds mainly on aquatic insects, and its reproductive season runs through the summer.
- **Threats:** The Virgin River populations face modification by highway construction and possible future damming, which the author called a bleak prospect for the woundfin. Competition and displacement by introduced fishes (red shiner) threaten the spikedace.
- **Population status:** The Little Colorado Spinedace came dangerously close to extinction in the early 1960s from natural and human-induced factors. It recovered in the mid-1960s when precipitation and habitat improved. Sampling in 1969 indicated populations were again at a low level, though the study was not extensive enough to define the decline or its causes.
- **Conservation actions:** The Game and Fish Department and [[Arizona State University]] personnel were maintaining a woundfin stock in hatchery raceways and planned to introduce woundfin into other natural waters of the state, primarily to preserve the species and to produce additional forage for game fishes.
- **Ecological role:** The four small fishes have little direct economic value. They may link invertebrates and larger game fishes in the food web and are occasionally used as bait. Their major worth is scientific: they form a closely-knit group isolated in the lower part of a major western drainage, and their distributions reflect past drainage relationships.

## Key Findings

1. **Four Arizona spiny-rayed minnows are described**, all restricted to stream habitats of the Colorado River system and all of conservation concern in the article.
2. **Woundfin** survives only in the lower Virgin River; three historic records exist and the last Gila system specimen was taken in 1894.
3. **Spikedace** has been displaced in the Verde River basin by the introduced red shiner and is now rarer than in the 1950s.
4. **Little Colorado Spinedace** recovered in the mid-1960s but was again at low levels in 1969.
5. **Virgin River Spinedace** is common in several tributary creeks across three states, but its Arizona populations face the same threats as the woundfin.
6. **Management response:** Hatchery propagation and planned reintroductions of woundfin were underway.

### Other Articles and Plans

- **Winter survival:** Sudden cold and wind kill people in Arizona's higher country. The article cites 210 deaths from a single 1966 storm, a six-year-old girl lost between a chicken coop and barn, and two students who died of exposure near a 6,000-foot crash site. It states that about 200 cold-related deaths occur per year in the U.S. Wind chill example: at 34°F with 20 mph winds, exposed flesh feels as cold as 38° below zero. Advice includes layered clothing trapping dead air, thermal underwear, head coverings, mittens for warmth, lug-sole boots, and avoiding frostbite damage by rubbing or applying snow. An inactive man at zero degrees may need seven layers, and a hunter should build shelter and wait out storms rather than walk.
- **Hunting and deer management:** The article argues that regulated public hunting removes surplus deer, preventing starvation, poor reproduction and range damage, which can take over 15 years to repair.
- **Madera Canyon:** Located 38 miles south of Tucson in the [[Santa Rita Mountains]]. More than 200 bird species have been recorded there. The [[Coppery-Tailed Trogon]] (*Trogon elegans*) is the only one of 35 trogon species found in the United States. Its population in Arizona appears to be increasing. The Forest Service, with the Game and Fish Department, excludes livestock and manages roads and trails to limit disturbance near sycamore nesting groves.
- **Habitat essentials:** The author defines five essentials of wildlife habitat: food, water, shelter (cover), space, and the arrangement of these in relation to one another. For example, an adult mule deer eats 8 to 10 pounds of vegetable matter per day, and one deer's stomach contained traces of 180 plants.
- **Blythe Backwater A-7:** The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] isolated this backwater in 1964 with structure A-7. The Game and Fish Department proposed dredging in 1967 to keep the channel as fish habitat. Work began in 1968. The proposal called for removing 106,175 cubic yards, but 595,129 cubic yards were actually excavated. The Bureau made deeper channels than the department recommended, widened side channels to 50 feet, and eliminated shallow flats that caused evaporation losses. A recent visitor survey found fishing pressure higher than anticipated. The author credits the Bureau of Reclamation for the outcome and calls the project an example of inter-agency cooperation.
- **Hunting losses:** A Texas Parks and Wildlife study estimated 8,563 shots were fired to bag 1,355 birds and 579 other waterfowl, scattering about 670 pounds of lead, or roughly 15 shots per bird taken.
