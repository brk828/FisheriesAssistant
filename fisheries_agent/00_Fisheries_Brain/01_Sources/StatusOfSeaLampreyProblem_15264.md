# Status of Sea Lamprey Problem

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 Michigan Department of Conservation report documents the early recognition and response to the [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) invasion of the [[Great Lakes]]. Native to salt water, the sea lamprey adapted to freshwater life after presumably bypassing the [[Niagara Falls]] barrier via the [[Welland Canal]]. Initially considered relatively harmless in the lower lakes, the species began causing severe damage to commercial [[Lake Trout]] fisheries in upper [[Lake Huron]] and northern [[Lake Michigan]] by the early-to-mid 1940s. The report outlines a three-step control framework (life cycle study, weak-point identification, cycle disruption), summarizes early findings on lamprey reproductive biology, describes trapping-weir experiments, and requests public cooperation in reporting spawning populations. An interstate/international committee involving Great Lakes states, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the Province of Ontario was formed to coordinate research and avoid duplicated effort.

## Background & Description

- **Body form:** Slimy, eel-like body plan typical of [[Lamprey|lampreys]].
- **Feeding apparatus:** Vacuum-cup (sucker-disc) mouth used to clatch onto host fish and feed on blood and body fluids (a parasitic/hematophagous feeding mode).
- **Adult size:** Typically more than one foot (≥12 inches) in length.
- **Coloration:** Backs mottled with brown and black; body may show a somewhat golden tint.
- **Larval stage:** Small, non-parasitic larvae; morphologically and ecologically distinct from the parasitic adult phase.
- **Species differentiation note:** Four additional native lamprey species occur in Michigan streams and can be mistaken for immature or young adult sea lampreys; only the sea lamprey is of economic/ecological concern. Concentrations of lampreys longer than 14 inches were flagged as warranting expert identification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range/origin:** Salt water (Atlantic Ocean); considered anadromous-like in its invasion pattern, moving from marine to freshwater systems.
- **Invasion pathway:** Entered the interstate/international waters of the Great Lakes over preceding decades, likely via the [[Welland Canal]], circumventing the natural barrier of [[Niagara Falls]].
- **Habitat use across life stages:**
  - **Spawning adults:** Migrate up tributary streams and rivers each spring to spawn, then die and decompose after spawning.
  - **Larvae ("ammocoetes"):** Burrow in stream-bed mud for several years, non-parasitic, presumed harmless during this phase.
  - **Metamorphosed juveniles:** Undergo metamorphosis enabling parasitic life, then migrate downstream into the Great Lakes.
  - **Parasitic adults:** Prey on fish in open Great Lakes waters for an unknown number of years before reaching sexual maturity and returning to streams to spawn, completing the cycle.
- **Host impact:** Primary documented impact on [[Lake Trout]] populations in upper Lake Huron and northern Lake Michigan; commercial catch declines correlated with rising incidence of lamprey-marked fish (up to one-third of catch by 1946).
- **Geographic distribution (as of 1947):** Spawning runs confirmed in 83 Michigan streams; present in every major stream system entering [[Lake Michigan]]; four of five major drainages entering Lake Huron north of Saginaw Bay; at least one stream entering [[Lake Superior]].
- **Inland lake infestation:** Established populations found in [[Burt Lake]], [[Mullett Lake]], [[Pentwater Lake]], and [[Lake Charlevoix]] — raising concern for inland sport fisheries beyond the Great Lakes proper.
- **Behavioral note:** Observations at an inland river-lake site indicated resident fish populations were not molested by lampreys during the spawning migration period, suggesting spawning-phase adults may not actively feed en route.

## Key Findings

- **Reproductive output:** Egg counts from a controlled weir study showed each female sea lamprey produces an average of over 60,000 eggs.
- **Spawning run timing (1947 study stream):** Run began April 19 and ended July 13.
- **Spawning substrate dependency:** Experiments demonstrated that female lampreys prevented from accessing suitable stream-bottom spawning substrate die without releasing eggs — identified as a potential control leverage point.
- **Control framework:** Three-step conceptual model for predator/parasite control: (1) characterize life cycle, (2) identify weakest link, (3) disrupt cycle at that point. Stream-phase (spawning and outmigration) stages were identified as the most tractable targets for intervention, since open-lake control was deemed impractical.
- **Trapping weir program:**
  - First installed in 1944 on the [[Ocqueoc River]] in cooperation with the East Presque Isle Sportsmen's Club; operated 1944–1946.
  - A more robust weir was installed in 1947 on a second northern Michigan stream, tended 24 hours/day by Fish Division biologists.
  - For 1948, trapping weirs were planned to run April–August, plus a new dam-and-inclined-screen trap near the Straits of Mackinac designed for year-round operation to intercept recently metamorphosed lampreys migrating downstream toward the Great Lakes.
- **Economic/commercial-use research:** Attempts to develop sea lamprey as a human food product (in collaboration with Michigan State College) were unsuccessful in producing an appealing product for the American palate; vitamin-value assays conducted via U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service arrangements showed little commercial promise.
- **Institutional coordination:** A formal interstate/international committee (Great Lakes states, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Province of Ontario) was established to coordinate research and avoid duplicated control efforts — an early precursor to what would become the [[Great Lakes Fishery Commission]].
- **Public engagement:** The Department relied on sportsmen and conservation officers for reporting spawning locations and unusual lamprey concentrations, underscoring citizen science's role in early distribution mapping.
- **Open uncertainty:** As of 1948, it remained unresolved whether any devised control method would be economically feasible, with concern that effective methods might carry prohibitive costs.
