# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Research Flow Package (1990)

#environmental-studies #dam-operations #flow-management #aquatic-ecology #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This document is a draft research plan developed by Dr. Duncan Patten and core scientists for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, presented to the Executive Review Committee in February 1990. The plan proposes a comprehensive series of controlled discharge experiments from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to assess impacts of dam operations on downstream resources in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]]. The research framework addresses four major operational and management issues: (1) effects of dam discharge operations (fluctuations, minimum flows, ramping rates), (2) effects of recreation (fishing, rafting, camping), (3) economic balances (power, recreation, non-use values), and (4) potential future mitigation alternatives. The plan includes 20 primary research questions with associated null hypotheses, requiring 12 distinct research discharge regimes ranging from 1,000 to 33,200 cfs over an 18-month period (1990-1991). Final approval rests with the Bureau of Reclamation Regional Director, contingent on Executive Review Committee recommendations, Environmental Impact Statement requirements, and economic impact assessment by Western Area Power Administration.

## Background & Description

The Glen Canyon Environmental Studies program was initiated to evaluate environmental impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on downstream resources. The dam, completed in 1963, fundamentally altered the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem by trapping sediment and nutrients in [[Lake Powell]], releasing cold hypolimnetic water, and creating daily discharge fluctuations for peak power generation. Normal operations produce fluctuating flows with daily peaks and minimums that vary seasonally, with maximum releases reaching 31,500-33,200 cfs during high-demand periods.

The research plan identifies discharge fluctuations, minimum discharge levels, and ramping rates (rate of change in discharge) as primary driving variables affecting downstream resources. Aerial photo interpretation suggests camping beaches have diminished over 20 years (except during 1983-86 high water), potentially linked to fluctuating discharges and steep ramping rates. The tailwater reach below the dam supports a "blue ribbon" trout fishery, primarily [[Rainbow Trout]], maintained through stocking and natural reproduction. Native fish species, especially the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), persist in mainstem habitats, backwaters, and tributaries, particularly near the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence.

The research design employs response curves relating resource variables (beach topography, fish populations, productivity) to perturbation variables (discharge characteristics, human activities). A dendrogram framework (Figure 1) organizes 12 research discharge regimes: 5 constant discharges (1,000, 3,000, 5,000, 8,000, 11,000, 15,000 cfs) and 7 fluctuating discharges testing combinations of low/medium/high fluctuation magnitudes, minimum discharges, and ramping rates. Each 11-12 day research discharge period is followed by 2-4 days at low constant flow (3,000 cfs) for impact assessment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been fundamentally transformed from a warm, sediment-laden river to a cold, clear tailwater system. [[Lake Powell]] removes >90% of incoming sediment and associated phosphorus, potentially limiting primary productivity. Deep hypolimnetic releases are colder and higher in salinity than pre-dam conditions, affecting temperature-dependent biological processes throughout the system.

The trout fishery in the [[Lee's Ferry]] reach (Glen Canyon Dam to river mile 14) represents a novel ecosystem supported by cold-water conditions. However, fluctuating flows create challenges for natural reproduction through redd desiccation, alevin mortality, and adult stranding during spawning periods. The trout population serves as primary forage for wintering [[Bald Eagle]] populations near [[Nankoweap Creek]], where trout access to spawning tributaries may be limited by low mainstem discharges.

Native fish populations, particularly [[Humpback Chub]], depend on tributaries and backwaters for reproduction and early life stages. The [[Little Colorado River]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], and [[Paria River]] provide critical warm-water spawning habitat. Larval fish must transition from warm tributaries to the cold mainstem, with survival potentially affected by discharge fluctuations, minimum flows, and food availability. Interactions between native species and introduced species (trout, other non-natives) may influence population dynamics.

Sediment dynamics represent a critical concern. Pre-dam alluvial terraces and beaches are eroding, with sediment transport through the system potentially exceeding inputs from tributaries. Daily fluctuations may increase organic drift through detachment and stranding/desiccation of algae, invertebrates, and fish. The fluctuation zone limits effective benthic and planktonic production areas. Beach stability is influenced by discharge characteristics, vegetation establishment (including exotic [[Tamarisk]] *Tamarix ramosissima* and *T. chinensis*), human trampling, wind, and eddy hydrodynamics.

Cultural resource sites along the river show evidence of erosion, potentially initiated by 1983-1986 spillover flooding, with progressive degradation threatening archaeological deposits. Recreational values depend on beach availability, safety conditions, angler access, and experiential quality, all influenced by discharge regimes.

## Key Findings

**Research Framework and Hypotheses:**

The plan proposes testing 20 primary null hypotheses organized into four categories:

**I. Effects of Dam Operations (10 hypotheses):**

- **H₀-1.1**: No significant relationship between discharge fluctuations, minimum discharges, ramping rates and beach degradation/aggradation. Requires intensive microtopographic monitoring of representative beaches under controlled discharge regimes, measuring relationships to fluctuation magnitude, minimums, ramping rates, seasonal differences, wind, surface moisture, topography, eddy dynamics, sediment composition, and armoring.

- **H₀-2.1**: No significant relationship between recreational beach use and discharge parameters affecting sediment degradation. Requires paired beach studies with controlled human use under varying discharge regimes.

- **H₀-2.2**: No significant relationship between vegetation as beach stabilizer and discharge fluctuation magnitude. Tests vegetation resistance to erosion based on composition, cover, density compared to unvegetated controls.

- **H₀-3.1**: No significant relationship between discharge parameters and sediment storage/transport through canyon system. Requires discharge routing model, channel bathymetry from 1,000 cfs aerial photography, suspended sediment and bedload sampling at six gaging stations, and tributary flood frequency analysis.

- **H₀-4.1**: No significant relationship between trout stranding rates and discharge fluctuations, minimums, or ramping rates. Requires stranding surveys during spawning season (Lee's Ferry to Glen Canyon Dam) with fish measurements, redd surveys, and limnological variables.

- **H₀-4.2**: No significant relationship between rainbow trout behavioral activity (movement, reproduction, feeding) and discharge parameters. Requires radio tracking, reproductive surveys, feeding studies, and controlled sluiceway experiments on food resources, growth, and survivorship.

- **H₀-5.1**: No significant relationship between trout availability/access, bald eagle presence/abundance/foraging success at [[Nankoweap Creek]] and discharge magnitude. Tests whether low mainstem flows prevent trout spawning migration and reduce eagle foraging success.

- **H₀-6.1**: No significant relationship between native (especially [[Humpback Chub]]) and introduced fish population dynamics and discharge parameters. Requires food and habitat availability studies in tributaries ([[Little Colorado River]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], [[Paria River]]) and backwaters, larval survival studies, adult sampling, and reproductive success monitoring.

- **H₀-7.1**: No significant relationship between nutrient availability, productivity (algae, macroinvertebrates), organic matter import-export rates in [[Lee's Ferry]] reach and discharge fluctuations/ramping rates. Requires seasonal water quality profiling in [[Lake Powell]], downstream nutrient/sediment/organic matter sampling, system-level production/respiration measurements, and site-specific nutrient uptake/regeneration studies.

- **H₀-8.1**: No significant relationship between recreational response variables (angler/rafting safety, satisfaction, experiential quality, economics) and discharge parameters.

- **H₀-9.1**: No significant relationship between camping beach availability and maximum normal operations discharges (31,500 or 33,200 cfs). Requires detailed topographic/bathymetric mapping from 1,000 cfs aerial photography with 0.5 m contours.

- **H₀-10.1**: No significant relationship between dam operations and cultural resource site stability. Requires aging of eroding sediments to determine if erosion affects young post-dam deposits or ancient pre-dam sediment beds.

**II. Effects of Recreation (2 hypotheses):**

- **H₀-11.1**: No significant relationship between beach stability and fishing activity (including motorized boat wakes) in [[Lee's Ferry]] reach. Also addresses impacts on newly discovered *Euphorbia* species in new high water zone.

- **H₀-11.2**: No significant relationship between trout population and fishing activities (catch-and-release vs. keep). Requires evaluation of Arizona Game and Fish records combined with stranding loss data.

- **H₀-12.1**: No significant relationship between beach sediment volume and recreation (overlaps with H₀-2.1). Uses paired beaches with different camping intensities and experimental manipulation.

**III. Effects on Economic Balances (3 hypotheses):**

- **H₀-13.1**: Changes in operations will not result in significant economic losses from power production. Power Economics Team developing methods consistent with Principles and Guidelines to estimate value changes from operational modifications.

- **H₀-14.1**: Economic benefits of downstream recreation and associated tourism services not affected by dam operations. Proposes updating 1982 Phase I contingent valuation surveys to reflect current conditions and changed trip characteristics.

- **H₀-15.1**: No significant non-use values associated with Grand Canyon resource existence/condition unless individuals intend direct recreational use. Requires surveys of western states residents, U.S. residents, or CRSP power contract utility customers.

**IV. Potential Future Mitigation Alternatives (5 questions):**

- **Question 16**: Economic and environmental costs of "No Change" alternative (to be addressed after research program completion).

- **H₀-17.1**: No significant relationship between potential intake levels from [[Lake Powell]] (via variable intake structure) and downstream water quality/biological productivity. Requires seasonal Lake Powell water quality profiles, nutrient dynamic and temperature models for river to [[Grand Canyon]], and refined nutrient-productivity relationships.

- **H₀-18.1**: No significant differences between downstream ecosystem response to reregulation dam discharges vs. Glen Canyon Dam discharges. Requires hypothetical modeling of dam parameters, impoundment characteristics, and discharge quality/quantity.

- **H₀-18.2**: No significant impact on [[Lee's Ferry]] reach from reregulation dam construction and inundation.

- **H₀-19.1**: No relationship between potential success of beach rehabilitation and dam operations (requires determining protection device types and construction/maintenance methods).

- **Question 20**: Feasibility of sediment augmentation to establish equilibrium and build beaches (requires determining augmentation volumes from H₀-3.1 and developing augmentation technology).

**Research Discharge Schedule (1990-1991):**

The plan proposes 12 distinct discharge regimes over 18 months:

**Constant Discharges:**
- 1,000 cfs (4 days, April 1990): Aerial photography for GIS base mapping
- 3,000 cfs (multiple 2-4 day periods): Impact analysis following each research discharge
- 5,000 cfs (10 days, October 1990)
- 8,000 cfs (10 days, October 1990)
- 11,000 cfs (10 days, December 1990)
- 15,000 cfs (10 days, May 1990)

**Fluctuating Discharges:**
- **Discharge A**: 3,000-13,000 cfs, low fluctuation, low minimum (October-November 1990)
- **Discharge B**: 5,000-15,000 cfs, low fluctuation, medium minimum (December 1990-January 1991)
- **Discharge C**: 10,000-20,000 cfs, low fluctuation, high minimum (February 1991)
- **Discharge D**: 3,000-26,200 cfs, high fluctuation, low minimum, low up-ramp, high down-ramp (May 1990)
- **Discharge E**: 3,000-26,200 cfs, high fluctuation, low minimum, high up-ramp, high down-ramp (July 1990)
- **Discharge F**: 10,000-33,200 cfs, high fluctuation, high minimum, high up-ramp, low down-ramp (July-August 1990)
- **Discharge G**: 10,000-33,200 cfs, high fluctuation, high minimum, high up-ramp, high down-ramp (August 1990)

Normal operations discharge periods interspersed for comparison. Schedule designed to fit monthly volume release requirements and resource response timing (e.g., trout spawning, humpback chub larval dispersal).

**Methodological Considerations:**

The plan acknowledges several limitations: (1) short time frame may produce results with wide variation and non-absolute interpretations, (2) system equilibration time required after discharge changes (especially low minima) due to bank storage effects, (3) complex interactions between discharge variables prevent complete isolation of single factors, (4) response curves will show multiple curves for interactive variables, (5) extensive manpower and complicated logistics required, (6) possible need to extend research over additional year(s).

Bank storage effects will be monitored with multiple piezometers (wells) in beaches, as conducted during October 1989 5,000 cfs study. The plan emphasizes comparing controlled research discharges with normal operations (highly variable) and constant discharge controls having equivalent daily volumes.

**Long-term Monitoring:**

The document notes this short-term research plan will eventually be supplemented by a long-term monitoring/research program addressing ongoing information needs and data interpretation. This longer-term program should become a component of the EIS mitigation program.

**Decision Timeline:**

Final decision on research flow program expected by mid-March 1990, based on: (1) Executive Review Committee recommendations, (2) ability to meet Environmental Impact Statement and GCES program goals, and (3) Western Area Power Administration economic impact assessment. Study plan development for implementation to be discussed at March 8-9, 1990 GCES Technical Team meetings.
