# Life History of the Brindled Madtom (*Noturus miurus*) in Mill Creek, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #freshwater-ecology #Noturus

## Executive Summary

This study presents a comprehensive life history analysis of the [[Brindled Madtom]] (*Noturus miurus*), based on 318 specimens collected over 14 months from Mill Creek, a small tributary of the Cache River in southern Illinois. The species was found to be syntopic but ecologically segregated from [[Noturus nocturnus]], with *N. miurus* favoring mud-bottomed pools and *N. nocturnus* favoring rocky/brushy riffles. Key findings include sexual maturity at 2 years for males, a breeding season spanning June–July, detailed nesting and clutch data (six nests documented), egg and larval developmental staging, growth curves for length and weight, survivorship analysis by sex and age class, dietary composition dominated by aquatic dipteran and ephemeropteran larvae, and parasite prevalence dominated by nematodes of the genus *Spinitectus*. This represents one of the more detailed life history accounts among the 25 described *Noturus* species, most of which remain poorly studied.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy**: *Noturus miurus* (Pisces: Ictaluridae), one of 25 described madtom species, most of which are nocturnal and cryptic, with life history data available for only 12.
- **Distribution**: Wide-ranging across the Great Lakes basin, lower Mississippi River basin, entire Ohio River basin, and the Pearl River/Lake Pontchartrain drainages of the central Gulf Coast.
- **Regional occurrence**: In Illinois, most common in the eastern Wabash drainage; also occurs in the Cache River system (Ohio drainage) in southern Illinois, where this study was conducted.
- **Prior work**: External morphology, taxonomy, and preliminary nesting/developmental observations from Huron River, Michigan were reported by Taylor (1969). Egg counts from Cayuga Lake, NY were reported by Menzel and Raney (1973). This study builds on the authors' companion work on [[Noturus exilis]] (Mayden and Burr, 1981), the only other thoroughly studied *Noturus* species at the time.
- **Study site**: Mill Creek, 2.4 km NW of Ullin, Pulaski Co., Illinois — a channelized, cultivated-field-bordered stream with mixed mud/silt/rock substrate, slow current except during flooding, and heavy anthropogenic debris (cans, bottles, auto parts) that served as important nesting substrate. Water temperatures ranged from 1°C (January) to 30°C (July). No rooted aquatic vegetation present.
- **Co-occurring fish fauna**: 23 of 83 Cache River system fish species occur at the study site, most commonly *Notropis lutrensis*, *Semotilus atromaculatus*, *Pimephales notatus*, *N. nocturnus*, and *Lepomis megalotis*; *Noturus gyrinus* occasional.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning**: *N. miurus* consistently occupied mud-bottomed pools, often among rocks, sticks, leaves, tree roots, and tin cans, and sheltered under overhanging banks during high water. *N. nocturnus* generally occupied rocky or brushy riffles, though large adults sometimes used pools with *N. miurus*. During the breeding season (June–July), both species converged in pools.
- **Range-wide habitat**: Occupies both streams and lakes; generally avoids strong current and cool water, favoring soft bottoms and debris accumulations (per Taylor, 1969).
- **Anthropogenic influence**: The abundance of discarded cans and bottles at the study site likely enhanced habitat suitability by providing artificial nesting cavities; nest cans with narrow "pop-top" openings were strongly preferred over wide-mouthed containers, likely for ease of nest defense.
- **Predation**: No direct evidence of predation on *N. miurus* was found in stomach contents of *Lepomis cyanellus*, *Ictalurus natalis*, or *Aphredoderus sayanus* collected from Mill Creek. Historical data (Lagler and Hubbs, 1940) documented 17 *N. miurus* in the stomachs of 136 *Lepisosteus osseus* (longnose gar) from Grand River, Michigan.
- **Parasitism as ecological indicator**: High nematode (*Spinitectus* sp.) infestation (59% of 150 examined) suggests a well-established host-parasite relationship, with peak infestation in November (48%) and minimum in June (0.15%), possibly reflecting seasonal energetic trade-offs during the breeding season.
- **Mortality events**: Bailey (1955) documented mass mortality of *N. miurus* in Bass Lake, Michigan, during mid-July due to extreme high temperatures (38°C) combined with restricted egress — an example of thermal stress vulnerability in this species.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction
- **Male maturity**: Attained at 2 years (3rd summer of life), marked by elongate testes with fingerlike projections, enlarged cephalic epaxial muscles, swollen lips, and swollen genital papillae during breeding season.
- **Gonadosomatic Index (GSI)**: Peaked at 8.9 in June–July for males aged 1+; testis weight–length relationship was curvilinear: `1000T = -15.9 - 0.2L + 0.007L²` (r = 0.685). Testis development was minimal below 50 mm SL or 22 months of age.
- **Female maturity**: Some females matured by 1 year; ripe with mature oocytes May–July. GSI ratios ranged 64–248 (x̄ = 145) in 12 females. Largest ovaries recorded were 24.8% of adjusted body weight in a 2-year-old, 59-mm female.
- **Fecundity**: Mature oocyte counts ranged 42–90 (x̄ = 66.2) in 12 females (51–70 mm SL); no significant correlation between oocyte number and length, age, or weight (r < 0.54 for all). Comparable Cayuga Lake, NY females had 31–143 (x̄ = 88.9) mature ova, suggesting geographic variation in fecundity.
- **Nesting**: Breeding/nesting activity documented 19–28 June 1979 at water temperatures of 24–27°C. Six nests found — four in 355-ml beer/soda cans (narrow "pop-top" openings preferred), two under large flat rocks in shallow water. Nest depths ranged 12–20 cm. Guardian males (all single-male, single-clutch nests) ranged 63–70 mm SL, aged 2–3 years, with mostly empty stomachs (suggesting reduced feeding while guarding).
- **Clutch size**: 56–81 eggs per nest (x̄ = 66.2), closely matching mean mature oocyte counts, indicating most females spawned a single clutch in one nest (contrary to Menzel and Raney's 1973 hypothesis of multiple matings per female in some *Noturus* populations).
- **Spawning timing**: Estimated to occur late at night or early morning based on backdated embryo staging.
- **Comparative nesting data**: Taylor (1969) found Michigan nests (10 guardian males, 51–71 mm SL) with 28–46 eggs/young in late July–early August at 24.5°C. Trautman (1948) reported Ohio spawning in May–July in riffles/pools under rocks, logs, boards.

### Egg & Larval Development
- **Eggs**: Spherical, yellow/amber, 3.4–3.8 mm diameter (x̄ = 3.5, N=11); yolk diameter 3.0–3.4 mm (x̄ = 3.2). Temporarily adhesive to each other but not to substrate.
- **Incubation**: Four clutches (280 eggs total) incubated at 25°C hatched in 189–215 hr (7.9–9.0 days); hatching success 86%.
- **Larval staging**:
  - Hatchlings: 6.9–7.4 mm TL (x̄ = 7.2); scattered head melanophores, pigmented eyes, only caudal fin rays differentiated, three pairs of rudimentary barbels.
  - 3-day-old larvae: 10.0–11.0 mm TL (x̄ = 10.7); rays developed in all fins except adipose; four pairs of barbels.
  - 12-day-old larvae: ~14.0 mm TL; dense melanophore concentration, yolk sac nearly absorbed.
  - 24-day-old larvae: 15.4–17.5 mm TL (x̄ = 16.4); adult-like pigmentation pattern and body shape.
  - Smallest field-collected juvenile: 12.1 mm SL (July), consistent with yolk sac absorption size.

### Growth
- **Length**: Decelerating growth rate; half of first year's mean length growth achieved in ~8 weeks. Regression: males `Y = 29.27 + 24.89 log X` (r = 0.440); females `Y = 16.60 + 33.92 log X` (r = 0.849). Males ultimately grew larger due to longer lifespan, not faster growth rate.
  - At 13–18 months: males 61.9 mm, females 59.4 mm.
  - At 20–24 months: males 64.1 mm, females 60.4 mm.
  - Largest specimen: 79.0 mm male (20 Oct 1979); largest female: 68.9 mm (21 Dec 1978). Michigan record (Taylor 1969): 88.0 mm.
- **Weight**: Constant growth rate; half of first year's mean weight reached in ~10 months. Regression (sexes combined): `W = 0.024 + 0.218A` (r = 0.880); no significant sex difference.
- **Weight–length relationship**: `Log W = -4.88 + 3.07 Log L` (r = 0.983, N=106).

### Demography
- **Age structure** (N=318): 74.5% ≤1 year, 22.6% 1–2 years, ~3% 2–3 years.
- **Sex ratio**: No significant deviation from 1:1 in age-0 or age-1+ classes.
- **Survival**: 41.1% of first-year males and 21.5% of first-year females survived to year 2 (relative survival proportions). Lowest survival occurred in year 2 for all groups. Males lived longer (oldest = 36 months) than females (oldest = 25 months). Comparatively, *N. exilis*, *N. flavus*, *N. funebris*, *N. insignis*, and *N. gyrinus* are all reported to live beyond 3 years.

### Diet
- Based on 107 stomachs examined: dominant prey were aquatic **dipteran larvae/pupae** (especially [[Chironomidae]] and [[Simuliidae]]), **ephemeropteran naiads** (*Potamanthus*, *Stenonema*), **trichopteran larvae** (primarily *Cheumatopsyche*), and adult isopods (*Lirceus*). Copepods and other microcrustaceans were secondary.
- **Diet diversity** increased with body size; smallest madtoms relied more on copepods, while coleopteran larvae were consumed only by larger adults.
- Dietary prey taxa richness ranged from a low of 4 taxa (January, June) to 6–10 taxa in other months.
- Males largely ceased feeding while guarding nests (empty stomachs in guardian males).

### Parasitism
- **Nematodes** (*Spinitectus* sp.): 59% prevalence (89/150 examined), found encysted in gastrointestinal mesenteries and stomach exterior year-round; peak in November (48%), minimum in June (0.15%). Females slightly more parasitized than males.
- **Trematodes**: *Crepidostomum* sp. found around stomach/intestine in April–May.
- **Acanthocephalans**: *Leptorhyncoides* sp. found around stomach/intestine in April–May.
- **Ectoparasitic copepods**: *Lernaea* sp. found on 15 individuals, mostly autumn/winter, typically at fin bases, infecting both sexes.
- Historical records: Hoffman (1967) listed four trematode species for *N. miurus*; Bangham and Hunter (1939) found two trematodes and two cestodes in Lake Erie specimens; Van Cleave and Mueller (1934) reported trematodes from Oneida Lake, NY specimens; Schwartz (1956) documented fatal *Clinostomum marginatum* infestation in three Pennsylvania specimens.
