# Food of and Habitat Use by Juveniles of Micropterus and Morone in a Southwestern Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Moronidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines juvenile [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Striped Bass]] and [[White Bass]] in [[Lake Texoma]], a 36,000-hectare impoundment on the Red and Washita rivers. Striped bass are introduced, while the other three species are native to the basin. Sampling spanned 1981 to 1986 using seining and electrofishing, with stomach analysis of fish under 150 mm total length (TL). Juvenile striped bass overlapped strongly in both diet and habitat with juvenile white bass, but overlapped little in habitat with largemouth bass. The authors conclude that habitat separation appears to ameliorate potential competition between striped bass and largemouth bass, whereas greater potential for negative interactions exists between the two Morone species. The authors caution that high niche overlap does not by itself demonstrate competition.

## Background & Description

Introductions of exotic fishes can strongly affect native species, and comparative resource-use studies help predict the outcomes of introductions. Relatively few studies have addressed juveniles of piscivorous species, even though juveniles often share zooplankton and invertebrate prey and similar microhabitats before adult niches diverge.

Striped bass had been introduced into more than 100 southern U.S. reservoirs, most of which already held largemouth or spotted bass, and many held native white bass. In Lake Texoma, striped bass were stocked from 1969 to 1974 and became a self-sustaining population. Juveniles were the focus here, using a threshold of less than 150 mm TL.

The study area is a gently sloping littoral zone with substrates ranging from hard sand and gravel on wind-exposed shores to soft mud and detritus in protected coves. Rooted macrophytes are scarce. Sampling used four fixed stations near the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Station]] plus supplementary sites added in 1985 and 1986.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat analysis.** Fixed-site seining was conducted at roughly weekly intervals (varying by year), and supplementary sites were sampled with multiple small-mesh seine hauls. Habitat breadth and overlap were computed with the [[Levins Index]] and [[Pianka Overlap Index]]. Overlap values above 0.75 were treated as high, and values below 0.40 as substantially different use.

**Co-occurrence.** Because many collections contained neither species, the authors tested co-occurrence with a one-tailed binomial probability that considered only collections in which at least one species was present.

**Diet analysis.** Stomachs were examined and prey counted. In 1981–1985 prey biomass was estimated from assigned weights, and a second set of alternative weights was used to test sensitivity. In 1986 prey volume was estimated instead.

**Feeding experiment.** In August 1986, greenhouse tanks (1,200 L) with filtered Lake Texoma water tested whether juvenile largemouth bass and striped bass shifted diets when kept together. Prey included corixids, notonectids and copepods, which were supplied at high densities. Water temperature ranged from 26 to 31.5 °C, and dissolved oxygen from 6.4 to 7.5 mg/L.

**Reservoir context.** Water-level fluctuations and seasonal inflow variation in southern reservoirs can produce food shortages, which the authors link to the "crunch" paradigm of [[Wiens 1977]]. Winterkill of [[Threadfin Shad]] and strong year-classes were discussed as factors in white bass decline.

## Key Findings

**Sample sizes (all years, <150 mm TL):**
- Striped bass: N = 793, mean TL 70.1 mm
- White bass: N = 618, mean TL 77.1 mm
- Largemouth bass: N = 255, mean TL 75.6 mm
- Spotted bass: N = 48, mean TL 71.0 mm

**Habitat use:**
- In 1981–1983 at fixed sites, striped bass and white bass overlapped at 0.87. Striped bass and largemouth bass overlapped at 0.83, but this value was considered tenuous because only 63 largemouth bass were collected.
- In 1985 and 1986, the striped bass–white bass overlap was 0.62 and 0.81. Striped bass–largemouth bass overlap was 0.27 and 0.07. No other pair exceeded 0.27 in those years.
- Striped bass occurred in only 43% of collections with largemouth bass (1981–1983), fewer than expected by chance (binomial P = 0.004). Striped bass were present in 58% of collections with white bass in that period (P = 0.049 for less co-occurrence than chance).
- Across five years, striped bass occurred in 68% of white bass collections versus 41% of largemouth bass collections (ANOVA F = 11.10, P < 0.05).
- In 88 seine hauls on 20 July 1988 (91 total), striped bass co-occurred less than expected with both largemouth bass (P = 0.025) and white bass (P = 0.015).

**Diet:**
- Striped bass ate mainly copepods and chironomids by number, but inland silversides (*Menidia beryllina*) dominated by weight.
- White bass diets resembled striped bass diets, but included more corixids and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*).
- Largemouth bass ate no copepods in the field. Their main prey were corixids, baetid mayflies and inland silversides.
- Diet overlap between striped bass and white bass was 0.97 by number and 0.93 by weight (1981–1985). Striped bass and largemouth bass overlapped 0.05 by number and 0.85 by weight, and 0.47 by volume in 1986.
- Overlap values were robust to the alternative prey-weight set.

**Experimental results:**
- Striped bass in tanks ate about 200 copepods per fish, compared with about 85 for largemouth bass feeding alone (both results reported as tank means).
- Presence of the other species did not significantly change diets. For largemouth bass, copepods fell from 84.8 to 57.8 (P = 0.19), and corixids rose non-significantly (P = 0.22).
- The experiment had low power (N = 3 tanks per treatment), so only large differences were detectable. The authors treated P < 0.10 as marginally significant.

**Conclusions and management implications:**
- The native juvenile analogue of striped bass is white bass more than largemouth bass.
- Habitat differences appear to reduce overlap between striped bass and largemouth bass.
- Declines in white bass since striped bass establishment, versus stable largemouth bass numbers, are consistent with juvenile bottlenecks but cannot yet be attributed to competition. A 55% decline in white bass catch was reported between 1972–1975 and 1980–1983.
- The authors recommend that managers consider competition risk when introducing species into reservoirs with limited resources.
