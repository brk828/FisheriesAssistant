# The Biology and Economic Importance of the Alaska Pollock (Theragra chalcogramma)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary
This 1983 student paper (Rod Campbell, Biology 328) reviews the biology, fishery history and economic importance of the Alaska pollock, *Theragra chalcogramma*. It is the most abundant groundfish in the eastern [[Bering Sea]] and Gulf of Alaska. The paper concludes that pollock is the most important single finfish species by weight in the world. It also argues that its abundance in Alaskan waters gives the domestic bottomfishing industry real long-term potential.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomy:** The Alaska, blue eye or walleye pollock is a member of the cod family, [[Gadidae]], and the only representative of the genus *Theragra*.
- **Diagnostic features:**
  - The anus lies halfway between the first and second dorsal fins.
  - The lower jaw may protrude slightly beyond the upper jaw.
  - Barbels are tiny or absent.
  - The eyes are relatively larger than those of the [[Pacific Tomcod]] (*Microgadus proximus*) and the [[Pacific Cod]] (*Gadus macrocephalus*).
  - It has 3 dorsal fins and 2 anal fins.
- **Range:** From central California north to the Gulf of Alaska, across the Bering Sea to the U.S.S.R., and down the western Pacific coast to the Sea of Japan (Hart, 1973).
- **Flesh and market qualities:** Pollock are small with relatively soft flesh, so they are less desirable for human consumption than many species. The flesh is white with little dark meat and a coarse-grained texture. It is low in fat and high in protein, and is favored by fast food franchises and restaurants. The paper calls the species highly perishable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Depth distribution:**
  - First-year larvae and pelagic eggs are highly dispersed in the upper 30 m of the central and outer continental shelf.
  - By the second year, fish move from the shallow shelf into water up to 400 m deep.
  - The major eastern Pacific concentration is on the outer shelf of the Bering Sea at 100–250 m.
  - Clark (1979) attributes this to the width of the shelf and to ice cover from mid-December to March. The ice supports under-ice algae and high ice-edge production.
- **Daily movement:** Fish school on or near the bottom by day and may disperse up into the water column at night to feed.
- **Seasonal movement:**
  - Pollock generally move onto the shelf in summer and off it in winter.
  - Timing, distance and duration of inshore movement are unpredictable.
  - Smith (in press) links this variability to ocean bottom temperature. He identifies 3°C as a critical value, and a cold water mass below 3°C may prevent inshore movement.
  - Summer dispersal may relate to seasonal variation in main foods: euphausiids, amphipods and copepods.
- **Spawning:**
  - Spawning is in high concentrations from January through March at water temperatures of 2.5–3.5°C.
  - Adults spawn near the bottom in deep water off the shelf.
  - The eggs are pelagic and buoyant and float to the surface.
  - Eggs drift with the current for 14–15 days. At about 17 days the embryo uses up its yolk and sinks.
  - Larvae hatch in the pycnocline, roughly 30–40 m deep.
  - Incubation normally takes 20 days at 3°C and lengthens at lower temperatures.
  - Newly emerged larvae are 3–4 mm long and live on the yolk for 1–2 days while the mouth and anus develop.
- **Diet:**
  - Newly hatched larvae feed on copepods and copepod larvae.
  - Juveniles (2–20 cm) eat a wider range of foods: shrimp, euphausiids, sand lance (*Ammodytes hexapterus*), herring (*Clupea harengus pallasi*) and others.
  - Stomach content tables for the northern Gulf of Alaska and southeast Bering Sea show crustaceans, especially euphausiids, dominating numerical frequency. In the Gulf of Alaska they also dominate by volume. In the Bering Sea, volume was dominated by a small percentage of fish in the diet.
  - The Bering Sea sample had 163 feeding individuals, with standard lengths of 85–526 mm.
- **Predators:**
  - Larvae are eaten by seabirds, including parakeet auklet, tufted and horned puffins, black-legged and red-legged kittiwakes, common murre and red-faced cormorant.
  - Juveniles and adults are major prey for the [[Pacific Cod]], [[Pacific Halibut]] (*Hippoglossus stenolepis*), [[Greenland Turbot]] (*Reinhardtius hippoglossoides*), [[Arrowtooth Flounder]] (*Atheresthes stomias*) and [[Sablefish]] (*Anoplopoma fimbria*).
  - Adults are also eaten by toothed whales, [[Northern Sea Lion]] (*Eumetopias jubatus*) and seals (*Phoca* spp.).
  - A food web for the eastern Bering Sea is presented.

## Key Findings
- **Growth:** Pollock grow rapidly on a euphausiid diet. By age four they reach about 1 pound and 40 cm (Ryan, 1979). This yields a relatively small mature fish, with maximum biomass added at age three (Alverson, 1982).
- **Rising demand:** Overfishing of the [[Yellowfin Sole]] (*Limanda aspera*) and [[Pacific Ocean Perch]] (*Sebastes alutus*) has drastically increased demand for pollock.
- **Fishery history:**
  - 1882: U.S. commercial bottomfishing began in the eastern Bering Sea, targeting Pacific cod.
  - 1928: The U.S. and Canada began fishing Pacific halibut.
  - World War II reduced or eliminated commercial fishing in the North Pacific until 1954.
  - 1954: Large foreign fleets, primarily Russian and Japanese, moved into the eastern Bering Sea, initially fishing flounder off [[Bristol Bay]] and the [[Aleutian Islands]].
  - 1966–1967: South Korea joined, and pollock first became a main target.
  - By 1969: The fishery had expanded northward along the shelf into a year-round operation.
  - 1971: Effort shifted primarily to pollock, and the eastern Bering Sea became one of the world's largest fisheries, with landings exceeding 2 million metric tons per year (Bakkala et al., 1979).
- **Economic importance:**
  - Pollock is the most abundant semi-demersal fish by weight in the eastern Bering Sea.
  - It is the largest single biomass of any exploited finfish in the world, about 50% of world fish landings (approximately 30 of 60 million metric tons; Alverson and Crutchfield, 1982).
  - Worldwide landed value exceeds one billion dollars annually.
  - The world groundfish market, including pollock, accounts for nearly 50% of landings entering the international fish market.
- **Conclusion:** Pollock is abundant, versatile and perishable, and occupies the entire North Pacific rim. The groundfish resource is described as currently underutilized by Alaska, and the author is optimistic about the domestic bottomfishing industry. The paper is motivated by Alaska's narrow economic base and declining North Slope oil revenues.
