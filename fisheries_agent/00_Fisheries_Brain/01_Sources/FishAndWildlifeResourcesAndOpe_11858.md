# Fish and Wildlife Resources and Operations of Glen Canyon Dam - Planning Aid Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #dam-impacts #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Planning Aid Report, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) under the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]] (FWCA), documents study needs for fish and wildlife resources in the [[Colorado River]] affected by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The project area extends from Glen Canyon Dam to Separation Rapids (river mile 239), encompassing 15 miles of Glen Canyon and 277 miles of Marble and Grand Canyons. Since dam closure in 1963, the Colorado River has been drastically altered: seasonal water fluctuations replaced by daily fluctuations for hydropower production, warm turbid waters replaced by cold clear waters, and native warmwater fish communities displaced by cold-water trout fisheries. The report identifies impacts on endangered species including [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), as well as threatened [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) and [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus anatum*). The report was developed in coordination with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), [[National Park Service]] (NPS), and [[Bureau of Reclamation]], supporting the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) process initiated in 1989.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] was closed in 1963, fundamentally transforming the Colorado River ecosystem. The dam is administered by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] pursuant to the [[Colorado River Compact]], with hydropower marketed by [[Western Area Power Administration]] to the [[Colorado River Energy Distributors Association]] (CREDA). The project area includes [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] and [[Grand Canyon National Park]], with major portions of adjacent lands under Navajo Indian administration.

Pre-dam conditions featured seasonal floods, warm turbid waters (suspended sediment commonly 10,000 parts per million at Lee's Ferry 1928-1959), and native warmwater fish assemblages. Post-dam conditions include: daily flow fluctuations (primarily for hydropower production), cold clear water releases (approximately 10°C), drastically reduced sediment loads (approximately 200 ppm), and establishment of cold-water trout fishery dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*). Current operational impacts include floods (releases >31,000 cubic feet per second), daily fluctuations, fluctuation rates, and low flows.

The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) began December 1982, with Phase I ending 1987. During Phase I, the Colorado River experienced record high flows: 32,566 cfs approximately 67% of the time between 1983-1986. Between 1983-1986, approximately 15.4 million tons of sand were lost from Grand Canyon. The GCES Phase II was underway at report preparation, planned to include in-depth analysis of fluctuating flow impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Productivity**: The filamentous green alga *[[Cladophora glomerata]]* provides the food chain basis for many organisms. Cold tailwater releases create ideal habitat for this alga. Decreased water levels cause extensive detachment and desiccation. The amphipod *[[Gammarus lacustris]]*, introduced by AGFD in 1967-1968, provides major food base for rainbow trout. During fluctuating flows, Gammarus drift increased from 15.4% (steady flows) to 49.2%, with juveniles more susceptible than adults. Aquatic invertebrate samples (1985, Lee's Ferry to river mile 220) included chironomid midges (Chironomidae), freshwater earthworms (Oligochaeta), buffalo gnats (Simuliidae), and snails (Gastropoda, common only at Lee's Ferry and Nankoweap Creek). Noticeable decline in all taxa occurred below the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR).

**Lee's Ferry Fishery**: The trout fishery at [[Lee's Ferry]] constitutes a nationally significant sport fishery. Creel surveys (January-May 1986) collected data on 6,266 trout: 96.4% rainbow trout, 3.5% brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), 0.1% striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarki*), and brown trout (*Salmo trutta*). During GCES Phase I, approximately 80% of trout harvested were of wild (naturally reproduced) origin. Constant temperatures (~10°C) provide good growth and reproduction. Food resources abundant but not diverse: gut analyses revealed Cladophora, chironomid midges, amphipods, and detritus. Experimentation with pre-emergent alevins showed fish from steady flow redds 20 times more likely to survive than fish from dewatered redds. In October 1984, experimental flow reduction to 5,000 cfs for three days resulted in over 800 stranded trout observed (639 measured, 635 rainbow trout, mostly large reproductive adults). Anglers report stranded trout weekly during low flows.

**Downstream Trout Populations**: Rainbow trout occur throughout study area to LCR confluence. Growth rates decline downstream: Lee's Ferry fish averaged 406 mm total length at age 5; fish 48 km downstream averaged 310 mm. Difference attributed to increased turbidity below LCR limiting light penetration and food production. Informal observations indicate high quality trout fishery exists between Lee's Ferry and LCR, with trout presence downstream from LCR.

**Tributaries**: AGFD evaluated eleven major tributaries during GCES Phase I. Spring collections: 89% native fishes; summer: 96% native fishes. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*) caught in Paria River, Kanab Creek, and Shinumo Creek. Humpback chub primarily restricted to LCR, but specimens caught in Shinumo and Kanab Creeks. Summer temperatures ranged 13.0-28.0°C; winter 4.0-11.0°C (mainstream 7.0-9.0°C). Winter: 68% of fish caught in tributaries were rainbow trout. Rainbow trout found in all eleven tributaries, spawned in eight, usually December. LCR contributes significant suspended sediments and phosphates during floods. Tributary mouths highly influenced by mainstream flow, creating physical, biological, and chemical mixing zones.

**Sediment and Erosion**: Between 1983-1986, approximately 15.4 million tons of sand lost from Grand Canyon. Pre-dam (1928-1959): suspended sediment commonly 10,000 ppm at Lee's Ferry; post-dam: approximately 200 ppm. Selective erosion removes fine sediments, leaving coarser particles exposed. Recirculation zones (shallow river edges where water temporarily moves upstream) highly susceptible to erosion; many beaches border these zones. Minimal sediment enters system through dam. Some aggradation (beach building) occurs at slower rate, primarily from sediment redistribution or tributary introduction. Floods expected every 20 years under current operations.

**Riparian Vegetation**: Saltcedar (*Tamarix ramossissima*), sandbar willow (*Salix interior*), and seep willow (*Baccharis salicifolia*) thrive in areas where they previously existed in small numbers. Success due to elimination of destructive pre-dam floods, abundant propagules, and dependence on moist sand. Saltcedar well-established throughout canyon. 1983 flood eliminated approximately 50% of riparian vegetation. Experimental studies on re-establishment: less than half of sites (21 of 49) resulted in significant seedling colonization. Plants subject to fluctuating flows had higher survival than those subject to complete inundation. Flood duration had more severe impact than amplitude. No seedlings survived more than five days of desiccation. Complete plant recovery may require over 10 years.

**Terrestrial Invertebrates**: Ground-dwelling and plant-feeding insects important food sources for birds, lizards, and other wildlife. Specific impacts of flood, low, and fluctuating flows on insect populations unknown.

**Reptiles and Amphibians**: Species include side-blotched lizard (*Uta stansburiana*), red spotted toad (*Bufo punctatus*), western rattlesnake (*Crotalus viridis*). Amphibian and reptile reproduction may depend on backwaters and river edges. Lizard reproductive and egg-laying activity occurs late spring through summer along cobble bars where fine sand available for nest sites.

**Riparian Songbirds**: Over 30 bird species inhabit Lower Colorado River, 11 obligate riparian species. Regulated flows increased riparian habitats, benefiting some species; however, many birds within flood range lost nests and young. Most obligate riparian birds nest in New High Water Zone vegetation, where most flood damage occurs. Many obligate riparian birds resilient, will re-nest once or twice if nests destroyed by fluctuating flows and occasional flood releases. Between 1983-1986, approximately 35% of marshes eliminated due to flooding.

**Waterfowl and Wetlands**: Dam closure (1963) created wetland habitats (backwaters) important to [[Canada Geese]] (*Branta canadensis*), buffleheads (*Bucephala albeola*), mallards (*Anas platyrhynchos*), American wigeon (*Anas americana*), and other migratory waterfowl. Phalaropes (*Phalaropus* sp.), sandpipers, and wading birds utilize river. FWS recognizes limited backwater and wetland habitats as critical components of Greater Grand Canyon ecosystem, especially important in dry western areas. Colorado River constitutes migratory corridor for passerine birds and birds of prey.

**Mammals**: Species include mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*), bighorn sheep (*Ovis canadensis*), beaver (*Castor canadensis*), and many bat species (Chiroptera). Impacts from dam operations on these species not known at time of report.

## Key Findings

**Endangered Species Status and Concerns**:

1. **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Grand Canyon population represents one of two high concentrations of this endangered species. Effect of flows on humpback chub and habitat not well known, major concern. Jeopardy biological opinion issued 1978 exists for Glen Canyon Dam operations. FWS cooperatively working with AGFD, NPS, and Reclamation to develop conservation measures of sufficient biological effect to warrant removal of jeopardy opinion. Issuance of next biological opinion expected to coincide with EIS completion.

2. **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Endangered species extirpated from study area with dam closure. Suitability of re-introduction into Colorado River tributaries and other historic sites being evaluated by Lower Basin fishery management group. Interagency support of these efforts needs to be sustained.

3. **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Arizona considers endangered species (AGFD 1988). FWS petitioned March 1989 to list as endangered under ESA. Proposed Rule to list as endangered published May 22, 1990. Status in study area not determined by Glen Canyon operation Section 7 Consultation Team. Specimens observed 1973 at Paria River confluence and above Bass Camp (river mile 107). Single specimen (initially thought flannelmouth sucker hybrid) collected May 1989 at Little Colorado River, re-identified as razorback sucker. Three razorbacks taken in LCR during humpback chub monitoring program. Management actions and research needs being evaluated by Lower Basin fishery management group, Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team, and Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in Upper Basin.

4. **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Endangered species. Lake Powell backwaters provide rearing habitat.

5. **[[Peregrine Falcon]]** (*Falco peregrinus anatum*): Endangered species nests in small numbers on cliffs along river. Surveys 1982-1987 indicate Grand Canyon may contain largest nesting population of peregrine falcons in lower 48 states. Limited information available on food resources. Surveys indicate peregrines feed on white-throated swifts, swallows, and bats in summer. Degree of waterfowl predation not known but should be determined, since waterfowl available when other prey species may be absent. Interagency coordination initiated to determine data collection needs for potential downlisting in Southwest.

6. **[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): Endangered species not evaluated in GCES Phase I. Bird surveys conducted periodically since 1976. January 1985: no bald eagles seen during 18-day boat survey. December 1985: four bald eagles seen at mouth of Nankoweap Creek. No bald eagles known to nest in canyon. Numbers of wintering bald eagles increased, apparently in response to trout availability in Nankoweap Creek during spawning season. Bald eagles also observed in Marble Canyon. Speculation that permanent (non-migrating) populations may become established in Grand Canyon, perhaps in response to continued expansion of Southwest populations. Wintering bald eagles also known in Glen Canyon National Recreation Area.

7. **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]** (*Empidonax traillii extimus*): Arizona lists as endangered (AGFD 1988). FWS considers Category 2 candidate subspecies for Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Mexico. Population along Colorado River in Grand Canyon constitutes no more than few dozen pairs, but represents highest numbers of willow flycatchers in Arizona. Decline directly related to loss of riparian habitat in Southwest. Nests close to water and ground, making especially vulnerable to water fluctuations resulting in nest flooding or loss. Numbers increased from 1982-1986 with dam closure and creation of increased habitat due to New High Water Zone. Numbers declined 1987, possibly due to water levels occurring much lower than suitable habitat. Currently no riparian bird species known lost from Glen Canyon dam operations; however, loss of habitat due to streambank erosion could have negative impact.

8. **[[Yuma Clapper Rail]]** (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*): Endangered species. Lake Mead has cattail/sedge habitat in shallow upper backwaters which can be used by this species.

**Specific Management Recommendations and Concerns**:

1. **Algal and Zooplankton**: Care needed to ensure mortality does not exceed reproductive and recolonization capacity. Algal and invertebrate production should be considered when assessing relative impacts of each operational and non-operational alternative in EIS.

2. **Aquatic Invertebrates**: Various life history stages and species composition susceptible to different flow releases. Change in invertebrate distribution may affect food base for other species. Pending GCES Phase II results, additional life history information to develop guidelines may be needed. Aquatic invertebrate production and maintenance should be considered when assessing each alternative.

3. **Lee's Ferry Fishery**: Trout reproduction occurs within hydrologic conditions, but recruitment and life expectancy may be greatly reduced by low and fluctuating flows that dewater redds and strand adult and juvenile fish. Maintenance of nationally significant sport fishery should be considered when assessing each alternative.

4. **Downstream Trout**: Area does not receive as high recreational fishery use as segment above Lee's Ferry. Additional information needed to evaluate these resources. Game and non-game fishes should be considered when assessing relative impacts of each alternative.
