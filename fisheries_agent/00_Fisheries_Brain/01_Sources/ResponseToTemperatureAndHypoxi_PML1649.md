# Respiratory Response of Corbicula fluminea to Temperature and Hypoxia

#malacology #marine-biology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by [[Ronald F. McMahon]] of the Department of Biology, University of Texas at Arlington, examines how the introduced [[Corbicula Fluminea]] (Asiatic freshwater clam) regulates [[Oxygen Consumption]] under changing temperature and declining oxygen tension. Clams were collected from [[Lake Arlington]] and acclimated to 10, 20, or 30 °C. Oxygen uptake was measured in closed respiration chambers while temperature was raised to an acute upper lethal limit of 50 °C, and while ambient oxygen was reduced from full air saturation.

The study finds a typical temperature-acclimation pattern, with a sharp drop in oxygen uptake above 25 °C. It also finds that *C. fluminea* is a poor regulator of oxygen consumption under [[Hypoxia]]. The author argues that this physiological limitation, together with its recent origin in fresh water, may restrict the species' distribution in North America and its ability to displace native bivalves.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula fluminea* (Superfamily Corbiculacea; Family Corbiculidae) was introduced into North America in the [[Columbia River Drainage]] of Washington State in 1938. It has since spread across the southern United States and is an important member of many freshwater benthic communities. Concern has been raised that it may displace native species. It is also a significant pest, fouling industrial and power-plant facilities that use raw water for cooling.

Earlier work on the species covered several physiological traits:

- Population bioenergetics ([[Aldridge and McMahon]], 1978)
- Temperature tolerance limits ([[Mattice and Dye]], 1976)
- Desiccation resistance ([[McMahon]], 1978)
- Salinity tolerance ([[Gainey]], 1978a,b)

The present study extends this work to respiratory biology, including temperature acclimation and responses to rising temperature and falling O₂ concentration. Its stated aim is to compare these responses with those of other freshwater and estuarine bivalves and so estimate how well *C. fluminea* can compete with native North American species.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Collection:** Fall 1975, sandy substratum, 1–3 m depth, small cove on the southeast shore of Lake Arlington (Tarrant County, Texas).
- **Acclimation:** 3–4 weeks at 10, 20, or 30 °C.
- **Sample:** 7–9 individuals or groups per acclimation group, selected to a common shell length; shell-length range of the natural population given as 31.1–39.3 mm.
- **Apparatus:** Respiration chambers of 4–40 ml fitted with Clark-type polarographic oxygen electrodes (YSI Model 53). Chamber water was filtered lake water, sterilized by boiling.
- **Temperature trials:** Temperature raised from acclimation temperature at 1 °C/5 min to 50 °C, with uptake recorded at 5 °C intervals; siphon extension noted at each step.
- **Oxygen trials:** Uptake measured from air saturation (PO₂ ≈ 159.1 mm Hg) down to about 10% tension, or until consumption ceased. A second set used three adults per acclimation group.
- **Standardization:** Uptake was normalized to a "standard" adult of 30 mm SL and 306 mg dry tissue weight, using log-transformed regressions. Dry weights were obtained by drying at 95 °C for more than 2 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal ecology.** The author notes that the species is relatively intolerant of high summer ambient temperatures. Reported temperatures above about 25 °C depress the release of larvae, and larval release ceases above about 37 °C ([[Aldridge and McMahon]], 1978). Populations are reported to be active mainly in cooler seasons. Respiratory stress is suggested as directly associated with both spawning inhibition and reduced activity. In the southern United States, where *C. fluminea* is most common, ambient summer temperatures of about 30 °C occur frequently.

**Oxygen availability.** Recent, more detailed studies indicate that the species is intolerant of hypoxia. Its distribution is restricted to rivers or to shallow lake areas with well-oxygenated substrata ([[Sinclair and Isom]], 1961, 1963; [[Aldridge and McMahon]], 1978). Most freshwater bivalves are more tolerant of hypoxia and better regulate oxygen consumption. Examples cited include *Musculium*, *Pisidium casertanum* (which lives in nearly anoxic substrata), *Polymesoda*, and several Unionidae.

**Evolutionary context.** The author argues that *C. fluminea* is a relatively recent invader of fresh water from estuarine ancestry. Its respiratory and temperature responses resemble those of estuarine bivalves more than those of established freshwater taxa. By contrast, [[Pisidiidae]] and [[Unionacea]] have long freshwater histories and appear physiologically better adapted to variable, freshwater temperature and oxygen regimes.

**Comparative physiology.** The author compares *C. fluminea* with several other bivalves:
- Estuarine species: [[Mercenaria Mercenaria]] shows no oxygen regulation as tension declines; [[Rangia Cuneata]] is a moderate regulator; [[Geloina Ceylonica]], an estuarine member of Corbiculidae, shows good regulation.
- Freshwater species: *Polymesoda* survives temperatures up to about 34 °C, and *Anodonta cygnea* shows relatively low tolerance of anoxia.

## Key Findings

1. **Temperature acclimation pattern.** Oxygen uptake rose with temperature to a peak at 20–25 °C in clams acclimated to 20 or 30 °C, then dropped sharply at 30 °C and above. Acclimation to 10 °C produced the highest mean uptake over 5–25 °C. Uptake was least in 30 °C acclimated clams. No significant differences among acclimation groups occurred above 25 °C.

2. **Siphon behaviour.** Siphon extension was clearly related to temperature. Over 50% of 10 °C acclimated clams extended siphons between 5 and 25 °C. Above about 25 °C, siphon extension declined sharply, with siphon closure accompanying a marked drop in uptake. A second period of siphon extension occurred at 45 °C, associated with acute thermal stress.

3. **Upper thermal responses.** Uptake rose slowly to a second maximum as clams became thermally stressed, at 40 °C in 10 °C acclimated clams and at 45 °C in 20 and 30 °C acclimated clams. The acute upper lethal temperature was 50 °C. A long-term lethal value of 33.5 °C was reported by [[Habel]] for a wide range of acclimation temperatures.

4. **Oxygen regulation.** *C. fluminea* is a poor regulator of oxygen consumption. At an O₂ tension of 20% of air saturation (PO₂ ≈ 31.4 mm Hg), uptake averaged only 13.7% of the rate at full air saturation. Below that tension, rates stayed low and stable.

5. **Regulation by acclimation temperature.** Clams acclimated to 10 °C behaved as strict oxygen conformers, with oxygen uptake depending directly on ambient tension. Clams acclimated to 20 or 30 °C were less than strict conformers and showed positive β values (the [[Oxygen Regulation Index]] of [[Mangum and Van Winkle]], 1973), which the author notes have been reported previously only for the estuarine snail *Littorina*.

6. **Comparison with other bivalves.** Most freshwater bivalves tolerate hypoxia better than *C. fluminea*. Survival times in air are short: in moist air, apparent anoxia was survived for 14 days at 20 °C and 7 days at a higher temperature, and dry-air emergence tolerance was limited. Only *Geloina ceylonica* among estuarine relatives matched its tolerance ranges.

7. **Ecological implications.** The author concludes that the inability to maintain oxygen consumption under low oxygen directly limits the species' tolerance of hypoxic environments. This in turn restricts its potential distribution in North America and its ability to outcompete and replace better-adapted native bivalves. The author considers *C. fluminea* unlikely to pose a serious threat to most North American freshwater habitats.
