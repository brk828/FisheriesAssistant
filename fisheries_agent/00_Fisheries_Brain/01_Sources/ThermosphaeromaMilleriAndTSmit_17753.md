# Thermosphaeroma Isopods of Hot Springs in Chihuahua and the Southwestern United States

#carcinology #marine-biology #sphaeromatidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper describes two new species of [[Thermosphaeroma]] isopod crustaceans (family [[Sphaeromatidae]]) from hot springs in Chihuahua, Mexico. It raises the known number of species in the genus from three to five, adds descriptive notes on the endangered [[Thermosphaeroma thermophilum]], provides a key to all five species, and proposes a new subfamily name, [[Dynameninae]]. The author also considers why flabelliferan isopods are restricted to caves, hot springs and fish parasitism, and not to ordinary freshwater habitats. The two new species were collected while [[Robert R. Miller]] and [[Michael L. Smith]] were studying [[Mexican Desert-Spring Pupfishes]]. The paper is a systematic and biogeographic study of invertebrates. It contains no fish-specific data.

## Background & Description

The genus [[Thermosphaeroma]] was established by Cole and Bane (1978) and is restricted to warm springs. Before this study, three species were known:

- [[Thermosphaeroma dugesi]] (Dollfus 1893) from Ojo Caliente, Aguascalientes, Mexico, the type-species.
- [[Thermosphaeroma thermophilum]] (Richardson 1897) from Socorro, New Mexico.
- [[Thermosphaeroma subequalum]] Cole and Bane 1978, from two springs in Boquillas Canyon, [[Big Bend National Park]], Texas.

The new species are:

- **[[Thermosphaeroma milleri]]**, described from three springs around Ejido Rancho Nuevo in the arid Bolsón de los Muertos, about 36 km W of Villa Ahumada, Chihuahua. Named for Dr. Robert R. Miller. Length up to 11.5 mm. Holotype USNM 181123.
- **[[Thermosphaeroma smithi]]**, described from Balneario San Diego, about 37 km E of Ciudad Chihuahua. Named for Dr. Michael L. Smith. Length up to 11.7 mm.

**Taxonomic notes**

- Under Article 11(e) of the ICZN, the subfamily names of Hurley and Jansen (1977) are not available as family-group names. The author proposes [[Dynamene]] Leach 1814, the oldest of the 10 genera Hansen included in Eubranchiatae, as the type-genus of Dynameninae, replacing Eubranchiatinae.
- The genus has an unusual combination of characters for Dynameninae. The telson is not notched, the free pleonite lacks lateral sutures, branchial folds are weak on pleopod 4, and the pleopod 5 exopod is undivided. These features make its relationships hard to infer.
- Species are separated mainly by mouthpart spine counts, pleotelson shape, appendix masculina shape, and the length ratio of the uropod rami.

**Diagnostic differences (from the key and descriptions)**

- *T. smithi*: the only species in which the uropodal exopod exceeds the endopod. The uropods are sexually dimorphic, and the exopod:endopod ratio tends to be higher in females.
- *T. subequalum*: uropod rami subequal in length, with a truncate endopod apex.
- *T. dugesi*: uropodal endopod distinctly longer than the exopod, with an angular apex.
- *T. thermophilum*: uropodal endopod nearly twice the exopod length, with a rounded apex and an evenly rounded pleotelson lacking a ventral shelf.
- *T. milleri*: pleotelson with concave subterminal margins and a rounded uropodal endopod apex. It is the most hirsute species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitats of the new species**

- *T. milleri*: The three springs form an isolated cluster. Ojo El Medio and Ojo de las Varas are irrigation ponds fed by impounded springs, with water rising through quicksand. Water temperatures were 27.1°C (Ojo de las Varas, 2 June 1979) and 28°C (Ojo El Medio, 2 June 1979). Ojo de Carbonera is the least modified, with a main spring about 3.7 m wide and water about 1 m deep. Its water temperature was 27°C on 28 May 1978 and 6 June 1979. Vegetation is sparse, including filamentous algae, Chara, Nasturtium, and submerged grass.
- *T. smithi*: The species lives in about 15 small spring-heads (<0.5 m) that drain into a creek about 1 m wide. Water temperatures on 26 May 1979 ranged from 32.1 to 43.8°C. Isopods occurred in the spring-heads (43.8°C) and on silt bottoms of pools and banks (32.1–43.4°C), often covering the substrate. They were absent from loose sand and areas with significant current.
- *T. thermophilum*: The population lives in the water system of the abandoned bathhouse "The Evergreen," supplied by Sedillo Spring. The population was estimated at about 2,400 individuals in two small pools (20–26 cm deep, about 7 m total length) and two runs (about 39 m total length). Water temperatures were 31–32°C. Historic collections include 630+ specimens from Evergreen Ranch Spring (31°C) and 430+ from City Spring (34°C), both collected 8 June 1938.
- *T. dugesi*: Known only from a warm spring at more than 35°C at its type-locality.
- *T. subequalum*: Known from two springs near Boquillas Canyon and a site on the Rio Grande Village Nature Trail, at 32–35°C.

**Sex ratio of *T. smithi***: Of 56 specimens picked at random from the Landye sample, 40 were male and 16 were female (2.5:1). None of the females carried eggs, young, or oostegites. The author suggests brooding females may have escaped collection.

**Conservation status**

[[Thermosphaeroma thermophilum]] was classified as endangered by the State of New Mexico in February 1978 and by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on 27 March 1978 (43 Federal Register 9612). It is the only crustacean listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Main threats:

- The original spring source was capped, and most flow was diverted to the Socorro city water supply.
- Remaining flow can be shut off by a readily accessible valve.
- The habitat is on private land, so flow and protection from contamination cannot be secured at present.

Captive populations are held at the University of New Mexico and the Rio Grande Zoo in Albuquerque. The New Mexico Game and Fish Department has drafted a recovery plan (unpublished technical review draft) that includes enhancing habitat at Sedillo Spring and establishing introduced populations at other suitable sites.

**Origin of the genus**

- Most sphaeromatids are marine or brackish, although a few, such as [[Gnorimosphaeroma]] species, live in freshwater streams and tolerate osmotic regulation.
- The author proposes that the ancestral stock inhabited a marine embayment covering central and eastern Mexico, southern Texas, and most of New Mexico in the middle to late Mesozoic. Regression at the end of the Cretaceous stranded these ancestors in continental freshwaters.
- Fossil flora analyses show a progressively drier Tertiary climate. Extreme regional deserts appeared mainly during interglacial periods, especially after the Wisconsin glaciation (Axelrod 1979). The author therefore considers the confinement of ancestral *Thermosphaeroma* to hot springs and the speciation within the genus to be a recent event.

**Restricted freshwater occurrence of Flabellifera**

The author argues that flabelliferan isopods are excluded from typical epigean freshwater, unlike asellids and gammarids. Freshwater flabelliferans fall into three groups:

1. **Fish and shrimp parasites**: about 15 cymothoid species parasitize freshwater fishes in South America, with others in Africa and Asia. Aegidae, Argathonidae, and Corallanidae also include freshwater parasites.
2. **Subterranean species**: mainly cirolanids in caves, including *Mexilana saluposi* from San Luis Potosí and *Arubolana imula* from Aruba.
3. **Hot-spring species**: the five *Thermosphaeroma* species.

The author proposes that biotic competition explains the exclusion. Predatory aquatic insects fill the niche that flabelliferans occupy elsewhere. Eleven of the 30–35 insect orders contain aquatic or semi-aquatic species, and 8 of the 13 orders in Pennak's (1978) manual include at least partly predaceous species. Aquatic insects are rare in caves, which leaves subterranean flabelliferans with little competition. Parasitic isopods are also free from insect competition, and the shelter that a host fish provides may matter more than food.

## Key Findings

- **Five species** are now recognised in *Thermosphaeroma*: two new species (*T. milleri* and *T. smithi*) and three previously described species (*T. dugesi*, *T. thermophilum*, *T. subequalum*).
- **A key** separates the species by uropod ramus length ratios, mouthpart spine counts, pleotelson shape, and appendix masculina form.
- **Taxonomic revision**: [[Dynameninae]] is proposed as the replacement name for Eubranchiatinae because the earlier names are not available under ICZN Article 11(e).
- **Sexual dimorphism** in the uropods of *T. smithi* is unique within the genus.
- **Thermal tolerance**: all species occur in warm waters, from about 27°C to over 43°C.
- **Endangered status**: *T. thermophilum* is the only crustacean listed under the US Endangered Species Act, and its main threat is habitat loss from water diversion.
- **Origin**: the genus most likely descends from marine sphaeromatid ancestors stranded in continental waters after the Cretaceous regression, with hot-spring specialisation a recent development.
- **Freshwater exclusion**: the author attributes the absence of flabelliferans from ordinary freshwater to competition with predaceous aquatic insects. Their occurrence in caves, hot springs, and as fish parasites is linked to the absence of such competitors.
- **Collection context**: the two new species were gathered during fieldwork on [[Mexican Desert-Spring Pupfishes]] by Miller and Smith at the University of Michigan.
