# Southwestern Fishes and the Enigma of "Endangered Species"

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

Minckley and Deacon (1968, *Science*) examine the concept of "endangered species" using freshwater fishes of the American Southwest as examples. They argue that a species' status can only be objectively defined by its ability to maintain populations in nature, which requires continuously available suitable habitat. The authors sort organisms into four habitat-need categories and show that most declines in native southwestern fishes stem from habitat change caused by [[Human Impact on Aquatic Habitats]], especially impoundment, groundwater pumping, and the introduction of exotic fishes. They call for comprehensive documentation, ecological research, and laboratory maintenance of depleted species, and they question how conservation priorities are chosen.

## Background & Description

The article responds to growing public and institutional concern for biodiversity. The authors note that organizations such as [[The Nature Conservancy]] and the [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]] are active, that the Nevada Game and Fish Commission accepted responsibility for endemic fishes in January 1967, that California took similar action in December 1967, and that the [[U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] has defined and begun cataloging rare and endangered species.

The authors propose four categories of organisms by habitat need:

1. Species whose habitats were produced or changed by man and that have expanded (largely irrelevant except where introduced species harm natives).
2. Widespread, tolerant species that are currently common, with broad distributions that protect against extinction.
3. Animals needing large, special habitats, such as [[Big-River Fishes]] that depend on strongly flowing rivers.
4. Species in small, unique habitats as relicts or isolated endemics, such as [[Desert Spring Fishes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Big-river fishes (category 3).** Dams, siltation, reduced silt loads, increased light penetration, temperature changes, channelization, and drying of streams disrupt these fishes. Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus*) runs have declined where rivers were obstructed, though no species of Pacific salmon is immediately endangered. The Salt River at Tempe lost much of its fauna between 1890 and 1926, coinciding with the creation of [[Roosevelt Lake]] in 1910 and later impoundments. [[Bartlett Reservoir]] closure in 1939, followed by another dam, left the channel almost totally desiccated by the late 1950s, with only subsurface percolation from municipal waste water sustaining isolated fish habitats.

**Desert springs as aquatic archipelagos.** Desert springs are isolated like oceanic islands, but they often hold relict organisms rather than chance invaders. Their small, simplified habitats are especially vulnerable where water demand exceeds supply. Introduced fishes and [[Water Table Decline]] from pumping are recurring threats.

**Exotic species.** Introduced fishes such as the [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquito Fish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), goldfish (*Carassius auratus*), largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), and mollies (*Poecilia*) have displaced or depleted native fishes. The authors also note the role of parasites: native fishes may be more susceptible to existing parasites when competition from exotics is present, or when new parasites arrive with introduced fishes.

**Water law and economics.** The authors observe that most water laws allow "beneficial" use without regard to wildlife, and that habitat destruction is often treated as a landowner's right or a governmental duty when it yields economic gain.

## Key Findings

- **Salt River, Tempe (Table 1):** Native and introduced fish records from 1890 to 1967 document major extirpation between 1890 and 1926. All species that occupied the river at Tempe in 1890 still exist somewhere in the Colorado River basin, but their success varies: some are abundant, others reduced, and a few near extinction.
- **Colorado River squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and humpback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Both have declined sharply. Humpback suckers in the Gila River were effectively eliminated, and the Gila population is considered probably extinct. Squawfish persist in the Green River and Grand Canyon, and in the lower Colorado no specimen shorter than 15 inches has been caught in recent years. Only two specimens from the lower Colorado were recorded in 1962–67.
- **Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** The last reproducing population lives in the lower Virgin River. A proposed dam 8 miles above St. George, Utah, would affect about 80 of approximately 90 miles of suitable habitat. Because the species disappeared from the Gila River early in the century after man-induced changes, the authors advise avoiding any change until habitat requirements are defined.
- **Gila spinedace (*Meda fulgida*):** Once widespread in the upper Gila basin, it has not been taken anywhere in the Verde River drainage in recent years, where it was once abundant. Its outlook is described as bleak given proposed dams and spread of the red shiner.
- **Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Once throughout the Gila drainage, it persists in only one spring area in Santa Cruz County, Arizona. Replacement by *Gambusia affinis* was rapid in Arivaca Creek (introduced 1936, replaced by 1959) and near Safford, Arizona. The species is now placed in category 4.
- **Pahrump killifish (*Empetrichthys latos*) and Ash Meadows killifish (*Empetrichthys merriami*):** *E. merriami* is extinct. Of three *E. latos* springs, Pahrump Spring failed in 1958 and Baycraft Spring was filled, leaving only Manse Spring (about 1,300 fish in July 1967). Pumpage rose from 16.1 thousand acre-feet in 1951 to about 41,000 acre-feet, against an estimated annual recharge of 22,100 acre-feet. The authors estimate Manse Spring may fail in 10 to 11 years.
- **Moapa dace (*Moapa coriacea*):** Rare in 1964 studies after abundance into the early 1950s. Population density fell after introduction of the shortfin molly (*Poecilia mexicana*) and rose again after about 2 years, with no physical habitat change. Stream discharge has been remarkably stable.
- **White River springfish (*Crenichthys baileyi*):** Some populations tolerate the highest temperatures and lowest dissolved oxygen recorded for fishes. Several stocks are depleted after introductions, and one population went extinct after introduction of largemouth bass and mollies. Three control springs were each contaminated by exotic fishes within two months.
- **Other examples:** *Cyprinodon diabolis* (Devils Hole) is represented by no more than 700 individuals and is protected in Death Valley National Monument. *Eremichthys acros* (desert dace) was listed owing to a premature judgment about irrigation, yet it is expanding into irrigation ditches and is not endangered.
- **Extinct fishes (count and examples):** The authors list several extinct taxa, including *E. merriami*, *Lepidomeda altivelis*, *Lepidomeda mollispinis pratensis*, and several local populations. Exotic fishes contributed to six of these losses, while habitat destruction caused the last two.
- **Sampling reliability:** Survey results depend on method and season. Students using electrofishing failed to find the Yaqui chub in a stream section that held it nearby, and winter collections may falsely suggest extinction.
- **Conservation recommendations:** Three objectives are proposed: (i) thorough documentation of past and present population status with wide publication; (ii) basic ecological data and life histories of depleted species; and (iii) laboratory maintenance of depleted species in seminatural conditions. The authors argue that eradicating exotic fishes must consider effects on other organisms, and that the list of endangered species should reflect demand as well as supply.
- **Central conclusion:** Declines are "largely due to habitat changes associated with man's modification" of aquatic environments. The authors call the program a "great natural experiment of evolution" that could become "an exercise in extinction, if man will have it so."
