# Pliocene Fossil Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) from Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Geoffrey M. Hoetker and Kenneth W. Gobalet (Department of Biology, California State University, Bakersfield) describes a nearly complete fossil catostomid from the Anza-Borrego Desert, purportedly of early Pliocene age. The authors compare its skeletal and meristic characters with living [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and related catostomids, including [[Bonytail Chub]]-associated [[Colorado River Fishes]] such as [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and *Chasmistes* spp. They conclude that the fossil is *X. texanus*, with minor meristic and morphological differences, most notably a more pronounced nuchal hump and fewer vertebrae. If the Pliocene age holds, the fossil extends the *Xyrauchen* lineage back more than five million years.

## Background & Description

The genus *Xyrauchen* is monotypic, containing only the razorback sucker, and is regarded as one of the more primitive members of the subfamily Catostominae, tribe Catostomini (Smith 1992). *Xyrauchen*, *Deltistes* and *Chasmistes* are considered sister groups. All are large-bodied genera inhabiting extensive river systems.

The federally endangered razorback sucker is among the largest native freshwater fishes of the Colorado Basin, reaching nearly one meter total length. Its name refers to the pronounced nuchal hump of adults, a feature shared to a lesser degree by the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The function of the hump is debated. It may act as a stabilizer in the swift, deep waters of the Colorado River, but the effect on swimming performance has not been empirically tested. Minckley and Meffe (1987) found that native humped fishes survived flooding in Arizona and New Mexico, whereas introduced fishes lacking a hump did not, which the authors link to differing evolutionary histories.

The fossil (SBCM A768-1) was collected from the north side of the San Felipe Hills, near the western boundary of Imperial County and north of State Highway 78 (Stewart and Roeder 1993). It is nearly complete and embedded in an oval arenitic sandstone concretion split lengthwise, with skeletal material in one half and mostly imprints in the other. The sandstone is deltaic, suggesting deposition in the delta of the ancestral [[Colorado River]], with subsequent displacement to its present location. The authors state that more precise locality and age data are unlikely to become available.

**Methods.** Characters and meristic counts were taken from the fossil and from comparative specimens (see Materials Examined). Characters were selected for accessibility on the fossil and taxonomic value, following Smith's (1992) phylogeny of the Catostomidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hybridization:** *X. texanus* hybridizes with *C. latipinnis* in the upper Colorado River system (Hubbs and Miller 1953; Buth et al. 1987; Tyus and Karp 1990), with potential for introgression (Smith 1992a). The authors note this complicates the taxonomic assignment of catostomid fossils from the Colorado drainage. Hybrids have a rounded hump that is less pronounced than in pure *X. texanus*, so the fossil's more prominent hump argues against hybrid origin.
- **Development:** The hump appears about 200 days after fertilization and becomes fully developed at adulthood (Minckley and Gustafson 1982).
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males are smaller than females and may have a more exaggerated predorsal keel (Minckley et al. 1990). Pelvic and anal fin traits also differ between sexes.
- **Biogeography:** No fossil catostomids were known from the Bidahochi Formation, Arizona (Uyeno and Miller 1965). Before this specimen, catostomids older than Pleistocene were unknown (Minckley et al. 1986).
- **Chronology:** Late Miocene (8.5 to 5.5 million years old) *Chasmistes* and *Deltistes* from the Chalk Hills Formation, Idaho, are morphologically modern (Miller and Smith 1981; Smith et al. 1982). This suggests an earlier divergence for these groups and probably for *Xyrauchen*. The Bidahochi Formation's cyprinids are also considered modern, and Baskin (1978) argued that the formation is late Miocene.

## Key Findings

**Meristic and skeletal counts of the fossil**

- 16 dorsal fin rays and pterygiophores, plus two "rudimentary" anterior rays. The norm for *X. texanus* is 14 to 15, with a documented range of 13 to 16. *Deltistes*, *Chasmistes* and *C. latipinnis* have 12 to 13 rays (Smith 1992).
- More than 9 pectoral fin rays; at least 8 pelvic fin rays; 7 anal pterygiophores.
- About 42 post-Weberian vertebrae (about 46 total). This is one vertebra below the range of five comparative *X. texanus* specimens (43 to 44 post-Weberian; 47 to 48 total). *Chasmistes cujus* has 46 to 47 total, *Deltistes luxatus* has 49, and *C. latipinnis* has 44 to 45 total (Smith 1992).
- The nine caudal vertebrae were estimated, not observed, from six comparative *X. texanus* skeletons.

**Head and jaw morphology**

- The dermethmoid spine flares laterally, giving a "house with a chimney" appearance in dorsal view. The head skeleton shows the "domed rectangle" shape of *Chasmistes* and differs from the shorter, wider shape of *C. latipinnis*. Part of this may reflect distortion during fossilization.
- The dentary is long, with little or no ventral deflection (unlike *C. latipinnis*). Shaft length is 21 mm and ventral quadrate recess to coronoid tip is 22 mm.
- The dentary foramen is oriented medial-lateral, as in *Xyrauchen* and *Chasmistes*, not anterior-posterior as in *C. latipinnis*.
- The maxilla body measures 27 mm; the dentary process is missing.
- The preopercle's anterior tip lies 5 mm caudad of the quadrate's mandibular condyle. The authors consider Smith's preopercle character equivocal.

**Nuchal hump**

- Six interneurals lie anterior to the dorsal fin. Three are enlarged to form the hump. Comparative *X. texanus* have three or four enlarged elements, *C. latipinnis* usually has 0 to 2, and five *Chasmistes cujus* each have one.
- At its apex the hump rises 38 mm dorsally and measures 87 mm in length. These equal 10.3% and 23.5% of standard length, versus 7.6% (twelve specimens) and 18.2% (three specimens) in comparative material. The hump is therefore larger than in living razorback suckers.

**Size and sex**

- Estimated 435 to 440 mm total length and 370 mm standard length place the fossil at the lower end of the adult *X. texanus* size range.
- Because of its small size and its pronounced hump, the authors suggest the fossil is a male. Pelvic fin flaring (Minckley, pers. comm.) and the hump support this. The anal fin-ray curvature that would confirm sex is missing.

**Conclusions**

- The fossil is most similar to *X. texanus*. It has fewer vertebrae, more dorsal rays and a larger hump than known *Xyrauchen* material. The authors interpret these as evidence of greater character variation in the Pliocene.
- The species appears to vary little morphologically across its range (Hubbs and Miller 1953).
- The authors speculate that the lineage may have reduced its hump and increased vertebral number in response to changing climate and habitat.

**Materials and collections**

- The study examined the fossil and about 20 comparative specimens: 12 lots of *X. texanus* (Arizona State University, with three unnumbered skeletons), one *Deltistes luxatus* (UCMP 122582), five *Chasmistes cujus* (Gobalet collection), and one *Catostomus latipinnis* (ASU 13844).
