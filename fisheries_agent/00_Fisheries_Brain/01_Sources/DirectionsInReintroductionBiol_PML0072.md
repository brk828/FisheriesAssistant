# Directions in Reintroduction Biology

#conservation-biology #reintroduction-biology #vertebrates

## Executive Summary

This perspective article by [[Doug Armstrong]] and [[Philip Seddon]] (IUCN/SSC Reintroduction Specialist Group) argues that [[Reintroduction Biology]] should shift from descriptive and retrospective research toward a strategic framework of questions identified in advance. The authors propose ten key questions spanning the population, metapopulation and ecosystem levels, explain the conceptual basis for each, suggest methods, and link the field to [[Restoration Ecology]] and [[Invasion Biology]]. The paper is a framework and position piece rather than an empirical study; it reports no new original dataset.

## Background & Description

- **Definition of reintroduction:** attempts to return species to parts of their historical range where they were extirpated, using captive-bred or wild-caught individuals.
- **Historical context:** reintroduction attempts have occurred for at least 100 years, but the field emerged in the 1980s after most attempts were found to fail with little learned. The [[IUCN/SSC Reintroduction Specialist Group]] was formed in 1988.
- **Literature growth:** publications rose from fewer than 5 per year in the early 1990s to more than 50 per year at the time of writing.
- **Core critique:** the literature is largely descriptive or retrospective, so research questions have been driven by available monitoring data rather than monitoring being driven by questions. The authors argue that unfocused monitoring wastes conservation funds and that post hoc induction is a poor basis for management.
- **Terminology (Box 1):** the authors adopt the original [[IUCN Translocation Terminology]] (1987), which defines translocation as any movement of living organisms and distinguishes three mutually exclusive types: introduction (outside historic native range), reintroduction (into part of native range where extirpated), and re-stocking (building up an existing population). They criticise the later IUCN guidelines for restricting "translocation" to wild individuals.
- **Habitat concept (Box 2):** following [[Hall et al. 1997]], "habitat quality" should mean the ability of the environment to support persistence and be linked to survival and reproduction, not equated with vegetation. Habitat conditions include food, predators and parasites.
- The text ends with an unrelated appended item on the [[HINARI]] programme giving developing-country institutions free access to biomedical journals (over 2,000 journals; 22 additional publishers joined in 2002; more than 70 publishers participating at the time of writing).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population level.** Persistence is distinguished from establishment: establishment concerns whether small release groups survive the initial phase (affected by demographic stochasticity and [[Allee Effects]]), while persistence concerns long-term growth once established. Post-release dispersal and mortality create a gap between release group size and effective initial population size. The authors caution that comparative analyses of success rates across species (e.g. [[Griffith et al. 1989]]) may be biased, because low numbers are typically released where success is judged unlikely, and that populations with low dispersal and mortality may establish from fewer than 10 released individuals ([[Taylor et al. 2005]], New Zealand robins and saddlebacks).

**Habitat and genetics.** Habitat conditions are considered the main driver of population growth. The authors note that the original cause of decline must be identified and removed before reintroduction. Genetic effects include founder inbreeding, maladaptation from inappropriate provenance, and loss of diversity leading to [[Inbreeding Depression]] and reduced immunocompetence. Periodic translocation among populations can reduce inbreeding but may prevent local adaptation.

**Metapopulation level.** Translocation creates at least a simple metapopulation linking source and recipient populations. Harvesting sources carries impacts that depend on compensatory density responses. Distribution of individuals among sites is currently often decided ad hoc.

**Ecosystem level.** Reintroduction literature has been single-species and animal-focused, while restoration literature has focused on abiotic factors and vegetation, leaving little overlap. Climate change is shifting habitat suitability, so some programmes may need to build ecosystems suited to new climates rather than restore historic assemblages.

## Key Findings

The authors propose ten key questions, grouped as shown below:

**Population level: establishment**
1. How is establishment probability affected by the size and composition of the release group?
2. How are post-release survival and dispersal affected by pre- and post-release management? (Soft-release effects are not assumed; holding animals at the release site has often not reduced dispersal or mortality.)

**Population level: persistence**
3. What habitat conditions are needed for persistence of the reintroduced population? Positive growth is the essential prerequisite. Adaptive management (e.g. food supplementation experiments for hihi, [[Armstrong et al. 2007]]) is suggested to identify requirements.
4. How will genetic makeup affect persistence? Predicting this requires linking genetic diversity to survival and reproduction, then population modelling. The authors state they know of no study that has done this.
5. How heavily should source populations be harvested? Adaptive harvesting of North Island robins on [[Tiritiri Matangi Island]] is cited as an example ([[Dimond and Armstrong 2007]]).

**Metapopulation level**
6. What is the optimal allocation of translocated individuals among sites? Optimisation approaches are proposed.
7. Should translocation be used to compensate for isolation? It may restore distributions where fragmentation drove extinction, but would be "disastrous" where declines reflected habitat quality loss.

**Ecosystem level**
8. Are the target species/taxon and its parasites native to the ecosystem? The authors argue disease screening should aim to restore host-parasite relationships and prevent non-native parasite introduction, and that historic parasite distributions need reconstruction.
9. How will the ecosystem be affected by the target species and its parasites? Restoring ecosystem function, including reintroducing [[Ecosystem Engineers]], may be a better justification than restoring species composition alone.
10. How does the order of reintroductions affect ultimate species composition? This question arises frequently in New Zealand restoration and is currently decided by intuition; predator-prey functional responses are proposed as a research area.

**Strategic conclusions**
- Designing monitoring around a priori questions maximises knowledge from limited funds.
- Reintroduction research has so far addressed only four of the ten questions (Q1–Q3 and Q5), with little attention to metapopulation and ecosystem questions.
- The framework can improve communication between researchers and practitioners; New Zealand translocation standard operating procedures were being revised to incorporate it.
- Projects should be designed to answer questions of value beyond their own site, and prior data can be incorporated via [[Bayesian Analysis]] (e.g. [[McCarthy 2007]]).
- The authors conclude that greater integration across projects and disciplines is the best response to decisions made with few data.
