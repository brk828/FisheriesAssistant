# Aquatic Study of the Lower Colorado River (USFWS, 1981)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared for the Water and Power Resources Service (Contract 9-07-03-X0066), is a literature-based guide to the historic and present aquatic biota of the Colorado River from Lees Ferry to the International Boundary. It covers the river's recent history, the distribution of aquatic plants and invertebrates, life histories of important native and sport fish, fish diseases and parasites, and changes in habitat. The authors state that the work drew on literature searches and communications with agency personnel, with minimal field observations and no biological inventories. Endangered-species information is included for general knowledge only and is not official for Section 7 consultation.

The central theme is that dams, sediment control, channelization, bank armoring, and introduced species have transformed a turbid, seasonally variable desert river into a sequence of regulated reservoirs, cold tailraces, and modified channels. Native "big river" fishes have declined sharply, while introduced game and forage fishes now dominate the sport fisheries.

## Background & Description

**Historic river.** When first recorded by Spanish explorers, the river was silt-laden, flowed through flat desert valleys lined with cottonwood galleries, and had transitory backwaters and marshes. The upper section (Lees Ferry to Black Canyon) had a gradient of 8 ft/mile; the lower river fell to 1.8 ft/mile. Spring floods were large (86,000–100,000 cfs at Lees Ferry; 250,000 cfs at Yuma in January 1916), while late-summer flows could be very low (538 cfs at Imperial Dam, August 1934).

**Human history.** Key milestones include the 1877 first water-use permit on the lower river, Imperial Canal construction (1895–1901), the 1905–1907 flood that created the Salton Sea, levee construction, and the completion of Hoover Dam (1935), Parker Dam (1938), Imperial Dam (1938), Davis Dam (1951), and Glen Canyon Dam (1964). Dredging and channelization began in 1949 with the dredge "The Colorado." See the timeline in Appendix 1 and dredging chronology in Appendix 2.

**Native fish fauna.** The river and associated tributaries supported a unique assemblage of about 16 native species (Table 3), including [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Early settlers described squawfish "runs" so abundant they could be pitchforked from the river.

**Introduced fauna.** Between the late 1800s and 1976, more than 60 non-native fish species were introduced (Table 4), many for sport fishing or forage. Crayfish, the Asiatic clam [[Corbicula Manilensis]], and freshwater shrimp were also introduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types.** Three main types existed historically: the mainstem channel, tributary streams, and backwaters. Today the river comprises reservoirs (Mead, Mohave, Havasu), marshes and backwaters, tributaries, and modified and unmodified channel reaches. Tailraces below Glen Canyon, Hoover and Davis dams are cold and clear.

**Physical and chemical changes.**
- Flows: Glen Canyon Dam reduced average flow from 2,486 to 830 m³/s (median 210 to 346 m³/s), replacing seasonal patterns with daily and weekly fluctuations. Weekend flows can be up to 80 percent lower than weekday flows.
- Sediment: Pre-dam sediment load was reported at 374 metric tons per day at Lees Ferry. Post-dam turbidity dropped from 1,500 to 7 ppm at Lees Ferry, though erosion later increased sediment inputs.
- Salinity: Salinity has been rising due to irrigation return flows. Current concentrations at Hoover Dam are 700–800 ppm, with projected values of about 900 ppm below Parker and 1,000 ppm below Imperial by 1990.
- Temperature: Upper-river temperatures ranged from 29.5°C in summer to near 0°C in winter; lower-river extremes were 32°C to 4°C.

**Aquatic plants.** The mainstem supported very little vegetation because turbidity limited light and shifting substrate prevented anchorage. Tributaries and backwaters supported richer flora (cattails, sedges, pondweeds, *Najas*, *Chara*, *Cladophora*). Reservoir coves developed plant beds where water levels were stable, notably in Lake Havasu.

**Invertebrates.** Like other large erosive rivers, the Colorado had a non-diverse but locally abundant fauna. Chironomids and oligochaetes dominated most reaches. Mainstem Glen Canyon supported about 20 aquatic insect species versus 91 collected in tributaries. Tailraces below dams supported gammarid amphipods, simuliids, and hydropsychid larvae.

**Key drivers of change.** The report identifies dam regulation, sediment and salinity shifts, channelization and bank armoring (which eliminated eddies, shallows and backwaters), introduced fish predation and competition, and loss of breeding habitat as the main drivers of native fish decline.

## Key Findings

**Native fish decline**
- Squawfish disappeared from Lake Mead by the mid-1950s and from Lake Havasu by the 1940s; the last verified bonytail in Lake Mead was seen in 1967.
- Razorback sucker persists in several reservoirs but is rare; flannelmouth suckers survive upstream of Lake Mead.
- Woundfin, Moapa dace and Virgin River spinedace were never seen in Lake Mead.
- Humpback chub persists in the Grand Canyon, especially near the Little Colorado River mouth, with hybridization with roundtail chub a documented threat.

**Introduced sport and forage fish**
- Threadfin shad (introduced 1953–1954) became the main forage base for striped bass, trout, largemouth bass and crappie.
- Rainbow trout stocking began below Hoover Dam in 1935; Lake Mohave trout made up 48–68 percent of the catch in 1975–1979.
- Striped bass were introduced into Lake Mead in 1969; they now dominate the Mead sport fishery. Striped bass predation on stocked trout is documented (20 bass ate 24 trout within 13 days below Davis Dam).
- Channel catfish provide 10–50 percent of the sport catch in the lower mainstem, with the best areas at Parker, Ehrenberg and Imperial Reservoir.
- Largemouth bass, once 78 percent of the Lake Mead catch in 1962, had fallen to 9 percent by 1979.

**Life history highlights**
- Threadfin shad: highly prolific (up to 12,000 eggs in a 10.2 cm female); eggs hatch in 3 days at 26.7°C.
- Rainbow trout: stocked 20–25 cm catchables; fall and spring spawning strains; lethal oxygen levels of 1.05–1.51 ppm depending on temperature.
- Channel catfish: optimal spawning temperature 26.7°C; eggs do not develop below 15.5°C.
- Largemouth bass: spawning begins at 18–24°C; embryos hatch in 31.7 hours at 10°C versus 4.9 hours at 28°C.
- Black crappie: fecundity 11,000–48,000 eggs for small fish; spawns March to April or May.
- Bluegill: fecundity 2,360 to 58,000 eggs depending on size; nests form colonies of up to 500.

**Fish health**
- Infectious pancreatic necrosis (IPN) was present at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery in 1970; a 1974–1976 study found one-quarter of Lake Mohave fish outside the hatchery were carriers. Later testing of hatchery fish was negative.
- Contracaecum nematodes are consistently found in fish from Topock, Gene Reservoir, Parker II and the Yuma Division.
- The Colorado River Wildlife Council (adopted 1973, amended through 1980) requires stocked fish to be free of listed certifiable diseases (Table 11), including IHN, VHS, IPN, whirling disease and ceratomyxosis.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- The authors describe the present biological system as unstable and changing rapidly; equilibrium may never be reached.
- Backwaters and marshes remain critical nursery and refuge habitat, but many are degrading from water-quality decline and isolation by bank armoring; maintaining them is necessary for river viability.
- Aquatic vegetation provides essential cover for young centrarchids; fishery success is closely linked to available vegetative cover.
- Concern is noted about possible striped bass impacts on trout fisheries and native fish in the Grand Canyon.
