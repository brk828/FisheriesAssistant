# Determination of Balance in Farm Fish Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the Twenty-First North American Wildlife Conference (1956) by H. S. Swingle of the Alabama Polytechnic Institute Agricultural Experiment Station, sets out four methods for determining whether a warm-water pond fish population is in **balance**: [[Pond Analysis]] (a seining technique), use of [[Catch Records]], [[Population Values]] and ratios, and the [[Marking and Recovery Technique]]. Balance is defined as a condition that sustains annual harvestable crops in proportion to the water body's fertility. The core system is a [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] pond, with supplemental species. The paper draws on experiments and records from Auburn, Alabama, spanning 1934 onward, and concludes that pond analysis, catch records, and population values are useful, while marking and recovery estimates were inaccurate.

## Background & Description

**Management context.** American warm-water pond culture aims to produce sport fishing through balanced populations. The simplest and most widely used combination is the [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Supplemental species include the red-ear or shellcracker (*Lepomis microlophus*), crappies (*Pomoxis* spp.), bullheads (*Ameiurus* spp.) and channel catfish (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*, as printed).

**Expanding versus balanced populations.** Wood (1951) defined an *expanding population* as one rapidly increasing in numbers, average size and weight. Such populations, typical of new impoundments, supply large numbers of small forage fish and can briefly yield high poundage of piscivores such as bass, crappies and [[Walleye]]. Expansion cannot continue for more than one to two years, so good predator fishing lasts only into the second and part of the third year. A balanced pond is operated for at least two species, stocked to maximize the poundage of each, and is expanding only in its first year and after each harvest.

**Experimental setting.** Investigations at Auburn began in 1934. Over 17 years, 189 annual catch records were obtained from experimental ponds open to public fishing, ranging from 1 to 26 acres. Records covered stocking methods, catch per hour, catch per acre, seine sizes, rotenone samples and end-of-experiment composition. Seining was also done in 121 private ponds.

**Key terms used in the analysis:**
- *Recently hatched bluegills*: under 1 inch, hatched within the preceding month. Translucency helps separate them from fall-hatched young in early spring.
- *Intermediate bluegills*: the 3-, 4- and 5-inch groups.
- *Young bass*: fry or fingerling young-of-the-year largemouth bass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors affecting reproduction.** Pond analysis rests on ten factors that govern spawning success of bluegill and bass:

1. **Size of brood fish.** Bluegills spawned at about 0.3 ounce (3-inch group) under favorable conditions. Bass did not spawn alongside bluegills unless they reached about 6 ounces (9-inch group). In nine ponds with 1- to 4-ounce brood bluegills, the correlation between initial size and young per female was r = 0.95, with about 70 young per female at 1 ounce and about 2,560 at 4 ounces.
2. **Food during egg formation.** In 18 ponds, brood bluegills with growth ratios from 0.3 to 2.4 produced between 5 and 116 young per female (r = 0.71). For 0.2-pound brood fish, those losing weight produced none, a growth ratio of 1.0 produced 700 young, and a ratio of 2.0 produced 1,700.
3. **Crowding.** Crowding reduces food per fish, slowing growth and eventually causing weight loss. Crowding by bluegills, bullheads or crappies can prevent spawning by bluegills and other species.
4. **Egg-eating.** Crowded bluegills eat eggs. This is tolerable among bluegill and competitor eggs but detrimental when bass eggs are eaten. Old balanced Alabama ponds produced adequate hatches of both species yearly for 10 to 15 years.
5. **Repressive factor.** A hormone-like factor, first seen in goldfish, carp and buffalo, appears to suppress spawning. Fish transferred to fresh water often spawned within 24 hours. In 21 bluegill-only experiments, young per female varied inversely with brood fish per acre (r = −0.63). Bluegill crowding may also inhibit bass spawning.
6. **Water temperature.** Largemouth bass lay eggs at 68° to 75° F in spring. Surface water at 6-inch depth when first young hatch is listed for 14 species; bluegill hatch occurs at about 80° F, largemouth bass at 70° to 74° F, and white and black crappies at 68° F.
7. **Silt.** Heavily silt-laden water (windswept clay-soil ponds, gully ponds, livestock-muddied ponds) suppresses bass and bluegill spawning. Bluegills tolerate somewhat more silt than bass, and bullheads can reproduce under these conditions.
8. **Water fluctuation.** Drawdowns during bass spawning can prevent hatching. Summer drawdowns can crowd bluegills.
9. **Salinity.** Salinities of 5‰ (5,000 ppm) and above appear unsuitable for reproduction of either species. Bluegills died at 7.9‰ in South Carolina ponds (W. W. Neely, personal communication).
10. **pH.** Spawning is sparse at pH 5 to 5.5 and absent at lower values. Alkalinities above 10.0 to 11.0 appear toxic to young.

Light effects were not formally studied, though bass spawned earlier in clear water, sometimes 30 days earlier than in muddy ponds at Auburn.

**Timing.** Bluegills mature eggs in about one month above 80° F and two to three months at 65° to 75° F, so their spawning reflects conditions one to three months earlier. Largemouth bass eggs begin developing in September or October and are laid the following April, so bass reproduction depends on fall, winter and spring conditions.

## Key Findings

### 1. Pond analysis (seining key)

Pond analysis is recommended for ponds in their second summer or older, when populations have reached equilibrium with food. Seining is done June to October in Alabama. Equipment is a 15-foot, 6- or 8-mesh minnow seine and a 50-foot, 2-mesh seine (given as 0.5-inch mesh in the summary). The method was checked over 16 years against catch records and drained-pond counts. The paper finds it accurate and usable without destroying the population.

The key contains these categories:

- **I. No young bass present:**
  - **A.** Many recent bluegill hatches, few intermediate bluegills. *Temporary balance, bass crowded.* Bluegills large and in good condition; bass small and poor (typically under 1 lb).
  - **B.** No recent bluegill hatch, many intermediate bluegills. *Unbalanced, overcrowded bluegills.* Fifty-foot seine often exceeds 35 intermediate bluegills per quadrant, sometimes hundreds. Bass large (over 1.5 lb).
  - **C.** As B, with many tadpoles, minnows or crayfish. *Unbalanced, very few bass.*
  - **D.** No recent hatch, few intermediate bluegills. *Crowding by competitors* such as bullheads, crappies, suckers or shad.
  - **E.** No recent hatch, few intermediate bluegills, many intermediate [[Green Sunfish]]. *Unbalanced, green sunfish crowding*, common when green sunfish invade before bass are stocked.
  - **F.** No recent hatch, no intermediate bluegills. *Unbalanced or possibly no fish*, often from cold, saline or silty water.

- **II. Young bass present:**
  - **A.** Many recent bluegill hatches, few intermediate bluegills. *Balanced population.* The 50-foot seine typically yields 5 to 30 intermediate bluegills per haul. Best ponds average 10 to 18 per haul with heavy recent reproduction. Adequate bass: about 1 per 3 minnow-seine hauls in early summer, or 2 per 50-foot haul late summer.
  - **B.** Many recent bluegill hatches, very few or no intermediate bluegills. *Balanced but bass-crowded*, often desirable for bluegill fishing.
  - **C.** No recent hatch of bluegills, no intermediate bluegills. *Unbalanced*, uncommon; caused by water too cold for bluegills (below 80° F) or slight brackishness.
  - **D.** No recent hatch of bluegills, few intermediate bluegills. *Temporary balance* with a risk of unbalance, from competitors such as bullheads or shad, or reduced food.

### 2. Catch-based assessment

Catch data alone cannot measure balance without known fertility and effort. Balanced unfertilized Auburn ponds averaged about 20 lb/acre per year. Balanced fertilized ponds yielded 150 to 200 lb/acre with adequate effort, and unbalanced ponds 19 to 100 lb/acre. Restricted-fishing balanced ponds yielded 50 to 150 lb/acre, so yield does not reflect capacity. Fishermen's catches still distinguish states:

- **Unbalanced:** mostly 3- to 5-inch bluegills; bluegills no longer bite and are not found on beds; bass are few but over 2 lb.
- **Balanced:** most bluegills above the 6-inch group; bass averaging 1 to 2 lb; bluegills on beds several times a year.
- **Bass-crowded:** bluegills averaging over 0.38 lb, found on beds; bass under 1 lb in poor condition.

Ponds open to the public were easy to classify, since almost no anglers fished unbalanced ponds.

### 3. Population values and ratios

Data came from draining 85 ponds and poisoning four. Six population values were derived; two are highlighted:

- **F/C ratio:** pounds of forage fish (bluegills) per pound of piscivores (bass). Balanced and unbalanced ponds occur up to F/C = 10, and higher ratios occur only in unbalanced ponds. The most efficient balanced ponds fall within a range printed as "8 to 6" (apparently a typographical error), and crowded-bass ponds were 1.4 to 2.0.
- **Ar value:** percent of total weight in harvestable fish. Below 33 is unbalanced; 33 to 40 is inefficient borderline; above 40 is balanced; 60 to 85 is most desirable; and 80 to 90 indicates overcrowded bass.

These values are most useful as a post-mortem after draining or total poisoning, and they checked pond-analysis conclusions and experimental stocking and thinning results.

### 4. Subsampling accuracy

Subsamples from rotenone, seining or electrofishing cannot be direct replicas of the whole population. Pond analysis succeeds because it interprets sample composition in light of the conditions that would produce it.

- **Example 1 (2-acre pond, bluegill, warmouth, chain pickerel):** rotenone and seine samples both found an unbalanced, bluegill-crowded pond lacking predators. Estimated Ar was 12.4 (rotenone, 0.14 acre) and 20.6 (seine, 0.135 acre), against 5.7 for the total population (411.9 lb/acre total). All methods agreed on the diagnosis, but pounds per acre were the most inaccurate estimate.
- **Example 2 (22-acre pond, bluegill, shellcracker, green sunfish, bass):** after marginal rotenone thinning reduced intermediate bluegills to 18 per seine haul, two 1-acre samples and a full drain were compared. Total population was 94.7 lb/acre with F/C 39.7 and Ar 14.2. Sample areas represented 7 and 9 percent of the pond, so pounds per acre were underestimated and bass estimates varied widely, though the sampling still gave a usable balance check.

### 5. Marking and recovery

Marking and recovery estimates were inaccurate, and their assumptions are rarely met. Marked fish must suffer no higher mortality, which is practically impossible with bluegill capture, and must mix randomly. Table 2 compares estimates with actual counts in Auburn ponds:

- Bluegills, 1.5-acre pond: 15,685 actual, estimated at 44.2% of actual.
- Round flier, 1.5-acre pond: 265 actual, estimated at 223.7%.
- Goldfish (four small ponds of 0.008 to 0.05 acre): estimates from 32.6% to 68.7% of actual.
- Hybrid bluegill × green sunfish, 0.008-acre pond: 15 actual, estimated at 134.7%.

The paper calls this a high-priority research need.

### 6. Repressive factor and discussion

The repressive factor is an open question. Swingle could not identify the chemical and said a protein chemist might resolve it. Carp and buffalo reproduction was enhanced by fresh-water inflow after drawdown. Conference discussion (Wiese, Bennett, Everhart, Van Maren, Carlander, Coope, Krumholz) raised impoundment drawdown management for carp control, the reliability of voluntary and selective creel censuses, the comparability of Schnabel and Schumacher–Eschmeyer population estimates (Coope found little difference across 20 to 30 Michigan estimates), and error in age reading from scales. Swingle noted that beans and no fish gave estimates 30 to 50 percent of the actual drained stock.

### 7. Management implication

Balance is necessary for continued efficient production and good fishing. Once balance is determined, managers can apply corrective measures such as thinning, fertilization, stocking adjustments and water-level control to maintain high production.
