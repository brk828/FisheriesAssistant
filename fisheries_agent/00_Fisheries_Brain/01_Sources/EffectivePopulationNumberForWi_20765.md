# Effective Population Number for Winter Run Chinook Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #population-genetics #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document presents a theoretical framework for calculating genetically effective population size (Ne) in [[Winter Run Chinook Salmon]] populations that include both wild and hatchery-supplemented components. The analysis demonstrates that hatchery supplementation can either increase or decrease overall effective population size depending on the relative sizes and proportions of wild versus captive populations. When wild populations are small (New = 85), hatchery supplementation with adequate breeding numbers (Nec = 54) can increase effective population size. However, when wild populations are larger (New = 500) and captive proportions are high, effective population size may be reduced to less than 20% of the wild-only scenario despite doubling total fish numbers.

## Background & Description

The document addresses the critical issue of genetic variation in salmon populations with low numbers, particularly when both wild reproduction and hatchery supplementation occur. The genetically effective population size must account for contributions from both sources using the formula:

1/Ne = xw²/New + xc²/Nec

where xw and xc represent proportions of fish migrating downstream from wild and captive sources respectively (xw + xc = 1).

The effective population size differs from census population size due to several factors:
- Unequal sex ratios among breeding fish
- Variance in reproductive success among individuals
- Mean progeny number per individual

For populations with different numbers of breeding males and females:
Nec = 4NefNem / (Nef + Nem)

The effective numbers for each sex depend on actual numbers and variance in progeny production:
Nef = (Nfkf - 1) / [kf + (Skf²/kf) - 1]
Nem = (Nmkm - 1) / [km + (Skm²/km) - 1]

where k represents mean progeny number and s² represents sample variance in progeny number.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The analysis focuses on [[Winter Run Chinook Salmon]], an anadromous species requiring both freshwater spawning habitat and marine rearing environments. The population structure involves multiple cohorts, with the document noting that total effective size would be approximately three times the single-year estimate if three cohorts are similar in size.

The document references estimates from [[Bartley et al.]] (1992) suggesting wild effective population size around 4% of census numbers, while [[Waples]]' general rule suggests 25% of breeding numbers as a reasonable estimate. The 1991 captive effective population size was estimated at Nec = 4, with a best-case 1992 estimate of Nec = 54.

The ecological context emphasizes the dual management goals of:
1. Reducing extinction probability through increased total population numbers
2. Maintaining genetic variation for long-term population viability

## Key Findings

**Modeling Results for Small Wild Populations (New = 85):**
- When captive effective population size is adequate (Nec = 54), hatchery supplementation can substantially increase overall effective population size
- Enhancement provides genetic benefits across a substantial range of captive contribution proportions
- This scenario demonstrates that hatchery programs can improve both demographic and genetic population parameters simultaneously

**Modeling Results for Larger Wild Populations (New = 500):**
- High captive proportions with moderate hatchery effective sizes greatly reduce overall effective population size
- Example: With Nec = 20 and xc = 0.5, effective population size drops below 100 (less than 20% of wild-only scenario)
- Total fish numbers double through supplementation (reducing extinction risk), but genetic effective size is severely compromised
- This represents a fundamental trade-off between demographic and genetic conservation goals

**Critical Assumptions Identified:**
1. Effective population size of captive-released individuals must be known (estimable from actual matings and release proportions)
2. Effective population size of wild population must be known (estimates range from 4% to 25% of breeding numbers)
3. Proportions of captive and wild fish returning to spawn must be known
4. If return proportions unknown, equal survival to spawning must be assumed
5. Random mating between captive and wild fish (no assortative mating by origin)
6. Equal fecundity and spawning timing between captive and wild fish, ensuring equal early survival

**Management Implications:**
- Hatchery supplementation effectiveness depends critically on maintaining adequate breeding numbers in captivity
- Small hatchery breeding programs (Nec = 4 to 20) can severely reduce genetic effective population size even while increasing census numbers
- The relationship between wild population size, hatchery contribution, and overall genetic health is non-linear and requires careful quantitative assessment
- Population enhancement strategies must balance extinction risk reduction against genetic variation maintenance
