# Aravaipa Creek Fishes: October 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #Centrarchidae #desert-streams

## Executive Summary

This report documents a resurvey of fish distribution and abundance in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a hot-desert stream in Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona, conducted 18–20 October 1985 by W.L. Minckley and Catherine A. Karp for the U.S. Bureau of Land Management and the George Whittell Trust. Ten localities were sampled across the mainstream and lower [[Virgus Canyon]], yielding 10 fish species: 7 native and 3 non-native. Notably, [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Mosquitofish]] were recorded for the first time in the Aravaipa Creek system. All seven indigenous species — considered a rare, largely intact native ichthyofauna for the region — were collected in substantial numbers, with [[Spikedace]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] unusually abundant and [[Loach Minnow]] more widespread than historically typical. Changes in fish assemblage structure since 1977-78 are attributed to sustained high stream discharge altering substrate composition, habitat structure, and thermal regime. Management recommendations emphasize non-native fish removal and expanded watershed surveys, including artificial waters (cattle tanks) that serve as sources of introduced species.

## Background & Description

The paper is a distributional/ecological survey rather than a morphological study; however, species-specific habitat and ecological (functionally morphological) associations are noted:

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*): Typically the most abundant native species; associated with sand substrate, run habitats, and warmer water. Feeds via detritivory/omnivory, indicating a generalized, non-specialized feeding morphology.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): Small-bodied cyprinid favoring pool habitats; benefits from increased pool proportion and cooler water.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): Larger-bodied native cyprinid; more abundant in reaches above and below the gorge, consistent with autumn-winter dispersal patterns; favored by greater pool habitat.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Obligate riffle-dwelling benthic cyprinid; specialized for gravel-riffle habitats; feeds on simuliid dipterans and mayflies, indicating adaptation to swift, cool, well-oxygenated riffle microhabitats.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): Distribution/abundance comparable to historical baseline; generalist benthic cyprinid.
- **[[Sonoran Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*) and **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*): Larger-bodied catostomids with subterminal/ventral mouths adapted for benthic feeding on algae/detritus (per Fisher et al. 1981 diel feeding studies); more abundant in reaches outside the gorge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aravaipa Creek functions as a hydrologically dynamic, spatially heterogeneous refuge stream supporting one of the last predominantly native fish assemblages in the hot-desert Southwest.

- **Habitat structure shifts (post-1977-78):** Sustained elevated discharge drove a substrate transition from sand-dominated to gravel-rubble, increased pool and pool-like habitat via scouring, likely increased benthic algal and invertebrate biomass on harder substrates ([[Bruns and Minckley 1980]]), and moderated temperature extremes.
- **Niche partitioning by flow/habitat preference:**
  - [[Longfin Dace]] favors sandy runs and warmer water — declined as sand substrate diminished.
  - [[Spikedace]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] favor pool habitats, cooler water, and higher invertebrate abundance — increased with habitat change.
  - [[Loach Minnow]] is a riffle-obligate specialist benefiting from improved gravel-riffle quality and cooler water.
- **Spatial distribution:** Larger native fishes ([[Roundtail Chub]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]]) were more abundant in reaches above and below [[Aravaipa Gorge]] than within it, consistent with autumn-winter dispersal patterns described by Siebert (1981).
- **Non-native fish niches:** Restricted to downstream, lentic, or artificial habitats:
  - [[Green Sunfish]] — backwaters/oxbows near Virgus Canyon mouth, likely originating from cattle-watering tanks.
  - [[Fathead Minnow]] — backwaters near an irrigation intake at Wood's Ranch; likely introduced via bait-bucket transfer or tank stocking.
  - [[Mosquitofish]] — shoreline vegetation (watercress beds) near the lower stream reach; possibly derived from a horse-watering trough stocked for mosquito control, or immigration from the San Pedro River where the species is well-established.
- **Watershed connectivity:** Sporadic aquatic connections between Aravaipa Creek and the [[San Pedro River]] may facilitate non-native fish immigration, historically limited by intermittent flooding-driven isolation (1964–1977-78).
- **Disturbance ecology:** Native fish assemblage has demonstrated statistical stability since 1943 despite periodic flash flooding, which has historically proven insurmountable for non-native fish establishment ([[Minckley and Meffe 1986]]).

## Key Findings

- Ten fish species were recorded across 10 localities in October 1985: 7 native, 3 non-native.
- **First system records** of [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) in Aravaipa Creek.
- All seven native species remained present in substantial numbers, reaffirming long-term faunal stability since 1943.
- [[Spikedace]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] were unusually abundant; [[Loach Minnow]] was more widespread than historically typical.
- [[Longfin Dace]], normally the most abundant native species, was comparatively scarce during this survey.
- [[Speckled Dace]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], and [[Desert Sucker]] abundances were consistent with historical baselines.
- Non-native species ([[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Mosquitofish]]) were restricted to downstream, lentic/backwater habitats and considered established as small reproducing populations, though not abundant.
- Long-term high discharge since 1977-78 is implicated in substrate coarsening (sand → gravel-rubble), increased pool habitat, likely increased benthic productivity, and moderated thermal extremes — favoring pool- and riffle-associated natives over sand/warm-water specialists like longfin dace.
- Cattle-watering tanks in the watershed (e.g., Virgus Canyon drainage) are identified as probable sources of recurring non-native fish introductions via flood-mediated transport.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Conduct an in-depth watershed resurvey, including helicopter-based reconnaissance of remote cattle tanks and artificial waters.
  - Continue on-foot surveys of mainstream and tributaries due to inaccessibility.
  - Establish a long-term abundance/distribution database (from 1964 onward) for future dispersion analyses.
  - Actively remove and exclude non-native fishes using ichthyotoxins, explosives, or water removal, timed to droughts/low-flow periods to minimize non-target impacts.
  - Evaluate and mitigate impacts of removal efforts on species of special concern (e.g., [[Black Hawk]], *Buteogallus anthracinus*).
  - Pursue cooperative management among private, state, and federal stakeholders for long-term native fish perpetuation.
