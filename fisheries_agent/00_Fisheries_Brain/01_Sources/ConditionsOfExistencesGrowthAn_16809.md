# Brook Trout Survival, Growth and Food Depletion in a High-Altitude Sierra Nevada Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by Norman Reimers (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Reno, Nevada) follows a population of [[Eastern Brook Trout]] introduced into [[Bunny Lake]], a fishless, 24-acre snow-fed cirque lake at 10,900 ft in the [[Convict Creek Basin]], [[Mono County]], California. About 1,790 fingerlings were stocked in August 1951 and the population was monitored through summer 1957. The trout overgrazed their food base, which was dominated by [[Midge Larvae]] and other benthic organisms, and grew poorly. No natural reproduction occurred. The author concludes that small alpine lakes can be quickly depleted by overpopulation and may take years to recover.

## Background & Description

- **Study motivation:** Brook trout are stocked in alpine lakes on a population-sustaining basis because they can reproduce by spawning on spring-fed lake bottoms rather than requiring streams. The study asked how well brook trout survive and how long they live in poor, high-altitude habitat.
- **Study lake:** Bunny Lake was barren of fish before stocking, and so was excluded from the 1950–51 survey of ten other basin lakes ([[Reimers, Maciolek and Pister]], 1955). Angling pressure was assumed to be minimal because the lake is remote from trails.
- **Project:** Directed by Reed S. Nielson of the California-Nevada Sport Fishery Investigations; staff included [[John A. Maciolek]] and [[Harry D. Kennedy]]. Trout were supplied by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]].
- **Physical setting:** The lake is bounded by granite crest and talus, with a snowfield on a north-facing slope that keeps the lake filled through summer. Vegetation is sparse.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality**
- Water was highly transparent; a 10 cm Secchi disk was visible at maximum depth.
- Total dissolved solids were 8.2 ppm in 1951 and 5.6 ppm in 1953. Water approaches chemical purity because of short contact with poorly soluble [[Silicate Bedrock]] (quartz monzonite and granodiorite).
- pH ranged from 5.8 to 6.3.
- Dissolved oxygen ranged from 8.0 to 9.7 ppm, above altitude-corrected saturation, indicating supersaturation throughout the lake.
- Temperatures were stratified in July but almost uniform (44.2–44.6°F) on 29 August 1957 after wind mixing and the onset of autumn cooling.

**Primary production**
- Phytoplankton was scanty (diatoms, green and blue-green algae). Most algal production was periphytic on rocks and the bottom.
- Attached algae accumulated at 2.98 mg dry weight per square decimeter over 25 days. This was within the range of other high lakes in the area, so the lake's microflora was not exceptionally poor.

**Zooplankton (1951)**
- [[Daphnia Pulex]] and [[Diaptomus Signicauda]] were the only prominent species.
- Estimated densities were 10–30 individuals per cubic foot in calm weather. *Diaptomus* was about five times as abundant as *Daphnia* when both were near the surface.

**Insects and benthos**
- In July and August 1951, mayfly nymphs, caddisfly larvae and dytiscid beetles were abundant in shoal areas, at an average of 16 visible insects per square foot across 18 rocks.
- 107 Ekman dredge samples were taken in 1951, 1952, 1953 and 1957. Midges (Tendipedidae) dominated, with [[Pisidium]] clams and [[Aquatic Oligochaetes]] as the next most common groups.
- In 1951 the bottom fauna averaged 245 organisms per square foot, slightly above the basin average of 217 (range 83–434). Volume was far lower (0.532 cc vs. 1.73 cc basin average) because midge larvae were small.

## Key Findings

**1. Rapid depletion of food organisms**
- Surface insects (mayflies, caddisflies, beetles) were gone by August 1952, one year after stocking. The author judges that they were eaten before winter freeze-up.
- No free-swimming or bottom-foraging insects were found in 1952 or later.
- Larger zooplankton (*Daphnia* and *Diaptomus*) were scarce by 1952 and were absent in later years except as occasional immature stages.
- Benthic abundance and volume declined steadily. The Summary reports benthos at less than one-third of original numbers within two years and less than one-sixth within six years. The Discussion gives "less than one-fourth" for the eventual decline, so the two figures should be read with that discrepancy in mind.

**2. Diet**
- 40 trout stomachs were examined (20 from 1952, 20 from 1957). None were empty, and the diet consisted almost entirely of immature midges.
- Mollusks (Pisidium) occurred in 6 of 20 stomachs in 1952 and 2 of 20 in 1957.
- Free-swimming water mites were present in trace amounts in 1952 and absent in 1957.
- Sand and detritus appeared in 15 of 20 stomachs in 1957, up from none in 1952. The author interprets this as trout foraging deeper into the substrate for the diminishing midge larvae.

**3. Growth and condition**
- Growth was near normal in the first year and poor thereafter. Total length increased by 9.7 cm (3.8 in) over six years, and more than half of this came in the first year.
- Mean length went from 6.6 cm (2.6 in, 4.2 g) in August 1951 to 13.3 cm (5.2 in) by August 1957. Growth was 2.1 inches in year one and 1.7 inches in the following five years.
- Mean condition factor (K) fell from 1.099 in 1951 to 0.779 in the final sample, showing a steady loss of weight relative to length.

**4. Aging**
- Scales were reliable age indicators only for age groups I and II. Later annuli could not be read.
- A rainbow trout that had been in the lake for years developed a clear annulus for each winter, which confirmed the scale method in normal growth conditions.

**5. Reproduction and maturity**
- No trout eggs, young fish or spawning activity were found in six years.
- Fish were immature in 1952, and several males and females were maturing or nearly mature in 1954. Only a few females were ripe in 1957, with small numbers of eggs, suggesting poor nutrition.
- Males were highly coloured and advancing in gonadal development from 1954 onward.

**6. Survival and longevity**
- Six years of sampling removed 319 trout (17.8% of the original stock). Natural mortality is unknown but was judged moderate.
- Stocking density was just over 700 fingerlings per acre. The author suggests that about 100 per acre might have been sufficient to avoid overpopulation.
- Trout were nearly seven years old (including hatching and rearing) in August 1957. The author suggests that this longevity is not unusual but goes unobserved in heavily fished lakes.
- In [[Castle Lake]], California, [[Wales and German]] (1956) found that brook trout lived no beyond three years in the presence of large predatory trout, but reached seven years after predator removal.

**7. Management conclusions**
- Brook trout can run into food shortages quickly in small, lightly fished, rocky-basin lakes and can persist for years on very short rations.
- In lakes where reproduction is successful, apparent declines in catch and size may reflect food limitation rather than a need for restocking. Leaving the lake alone or removing some fish may be the safer option.
- Full recovery may require removing all fish, a rest period, and then very light stocking.
