# Radio Telemetry Monitoring of Stocked Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*) in the Green River, Utah: 1988 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This 1988 annual report from the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]] documents the first year of a radio telemetry study of hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) stocked into the [[Green River]] in Utah. Of 39 fish released in 1988, 33 died and 4 survived. Radio contact was maintained with 37 of the 39 fish to their ultimate location. The study tested several [[Pre-Release Procedures]] (holding time before and after surgery, transport source, and hold site), and the results were inconclusive. The authors concluded that, under the conditions tested, survival was expected to be low. They planned changes for 1989, including longer acclimation and recovery periods and a larger release.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub is a federally listed endangered fish, and the study was designed to support its [[Recovery Plan]]. The report's stated objectives were to:

1. Monitor general behavior and response of stocked fish.
2. Determine diel (daily) periodicity of movements and activity.
3. Identify habitat preferences and their relationship to stream flows and fluctuations.
4. Monitor survival of the fish.
5. Identify and record locations where larger fish could be stocked.

The fish were seven-year-old adults reared at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, where they were held in earthen ponds. Hatchery staff seined and selected large individuals for the study. Later releases used fish from the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (OWR) holding facility and a separate Ouray stock.

**Transmitters and surgery**
- Two types of radio transmitters were used:
  - *[[External Antenna Transmitter]]*: the more frequently used type, a cylindrical unit weighing 109 g in air.
  - *[[Internal Antenna Transmitter]]*: a padlock-shaped unit from a second manufacturer, also used.
- Fish were anaesthetized in an anaesthetic solution, with gills irrigated during surgery. Implantation followed the [[Shielded-Needle Technique]] of Ross and Klein (1982), with two modifications: the incision was made slightly lateral to the mid-ventral line, and the antenna exit hole was not sutured.
- Each fish was measured, weighed, and tagged with a large orange Carlin tag.

**Tracking methods**
- Fish were tracked mainly by boat, canoe, or raft, with a scanning receiver and a directional loop antenna used to pinpoint locations.
- *General tracking* covered locating fish, triangulating, and recording habitat type (classified under the USFWS [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] scheme), depth, substrate, and velocity.
- *Extended session tracks* lasted 12 hours (nocturnal), 24 hours, or through crepuscular periods, to measure periodicity and habitat preference.
- Triangulation intervals ranged from every half hour to every hour.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Release schedule and sample sizes (1988)**

| Release | Number of fish | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| March 22 | 10 (11 transported) | Released at Island Park; all died within two days |
| May 18 | 4 (2 survived) | Fresh from Dexter; short hold before and after surgery |
| June 18 | 9 | Three treatment groups (A, B, C) with different hold times |
| July 28–29 (Ouray) | 5 (2 survived) | Held at OWR for 46 to 76 days |
| July 28–29 (Dexter) | 9 | Fresh from Dexter; one died at 26 days |

Note: the report lists the March release as n=10 for movement analysis, and the June release as n=9 for movement analysis.

**Pre-release procedures**
- Fish were held for different periods before and after surgery, mostly at the OWR facility (a holding facility with circulating groundwater). Fish were never observed to feed while held at OWR.
- The June groups were: A (3 fish, surgery, held 36 days at Dexter), B (4 fish, held 20 days at OWR, surgery, held 14 more days), and C (3 fish, held 34 days at OWR, then surgery and release the next day). Group averages for longevity are reported only as a combined figure of 12.9 days.
- The authors concluded the May procedure (short hold, fresh fish) appeared to be the best direction, but the July (Dexter) results rejected it as an optimum procedure.

**Habitat and flow**
- Hydrologic context: flows during the summer of 1988 are noted for the Chew Bridge and Island/Rainbow Park areas, with the flow decline coinciding with fish departure from some sites.
- The Split Mountain Canyon section of the river (about 9 miles of high-gradient river) was a suspected mortality factor. The authors tested this by releasing a small group of July fish below the canyon at Chew Bridge; three of these four also died.

## Key Findings

**1. Survival was low.** Of 39 fish released, 33 died and 4 survived. Fish that died lived an average of 12.2 days post-release. All March fish died within two days, and the authors attributed the mortality in part to thermal shock and handling stress.

**2. Movement was largely downstream.** The March fish were located only by passive drift, with a maximum drift of 19.5 miles and a minimum of 0.5 miles (three fish). Fish that died often showed major downstream movement, which the authors interpreted either as stress-related or as behavioral. Three of the four surviving fish made major downstream moves of 8.8 to 30.8 miles, about three weeks after release for May fish and about four weeks for July fish.

**3. Habitat use.** Of 241 daytime contacts (May, June, and July releases, 27 fish), 174 yielded habitat data. Of those, 102 were in main-channel habitat. Runs and eddies were the most frequently used secondary habitats. Fish were often seen in a transitional "eddy-fence" zone between run and eddy habitat. The authors cautioned that results were strongly influenced by two surviving individuals.

**4. Activity periods.** Activity was greatest during nocturnal hours (approximately 1900–0559). The authors also believed that crepuscular and early nighttime hours were the most active periods; this was to be analyzed in the 1989 report.

**5. Visual sightings.** Three bonytails were seen visually. Two May-release fish were seen 3 and 15 days after release. Both were within three weeks of their last contact.

**6. Survivor behavior.** Fish #598 (released at Island Park, July 29) survived 108 days, last contacted November 14. It showed the least overall movement of the four survivors and was largely sedentary from October onward.

**7. Pen study (mortality investigation).** Thirteen fish (too small for transmitters) were held in a backwater pen at RM 213.3 from September 8 to December 6, 1988 (90 days). Five of 13 died, with fungal growth, abrasion, and cold temperatures (5–6 °C by early November) implicated. Fish placed in a flow-through pen had badly abraded fins and died more often. Transmitters in the carcasses were found to be in the body cavity, and no transmitter had migrated toward the antenna exit hole.

**8. Conclusions and 1989 plan.** The authors concluded that the 1988 results indicated little more than a low expected survival rate under these conditions, and that the mixed pre-release procedures made it hard to choose a best procedure. The 1989 plan includes:
- Holding fish in the Green River (in suitable low-flow habitat) instead of at OWR.
- Holding fish at least one week before implantation and one week after surgery.
- Increasing the number of bonytails to be released from 35 to 59.
- Adding internal antenna transmitters, which were developed and field-tested in 1988.
- A larger, more equal release at Chew Bridge to test whether Split Mountain Canyon is the cause of mortality.
