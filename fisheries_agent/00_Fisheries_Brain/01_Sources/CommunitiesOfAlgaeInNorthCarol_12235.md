# Communities of Algae in North Carolina Streams and their Seasonal Relations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phycology #freshwater-algae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by [[L. A. Whitford]] and [[G. J. Schumacher]], published in *Hydrobiologia* (Vol. XXII), reports a four-year study (begun August 1957; main work 1958–1960, with plankton counts in 1960–1961) of algal communities in the streams and rivers of North Carolina. The state spans three physiographic regions, the [[Coastal Plain]], [[Piedmont]] and [[Mountains]], and the authors describe benthic (aufwuchs) and planktonic algae across brooks, creeks, springs, seeps and eleven rivers. The central conclusions are that stream floras are dominated by diatoms (Bacillariophyceae) and Chlorophyceae, that community composition shifts with season largely through water temperature, and that river plankton is not characterised by distinctive species but by a "Melosira-chlorophycean community."

Note: the source is an algal ecology paper. It contains no fish data, so the species, family and habitat fields above reflect the general aquatic setting rather than a single taxon.

## Background & Description

**Scope and prior work.** The authors note that stream algae had been studied far less than lentic (standing-water) algae. Earlier European work included [[Butcher]] (1932–1946) and [[Fritsch]] (1929, 1931), and in the United States the classic plankton studies of [[Kofoid]] (1903, 1908) on the Illinois River. Only three prior US studies had included benthic algae, the most notable being [[Blum]] (1954–1957) on the Saline River, Michigan.

**Study area.** North Carolina extends from the Atlantic more than 1,000 km west to the Smoky Mountains, near 36° N latitude. Its three physiographic regions are:
- Coastal Plain: recent to Mesozoic sands, loams and clays.
- Piedmont: low rolling plateau of red clays or sandy loams (Proterozoic).
- Mountains: Proterozoic rocks.

Two main stream systems occur: mountain-origin streams flowing west/north or southeast through the Piedmont, and Piedmont-origin streams flowing southeast through the Coastal Plain. A third group rises in the Coastal Plain and carries brown, swamp-derived water. Mountain waters are clear, acid and low in minerals; Piedmont waters are yellowish with colloidal clay and circum-neutral; Coastal Plain waters are brownish and acid near the coast.

**Methods.** Sampling used stations at rapids, reaches and pools, with transects across streams. Glass microslides were "planted" in the current following [[Butcher]]'s method, and a cheesecloth pledget was used on rock surfaces. River plankton was sampled with a net (qualitatively) and, in 1960–1961, quantitatively using both the [[Sedgwick-Rafter Method]] and a modified [[Millipore Membrane Method]] (after McNabb, 1960), with Safranin and Malachite green staining. Water temperature and pH were recorded at most collections, along with light, current speed, dissolved oxygen (Winkler test) and free CO2. A 6 × 6 inch Ekman dredge was used in deep water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** Winters are mild and frost is short-lived, so there is rarely a complete spring scouring of the bed. Water temperatures in the Piedmont and Coastal Plain are generally 5–25 °C, and summer temperatures in the mountains rarely exceed 20 °C. Coastal Plain brooks have low gradients over sand or muck, and many go dry in late summer. Piedmont rivers carry a large clay and silt load from agricultural runoff and remain somewhat muddy year-round.

**Springs and seeps.** Permanent springs are rare and have a uniform flora, with light as the main variable. The most common spring green algae are *Stigeoclonium*, *Mougeotia* and *Microspora*. Seeps receive the most insolation of any habitat and support desmids such as *Closterium* and *Staurastrum*.

**Light.** In Piedmont brooks, incident light is highest in late winter (about 3,000 ft-candles, roughly 32,000 lux, on bright days). After the canopy closes around May 10 it falls to about 100–500 ft-candles. In deep shade the standing crop was estimated to fall by 90–95%.

**Current effect.** The authors argue that current speeds above about 15 cm/sec increase material exchange between attached algae and water, which explains the higher productivity of rapids relative to pools. This is the "current effect."

**Temperature thresholds.** For many freshwater algae, 15 °C is a critical temperature. Winter and early-spring species decline above it. Species were grouped as growing best below 10 °C, below 15 °C, between 15 and 20 °C, or above 20 °C.

**Water quality.** North Carolina streams are low in minerals and near neutral (oligotrophic). Brown-water Coastal Plain streams have pH 5.2–6.5, Mountain streams pH 6.0–6.5, and Piedmont streams pH 6.5–7.0. The authors note an absence of hard-water indicators such as *Cladophora*, *Chara* and *Hydrodictyon*.

**Reservoirs.** Impoundments decrease turbidity and age the water, which raises plankton numbers in the river below them. Numbers fall rapidly downstream. Ceratium hirundinella is one species that cannot persist in the flowing river.

## Key Findings

**Rivers and plankton (summarised by river):**
- **[[Neuse River]]:** Summer plankton is led by *Melosira granulata* var. *angustissima*, and the Chlorophyceae are the most diverse of any river in the state. June 1960 counts averaged 338 diatoms/cc, of which 64% was *Cyclotella meneghiniana*. Greens reached about 245/cc.
- **[[Roanoke River]]:** Highest total phytoplankton of any river. June 1960 counts averaged 384 diatoms/cc. Cyanophyceae (*Anacystis cyanea*, *Coelosphaerium naegelianum*) sometimes equal the diatoms in number. The river is somewhat eutrophic.
- **[[Tar River]]:** Generally sparser plankton than the Neuse or Roanoke. Diatoms averaged about 100/cc in summer counts.
- **[[Cape Fear River]]:** Almost entirely Coastal Plain (about 250 km). Tidal for nearly 100 km, with marine Centrales carried at least 75 km upstream.
- **[[Yadkin River]]:** *Melosira granulata* var. *angustissima* dominates year-round. Dam-affected stations show higher abundance of *Anabaena* and *Aphanizomenon*.
- **[[Catawba River]]:** Plankton most resembles the Yadkin. Asterionella formosa was very abundant in March. A new North American record was *Pseudotetraedron neglectum*, and about 25% of *Asterionella* colonies were parasitised by the chytrid *Rhizophydium planktonicum*.
- **Slow brown-water Coastal Plain rivers** (including the [[Chowan River]]): sparse flora, with *Eudorina elegans* common in summer and an almost complete absence of Cyanophyceae.
- **Mountain rivers** (French Broad, New, Watauga, Tuckasegee and others): essentially tychoplankton, not a true plankton community, because of rapid flow and shade.

**Stream communities:**
- **Piedmont brook:** Succession runs from *Tetraspora lubrica* and *Draparnaldia* in late winter, to *Batrachospermum boryanum* in April, *Mougeotia* and *Spirogyra* in sunny reaches, and autumn re-emergence of *Stigeoclonium* and *Microspora*. December has the least algal abundance.
- **[[House Creek]]:** Typical small Piedmont creek of under 9 km. Its characteristic spring aufwuchs is a *Phormidium subfuscum* mat with *Navicula rhynchocephala* and *Synedra ulna*, followed by *Oedogonium kurzii* in May. A warm late-April rain can strip the mat within hours.
- **Coastal Plain creeks:** The most species-rich aufwuchs was observed in clear-water creeks, with nearly 30 species named. Diatoms are pioneer, with *Eunotia pectinalis* sometimes making up 25% of the spring flora in brown-water brooks.
- **Rapid brown-water creeks (sandhill region):** Only three well-defined examples were found. Their flora is stable through the summer and dominated by *Batrachospermum macrosporum*.

**Springs and seeps:**
- Permanent springs show the same flora year-round, controlled mainly by light.
- No distinctive spring species were identified, apart from an unidentified *Spirogyra*.
- Seeps support many desmids and *Roya*, which the authors found only in seeps.

**Community synthesis and conclusions:**
- Diatoms are the most important group in most communities, with Chlorophyceae second.
- River plankton is not distinctive at species level, which the authors attribute to "polymixia" (Kofoid, 1908).
- The main river plankton community is designated the *Melosira*-chlorophycean community.
- Community change with season is related mainly to water temperature. Light matters for some species.
- The authors could not define stable, named communities in the way vascular plant ecologists do. They suggest that North Carolina's low-mineral, near-neutral (oligotrophic) waters produce more floristic variation than hard-water regions.
- The **[[Millipore Membrane Method]]** gave consistently higher numbers than **[[Sedgwick-Rafter Method]]** counts (for example, twice as many individuals for *Melosira granulata* var. *angustissima* and *Synedra ulna*). It is better for nannoplankton, while Sedgwick-Rafter is better for flagellates and larger species.
