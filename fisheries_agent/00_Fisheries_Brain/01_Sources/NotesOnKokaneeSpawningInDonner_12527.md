# Notes on Kokanee Spawning in Donner Lake, California, 1949

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oncorhynchus-nerka

## Executive Summary

Field observations from the fall of 1949 examined whether [[Kokanee]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi*) spawning on the shore of [[Donner Lake]], Nevada County, California, could produce viable offspring. Spawning began in early November 1949. Nests were exposed by a severe drawdown in December, and most eggs in exposed nests were repeatedly frozen. Eggs in spring-fed [[Seepage Areas]] stayed unfrozen, developed normally, and hatched. Eggs from frozen nests also hatched after flooding. Fry reared at the [[Tahoe Hatchery]] were normal. The author concluded that natural reproduction could succeed on a large scale if lake water levels were stabilized during the spawning and development period.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Kokanee had been seen spawning along the shore of Donner Lake in 1946, 1947, 1948, and 1949. Before this work, it was unknown whether their eggs developed to hatching. Earlier assumptions held that natural reproduction was virtually impossible because the lake was drawn down in January and February, leaving nests dry and frozen.
- **Prior observations:** [[J. C. Fraser]] observed shore spawning in November 1947 (Curtis and Fraser, 1948). [[A. F. Pollitt]] of the Tahoe Hatchery noted dry, frozen nests in 1946.
- **Hatchery incident (December 4, 1947):** 5,190 eggs at 230 per ounce were taken from Donner Lake kokanee and held at Tahoe Hatchery. After an accidental water shutoff during a cold spell, eggs that appeared dead were re-watered. They thawed, swelled, developed, and hatched with only normal loss. The author noted that these observations came from hatchery employees and were not formally recorded.
- **Comparison form:** The anadromous red salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka nerka*) was known to spawn successfully on lake shores, but little was documented about this habit in kokanee.
- **Study site:** The main observation area lay off the end of Waggoner's boat pier on the north shore, a few hundred feet from the highway. The shore slopes gently for about 100 feet, then drops to 30 to 50 feet. The surveyed section was about 100 yards long, with sand, gravel, and cobbles over clay, and about one quarter of it lay in a spring seepage area.
- **Author and affiliation:** [[J. B. Kimsey]], Bureau of Fish Conservation, California Division of Fish and Game. Published July 1951 (Vol. 37, No. 3), submitted January 1951.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning temperatures:** First kokanee were seen on November 4, 1949, at a surface water temperature of 51°F. Spawning fish were mostly males, with much fighting and splashing.
- **Lake drawdown:** Water levels fell sharply in December and January, exposing nests. The lake was drawn down until all nests were uncovered by December 23. Warm rains in January raised the lake about one foot, which flooded the lowest nests. On January 25, all nests were flooded again.
- **Nest microhabitat:** Eggs were buried under four to seven inches of gravel. Capillary water kept shallow nests moist even when the surface was dry. Seepage nests sat in slowly flowing spring water at 44 to 48°F, while lake-bottom nests were exposed to air and freezing.
- **Freezing conditions:** On December 12, the top three inches of nests were frozen. On December 23, the top five inches were frozen. On December 30, exposed eggs were frozen so hard they clinked like pebbles. Ice on the lake reached six to eight inches by February 16, with water beneath it at 34°F.
- **Stream spawning below the dam:** Donner Creek held kokanee that appeared to be larger (estimated 18 to 20 inches, one about 24 inches) than lake fish. These fish were battered and weak, and they were not laying eggs by December. Stream temperature was 46°F on December 12 and 39°F on December 30.
- **Predation/scavenging:** No dead fish were found on the shore on December 23. Coyote tracks suggested that coyotes may have removed carcasses.

## Key Findings

1. **Spawning onset:** Kokanee in Donner Lake began spawning around November 4, 1949, at 51°F surface water.
2. **Male-biased spawning run:** On November 6, a gill net caught eight males and one female. Average length was given as "184 inches" in the OCR text, most likely 18.4 inches. Average weight was about two pounds.
3. **Nest count:** Observer Joseph U. Verret marked nine nests on November 15 and 29. Nests were located by reconnaissance from November 15, 21, and 29.
4. **Drawdown exposure:** All nests were uncovered by December 23, 1949.
5. **Freezing effects:** All eggs except those in seepage areas experienced at least occasional freezing. Eggs subjected to prolonged freezing died.
6. **Seepage-area success:** Eggs in seepage nests developed normally at 44 to 48°F. Hatching began in the nests around January 20, 1950. Eggs moved to the Tahoe Hatchery began hatching in 43°F water on February 12, 1950.
7. **Frozen-nest recovery:** Eggs removed from nests with little freezing began hatching on March 15 at the hatchery. Eggs in lower nests stayed alive after flooding, even after prolonged freezing, through capillary seepage from warmer lake water.
8. **Egg survival estimate:** On January 6, 1950, 1,073 eggs were collected. Earlier removals from the same area totaled 354, for 1,427 eggs in six square feet. The nesting belt was estimated at 200 feet × 6 feet (1,200 square feet).
9. **Total egg deposition estimate:**
   - Area-based estimate: about 250,000 eggs.
   - Spawner-based estimate: about 200 fish with a 1:4 female-to-male sex ratio (about 40 females), each laying approximately 2,500 eggs, giving about 100,000 eggs.
   - Combined conclusion: between 100,000 and 250,000 eggs, most likely in the lower range.
10. **Freeze-related mortality:** An estimated 90 percent of eggs were lost to freezing.
11. **Nest location breakdown:** About 5 percent of all nests were in the seepage area, and another 5 percent were in the lower belt of frozen nests that continued developing despite freezing.
12. **Hatchery fry quality:** Fry from Donner Lake nests reared at Tahoe Hatchery were normal in every respect compared with hatchery kokanee.
13. **Water-level conclusion:** The author concluded that large-scale natural reproduction of kokanee in Donner Lake would be possible if water levels could be stabilized during the spawning and development period.
