# Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by Kevin S. Cobble and dated December 1994, addresses four listed fishes of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of southeastern Arizona, New Mexico and northwestern Mexico. Two are listed as endangered: [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]]. Two are listed as threatened: [[Yaqui Catfish]] and [[Beautiful Shiner]]. Four further native species are Candidate-2 taxa and are treated in the plan as part of the wider ecosystem.

The fishes formerly occurred throughout the basin in the USA and Mexico. U.S. populations are now limited mainly to [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (SBNWR, including Leslie Canyon) and West Turkey Creek, Cochise County, Arizona. The basin is about 73,000 km², of which only about 1,500 km² (<2.0%) lies in the USA.

The main threats are water development and pumping of underground aquifers, followed closely by non-native organisms. The plan sets out 6 objectives and a 10-year, ~$3.57 million cost estimate for 1995–2004. Downlisting should be considered 10 years after approval if criteria are met.

## Background & Description

The plan covers the Rio Yaqui drainage, which begins as the Rio Papigochic in western Chihuahua, enters Sonora, receives the Rio de Bavispe, and flows south to the Golfo de California near Ciudad Obregón. Annual discharge near the mouth averages 2,800 (units as printed in the source).

**Listed taxa (status per Table 1):**

- **Beautiful shiner** (*Cyprinella formosa*): Threatened federally and in Mexico; Endangered in Arizona. Designated critical habitat. Historically in the USA only in San Bernardino Valley and the Mimbres River, New Mexico. Extirpated from SBNWR by about 1970; reintroduced in 1990.
- **Yaqui chub** (*Gila purpurea*): Endangered federally, in Arizona and in Mexico. Critical habitat designated. Recognition of *Gila eremica* restricted its known range to the USA sites of SBNWR, House Pond, and West Turkey Creek, plus the Black Draw reach in Mexico.
- **Yaqui catfish** (*Ictalurus pricei*): Threatened federally; Endangered in Arizona; Rare in Mexico. Critical habitat designated. Imperiled in Mexico by habitat loss and hybridization with non-native channel and blue catfish.
- **Yaqui topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): Endangered federally and in Arizona; Threatened in Mexico. No critical habitat designated.

**Candidate-2 taxa** (all Candidate-2 federally; Threatened in Mexico or Arizona as noted):

- Longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- Mexican stoneroller (*Campostoma ornatum*)
- Roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*)
- Yaqui sucker (*Catostomus bernardini*)

Seven of the eight fish taxa are considered imperiled by Arizona. Four of the eight have been extirpated from the USA portion of the basin.

**Morphology highlights:**

- Beautiful shiner: 36–40 lateral-line scales; breeding males orange on fins.
- Yaqui chub: thickened head and anterior body; lateral-line scales fewer than 59.
- Yaqui catfish: slender body; anal fin with 23–25 rays; barbels jet-black.
- Yaqui topminnow: males rarely over 25 mm standard length; females usually 30–45 mm; live-bearing with internal fertilization.

**Historical context:** Human use dates to the Clovis culture (>10,000 years ago). Spanish presence dates to 1694. Feral livestock were abundant by 1822, and artesian wells were built in the later 1800s. The San Bernardino Ranch was held by [[Texas John Slaughter]] (1884–1937). The Nature Conservancy purchased the ranch in 1979, and the land was transferred to USFWS in 1982 to form SBNWR.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Most basin fishes occupy low-to-intermediate-elevation warmwater creeks, [[Cienegas]] (stream-associated marshes), and moderate-to-large rivers. Mountain creeks are cold and clear, with both native and non-native trouts. Ciénegas were historically common but have been severely degraded.

**Ecology of listed species:**

- Beautiful shiner: mid-water column; adapted well to ponds at SBNWR.
- Yaqui chub: deep pools, scoured ciénega areas and undercut banks; feeds on algae, insects and detritus; matures rapidly; spawns through the warm months.
- Yaqui catfish: captured in larger rivers over gravel and sand; grows rapidly in ponds.
- Yaqui topminnow: shallow, warm, quiet waters; abundant in thermal and artesian marshes; reproduction year-round where springs moderate winter temperatures.

**Principal threats:**

1. Livestock overgrazing, stream incision (arroyo cutting), and drainage of ciénegas. [[Black Draw]] changed from a marsh in the 1850s to an arroyo 3–5 m deep by the 1960s.
2. Aquifer pumping, including high-volume pumping in Mexico that is already affecting artesian flows.
3. Non-indigenous species, notably [[Western Mosquitofish]] (first in Arizona in the 1920s; in the Rio Yaqui USA portion from 1979), [[Bullfrog]], largemouth bass, and non-native catfish.
4. Mining, including groundwater pumping for smelting that reduced habitat.

**Associated biota:** Chiricahua leopard frog and Mexican garter snake have declined locally, mainly from bullfrog predation. The San Bernardino springsnail (*Pyrgulopsis bernardina*) is limited to a single spring. The Huachuca water umbel (*Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* subsp. *recurva*) has been destroyed at several sites by dredging and flood.

**Habitat loss in the USA:** Extirpations include Astin Spring (dried in 1969), where populations of Yaqui sucker and Yaqui chub were lost. Whitewater Draw was largely dried by the 1970s and polluted near the border.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** Four fishes are listed (two endangered, two threatened). Four Candidate-2 species co-occur. Seven of 12 aquatic-dependent organisms occur only near SBNWR.
- **Refugia:** SBNWR is the main refugium and source of stocks. U.S. habitat is small and at the northern range limit for most species, so recovery cannot be achieved in the USA alone.
- **Reintroductions:** Beautiful shiner reintroduced in May 1990 from 400 individuals (Mexican stock via [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]); it has established reproducing populations in three ponds. Yaqui chub is at SBNWR and West Turkey Creek. About 225 chub were moved to Dexter in 1976 and bred prolifically in a 0.04-ha pond.
- **Topminnow management:** Management on SBNWR, including mosquitofish removal and ciénega rehabilitation, has maintained 15 separate viable populations.
- **Downlisting/delisting conditions:** Criteria must be met for 10 years within occupied habitat. These require (a) securing aquifers so that artesian and other subsurface flows are perennial; (b) eradicating non-indigenous fishes and bullfrogs from critical habitat; (c) protecting habitats from grazing, irrigated agriculture, mining, non-native introductions and water diversion.
- **Delisting limits:** Yaqui chub cannot be delisted given its limited range; it can be downlisted to threatened once SBNWR and West Turkey Creek populations are self-sustaining. Yaqui catfish delisting depends on protecting Mexican wild populations from hybridization.
- **Six objectives:** (1) cooperate with Mexico; (2) manage existing habitats and populations; (3) determine biological requirements; (4) protect historic habitats in the USA; (5) assess and reestablish species in historic ranges; (6) run information and education programs in English and Spanish.
- **Costs:** Estimated total recovery cost is $3,570,000 (3,570.0 thousand). Year-by-year needs for 1995–2004 total about $216,000–$1,673,000 per year, with a peak in 1997 driven by a $1.5 million Need 2 item. Estimates cover only the first three years of priorities in the schedule and do not oblige any agency to spend funds.
- **Public comment:** The draft was distributed to 38 agencies and individuals. Responses confirm that instream-flow rights for Black Draw are probably impractical given only 5–6 months of surface flow, and that hatchery production is a last-resort measure. Critical habitat covers only aquatic habitats on SBNWR.
- **Research gaps:** Life histories, habitat utilization and aquifer capacity are poorly known. USFWS is funding studies of habitat use and of the aquifer, which is linked to precipitation.
