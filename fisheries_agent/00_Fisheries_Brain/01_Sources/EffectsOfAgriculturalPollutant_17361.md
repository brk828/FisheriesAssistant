# Agricultural Practices and Water Quality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This document is the proceedings of a November 1969 conference at [[Iowa State University]] on the role of agriculture in clean water, published by the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Administration]] (FWPCA) under Grant No. 13040 EYX (November 1970). It compiles six parts of expert chapters on sediment, plant nutrients (phosphorus and nitrogen), pesticides, animal wastes, groundwater and eutrophication effects, and legal, economic and policy aspects, plus workshop transcripts. The volume argues that agriculture contributes sediment, nutrients, pesticides and manure to surface and groundwater, but that the causes and sources must be quantified through research before control programs can be effective. Fish and aquatic life are discussed as indicators of damage (fish kills, oxygen depletion, eutrophication); the document does not study fish populations directly.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** Outgrowth of a regional meeting of land-grant university water resources directors (April 1968), which identified a need for shared understanding across disciplines. Planning committee included Don Kirkham, Robert L. Smith and George E. Smith.
- **Conference:** Held at Iowa State University, November 18-20, 1969, co-chaired by T. L. Willrich and George E. Smith. About 250 attendees from 32 states.
- **Authorship:** Roughly 50 contributors from universities, [[U.S. Department of Agriculture]] (ARS and SWCRD), [[U.S. Geological Survey]], FWPCA and state agencies.
- **Scope:** Only fundamentals and established research facts were to be presented, "not opinions," at a level understandable across disciplines.
- **Structure:** Introduction by Wadleigh and Britt, followed by Parts 1-6 (Sediment; Plant Nutrients; Pesticides; Animal Wastes; Agricultural Pollution Implications; Agriculture's Involvement), each with a workshop session.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and erosion.** Sediment is described as the greatest single pollutant of natural waters by volume. Suspended sediment loads are said to equal more than 700 times the load from sewage (USDA, 1968, as cited). Sediment reduces reservoir capacity, degrades recreational value through turbidity, raises treatment costs and carries nutrients, pesticides and pathogens. Most sediment in transport is suspended silt and clay; in Iowa, more than 85% of transported sediment is in suspension.

**Nutrients and eutrophication.** Chapter 5 (Verduin) contends that aquatic communities evolved under very low nutrient levels: forest streams carried about 7 µg/liter of phosphorus. Phosphorus rose about 480% in western [[Lake Erie]] over roughly 20 years while the N/P ratio fell from 35 to 9.2. Nuisance algal blooms (Sawyer, 1947) appeared above about 20 µg/liter phosphorus. Oxygen depletion from decaying organic matter was linked to the loss of Hexagenia mayflies in western Lake Erie in 1953, replacement by [[Chironomus]] bloodworms, and changes in dominant fish from walleye to yellow perch.

**Animal wastes and fish kills.** Concentrated feedlots and dairies produce very large waste volumes. Manure runoff caused 3 of 8 major fish kills in 1967 (FWPCA). In a Kansas study, BOD reached 90 ppm and dissolved oxygen fell to near zero one mile below a feedlot after a 1-inch storm; fish cannot survive below about 4 ppm oxygen.

**Groundwater.** Nitrate in wells is linked to feedlots, septic systems, improperly constructed shallow wells and, in some areas, heavy fertilizer use over high water tables. The U.S. Public Health Service limit for city potable water is 45 mg/liter nitrate.

**Pesticides.** Some persistent compounds sorb onto sediment and may enter food chains. A 1964 Mississippi River fish kill was attributed to endrin, though the source was not land runoff.

## Key Findings

**Sediment (Part 1)**
- Universal Soil Loss Equation (A = RKLSCP) remains the main planning tool; predictions apply best to uniform slopes.
- Gully erosion contributed 50% or more of sediment in about 20% of 113 humid-region watersheds studied (Glymph, 1956).
- Sediment delivery ratios decline as watershed area increases; Table 1.2 gives 3.80, 1.60, 1.01 and 0.50 acre-feet/square mile/year for watershed classes from under 10 to over 1,000 square miles.
- Sediment carries nutrients, and eroded soil is enriched in organic matter (enrichment ratios about 1 to 5).
- Mulch and minimum tillage are effective; a 1-ton/acre wheat straw mulch reduced soil loss on a 5% slope from 12.42 to 0.30 tons/acre in the mulch treatment shown.

**Plant Nutrients (Part 2)**
- Nitrogen and phosphorus chemistry in sediment: sediments are poor conservers of nitrogen (denitrification), and phosphate is released from bottom sediments only when water concentrations drop.
- Phosphorus does not significantly leach through soil; groundwater phosphorus averages about 0.011 µg/ml.
- Agriculture supplies about one-third of phosphorus in surface waters (Table 5.3 average 58 µg/liter); detergents are identified as a major urban source.
- Lagooning manure is criticized; the chapter argues manure "belongs on the land."
- Nitrate is linked to over-application and leaching; Smith (1968) suggested about 100 lb N/acre as an upper limit for groundwater protection in Missouri.
- Source-apportionment table (Task Group 2610-P, 1967) shows agricultural land runoff of 1,500-15,000 million lb nitrogen per year.

**Pesticides and Animal Wastes (Parts 3-4)**
- A typical fattening steer excretes about 110 lb nitrogen, 125 lb potassium and 365 lb BOD per year.
- A 90,000-head feedlot near Greeley, Colorado was cited as a recycling example.
- The Fox Creek study showed fecal coliform peaks and persistent fecal streptococci after rainfall.

**Policy and Workshops (Parts 4-6)**
- Participants agreed that more research is needed on economics of conservation and on acceptable sediment and nutrient levels.
- Proposals included conservancy districts (Iowa), county sediment-control ordinances (Washington), and cost-sharing tied to farm plans.
- Workshops called for watershed-scale water quality models and better sampling standards.
