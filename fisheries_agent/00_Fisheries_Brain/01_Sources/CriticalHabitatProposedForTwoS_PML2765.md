# Spikedace and Loach Minnow: Proposed Critical Habitat and Uplisting from Threatened to Endangered

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) published a proposed rule on October 27, 2010, to designate approximately 796 miles of streams and rivers in central and eastern Arizona and western New Mexico as [[Critical Habitat]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for the federally threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The Service also proposes reclassifying both species from threatened to endangered, citing prolonged drought, climate change and the expanding range of competitive and predatory [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]]. The re-proposal follows 1994, 2000 and 2007 designations, two May 2009 legal challenges, and an Inspector General audit and Solicitor's advice. Public comments were accepted until December 27, 2010, and hearing requests until December 13, 2010. The Court approved a settlement date of October 15, 2011, for the final determination.

## Background & Description

Both fishes are fewer than three inches long and require perennial streams with moderate to swift currents and substrates free of excessive fine sediment.

- **Spikedace:** silver-sided, with spines in the dorsal fin.
- **Loach minnow:** olive-colored, slender, with upward-directed eyes; males develop brilliant spawning coloration. It is a bottom-dweller of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble and rubble.

Both species were listed as threatened under the ESA in 1986. The loach minnow was previously recorded in Mexico only in the Río San Pedro in northern Sonora and is no longer believed to occur there, though Mexican Gila drainages lack extensive surveys. Spikedace probably occurred in the San Pedro River in Sonora, where it was collected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range contraction.** Original ranges have declined by 85–90%. Spikedace now occupies roughly 10% or less of its historical range; loach minnow occupies 15–20%. Remaining populations are small and fragmented, limiting natural recolonization.

**Current distribution.**
- Spikedace: upper, East Fork, Middle Fork and West Fork [[Gila River]] (New Mexico); lower [[San Pedro River]]; [[Aravaipa Creek]]; and the [[Verde River]]. Common only in Aravaipa Creek and parts of the upper Gila.
- Loach minnow: the same Gila system reaches; the San Francisco and Tularosa rivers and tributaries (Negrito and Whitewater creeks); the Blue River and tributaries; Aravaipa Creek and tributaries (Turkey and Deer creeks); and East Fork White, East Fork Black and North Fork East Fork Black rivers. Common only in Aravaipa Creek and the Blue River.

**Habitat requirements (essential features).**
- Perennial flowing water, generally less than one meter deep, with slow to swift velocities.
- Microhabitats of pools, runs, riffles and rapids over appropriate substrates.
- Water temperatures between 8.0 and 28.0 °C (46.4 to 82 °F).
- An abundant aquatic insect food base.
- Low pollutant levels and low or no nonnative aquatic species.
- Intermittent connective corridors that allow movement when habitat is rewetted.

**Threats.** Habitat destruction, nonnative competitors and predators, prolonged drought, and [[Climate Change]]. Recurrent natural flooding helps maintain habitat and gives native fishes a competitive edge.

**Land ownership of proposed units.** Over 61% federal, 21% state, 7% tribal and 30% private (as stated in the proposal's summary; the Q&A gives state 2% and private 30%, with tribal 7%). Proposed units occur on portions of the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, Gila, San Francisco and Blue rivers, and Bonita and Eagle creeks.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed designation:** approximately 796 miles across eight units in Apache, Cochise, Gila, Graham, Greenlee, Navajo, Pima, Pinal and Yavapai counties (Arizona) and Catron, Grant and Hidalgo counties (New Mexico).
- **Current designation:** the 2007 rule covers approximately 522 river miles and remains in effect until the re-designation is finalized.
- **Critical habitat width:** bankfull width plus 300 feet on either side of the banks, except where bounded by canyon walls.
- **Unoccupied areas:** included only if they extend occupied habitat, expand the species' distribution, and connect to occupied areas. Some intermittent reaches are included as corridors.
- **Exclusions:** areas protected by approved [[Habitat Conservation Plans]] or other management plans, or excluded for economic reasons if this would not cause extinction. Past exclusions covered White Mountain Apache and San Carlos Apache tribal lands, the middle Verde River, and Phelps Dodge (Freeport McMoRan) holdings.
- **Regulatory effect:** designation does not change land ownership or create preserves. Section 7 consultation applies only to federal actions, or federally funded or permitted actions, that may affect critical habitat. Livestock grazing is not necessarily incompatible if habitat remains in good condition.
- **Listing change:** the reclassification to endangered was first found warranted in 1994 but precluded by higher-priority listing actions. Endangered status gives less management flexibility than threatened status, notably in take exceptions and permits.
- **Timeline:** public comment closed December 27, 2010; a draft [[Economic Analysis]] will be released later for review; the final determination has a settlement date of October 15, 2011.
