# Threatened Freshwater Fishes of the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multiple-families

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Robert Rush Miller of the [[Museum of Zoology]] at the University of Michigan, presents the first attempt to list the native threatened freshwater fishes of each U.S. state. Threatened fishes are listed for 49 of the 50 states; only Rhode Island has none. The list was compiled by the Endangered Species Committees of the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] and the [[American Fisheries Society]], drawing on the [[Red Book of Rare and Endangered Fish and Wildlife of the United States]] (in preparation) and the [[IUCN Red Data Book]] (Vol. 4, Pisces, 1969). The author argues that recognizing which species or populations are threatened is the essential first step toward their preservation, and that up-to-date lists are needed before appropriate action can be taken.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Concern for native fishes grew mainly after 1965. The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1966 was described as the first concerted federal effort to protect native U.S. fauna. Committees dealing with vanishing U.S. wildlife had been set up by scientific societies, states, and the federal government, and the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]] Survival Service Commission (based in Morges, Switzerland) had taken action globally.
- **Protection efforts:** Since the late 1960s, western and eastern states had enacted legislation, enforced protection for endangered species and subspecies, maintained natural habitats, and created refugia. In June 1971 a bill was introduced in Congress to establish the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] National Monument to protect threatened pupfishes east of Death Valley.
- **Scope and method:** The list covers species and subspecies. Table 1 gives threatened fishes by family and by state. An asterisk marks forms nationally recognized as endangered. State lists use these status categories: *rare*, *depleted*, *endangered*, *possibly extinct*, *indeterminate*, and *protected*. The author notes that borderline cases had to be judged on their individual merits, and that the status of many fishes remained unclear.
- **Author's role:** Miller chaired the AFS, ASIH, and IUCN committees during preparation, which made coordination easier.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document shows that threatened freshwater fishes occur in many habitat types and regions:

- **Springs and caves:** Several listed species are endemic to small springs or subterranean waters. Examples include the Devils Hole pupfish, the White River springfish and Railroad Valley springfish (Nevada), the Comanche Springs pupfish (Texas), and cavefishes of the families Amblyopsidae and Troglichthyidae (for example, the Ozark cavefish and the Northern cavefish).
- **Western rivers and streams:** Many western species are cutthroat trouts (*Salmo clarkii*, with several subspecies), along with chubs, suckers, and dace in the Great Basin, Colorado River system, and Snake River drainages. The author reports that the humpback chub, bonytail, and Colorado squawfish are now believed extinct in Wyoming.
- **Great Lakes and northern waters:** Ciscoes and whitefishes (*Coregonus* and *Prosopium*), the lake sturgeon, and several darters and shiners are listed in states such as Michigan, Wisconsin, Ohio, and Pennsylvania.
- **Large rivers of the Mississippi and Atlantic drainages:** Sturgeons (shortnose, Atlantic, shovelnose, pallid), paddlefish, gars, and many darters and redhorses appear across many states.
- **Southeastern and Appalachian streams:** Many darters (family Percidae) and madtoms (*Noturus*) are listed in Alabama, Tennessee, Georgia, Kentucky, and Virginia.
- **Hawaii:** Several gobies (O'opu species) are listed for Hawaiian streams.

## Key Findings

1. **National coverage:** Threatened freshwater fishes are recognized in 49 of 50 states. Rhode Island is the only state with none.
2. **Taxonomic breadth:** Table 1 lists threatened taxa across many families, including lampreys (Petromyzontidae), sturgeons (Acipenseridae), paddlefish (Polyodontidae), gars (Lepisosteidae), bowfin (Amiidae), eels (Anguillidae), herrings (Clupeidae), mooneyes (Hiodontidae), trouts and whitefishes (Salmonidae), minnows (Cyprinidae), suckers (Catostomidae), catfishes (Ictaluridae), cavefishes (Amblyopsidae), killifishes (Cyprinodontidae), darters and perches (Percidae), gobies (Gobiidae), and sculpins (Cottidae). The full checklist is too long to reproduce here; consult the original table.
3. **Nationally endangered forms:** Several taxa are marked with an asterisk for national endangered status, including the Devils Hole pupfish.
4. **Notable sturgeons and large fishes:** Shortnose, Atlantic, shovelnose, pallid, and lake sturgeons are listed in multiple states. Blue pike and walleye are listed in Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, and Kentucky.
5. **Extinction concerns:** The humpback chub, bonytail, and Colorado squawfish are believed extinct in Wyoming. Other entries are marked "possibly extinct," including Cahaba shiner (Alabama), Pit sculpin (Oregon), and Longnose sucker (South Dakota).
6. **State patterns:** Some states have especially long lists, notably Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Alabama. Several southwestern and Great Basin states, such as Arizona, Nevada, and New Mexico, list desert and spring-dwelling species.
7. **California's protections:** Species marked with an asterisk in the California list are fully protected under the State Fish and Game Code enacted in 1970.
8. **Policy conclusion:** Accurate lists are a prerequisite for protection. The author calls for conservation effort to be extended to every state and emphasizes that recognizing local or regional threats is an essential step toward perpetuating many species.

**Data caveat:** The source is a published checklist. It reports no new sample sizes, population counts, or quantitative statistics. Status designations are qualitative expert judgments, and the author acknowledges that many are uncertain.
