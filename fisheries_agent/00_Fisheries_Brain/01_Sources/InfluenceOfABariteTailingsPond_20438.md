# Influence of a Barite Tailings Pond Rupture on the Fishes of Big River, Missouri (with New River, Tennessee Study Fragment)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two aquatic studies of mining-related stress in Ozark and Appalachian streams. The principal paper examines the effects of a barite tailings pond rupture on the [[Big River]] fish fauna in Missouri (1975–1976). The authors (Hocutt, Stauffer and Mills) found no detectable direct or chronic harm to Big River proper. [[Mill Creek]], the tributary that received the spill, was stressed in 1975 but had recovered to near pre-spill conditions by 1976. A shorter second study on the [[New River]] in Tennessee (Winger) reports depressed fish and benthic populations caused by acid mine drainage and sedimentation from coal mining. The second text is incomplete in the source.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Big River is a roughly 235 km stream draining about 1600 km² of the [[Meramec River]] system in east-central Missouri, within the Ozark uplands. Its headwaters are clear and swift, becoming sluggish and more turbid downstream. Bottom substrate is mainly coarse gravel and small boulders. The watershed contains most of Missouri's [[Barite]] mines, which are strip pits in erodible red clay soils. Barite is used as a drilling fluid, in ceramics and optical glass, and as a radio-opaque x-ray contrast medium.

**The spill.** Barite is separated from clay and gravel by washing, and wash water is routed to settling ponds. A 24 to 36 ha settling pond in Washington County, Missouri, ruptured on 15 August 1975. It released an unknown quantity of nontoxic clay and water into Buss Branch of Mill Creek. The slug moved about 50 km downstream by 17 August 1975, and increased turbidity was observed 114 km below the rupture (Duchrow 1978).

**Conceptual framework.** The authors frame the assessment around two principles, [[Inertia]] and [[Recovery]] (Cairns and Dickson 1977). Shelford's (1913) law of tolerance defines zones of tolerance and resistance for each environmental variable. The study distinguished acute, direct effects (1975) from indirect and chronic effects (1976).

**New River context.** The New River is a primary tributary of the Big South Fork of the Cumberland River, which was established as a National River and Recreation Area under Public Law 93-251 (1974). Sedimentation and [[Acid Mine Drainage]] from surface and deep coal mines are the principal pollutants. Reclamation of the watershed was planned at roughly $6 million.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stress types.** Cairns and Dickson's (1977) framework recognizes three stress types: habitat destruction, loss of essential habitat function (such as oxygen depletion), and toxic injury to biota. Stress effects depend on intensity, duration and the nature of the toxicant.

**Suspended solids.** The document reviews the ecological effects of [[Suspended Solids]]: mechanical or abrasive action, sediment blanketing, reduced light, substrate for microbial growth, adsorption of chemicals, and reduced temperature fluctuation. Fish defend against particulate loads with mucus secretion and increased ventilation, though this costs energy. Combined action with acids or chemicals can cause gill damage.

**Turbidity thresholds.** Published tolerance values conflict. [[Wallen (1951)]] reported no effects until turbidity neared 20,000 mg/l, with some species tolerating over 100,000 mg/l for a week or more. Fatal levels ranged from 175,000 to 225,000 mg/l for some species, with no gill injury found in survivors. Cairns (1968) recorded 29 species in a Kansas River reach at turbidities above 72,000 mg/l. By contrast, [[Kemp (1949)]] linked gill damage to turbidities as low as 3,000 mg/l, and a 1936 Potomac River flood (6,000 mg/l for 15 days) caused a large fish kill. The authors conclude that data are sparse and that fish generally persist in naturally or anthropogenically turbid waters.

**Life-history strategies.** The authors classify most minnows as r-strategists with high recovery potential after nonselective stress. Mortality is partitioned into natural (n) and stress-induced (m) components. Their interaction (n·m) means a stress may not add substantially to total mortality where density-dependent factors dominate.

**Benthic communities.** In the New River, benthic invertebrate populations were depressed at nearly all stations, and some recovery was seen at downstream sites.

## Key Findings

**Big River survey (Hocutt, Stauffer and Mills).**

- Surveys in 1975 and 1976 collected **27,013 specimens** representing **72 species** from 40 collections in the Big River system.
- Six species were new records for the Big River fauna: *Lepisosteus platostomus*, *Alosa alabamae*, *Hybopsis aestivalis*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Micropterus punctulatus* and *Ammocrypta clara*.
- The pre-spill baseline came from [[Mills (1975)]]: 50 collections, 39,166 specimens, 71 species (April 1974 to June 1975).
- Big River proper showed no significant direct effects. Species richness at downstream Big River stations was similar before and after the spill (Table 2).

**Mill Creek.**

- Station 7 (first station below the Mill Creek mouth) yielded 28 species (795 specimens) in 1975 and 28 species (804 specimens) in 1976. Its cumulative total was 39 species and 1,599 specimens.
- At Station 20, above the Buss Branch confluence, 24 species were collected across two collections. Just below the confluence in 1975, only 13 species (189 specimens) were found. In 1976 the same station yielded 19 species (429 specimens).
- At Station 22, 16 species (170 specimens) were collected in 1975, rising to 24 species (536 specimens) in 1976.
- Tributary stations averaged 19.4 species per collection, compared with 18.4 for non-Mill Creek tributaries in Mills (1975).
- Conclusion: Mill Creek was stressed in 1975 but had recovered to pre-spill diversity and abundance by 1976.

**Chronic-effects analysis (1976).**

- Condition factors (K = weight(g)/length(cm)³ × 10²) were calculated for **3,483 fish** of eight species across upstream (control) and downstream stations. Eighteen stations were sampled in 1976, and 17 were used for condition analysis.
- One-way ANOVA showed significant between-station differences for seven of eight species (P < 0.05). The exception was [[Longear Sunfish]] (F = 1.666, not significant).
- Duncan's multiple range tests (P = 0.05) showed no consistent upstream-downstream pattern attributable to the spill. Differences were distributed in no particular arrangement.
- Brook silverside showed a trend toward higher condition in lower Big River.
- The Missouri Saddled Darter's Duncan's test produced only one homogeneous subset, so differences were judged hard to discern.
- Juveniles (young-of-the-year) through breeding adults were present for most species, supporting the conclusion that chronic effects were minimal.

**Conclusions (Big River).**

- Direct effects on Big River proper were negligible.
- Mill Creek suffered a nonselective stress in 1975 and had recovered by summer 1976.
- Chronic effects were judged negligible, based on condition factors and age structure.

**New River survey (Winger, partial text).**

- Water chemistry, fish and benthic populations were studied at **22 stations** on the New River, its tributaries and the Big South Fork during fall 1977.
- Nearly all stations showed depressed benthic and fish populations and degraded water chemistry from acid mine drainage and sedimentation.
- Pollution enters in the headwaters and periodically downstream, with some benthic improvement at downstream stations.
- Recommendations: reclamation should address both acid drainage and sedimentation. Monitoring should combine biological analysis with water chemistry to evaluate reclamation success.
- The source text ends mid-sentence in the stated study objectives, so the full results are not available.
