# Georgia Fish Pond Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s Georgia Game and Fish Commission handbook, written by Fred J. Dickson, Chief of Fish Management, is a practical guide to building, stocking, fertilizing, fishing, and renovating private farm ponds in Georgia. It focuses on [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] as the core species, and argues that productive ponds depend on correct site selection, complete construction before impoundment, balanced stocking, regular commercial fertilization, and intensive harvest. The text draws heavily on research at the Alabama Polytechnic Institute Fishery School under [[Homer S. Swingle]]. The book was published in three editions (1954 to 1956), with 5,000 and then 10,000 copies per edition.

## Background & Description

The handbook was issued by the [[Georgia Game and Fish Commission]] under Governor Marvin Griffin and Director Fulton Lovell. The author states that fish management methods were developed for the Southeastern States by Dr. Homer S. Swingle, whose station directed "the largest fisheries research station in the world for warm water species." Dickson earned a Master's degree in Fish Management under Swingle. Parts of the text were adapted from Alabama Polytechnic Institute bulletins, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflets, and Texas and Virginia publications.

The text is organized into six parts:
- **Part I:** Principles of pond management and the value of ponds and state hatcheries.
- **Part II:** Warm water pond management (site selection, construction, stocking, fertilization, and fishing).
- **Part III:** How to find and catch fish, mainly bass and bluegill.
- **Part IV:** Fish bait culture (earthworms, crickets, mealworms, soldier fly larvae, and others).
- **Part V:** Renovation of old ponds and weed control.
- **Part VI:** Turtles in ponds.

Georgia had more than 30,000 ponds (1 to 200 acres), with about 2,000 new ponds built each year. The author notes that many were built poorly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The handbook treats the pond as an "aquatic pasture." Fertilizer dissolves into the water and stimulates [[Phytoplankton]] (algae), which feeds [[Zooplankton]] and [[Benthos]] (bottom fauna such as midge larvae, called blood-red worms). These in turn feed forage fish, while bass act as the carnivore that controls bream numbers. Key environmental points include:

- Fertilized ponds supported 400 to 600 pounds of fish per acre; unfertilized ponds supported 35 to 200 pounds per acre depending on watershed fertility.
- Most fish food is produced in water less than 4 feet deep.
- Excess inflow flushes fertilizer out, so [[Diversion Ditch]] systems are recommended where flood flows are large.
- Weeds (submerged, emergent, and floating) compete for nutrients and oxygen, and dense weeds harbor mosquitoes. Water hyacinth and duckweed reduce oxygen.
- Oxygen depletion from over-fertilization, decaying organic matter, or ice cover can cause fish kills.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and balance**
- Fertilized Georgia ponds: 1,000 bluegill fry or fingerlings plus 100 bass per acre (Combination A). Unfertilized ponds: 400 bluegills plus 30 bass per acre.
- Combination B (bluegill, shellcracker, bass): fertilized 850 bluegill, 150 shellcracker, 100 bass; unfertilized 340 bluegill, 60 shellcracker, 30 bass.
- Shellcracker should not exceed 20 percent (or one-fifth) of the bream population.
- Stocking only bluegill and bass gave better catches than combinations of three or more species, based on results from more than 100 ponds over 20 years.
- Adult fish stocking is unsatisfactory. Only hatchery-reared fry or fingerlings, free of disease, should be used.
- Midsummer bluegill stocking produced overcrowding in 17 cases, with no effective fishing.

**Growth expectations**
- Bluegills average about 4 ounces in one year when properly stocked and fed; bass average about 1 pound in one year.
- A single bluegill pair can produce 4,000 or more young in a year; one bream of half a pound may lay up to 51,000 eggs.

**Fertilization**
- Recommended formula: 8-8-2 (nitrogen, phosphate, potash), at 100 pounds per surface acre per application.
- Pond needs 7 to 15 applications per year. Costs run $15 to $25 per acre per year on small ponds.
- Commercial fertilizer is preferred over organic manures, which can deplete oxygen.

**Site and construction**
- Recommended depth profile: about five-sixths of the area at 2 to 6 feet, and about one-sixth at 8 feet or more.
- Build during late summer or fall, before impoundment, and clear stumps and brush first.
- Dams up to 20 feet high need 2:1 slopes; dams above 20 feet need 3:1 slopes.
- Spillways should keep flood flow no deeper than 3 to 6 inches. Over 95 percent of fish left a half-acre experimental pond when flow reached 2 feet deep.
- Drain pipe sizes: 4-inch for ponds up to 3 acres, 8 to 12 inches for ponds up to 15 to 20 acres. Asbestos-cement (transite) and cast iron are recommended; terra cotta and concrete tile should never be used.

**Harvest and fishing**
- Fish should be removed intensively each year. Ponds are "never ruined by over-fishing" when bass and bream are both fished.
- Three to 7 pounds of bream should be caught for each pound of bass.
- Do not throw back small fish, and do not impose size limits on warm water species.
- Good ponds can yield 150 to 250 pounds of fish per acre per year. The author's target is 45 pounds of bass and 160 pounds of bluegill per acre annually.
- The world record bass (22 lb 4 oz, Montgomery Lake, South Georgia, caught by George Perry) is cited.

**Myths corrected by the author**
- More water is not better; excess flow removes fertilizer.
- Deeper is not better; shade is not needed.
- Closed spawning seasons are harmful.
- Goldfish cannot reproduce in bass–bluegill ponds, but golden shiners can and reached about 50 percent of the population in some ponds.

**Renovation**
- Improperly built ponds, too little bass, or stream fish left after poisoning are common causes of poor fishing. Remedies include draining, restocking, and fishery biologist analysis.
- Submerged weeds can be controlled by fertilization. Copper sulphate (½ to 1 ppm) and DELRAD (0.3 ppm for pithophora, toxic near 0.7 ppm) are chemical options. 2,4-D is used for emergent and floating weeds.

**Turtles**
- Slider turtle diet was about 80 percent vegetable matter and 20 percent animal matter, with fish under 3 percent.
- Trapping is the most practical removal method.

**Bait culture**
- Red wigglers are the preferred earthworm. A tub 2 feet in diameter yields 3,000 to 5,000 worms per year.
- Black field crickets (*Gryllus assimilis*) grow fastest at about 80°F, and are fed poultry laying mash.
