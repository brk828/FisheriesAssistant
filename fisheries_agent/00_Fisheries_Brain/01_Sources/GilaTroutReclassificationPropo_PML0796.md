# Gila Trout Reclassification Proposal and Special Rule: Comments, Recovery History and Threats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled record for a 2005 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) proposal to reclassify the [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) from endangered to threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]], together with a section 4(d) special rule allowing recreational angling in New Mexico and Arizona. It contains the proposed rule text (Federal Register, May 11, 2005, vol. 70, no. 90, pp. 24750–24764), a reference list of roughly 140 works, and two critical public comments: one from [[Paul C. Marsh]] (PhD, Arizona State University) and one from [[Jerome A. Stefferud]], a former Forest Service fisheries biologist and Gila Trout Recovery Team consultant. Both commenters recommend rejecting the proposal. Several unrelated Federal Communications Commission radio-broadcasting notices, included as page debris, are not relevant to the species.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is a salmonid first formally described by [[Robert R. Miller]] in 1950 from fish collected in [[Main Diamond Creek]] in 1939. It is most closely related to the [[Apache Trout]] of east-central Arizona, and both are more closely related to rainbow trout than to cutthroat trout.

Identifying features include iridescent gold sides shading to copper on the opercles, small profuse spots above the lateral line, a large well-spotted adipose fin, and retained parr marks. Gila trout are distinguished from co-occurring non-native trout by coloration, parr marks and spotting. The [[Roundtail Chub]], a cyprinid, is sometimes confused with it.

Life history notes from the proposal:
- Spawning occurs mainly in April at 6–8 °C (43–46 °F); emergence occurs 56–70 days after egg deposition.
- Females mature at age 2–4 at about 130 mm (5 in); males typically at age 2 or 3. Most fish live to about age 5, with a reported maximum of 7.
- On average, only two of every 100 hatched eggs survive to adulthood (Brown et al. 2001).
- Aquatic insects are the primary food. Adults are generally sedentary; after eight months, 75 percent of tagged fish were within 100 m of release sites (Rinne 1982).

The proposal documents recovery history. The original plan (1979) was revised in 1984, 1993 and 2003. Downlisting was proposed in 1987 but withdrawn in 1991 after flooding, wildfire, hatchery shortfalls and brown trout presence. The current petition was filed in 1996.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats identified in the proposal:**
- **Wildfire and post-fire flooding:** Fire removed populations from Main and South Diamond creeks. Populations have been reestablished in some cases. The Service notes that fire risk to all populations is reduced by lower fuel loads and disjunct stream locations.
- **Hybridization and non-native salmonids:** Introduced rainbow, cutthroat and brown trout are the historic main threats. Brown trout are a predation threat; barriers and removal programs are in place.
- **Disease:** [[Bacterial Kidney Disease]] (carrier *Renibacterium salmoninarum*) occurs at low levels in the upper West Fork drainage, but no clinical cases were found in Gila trout. [[Whirling Disease]] (*Myxobolus cerebralis*) was judged a low risk because of cold, high-elevation habitat and low abundance of its intermediate host, *Tubifex tubifex*.
- **Drought and flood:** Hydrologic variability is considered the most important regulator of population size.
- **Livestock grazing and habitat alteration:** Mostly reduced by wilderness designation, with 9 of 10 streams in designated wilderness.

**Current status (Table 1, as of January 2001):** Twelve streams are occupied. Three of four original pure lineages are protected and replicated across about 100 km (62 mi) of stream. The Whiskey Creek lineage remains unreplicated. Estimated wild population rose from fewer than 10,000 fish (age 1+) in 1992 to about 37,000 in New Mexico in 2001.

## Key Findings

**Findings of the Service (proposed rule):**
- Gila trout no longer meet the criteria for endangered status. The Service concludes that threatened status is appropriate because threats persist.
- The number of populations increased from 4 to 12 since recovery efforts began.
- A special rule under section 4(d) would allow regulated angling in specified waters, excluding the four relict populations, and would allow excess hatchery fish to be stocked into non-recovery waters.
- Proposed hatchery production could reach about 20,000 fish for recovery, with about 100,000 excess fish produced through broodstock management.
- Excess fish could otherwise be euthanized.
- Brown trout could be controlled by barriers and removal. Monitoring occurs at least once every five years.
- Sport Fish Restoration funds received by New Mexico ($3,258,225) and Arizona ($3,556,597) in fiscal year 2004 could become available. The Service spent an estimated $137,500 on Gila trout in 2004.
- A population viability model (Brown et al. 2001) found that up to 15 percent annual angling mortality of adults had no effect on viability.

**Opposing comments (Marsh, 2005; Stefferud, 2005):**
- Threats from fire, flood, drought, hybridization, disease and predation remain severe. The species could be eliminated from a significant portion of its range within a short period.
- Downlisting criteria have not been met. Replicates have short histories (under one generation to under ten years). Dude Creek has no documented reproduction. Raspberry Creek has less than one generation. McKnight Creek lies outside the historic range. Total length of qualifying replicate streams is 63.7 km, less than 75 percent of the 85 km criterion.
- Emergency rescue plans were invoked three times in three years (Whiskey Creek 2002, Mogollon Creek 2003, Raspberry Creek 2004). Stefferud argues this shows the species is vulnerable.
- Angling would remove the largest, most fecund females. Only about 7 percent of fish exceed 8 inches, and a female of that size can produce several hundred eggs versus 20–50 for a small female.
- Sport-fish designation for [[Roundtail Chub]] in Arizona did not prevent continued decline, according to Marsh.
- The proposed rule does not require states to exclude relict and replicate streams from angling. Draft state regulations were not included.
- Stefferud argues that hatchery stocking into existing non-native populations would create hybridized populations, and that the "goodwill" rationale reflects a local political constituency rather than biology.
- Stefferud also asks that critical habitat be designated for relict-population streams. The current proposal does not address it.

**Note on scope:** The document is a regulatory and public-comment record, not a primary research study. No new field data were collected. Most figures are quoted from earlier studies.
