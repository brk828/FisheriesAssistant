## Executive Summary

This document is **not a primary fish-science paper** but an institutional/administrative proposal (dated April 12, 1993) advocating for the merger of the [[NAU/CPSU]] (National Park Service Cooperative Parks Studies Unit at Northern Arizona University) and the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program into a unified **Colorado Plateau Research Center (CPRC)** under the newly forming [[National Biological Survey]] (NBS). The GCES program, active since 1982, has been studying the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on riverine and reservoir ecosystems, including impacts on native and endangered aquatic species in the [[Colorado River]], [[Lake Powell]], and [[Lake Mead]]. While no specific fish taxa, morphological data, or population statistics are presented, the proposal explicitly frames its research mandate around biological monitoring required by the **Grand Canyon Protection Act** and the ongoing **Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement (EIS)** process.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although species-specific ecological data are absent, the text situates aquatic communities within a broader biome-scale ecological framework:

- **Habitat scope**: The Colorado Plateau watershed spans over 130,000 square miles, with the Department of the Interior (DOI) controlling ~72,000 square miles (~55% of land area) and over 90% of the riparian corridor along the rivers within this watershed.
- **Dam-modified ecosystems**: The [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has altered riverine and reservoir ecology since its operational studies began in 1982, affecting downstream conditions in the [[Grand Canyon]] and connected reservoirs ([[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]]).
- **Multi-jurisdictional habitat management**: Aquatic and riparian habitats intersect 21 NPS units, 27 Native American reservations, 4 National Wildlife Refuges, BLM wilderness lands, and 27 National Forests — reflecting a highly fragmented management landscape for any resident fish populations.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: The document lists water management (43 dams and reservoirs on the Colorado River system), irrigation, hydroelectric power generation, recreation, mining, and energy development as major stressors on aquatic and riparian ecosystems, serving over 15 million people.
- **International comparative context**: GCES research findings have been applied to evaluate dam management effects on aquatic ecosystems internationally, including in Chile, China, and Alaska, suggesting comparative relevance to other regulated-river fish communities (see [[Dam Impacts on Riverine Fish Communities]]).

## Key Findings

- The [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] and the Glen Canyon Dam EIS process **mandate** development of an ecosystem-based, long-term biological monitoring program to track impacts of dam operations on Grand Canyon, Lake Powell, and Lake Mead resources — implicitly including fish populations, though none are named.
- GCES and NAU/CPSU have already built **long-term, large-scale databases** on resource distribution, status, and trends for "major biological taxa" in the Colorado River and across multiple NPS units — a foundational dataset for future fish population studies, though details are not disclosed in this proposal.
- The National Academy of Sciences (NAS) has provided ongoing scientific review of GCES-related Colorado River research since 1985, lending external credibility to any fish/aquatic findings generated by the program.
- Eight Native American tribes bordering the Colorado River have been engaged in coordination efforts, with implications for co-management of fisheries and aquatic resources of tribal concern.
- The proposal does not report new empirical fish data; its primary "finding" is an **organizational recommendation** — consolidating GCES and NAU/CPSU into the CPRC — to strengthen the institutional capacity for biome-scale monitoring of endangered and native species within the [[Colorado River Ecosystem]].
- This document should be treated as **contextual/programmatic background** for interpreting subsequent GCES fish and aquatic ecosystem research outputs, rather than as a direct source of ichthyological data.
