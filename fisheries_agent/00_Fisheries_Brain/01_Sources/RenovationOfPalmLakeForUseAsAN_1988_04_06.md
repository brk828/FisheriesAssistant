## Executive Summary

This 1988 draft proposal by the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), in collaboration with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and The Arizona Nature Conservancy (TANC), outlines a plan to renovate **Palm Lake**, a 3–4 acre spring- and well-fed artificial pond near [[Wickenburg]], Arizona, adjacent to the [[Hassayampa River]]. The goal is to convert the pond—historically used for exotic fish production—into a **refugium and grow-out facility** for five critically imperiled native fishes of the [[Gila River Basin]]: [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Gila Topminnow]]. The plan combines endangered species recovery, captive brood stock security, predator-free grow-out of stocking-sized fish, public education, and ecological research into a single multi-agency facility. Renovation involves chemical removal (rotenone) of existing exotic species, physical habitat modification, and a phased restocking strategy.

## Background & Description

While this is a management document rather than a taxonomic study, it includes several size- and life-stage-specific details relevant to fish biology and predation vulnerability:

- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** and **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Described as long-lived, large-bodied, riverine species. Studies cited show that when stocked as **fry or 2–3 inch juveniles**, both species suffer extremely high predation losses in the wild.
- **Predator-resistant sizing**: Fish grown to larger sizes at facilities like Palm Lake are "far less susceptible to predation" than fry/small juveniles — a key driver for using grow-out ponds prior to river stocking.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Existing brood stock offspring at Dexter National Fish Hatchery have been pond-raised since hatching; the plan calls for stocking **500–700 "large" bonytail**, representing roughly half the offspring of the original Lake Mohave brood stock.
- Squawfish and razorback sucker were to be stocked "in numbers approximately equal to, or somewhat greater than" bonytail, at sizes **"beyond the range potentially preyed upon by the bonytail"** — implying a deliberate size hierarchy among co-stocked natives to minimize intraguild predation.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** and **[[Gila Topminnow]]**: Smaller-bodied species intended to serve as an established prey/forage base, stocked first to build population densities before larger predatory natives are introduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Site description**: Palm Lake is a spring- and well-fed artificial pond situated within the historic **Gila River drainage**, immediately adjacent to the Hassayampa River. Although no historic collection records confirm any of the five target species occurred specifically in the Hassayampa near Palm Lake, the broader Gila Basin represents historic range for all of them.
- **Existing exotic community** (targeted for eradication): [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Mosquitofish]] — identified as incompatible with native species conservation goals.
- **Predators of concern in the wild** (justifying grow-out strategy): [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are cited as the **primary predators** of stocked squawfish and razorback sucker in occupied habitats; [[Smallmouth Bass]] (Centrarchidae) and [[Yellow Bullhead]] (Ictaluridae) also contribute to predation pressure.
- **Habitat complexity**: The pond features subterranean spring inflows, Typha (cattail) marsh zones, variable substrates, and basin morphometry that pose challenges for complete piscicide treatment — the spring head area is specifically noted as the most difficult zone to fully renovate due to continual fresh water input.
- **Trophic structure design**: The proposed multi-species assemblage (predatory cyprinids/catostomids plus small-bodied cyprinodontids/poeciliids) is explicitly modeled as historically co-occurring in the Gila Basin, intended to replicate a natural predator-prey community structure not found elsewhere in captivity.
- **Bonytail chub conservation status**: Described as one of the most critically endangered fish species in the U.S. at the time; the only extant wild Arizona population (Lake Mohave, Colorado River) was a **senescent, non-reproducing adult population**, with the Upper Colorado River basin population considered "functionally extinct." This underscores the ecological urgency of establishing a **geographically isolated second refugium** for captive brood stock.

## Key Findings

- **Predation is the primary bottleneck** for successful reintroduction of Colorado River squawfish and razorback sucker; only stocking programs using larger, grow-out-reared fish are likely to achieve recovery success in Arizona rivers.
- **Existing hatchery infrastructure cannot produce large-bodied fish at scale**, creating a critical need for secure pond/grow-out facilities like the proposed Palm Lake renovation.
- **Bonytail chub brood stock security** is a top conservation priority; holding part of the Dexter National Fish Hatchery brood stock at a geographically separate site (Palm Lake) mitigates catastrophic risk (e.g., agricultural chemical drift/fish kills) to the single existing captive population.
- **Renovation methodology**: A two-phase toxin (rotenone) application plan — first isolating and treating the spring head (continual fresh water complicates full eradication), then treating the main lake body via one of two drawdown alternatives (full dike breach/drainage to the Hassayampa vs. partial drawdown with pumping) — was proposed to eliminate resident exotic fishes.
- **Environmental safeguards** included continuous groundwater bioassay (live fish in flow-through well water systems) to detect potential toxin contamination of the domestic water supply, plus on-hand detoxicants (potassium permanganate, sodium thiosulfate).
- **Habitat engineering recommendations**: contouring the basin to a single low-point drain, creating shallow bays for juvenile/small-species habitat, providing steep/root-covered banks for refuge cover, and varying substrates to promote spawning — informed by prior experience at Dexter National Fish Hatchery ponds.
- **Stocking sequence strategy**: Establish forage/prey species (pupfish, topminnow) first to build a supportive population base before introducing larger predatory natives (squawfish, razorback, bonytail), reducing reliance on supplemental feeding.
- **Multi-species sympatry as research value**: The proposed 5-species native assemblage would represent a historically-relevant community structure not replicated anywhere else, offering unique research opportunities in fish behavior, ecology, and recovery biology, with anticipated collaboration from [[Arizona State University]] and other institutions.
- **No net new ecological risk to the Hassayampa River**: All exotic and native species involved already occur within the Gila drainage; toxin concentrations were not expected to reach fish-kill levels in the river during treatment or drainage.
- **Budget structure**: Estimated project costs were jointly itemized across AGFD, FWS (Dexter and San Bernardino programs), and TANC, with substantial contributed value from existing brood stock (e.g., aquarium squawfish valued at $30,000; bonytail broodstock production valued at $2.5 million) reflecting sunk investment rather than new capital outlay.
