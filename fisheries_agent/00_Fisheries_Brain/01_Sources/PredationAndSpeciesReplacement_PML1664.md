# Predation and Species Replacement in American Southwestern Fishes: A Case Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines why the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*[[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]]*) has disappeared from much of its range in the American Southwest. Although [[Habitat Destruction]] has eliminated some populations, many others were lost with no apparent environmental change except the arrival of the introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish). The author tested several candidate mechanisms through laboratory and field experiments. Predation of juvenile topminnows by mosquitofish is identified as a major cause of decline. [[Parasites And Diseases]], [[Hybridization]], physiological stress, and [[Competition]] are judged unsupported as primary mechanisms.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Sonoran topminnow is a small poeciliid, generally under 50 mm in length. It is native to the [[Gila River Drainage]] of southern Arizona and several drainages in northwestern mainland Mexico. It formerly occupied most aquatic habitats in the region, including streams, springs, and cienegas.
- **Status:** Its range in the United States has contracted to a few isolated streams, springs, and creek segments in southern Arizona. It is listed as endangered (U.S. Department of the Interior, 1980).
- **Introduced species:** The mosquitofish is native to the eastern and central United States. It was introduced into Arizona about 1926 for mosquito control and is now ubiquitous. It has replaced the topminnow throughout much of the latter's former range.
- **Replacement timing:** Local replacement occurred from four months to several years after colonization, although long-term coexistence was also observed.
- **Geographic pattern:** Topminnows declined within the zone of overlap with mosquitofish. In northwestern Mexico, where mosquitofish had only recently colonized a few localities, topminnows remained abundant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and mechanisms considered**

The author evaluated five hypotheses:

1. **Habitat destruction:** Responsible for some population losses, but many extirpations occurred without environmental change. Topminnows tolerate a wide range of conditions, so subtle physical or chemical fluctuations are unlikely causes.
2. **Parasites and diseases:** Introduced fish can transfer parasites to natives elsewhere, but no new parasites or diseases were evident in topminnows. [[Mpoame]] (1981) found only one nematode, *Camallanus* sp., infecting *P. occidentalis*, with no evidence that introduced-fish parasites affected native Arizona species.
3. **Hybridization:** Hybrids were not observed in the field or laboratory, and conspecific mate choice was high in laboratory trials.
4. **Competition:** The two species are morphologically similar, but no competition could be demonstrated. Mosquitofish are largely piscivorous/insectivorous and have strong, conical teeth. Topminnows feed mainly on detritus and vegetation, with crustaceans important in some populations. Their trophic morphologies differ.
5. **Predation:** Mosquitofish predation on juvenile topminnows was identified as the most plausible mechanism.

**Predation as a community-level process**

Native western fishes evolved in habitats with few predaceous fish. Of the native fish species in the southwestern region, only four were piscivores. The author argues that native species lacking appropriate predator-avoidance behaviors may be especially vulnerable to introduced predators. Declines of native fish in the region have been linked to the [[Biological Invasion]] of predatory and competitive species, including [[Largemouth Bass]], catfish, and carp.

## Key Findings

### 1. Laboratory Coexistence Study
- Allopatric topminnow populations grew rapidly, stabilized, and then continued to grow, with recruitment observed.
- Sympatric populations (with mosquitofish) showed no successful recruitment and steadily declined.
- Simple and complex habitat types were combined for analysis.
- Most sympatric females were reported as gravid (36/42 sympatric). Offspring survival was not reduced by mosquitofish; all offspring survived for 63 weeks in the tested set.
- Cannibalism among topminnows was severe, so coexistence in a laboratory setting was low.

### 2. Field Coexistence Study
- Sympatric field cages showed no topminnow recruitment. All three sympatric populations were eliminated by the end of the experiment, with surviving individuals physically damaged in the first two weeks and all eaten by day 24.
- Allopatric cage populations experienced successful recruitment and persisted through the 24-day experiment.

### 3. Fin Damage Analysis
- At Bylas Springs, 122 of 178 topminnows (68.5%) examined from October 1980 to March 1982 had light or heavy caudal fin damage.
- Forty-two topminnows collected in 1968, before mosquitofish colonization, had no fin damage.
- In laboratory trials, 73 of 130 topminnows (56.2%) held with mosquitofish had damaged fins, while none of 144 held in allopatry were damaged.
- Mosquitofish females became aggressive immediately when placed with topminnows, chasing and biting them. Some fish were damaged within 15 minutes, and most were attacked within an hour. Mosquitofish did not generally attack one another, and no fin damage was observed on that species.

### 4. Survival and Stress Analyses
- In the laboratory, topminnow survival was slightly but significantly lower in sympatry (allopatric mean 11.92, SD 0.29; sympatric mean 10.92, SD 0.90; t = 3.664, P = .0029; 12 adults per group).
- In the first laboratory stress trial, sympatric fish were significantly lighter in somatic weight and produced fewer embryos than allopatric fish.
- In the second laboratory trial, differences appeared in ovary weight and fertility.
- Field results showed no detectable effect of sympatry on somatic or ovary weight, and regression slopes ran in the opposite direction to laboratory findings. Fertility also differed significantly between field populations, again in the opposite direction.
- Fecundity (brood size) did not differ significantly between allopatric and sympatric fish (P = .6063).

### 5. Predation Rate Estimate
- Stomach-content analysis was judged unreliable. Prey are recognizable in mosquitofish guts for less than four hours, so a single predation event during a 16-hour daylength would be detected in only about 25% of captures.
- Assuming a mosquitofish ingests about 1 juvenile per day (a conservative estimate from the cited literature), a single female mosquitofish could consume about 100 topminnow juveniles per year.
- Topminnow brood sizes range from about 5 to 15 individuals per year, so a single female mosquitofish could consume all offspring of a single topminnow female in one season.

### 6. Conclusions
- Predation of juvenile topminnows by mosquitofish is the primary mechanism of species replacement. The author cites experimental, field, and transplant evidence satisfying the criteria for predation-based extirpation.
- The mosquitofish's effect is shaped by habitat complexity. Shallow areas inaccessible to adults provide refugia for topminnows in nature, and coexistence has been documented in spatially complex and periodically flooded habitats such as the [[Santa Cruz River]].
- Laboratory results may overstate predation because mosquitofish recruit well in nature and aquaria lack refugia. However, the study illustrates the potential extent to which predation can limit population growth.
- Future work should combine transplant experiments and field observations, as gut-content analysis alone underestimates predation impact.
- Introduction of mosquitofish into new habitats across Arizona, with subsequent predation on topminnows, is a continuing threat.
