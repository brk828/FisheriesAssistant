# Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) Population Recovery — 1993 Status Update

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a [[Cui-ui Recovery Team]] meeting notice (October 14–15, 1993, Reno, NV) with a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) news release reporting a dramatic increase in the adult population of the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a catostomid fish endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Adult numbers rose from approximately 150,000 in the early 1980s to an estimated 1.1 million by 1993, attributed largely to a series of high-runoff years from the [[Sierra Nevada]] mountains combined with two decades of active management by FWS and the [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]]. Despite the increase, officials cautioned that the hydrologic conditions driving this recovery are rare and long-term population stability remains uncertain due to ongoing water diversion pressures on the [[Truckee River]].

## Background & Description

The source text is administrative/policy-focused and does not provide detailed morphometric or anatomical data. Life-history-relevant details noted include:
- Larvae hatch from eggs deposited in river gravel substrate.
- Newly hatched larvae emerge ("wiggle up") through gravel and are swept downstream to the lake.
- Larvae undergo rapid transformation into juveniles once they reach [[Pyramid Lake]].
- No morphological measurements (length, weight, fin counts) are reported in this document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat duality**: Cui-ui are lake-dwelling as adults, inhabiting the large alkaline [[Pyramid Lake]] northeast of Reno, Nevada, but require riverine conditions in the lower [[Truckee River]] for spawning — a migratory (potamodromous) life history.
- **Spawning behavior**: Adults migrate into the lower river in spring and spend only about one week spawning before returning to the lake.
- **Egg/larval development**: Eggs take approximately 3–4 weeks to hatch; larvae are then flushed downstream to the lake, where they rapidly metamorphose into juveniles.
- **Barriers to habitat access**: A delta forms at the mouth of the Truckee River due to reduced flows, historically blocking fish passage during most years. The [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]], built by the Bureau of Reclamation in 1976, is operated by FWS to facilitate passage around this delta.
- **Water dependency**: Historically the species benefited from the entire Truckee River flow (~600,000 acre-feet/year average); current average flows are reduced to ~400,000 acre-feet/year due to upstream diversions for agriculture and municipal use in California and Nevada. Cui-ui hold reserved water rights only to [[Stampede Reservoir]], yielding ~40,000 acre-feet/year on average, used to supplement lower river flows during critical spawning periods.
- **Hatchery role**: The Tribe's [[David Koch Cui-ui Hatchery]] has operated since the late 1970s primarily as a genetic/catastrophe refuge rather than a primary driver of wild population recovery; hatchery-reared larvae are released unmarked, so their contribution to the population increase is unquantified.

## Key Findings

- **Population trend**: Adult cui-ui numbers increased from ~150,000 (early 1980s) to ~1.1 million (1993) — roughly a 7-fold increase over roughly a decade.
- **Primary driver**: The increase is attributed mainly to a few thousand successful spawning adults during a series of high-runoff years in the early 1980s, which produced near-record river flows enabling larval survival and passage over the river delta.
- **Contributing factors**: Two decades of management interventions — operation of the [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]] and supplemental flow releases from [[Stampede Reservoir]] — are cited as secondary but meaningful contributors to species persistence and recovery.
- **Conservation status context**: Cui-ui were listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1967 due to river diversions that degraded spawning habitat and created a river-mouth delta blocking migratory access.
- **Uncertainty/caution**: FWS Pacific Regional Director Marvin Plenert cautioned that the environmental conditions (high Sierra Nevada runoff) responsible for the population surge are infrequent, and continued population growth is not guaranteed; future water flows cannot be assured given increasing regional water demands.
- **Management implications**: Recovery is framed as dependent on both (1) recurrence of favorable hydrologic conditions and (2) continued/improved management actions, including efforts to acquire additional water rights and improve fishway operations.
- **Institutional response**: The FWS convened the [[Cui-ui Recovery Team]] (originally formed in 1989) to reconvene on October 14–15, 1993, to evaluate the new population estimates against objectives in the existing [[Cui-ui Recovery Plan]] and recommend further actions.
- **Tribal collaboration**: The [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]] is working with the State of Nevada to prevent new appropriations of Truckee River water, reflecting ongoing tribal-state-federal coordination in species conservation.
