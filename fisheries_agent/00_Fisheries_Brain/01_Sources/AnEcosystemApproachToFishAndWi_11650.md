# An Ecosystem Approach to Fish and Wildlife Conservation

#conservation-policy #ecosystem-management #biodiversity #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This March 1994 policy document from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] outlines the agency's adoption of an ecosystem approach to conserve the nation's biological diversity more effectively. The approach emphasizes protecting or restoring ecosystem function, structure, and species composition while providing for sustainable socioeconomic use. The Service proposes using watershed-based units as building blocks for ecosystem delineation, establishing partnerships across federal, state, tribal, and private sectors, and implementing a planning/action framework focused on setting resource goals and monitoring outcomes. The document represents a fundamental shift from species-specific management to holistic, systems-based conservation.

## Background & Description

The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] mission is to conserve, protect, and enhance the nation's fish and wildlife and their habitats for the continuing benefit of the American people. The agency recognizes that environmental sustainability requires a holistic approach to resource conservation, moving beyond species-specific and project-oriented mandates to long-term maintenance of healthy ecosystems.

**Core Definitions:**
- **Biodiversity**: The variety of life and its processes, including genetic differences, species arrays, communities, and ecosystems
- **Ecosystem**: Dynamic and interrelating complex of plant and animal communities and their associated non-living environment
- **Ecosystem Approach**: Protecting or restoring the function, structure, and species composition of an ecosystem, recognizing that all components are interrelated

The Service adopted watershed units from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Hydrologic Unit Map as basic building blocks, grouping or segmenting them based on vegetation cover types, physiography, and optimal management size. The working draft identified approximately 52 ecosystem units covering the continental United States, Alaska, Pacific Islands, and Caribbean territories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasizes that biological systems are dynamic and ever-changing, with plant and animal populations inseparable from their environment and relationships with each other. Humans play a pivotal role in ecosystem dynamics and will play an increasingly important role in sustaining ecosystem processes and health.

**Key Ecological Principles:**
- All things are connected through systems, relationships, and processes
- Example provided: midwestern cropping practices affect [[Gulf of Mexico]] fisheries productivity through erosion, sedimentation, and marsh accretion vital to coastal marsh maintenance (nursery grounds for many fish species)
- Species must be addressed as components of the system within which they are found
- Natural processes (fire, floods) must be recognized in management

**Ecosystem Unit Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Significance of resources present (diversity, rarity, uniqueness, health of species and habitats)
2. Risk or threat to resources (imminence, recoverability, degree of protection elsewhere)
3. Service's ability to successfully address resource needs
4. Opportunities for partnerships

The [[National Wildlife Refuges]] are identified as centers for biodiversity protection that will play important roles in determining and accomplishing objectives for the ecosystems in which they occur.

## Key Findings

**Strategic Framework:**
The Service established eight guiding principles for ecosystem approach implementation:
- Ecosystem approach is critically important for conserving biological diversity and environmentally sustainable development
- Environmental and socioeconomic factors must be considered
- Natural resource goals must be established on ecosystem-wide basis
- Full participation of all partners (Federal, State, local, Tribal, public, private) is imperative
- Service resources and tools must be integrated internally and leveraged with partners
- Strategies must be based on best available science
- Efforts must focus on discrete landscape units of manageable size
- Flexibility, innovation, and delegation to lowest appropriate level are essential

**Partnership Requirements:**
Successful partnerships require considerable flexibility, awareness of all partners' missions and needs, early and frequent communication, humility, and appreciation of what partnerships can accomplish. The Service does not control or manage entire ecosystems; ecological integrity of refuges depends on actions of neighboring public and private landowners.

**Planning/Action Framework:**
The document outlines a four-step iterative process:
1. **Identify natural resource needs**: Determine historical perspective, status and trends of fish and wildlife and habitats, relationships and processes within and between ecosystems
2. **Set resource goals and objectives**: Establish broad goals (e.g., restore wetland quality, restore natural stream flows) balanced with public tolerance; create quantifiable short and long-term objectives
3. **Implement solutions**: Integrate tools (land acquisition, habitat restoration, endangered species recovery, regulatory evaluations, fish restoration, landowner assistance) through partnerships
4. **Monitor and report**: Evaluate success, report results, periodically review plans and priorities with feedback to assessment and goal-setting

**Needed Actions Identified:**
- Focus management on natural communities of plants and animals
- Incorporate needs of rare and ecologically important species
- Minimize habitat fragmentation
- Ensure availability of uncontaminated land and water
- Maintain naturally-occurring structural and genetic diversity
- Recognize role of natural processes

**Resource Prioritization:**
Limited funds and staff require concentration on areas most important to the Service's mission. Budget increases and resource shifts will target Ecosystem Units most important to trust resources where the Service has greatest likelihood of achieving results through partnerships. The document acknowledges that Service activity will continue in all parts of the country but with differential emphasis.

**Information Needs:**
Service information requirements will change, especially in inventory, monitoring, and research into ecological processes, demographics, socioeconomics, and techniques development. The [[National Biological Survey]] is identified as a primary information source, with universities, states, conservation organizations, and industry as potential partners.

**Implementation Flexibility:**
The document emphasizes that there is nothing rigid about the framework; what works at the local level and achieves good results is the right approach. The Service will refine its approach based on feedback received and evaluation procedures to be implemented. Input from all sectors is explicitly welcomed to form the basis for continued refinement.
