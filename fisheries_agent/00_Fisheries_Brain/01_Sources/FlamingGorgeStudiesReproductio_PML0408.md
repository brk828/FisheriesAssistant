# Flaming Gorge Studies: Reproduction and Recruitment of Gila spp. and Colorado Pikeminnow in the Middle Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This synopsis (Flaming Gorge Studies Project #39, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and Utah State University Geography Department, 1992–1996) combines three linked reports on the native chubs (*Gila* spp.) and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] of [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] on the middle [[Green River]]. The work asked whether test flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] could optimize reproduction and recruitment of these endangered fishes. Report A ([[Orchard and Schmidt 2000]]) assessed geomorphic change in habitat availability; Report B ([[Day et al. 2000]]) examined backwater nursery habitat use; Report C ([[Chart and Lentsch 2000]]) monitored the whole fish community and adult chub populations. The three studies were run by different researchers and only partially overlap, so flow relationships could not be firmly established. The strongest signals were that moderate-to-high spring peaks with sustained flows favored both chub and pikeminnow recruitment, while extended high flows and cold water suppressed them.

## Background & Description

The native chubs of the Colorado River system evolved in warm, turbid, seasonally variable rivers. The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is an obligate riverine species now found mainly in canyon reaches; the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) occupies tributaries and upper mainstem reaches; the [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*) was presumed extirpated from the system's canyons. The humpback chub is federally endangered (listed 1974). Desolation and Gray Canyons hold one of the largest concentrations of native chubs in the upper basin, but chub morphology there is ambiguous: many fish show humpback and roundtail traits and were recorded simply as *Gila* spp. Colorado Pikeminnow is the largest native minnow and spawns on the descending limb of the spring hydrograph; the [[Three Fords Rapid]] spawning area is one of only two documented spawning sites in the Green River.

The study aims were to: (1) determine timing and success of *Gila* spp. and pikeminnow reproduction; (2) measure growth and backwater habitat use of early life stages; (3) tag juvenile and adult chubs to estimate recruitment; (4) test for additional pikeminnow spawning sites; (5) accumulate biological and geomorphic information on nursery habitat; and (6) monitor non-target fishes. The four study reaches were Cedar Ridge, Surprise Canyon, Joe Hutch and Coal Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphology (Report A).** Desolation Canyon's valley has narrowed. Active channel area decreased on average 19% since the early 1900s, with two episodes of narrowing: one associated with the [[Cottonwood Terrace]] (stabilizing 1922–1936, linked to climate-driven flood decline and [[Tamarisk]] spread) and one with the [[Modern Floodplain]] (following closure of Flaming Gorge Dam in 1963). Eddy habitats are the dominant backwater habitat available to young fish; eddy character is controlled by discharge, but total eddy area is not. Eddies become more frequent and smaller from baseflow to about 7,000 cfs, then larger and less frequent above that. Rising discharge submerges bare sand and cobble bars and increases inundated vegetation, which Orchard and Schmidt suggest provides refuge for non-native fishes during high flow.

**Hydrology and temperature.** Peak flows and temperatures differed sharply between years. Table 2 of the synopsis summarizes the record: 1993 peaked at 25,400 cfs on 31 May with flows above 6,000 cfs for 68 days; 1994 peaked at 11,800 cfs on 21 May with 34 days above 6,000 cfs; 1995 peaked at 29,800 cfs late in the year per Report C (the 25,400 cfs peak is reported for 1993 in both reports); 1996 peaked at 24,000 cfs on 22 May. Water temperatures reached 25°C by late July in 1996 but were above 20°C earlier in 1994.

**Non-native fishes.** Non-native cyprinids (red shiner, fathead minnow, sand shiner) and channel catfish dominated backwater and low-velocity samples and in Desolation Canyon were far more abundant than in the Westwater Canyon reach of the Colorado River, where native fishes dominated. Flow manipulation alone was judged insufficient to control these competitors.

## Key Findings

**Chub reproduction and nursery habitat (Reports B and C)**
- Day et al. sampled 729 backwater and low-velocity habitats (June 1994–September 1996); shoreline eddies were the predominant class (36.2%, n=264). Constricted-reach eddies (n=193) and secondary channels (n=187) were next.
- Mean YOY chub CPUE was highest in 1994 (10.31/100 m³), second in 1996 (3.28) and lowest in 1995 (0.31). Captures of all native species were severely depressed in 1995.
- Chart and Lentsch's single annual trip found the highest YOY chub catch in August 1993 (n=162; 14.73/100 m² of habitat). Their 1994 July–August trip captured only nine YOY chubs in 56 seine hauls, which Day et al. considered anomalous, illustrating the risk of a single annual sampling trip.
- The 1994 YOY chub grew best: mean total length 60.8 mm in September, with growth of 0.5 mm/day; 1995 growth was 55.6% of 1994 (33.8 mm).
- Chubs used backwaters that were longer, wider, deeper, greater in area and volume, and more turbid than unused backwaters (mean depth used 0.59 m, nearly identical to Valdez and Clemmer's 1982 value). Preference for backwater type was not significant, and discriminant models could not classify chub-use backwaters better than about 60–65% accuracy.
- Back-calculated spawning dates for the 1993 cohort fell between 8 and 16 July and 16 June, on the descending limb at flows of about 14,000 to 16,700 cfs. Growth of Age 0 chubs averaged 0.36 mm/day from mid-July to late August 1993 at mean flow 2,924 cfs.
- Juvenile recruitment: the 1993 and 1994 cohorts recruited to Age 1+ best; Day et al. recorded the highest captures of age-1+ chubs in June 1994. Overwinter flows averaged 3,339 cfs in winter 1993–94 and were proposed as a partial explanation for better 1993 overwinter survival.

**Colorado Pikeminnow (Reports B and C)**
- Colorado Pikeminnow were the most abundant native species in samples for the 1995 (n=97) and 1996 (n=1,729) cohorts; the 1994 cohort (435 fish) ranked third among natives. The 1996 catch was split between August (n=812) and September (n=915).
- Mean total length of 1994 YOY rose from 24.9 mm (July) to 57.7 mm (September); 1995 YOY were first encountered in September at 24.8 mm; a 1996 subset averaged 38.4 mm (n=915).
- Pikeminnow used secondary-channel backwaters with greater volume, shallower mouths, and less cover; mean depth used was 0.47 m but mean surface area only 558 m².
- Greatest YOY pikeminnow production occurred at spring peaks near 25,000 cfs (1993 and 1996), with the 1996 cohort probably the most abundant (90 days above 6,000 cfs, peak 22 May, below 6,000 cfs by 7 July). CPUE showed a strong negative correlation with duration of flows above 75% of the spring peak (r² = 0.49).
- Monitoring trips were often timed too early to detect pikeminnow reproduction; the 1993 trip, delayed to late August, yielded 203 YOY, the highest CPUE of any monitoring trip.

**Spawning and adult populations (Report C)**
- At Rabbit Valley (RK 238), 89.7% of 39 pikeminnow handled over two nights were ripe males. In 1997 the Joe Hutch site (RK 256) yielded the greatest concentration ever seen in Desolation/Gray (n=41; 88% ripe males).
- No new spawning area was found. The Three Fords spawning area was redefined as an 18 km reach (RK 256–238), with the focus of activity shifting annually.
- Adult chub catch rates at four trend sites stayed below 0.2 fish/net-hour and were lowest in 1996; the population appeared rejuvenated in the late 1980s, declined after low flows (1988–1992), and was increasing again by 1997 after recruitment in 1993–1995.
- Chub recruitment was documented in 1994 and 1995; the 1993 year class recruited to juvenile size in the most substantial numbers since 1989. Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests showed the population structure varied significantly over time.
- Nine recaptured chubs were all taken at their original capture location; three chubs with *G. elegans* tendencies were collected.

**Sympatric species (Report C)**
- Flannelmouth sucker catch rates declined from 1989 through 1994, then rose sharply in 1995 (greatest at RK 295.7); population structure appeared to rebound from 1994–1996 after poor recruitment through 1993.
- Channel catfish were the most abundant main-channel species every year and nearly twice as abundant as *Gila* spp. over six years of monitoring. They appeared to fail to reproduce or recruit in 1993 but recruited successfully in 1995, the highest-flow year.

**Flow recommendations**
- Definitive flow relationships could not be established. The two most successful *Gila* spp. flow years (1993 and 1994) were very different, and 1996 produced a moderately abundant cohort with a 90-day peak.
- During moderate to wet years, peak flows near 25,000 cfs with the peak lasting 60 days or more appear to benefit both *Gila* spp. and Colorado Pikeminnow.
- Extended peak flows and low water temperatures suppressed both chub and pikeminnow numbers. Recommended single-year regime: avoid extended high flows and cold water during peak run-off, reach summer base flows by mid-July, and maintain summer and late-season flows between about 2,000 and 4,000 cfs to sustain backwater and shoreline habitat.
- Future work: continue monitoring in Desolation Canyon; protect the Three Fords spawning site; sample YOY fish twice yearly (early August and late September) in both floodplain and canyon reaches; estimate chub population size at trend sites; and incorporate fish-community monitoring into the Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program at flows of 7,000–8,000 cfs, avoiding flows above 10,000 cfs.
