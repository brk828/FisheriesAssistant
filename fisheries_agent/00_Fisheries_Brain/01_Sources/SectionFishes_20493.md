# Endangered Freshwater Fishes: Case Histories and Conservation Lessons

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation #cyprinodontidae #percidae #cyprinidae #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a conservation-focused essay by [[Robert Rush Miller]] on the imperilment of fishes, with emphasis on freshwater species. It argues that most fishes are hard to observe and poorly known, that freshwater fishes are especially vulnerable to habitat modification, and that species restricted to a few springs or stream systems face the greatest extinction risk. Five case histories illustrate the point: the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]], the [[Asprete]], the [[Maluti]], the [[Miyako Tanago]], and the [[Amur Sturgeon]]. A short 1996 email about the generic reassignment of Mesoamerican cichlids is appended, but it does not address conservation.

## Background & Description

The author notes that, apart from reef fishes, most species are difficult to observe in nature. Many are small and inconspicuous, so information on them is often lacking or incomplete. Conservation of fishes therefore operates under a weaker information base than that available for birds or mammals, where new species are rarely described and status is better known.

Two reasons are given for the vulnerability of freshwater fishes:

- **Stable environment of the sea:** Most marine fishes live in a more stable environment, so their declines are less visible than those of freshwater fishes, many of which have very limited distributions.
- **Habitat modification:** Freshwater fishes are more exposed to impoundment by dams, domestic and industrial pollution, channelization, drainage, pumping, and introduction of exotic species that compete with or prey on native fishes.

Endangered fishes are typically those confined to particular stream systems, circumscribed regions, or a few spring pools, such as the pupfishes of [[Southwestern North America]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Devil's Hole, Nevada/California:** A cave-like pool about 15 m below the surface of a barren hill, warm (33 °C), clear, with no outlet and a deep internal source. Divers reached 100 m without seeing the bottom. The pool surface is about 6.5 m by 3–5 m. Breeding and rearing occur on a rock ledge about 1.5 m long, covered by only a few centimeters to one meter of water. Sunlight sustains an algal food crop in spring and summer; when light declines in fall and winter, pupfish numbers crash. Most fish are probably annual.

**Asprete, Romania:** A small, bottom-dwelling perch relative that lives in clear, flowing streams among gravel or under stones. It is sensitive to changes in water velocity and depth. Deforestation of stream margins warms the water and allows minnows to enter, which compete with the asprete for food and space.

**Maluti, Lesotho/South Africa:** A trout-like minnow adapted to clear, swift mountain streams. It depends on high-altitude catchments with intact vegetation. Road-building clearance, overgrazing, soil erosion, siltation, and water warming are the main threats, with exotic trout a lesser pressure.

**Miyako Tanago, Japan:** A small bitterling of Tokyo-area brooks. Like other bitterlings, the female lays eggs in freshwater mussels through a long ovipositor. Urban sprawl fills and pollutes its brooks.

**Amur Sturgeon, USSR:** A freshwater-only sturgeon of the Amur River drainage and estuary, valued for caviar. Overfishing drove a severe decline.

## Key Findings

- **Devil's Hole Pupfish (Cyprinodon diabolis):**
  - Confined to a single spring; probably the smallest range of any vertebrate species.
  - Diver counts during 1972–1980 ranged from about 150 in winter to about 400 in summer.
  - A 40-acre tract including the pool was set aside within Death Valley National Monument in 1952 by President Harry Truman's proclamation.
  - A ranching operation starting in 1967 pumped groundwater from the aquifer faster than it could be replenished, lowering the water level.
  - The population fell from about 400 in 1968 to about 300. Estimates of the minimum viable size ranged from 150 to 200 fish year-round.
  - A 1973 court injunction temporarily halted pumping. In 1976 the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously ruled that federal reservation of land carries rights to the surface and groundwater needed to preserve it, including wildlife. The water level has risen since the ruling.
  - Responses included a 1970 Pupfish Task Force and the formation of the Desert Fishes Council.

- **Asprete (Romanichthys valsanicola):**
  - A member of the perch family discovered in 1956; described as the most endangered fish in Europe.
  - Maximum size under five inches; color pattern of broad dark saddles and strongly barred fins.
  - Originally known from three Danube tributaries in Romania. It became extinct in the Argeșul after dam construction, and is believed extinct in the Riul Doamnei since about 1964.
  - In the Vâlsan River it was thought to have vanished but was observed in 1975–1977 upstream of its former range. Its entire distribution is now probably one 25 km stretch of the Vâlsan with no villages.
  - Threats include dam construction, streamside deforestation, repeated herbicide poisoning, and removal of riverbed stones for building.
  - Strict government regulations now protect the last stretch.

- **Maluti (Oreodaimon quathlambae):**
  - A cyprinid discovered in 1967 in the Umkomazana River, Drakensberg Range, South Africa's Natal. It disappeared from that river a year later, attributed to siltation and possibly to competition and predation by exotic trout.
  - About two years later it was rediscovered in the Tsoelikana River, about 50 km southwest, on a Lesotho plateau. This is one of three high-altitude tributaries of the Orange River system where it survives.
  - Part of the Tsoelikana runs through Sehlabathebe National Park. The upper catchment is now fully protected, and habitat is reported to be in excellent condition.
  - A proposed dam would eliminate the population. Avoiding habitat degradation is considered the best protection.

- **Miyako Tanago (Tanakia tanago):**
  - Proclaimed a national monument by the Japanese government in 1974.
  - By 1977 fewer than 1,000 survived in the wild. A further 150 individuals, some captive-bred, were held in a pond at the National Science Museum in Tokyo.
  - Proposed remedies: a protected farm area within the natural range, supplemented by captive breeding.

- **Amur Sturgeon (Acipenser schrencki):**
  - Freshwater-only; commercial fishing prohibited by the Soviet government in 1958 after overfishing.
  - The author cites this as a case where a simple, seriously enforced action can resolve endangerment.

- **Overall conclusion:** Fishes with very limited ranges, especially in springs and single stream systems, are most at risk. Effective protection combines legal water-rights action, habitat protection, and, where needed, captive propagation.
