# How to Know Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #soleidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt comes from the second edition of *How To Know the Freshwater Fishes* by [[Samuel Eddy]], part of the Pictured-Key Nature Series published by Wm. C. Brown Company (Dubuque, Iowa; copyright 1957, revised 1969). It contains front matter (copyright, series list, and a foreword), a fragment of the identification keys covering the minnow genera *Gila* and *Ptychocheilus* and the dace genus *Clinostomus*, a brief entry for the sole family (Soleidae), and a bibliography of regional and general fish identification works. The foreword explains the revision goals: updating names, adding newly described species, and documenting exotic and introduced fishes in the United States.

## Background & Description

The book is a field identification guide built on dichotomous keys with illustrations. The foreword states that the keys follow the pattern of the first edition, but that some genera group species by general appearance rather than by true phylogenetic relationship. Wherever possible, the keys rely on external characters. The author notes that the first edition appeared about ten years before this revision.

The key fragment describes diagnostic characters such as:

- **Lateral line scale counts**, a major separating trait (for example, under 60 versus over 60 in the genus *Gila* key).
- **Dorsal fin origin** relative to the pelvic fins (behind, above, or over).
- **Fin ray counts** (dorsal, anal) and **pharyngeal teeth** formulas.
- **Body coloration** (dusky or olivaceous above, silvery or pale below).

Representative species described in the fragment include:

- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta*): 12–15 inches; several subspecies in the [[Colorado River]] drainage, with *G. robusta elegans* in the main channel.
- [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*): up to 20 inches; lower [[Sacramento River]] drainage, California.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): nearly 5 feet, described as the largest American minnow; lower Colorado River drainage.
- [[Umpqua Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus umpquae*): Umpqua and Siuslaw drainages, Oregon.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): distinctly humped back before the dorsal fin; upper Colorado River drainage.
- [[Leatherside Chub]] (*Gila copei*): 6 inches; Bonneville and Snake River drainages in Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming.
- [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila nigrescens*): 6–12 inches; Rio Grande drainage of Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas; introduced into the lower Colorado River.
- [[Pecos Chub]] (*Gila pandora*): similar to the Rio Grande chub but with fewer than 67 scales; Pecos River basin.
- [[Blue Chub]] (*Gila coerulea*): 12 inches; Klamath Lake drainage.
- [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*): 12–15 inches; native to the Bonneville drainage, introduced elsewhere in the West.
- [[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*): many subspecies, from the Columbia River system in Oregon to the Sacramento River and the Lahontan basin of Nevada.
- [[Thicktail Chub]] (*Gila crassicauda*): 12 inches; Sacramento River, California.
- [[Redside Dace]] (*Clinostomus elongatus*): 5 inches; southern Minnesota through the eastern Great Lakes and Ohio River drainages to northeastern Oklahoma.

The sole family (Soleidae) is described as small flatfishes with poorly developed or absent pectoral fins. Typical length is 5–6 inches.

- [[Hogchoker]] (*Trinectes maculatus*): common on the Atlantic coast; brown with about 8 cross bars; lacks both pectoral fins.
- [[Lined Sole]] (*Achirus lineatus*): enters freshwater along the southern Atlantic and Gulf coasts; dark with about 8 cross bars and a small right pectoral fin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The foreword addresses the biogeography and human impacts on US freshwater fish faunas:

- **Undescribed diversity:** The author estimates that more than forty undescribed species, mostly [[Minnows]] and darters, are known to exist. Some closely related species may eventually be reclassified as subspecies or varieties.
- **Alaska:** Its statehood added only a few species to the US fauna. Most are west coast species that range farther north.
- **Hawaii:** No strictly freshwater fish is native. Several marine gobies and sleepers live part of their lives in Hawaiian streams, and the [[Milkfish]] (*Chanos chanos*) may enter fresh water. Many mainland game fishes have been introduced successfully.
- **Exotic and introduced species:** Long-established Asian species not covered by the key include the [[Snakehead]] (*Ophiocephalus striatus*), the Chinese catfish (*Clarias fuscus*), and the ricefield eel (*Fluta alba*). Some African [[Cichlids]] and South American [[Poeciliids]] have also been introduced. Most aquarium discards do not survive permanently, so they are excluded.
- **Range complications:** Intentional or accidental movement of native game and other fishes outside their original ranges has further complicated distribution records.

The Western isolated-water forms mentioned in the key include the arroyo chub (*Gila orcutti*) of southern California coastal streams, the Sonora chub (*Gila ditaenia*) of southern Arizona and Mexico, and the Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*), known only from San Bernardino Creek, Cochise County, Arizona.

## Key Findings

- **Revision scope:** The second edition updates names, incorporates new species descriptions and synonymies, and corrects errors in the first edition's keys and distribution records.
- **Faunal estimate:** More than forty undescribed freshwater species, mainly minnows and darters, were believed to exist at the time of revision.
- **Key structure:** Identification relies mainly on external characters. Some genera (for example, several *Gila* groups) are arranged by appearance rather than strict phylogeny.
- **Diagnostic thresholds:** The *Gila* key uses specific lateral line scale counts (for instance, 67 or more separating the Rio Grande chub from the Pecos chub; 60 separating the two main groups) and dorsal fin position to distinguish species.
- **Western endemics:** Several squawfish and chub species are restricted to particular Colorado, Columbia, Sacramento, or Lahontan drainages.
- **Introduced fauna:** Alaska's addition added few species. Hawaii has no native freshwater fishes, and exotic species such as the snakehead have established populations.
- **Soles:** Soleidae in US fresh and brackish waters are small, with reduced pectoral fins. The hogchoker and lined sole are the main examples.
- **Reference base:** The bibliography lists roughly 55 works, ranging from classic surveys (Jordan and Evermann, 1896–1900) to regional guides for states and provinces (for example, [[Fishes of Ohio]] by Trautman, 1957; [[Fishes of the Great Lakes Region]] by Hubbs and Lagler, 1964) and checklists such as Bailey et al. (1960) for standard common names.
