# Review Letter: Strawberry Valley Environmental Assessment — Fish Fauna Comments

#ichthyology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Gila #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary
This document is a peer-review letter by ichthyologist [[Clark Hubbs]] critiquing a Draft Environmental Assessment of the [[Strawberry Reservoir]] system in Utah. Hubbs argues that the assessment relies too narrowly on [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] survey data and omits historical distributional evidence indicating that several native fish species — including [[Catostomus discobolus]], [[Catostomus latipinnis]], [[Gila robusta]], and [[Gila elegans]] — were once part of the [[Strawberry-Duchesne System]]. He contends that the decline of the native [[Cutthroat Trout]] stock may be linked to displacement by exotic congeners rather than solely habitat degradation, and questions why restoration efforts do not consider reestablishing native fish assemblages instead of relying on non-native cutthroat stock. He also raises a statistical critique of the game-fish vs. nongame-fish catch-rate data used to assess trout population trends, noting inconsistencies between percent game fish and fish-per-hour (fph) catch metrics across years (1973–1986).

## Background & Description
No direct morphological descriptions are provided in this letter; it is a policy/scientific critique rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. Species mentioned are referenced only via range and identity, not physical description:
- **[[Catostomus discobolus]]** (Bluehead Sucker) — family [[Catostomidae]], historically reported from the Strawberry/Duchesne system per faunal atlas records.
- **[[Catostomus latipinnis]]** (Flannelmouth Sucker) — family [[Catostomidae]], co-occurring historically with *C. discobolus*.
- **[[Gila robusta]]** (Roundtail Chub) — family [[Cyprinidae]], inferred native based on distributional data.
- **[[Gila elegans]]** (Bonytail Chub) — family [[Cyprinidae]], inferred native based on distributional data.
- **Native Cutthroat Trout stock** (family [[Salmonidae]]) — explicitly noted in the EA as extirpated from the system; a hatchery/non-native cutthroat stock currently maintains the fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The [[Strawberry Reservoir]] and its tributaries are characterized as an **artificial reservoir system imposed on a riverine ecosystem**, a recurring theme in Hubbs's broader critique of reservoir fisheries.
- Hubbs distinguishes trophic levels among "nongame" fish: **chubs** (*Gila* spp.) may be trophic equivalents to game fish (trout), whereas **suckers** (*Catostomus* spp.) occupy a **different trophic level** (benthic/detritivorous), and should not be lumped together in catch-rate analyses.
- The historical native fish assemblage (suckers + chubs + native cutthroat) is hypothesized to have coexisted in a functionally balanced native community prior to species introductions.
- Reservoir fish and invertebrate assemblages are described generally as **non-evolutionarily balanced**, composed of whichever organisms gained access to the impounded system — contrasting with naturally assembled riverine communities.
- Land-use changes in tributaries are acknowledged as potentially beneficial to restoring more natural conditions for the fishery, though the letter notes the EA fails to model outcomes if only land-use improvements (without other interventions) were implemented.

## Key Findings
- **Historical native fish records** (via distributional "Atlas" data) suggest that *Catostomus discobolus*, *C. latipinnis*, *Gila robusta*, and *G. elegans* were part of the native fauna of the Strawberry/Duchesne drainage — a fact omitted from the Draft EA, which relied solely on modern UDWR survey data.
- **Native cutthroat trout extinction**: The EA reportedly confirms the native cutthroat race no longer exists in the system; Hubbs suggests restoring the original native fish stock/assemblage as a potentially superior management strategy rather than continuing non-native cutthroat stocking.
- **Data inconsistency identified in Table 1**: 
  - % game fish varies substantially year to year (e.g., 63% in 1977 vs. 15.5% in 1978).
  - This does not align proportionally with reported trout catch rates (fish per hour, fph): 0.95 fph in 1977 vs. 1.40 fph in 1978.
  - Hubbs argues **game fish catch rate per gill net hour** is a more reliable metric of trout population status than composite % game fish figures, since nongame fish are not trophic equivalents (particularly suckers vs. chubs).
- **Ranked annual fish catch rates (fph)** cited from best to worst: 1973 > 1980 > 1981 > 1974 > 1975 > 1976 > 1986 > 1982 > 1978 > 1979 > 1984 > 1977 (lowest).
- **Management implication**: Displacement of native catostomids and cyprinids by exotic congeners (biological, not legal, sense of "exotic") may be a contributing factor to reduced trout fishery performance, an alternative hypothesis to purely land-use-driven habitat degradation.
- **General ecological caution**: Reservoirs are inherently artificial ecosystems, and their fish/invertebrate assemblages typically reflect colonization opportunity rather than co-evolved community structure — a caveat relevant to interpreting any survey-based management conclusions for [[Strawberry Reservoir]].
