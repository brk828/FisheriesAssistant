# Evaluation of the Fifteen-Inch Minimum Size Limit on Pickerel in Three New Jersey Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 New Jersey Division of Fish and Game report (Miscellaneous Report #15, by Roland F. Smith and Richard W. Gross) evaluates whether a [[Fifteen-Inch Minimum Size Limit]] on [[Chain Pickerel]] (*[[Esox Niger]]*) achieved its goals in New Jersey lakes. The regulation took effect in May 1951. The study found that the limit largely failed to produce larger pickerel in anglers' creels, did not increase harvest in lakes with mediocre growth, and that heavy [[Ice Fishing]] can depress pickerel populations. The authors recommended an experimental "no size" limit on two lakes and keeping the fifteen-inch limit on [[Lake Hopatcong]] for further study.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Chain pickerel, a major game and predatory fish in New Jersey. It occurs in nearly every aquatic habitat in the state, from northern glacial lakes to tidal estuaries, and is often the dominant predator. In acidic southern waters too poor for [[Largemouth Bass]], it is the only major predator.
- **Growth:** Growth is rapid in favorable waters. New Jersey held the world record for the species until the year before publication. Growth varies widely among lakes, and females often grow faster than males.
- **Regulatory history:**
  - 1676: earliest law affecting pickerel, a hunting and fishing access law.
  - 1870: fish warden force established.
  - 1873: Act restricting nets in stocked lakes.
  - 1901: open season set at May 1 to November 30; ice fishing banned.
  - 1904: 12-inch minimum size; ice fishing allowed in January.
  - 1915: daily ice creel limited to ten fish.
  - 1922–1923: season opening moved between June 15 and May 20.
  - 1935: statewide minimum raised to 14 inches, then reverted to 12 inches in 1945.
  - 1939: first creel limit of ten fish for open-water anglers.
  - 1954: ice season extended to the entire month of January.
- **Other states:** Pennsylvania adopted a 15-inch limit in 1951 and reported that fishermen felt the change improved catches. Connecticut tried 16 inches, then reduced it to 14 inches. Massachusetts moved to 14 inches, and New York kept 12 inches statewide except for one 14-inch lake. Appendix I summarizes pickerel regulations in 15 states from Maine to Alabama, with wide variation in season and creel limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake classes:** Surveys begun in 1950 showed pickerel growth varied greatly across New Jersey. A 12-inch limit protected nearly all pickerel in acidic Lake Absegami, while Lake Hopatcong had exceptionally rapid growth. Cranberry Lake had "mediocre" growth. Four acidic lakes had all size restrictions removed, and three lakes were set aside to test effects on bass.
- **Habitat use:** In lakes with landlocked alewife (*[[Landlocked Alewife]]*, reported as *Alosa pseudoharengus*), large pickerel cruised the outer edge of submerged vegetation at 15 to 30 feet depth. Large [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] shared this habitat.
- **Trophic interactions:** Pickerel and trout (stocked at Lake Hopatcong) fed largely on alewife and competed for food and space. The authors suggested this could limit pickerel carrying capacity.
- **Vegetation change:** At Budd and Musconetcong lakes, aquatic vegetation spread so much that open-water fishing became nearly impossible. This reduced open-water pressure in those lakes.
- **Study design:** Most data came from [[Creel Census]] during January ice fishing, with spring (May 20 to June 15) and summer 1954 work added. Population estimates used [[Tagging Study]] data with Petersen and Schnabel methods. Hooking and natural mortality were partly tested.

## Key Findings

1. **Limit ineffective for larger fish:** Under the conditions tested, the fifteen-inch limit did not produce larger pickerel above 15 inches in anglers' creels.
2. **Lake Hopatcong:** A statistically significant increase in mean size of 15-inch-plus fish occurred in 1953, but it was not "socially significant." The 1955 season showed a significant drop in mean size. Ice harvest more than doubled in recent years, though the authors could not separate the effect of the limit from increased effort. In 1954, 65% of hooked pickerel were sublegal. In 1955, 53% were throwbacks. Combined catch estimates were 6,690 fish (1954) and 7,340 fish (1955), with combined weight estimated at about 6,520 lb and 6,600 lb.
3. **Cranberry Lake:** About 1,200 to 1,600 pickerel of 12 inches and above were estimated. Of these, 9 to 12% were 15 inches or larger, and 35 to 40% of those were harvested in January 1955. A 12-inch limit would have allowed harvest of about 24 to 32% of the population. Between 70 and 74% of pickerel fell in the 12- to 15-inch range, and most males did not reach legal size until their fourth winter. Female-to-male ratios in the creel reached about 4:1 in the last two winters.
4. **Swartswood Lake:** Growth was slightly below Lake Hopatcong's. No significant increase in large pickerel or fishing success was found. Female dominance in the legal catch was also observed.
5. **Budd Lake collapse:** Harvest of about 3,500 pickerel in 1952 (3.4 fish per man) fell to about 300 in 1953 (0.6 per man). Anglers largely abandoned the lake, and the population had not fully recovered. The authors estimated the 1952 harvest of about nine 12-inch-plus pickerel per acre may have pushed the population below a critical threshold.
6. **Tagging and vulnerability:** Of 326 pickerel of 10 to 15 inches tagged at Cranberry Lake, 108 (33%) were reported caught once, 33 (10%) twice, and 3 (1%) three times. About 31% of once-caught fish were hooked again, and 9% of twice-caught fish were taken a third time.
7. **Hooking mortality:** Results were inconclusive. Tipup and rod-and-reel captures did not differ significantly at the 95% confidence level, but sample sizes were small.
8. **Natural mortality:** Pickerel aged four years and older rarely exceeded 20% of the catch and sometimes fell to 5%. The authors agreed with [[Stroud]] (1955) that pickerel mortality is rapid, so unharvested fish seldom survive to support later fisheries.
9. **Growth by sex (angler returns):** Females grew 0.207 inch on average, compared with 0.063 inch for males, a difference of 0.144 inch. Tag returns alone showed no significant difference (0.089 vs. 0.076 inch). The authors suspected faster female growth was real, though tag effects and measurement errors are possible.
10. **Ice versus open-water harvest:** The ice harvest is believed to be at least twice the open-water harvest on heavily fished New Jersey lakes. At Stillwater Pond, Connecticut, ice anglers were about 13% of effort but took 40% of the harvest (Webster 1943).
11. **Carrying capacity:** The data suggest pickerel biomass may be at a maximum density in Lake Hopatcong. Carrying capacity may have been limited by [[Trout Stocking]] and competition with pickerel for alewife prey.
12. **Panfish and bass:** Panfish catch fell when pickerel catch rose, which the authors attributed to shifting angler effort rather than panfish decline. Bass angling success increased at Budd Lake as pickerel declined.

**Conclusions and Recommendations**

- Fourteen conclusions were drawn. The main ones are that the fifteen-inch limit is unnecessary in lakes not heavily fished, that heavy ice fishing can collapse pickerel populations, and that regulation may be needed to maintain a safe density in heavily fished lakes.
- Recommended actions:
  1. Test an experimental "no size" limit at Cranberry and Big Swartswood lakes.
  2. Keep the fifteen-inch limit at Lake Hopatcong and study it for at least two more years.
  3. Study hooking and natural mortality further.
  4. Study lake carrying capacity.
  5. Study the effects of angler exploitation.
