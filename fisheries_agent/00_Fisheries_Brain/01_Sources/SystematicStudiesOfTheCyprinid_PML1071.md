# Systematic Studies of the Cyprinid Genus Gila in the Upper Colorado River Basin, and a Note on Lampanyctus jordani

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #myctophidae

## Executive Summary

This issue excerpt contains two papers. The main paper (Holden and Stalnaker, 1970) studies the systematics of the subgenus [[Gila]] in the [[Colorado River Basin]] using numerical taxonomy ([[Taximetrics]]) on 309 specimens. The authors conclude that the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), currently treated as subspecies, are well separated morphologically, ecologically and apparently reproductively, and should be treated as two species. The relationship of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) to *G. elegans* remains unresolved because of intergrade forms. The paper also restricts the name *seminuda* to the Virgin River population. A shorter second paper by Wisner describes the myctophid *Lampanyctus jordani* from the North Pacific, including its distribution and its placement among related species.

## Background & Description

The cyprinid genus *Gila* was divided into three subgenera (*Gila*, *Siphateles* and *Snyderichthys*), with *Richardsonius* sometimes added as a fourth. The study focuses on subgenus *Gila* in the Colorado River basin, emphasizing three forms: *G. robusta*, *G. elegans* and *G. cypha*.

The synonymy reviewed in the paper shows a long taxonomic history. *Gila elegans* was described by Baird and Girard (1853) from the Zuñi River, New Mexico, and was later placed as a subspecies of *G. robusta* by Miller (1946). *Gila cypha* was described by Miller (1946) from the Grand Canyon, Arizona. Many other names, including *G. emoryi*, *G. gracilis*, *G. grahami* and *G. nacrea*, were synonymized by Jordan and Evermann (1896) and others.

**Morphological methods:** Most measurements and counts followed [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1958). X-rays were used for vertebral and fin-ray counts, and vertebral counts excluded the four Weberian ossicles. Body measurements (except eye diameter) were expressed in thousandths of standard length. Gill rakers were counted as the sum of upper and lower limbs on the anterior left gill arch.

**Taximetric analysis:** Nineteen characters (Table 1) were scored, and similarity values (C) were computed for all specimen pairs and clustered by levels using the [[Taximetrics]] program (Sokal and Sneath-type numerical taxonomy, following [[Estabrook and Rogers]] 1966).

**Specimen localities:** Specimens came from the upper Green River (including Flaming Gorge and Desolation Canyon), the White River, the San Juan River, the Colorado River in Colorado (including the Gunnison River), [[Lake Powell]], [[Lee's Ferry]] and [[Glen Canyon]], the [[Virgin River]] in Utah, [[Lake Mohave]], and the Black River in the Gila River division of Arizona.

**Table 1 comparison (mature fish, 200 mm SL or larger):**
- Dorsal and anal fin rays: *robusta* 8–9 and *elegans* 10, a clear separation.
- Upper jaw length: *robusta* 83–117, *elegans* 56–84.
- Least depth of caudal peduncle: *robusta* 51–81, *elegans* 35–49.
- Head depth and other characters also differ.
- Thirteen of 19 characters show distinct separation, with four more showing lesser differences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors link the present fauna to the geologic history of the Colorado basin. During the Miocene, the Colorado Plateau was uplifted, which probably isolated the basin's fishes and helps explain the high level of endemism. The Kaibab Upwarp divided the basin into two systems: the [[Hualapai Drainage System]] to the west and southwest, and the [[Ancestral Colorado River]] in the upper basin in the early-mid Pliocene. The two systems were later connected when the river cut through the Grand Canyon. The authors suggest that this large-river environment acted as a barrier to small-stream lower-basin forms, which may explain the disjunct distributions of genera such as *Plagopterus* and *Catostomus (Pantosteus) clarki*.

**Life history and habitat differences (from Vanicek and Kramer, 1969):**
- *G. robusta* is more piscivorous than *G. elegans*.
- *G. robusta* puts on its largest growth increment in its first year, while *G. elegans* does so in its fourth year.
- *G. robusta* becomes heavier than *G. elegans* as length increases.
- *G. robusta* is found in the upper portions of main rivers, while *G. elegans* occupies different preferred habitat.

**Status:** *G. robusta elegans* (bonytail) was once widely distributed in the Colorado River basin but is now reported extinct in the lower basin. *G. cypha* is extremely rare. Its extreme forms are most abundant in the Colorado River of northern Arizona and southern Utah, and no recent specimens have been collected in the lower basin.

## Key Findings

1. **Morphology and clustering:** At Level 2 (similarity 0.94737), 89% of *G. elegans* and 81% of *G. robusta* had clustered. The two forms were largely homogeneous, with members from all sampled geographic areas. This indicates two distinct evolutionary lines rather than parallel evolution.

2. **Species status:** *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* are "better considered two species" because they are well separated morphologically, ecologically and apparently reproductively.

3. **Intergrading complex:** The *elegans–cypha* relationship is obscured by intergrade forms. Of 60 fish from the Lake Powell area, 58 fell into the extreme category. Only seven extreme forms were taken in the upper Green River. Six Desolation Canyon fish are considered intergrades nearer to *elegans*. Because of this unresolved complex, the authors retain the present taxonomy (*G. elegans* and *G. cypha*) and call for further study of the lower Green and middle Colorado rivers, plus serologic and genetic work.

4. ***G. cypha* status:** The authors describe *cypha* as an elusive entity. Its nuchal hump and fleshy snout are most extreme in the Lake Powell and Lee's Ferry fish. Its similarity to *elegans* and the apparent gradation between the two suggest *cypha* evolved from an *elegans*-like form, though this remains speculative.

5. ***G. robusta seminuda*:** Miller (1946) examined five type specimens of *seminuda* (collected by Cope and Yarrow, 1875), from the Virgin River. Ellis (1914) considered it a *robusta* with reduced squamation. The authors found that some specimens matched this pattern but agreed with typical *robusta* in all other characters, and they treat the scale reduction as phenotypic variation. They conclude that *seminuda* is not a distinct entity throughout the basin and restrict the name to the Virgin River population. Only six fish from the Virgin River were examined, which strongly limits conclusions. The taximetrics placed these six fish close to *robusta* but somewhat distinct, and their vertebral and gill-raker counts differ from both *robusta* and *elegans*. Additional Virgin River material is needed.

6. **Biogeography:** The authors propose a "two basin" hypothesis of speciation, supported by several large-river forms (such as *Ptychocheilus* and *Catostomus discobolus*) that are presumed to have evolved in the upper basin during the Pliocene, along with small-stream lower-basin forms.

**Lampanyctus jordani (Wisner):** This myctophid, described by Gilbert (1915) from the Pacific off Nemuro, Hokkaido, is figured and its phylogenetic position defined. The author places it in a group characterized by prominent cheek photophores and, except in one species, a small luminous gland on the anterior adipose fin base. Its distribution spans the North Pacific from southern Japan through most of the subarctic realm, with one record from southern California, and it occurs in the Okhotsk and Bering seas. Meristic characters are nearly uniform across the North Pacific, but significant morphological differences exist between Bering Sea and North Pacific specimens, which the author suggests may result from a size difference between the two groups. No significant relationship was found between capture depth and either specimen size or stomach eversion.
