# Temperature Effects on Early Life Stages of Big-River Fishes of the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Robert W. Clarkson]] (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) and [[Michael R. Childs]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department) tests how water temperature affects the growth, development, and behavior of early life stages of four native big-river fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Declines of these species have been linked partly to cold spring-summer releases from hypolimnial-release dams such as [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

Fish were reared at 10, 14, and 20°C. Growth was significantly lower at 10 and 14°C than at 20°C for the suckers, and larval-to-juvenile transformation was delayed. Abrupt transfer from 20°C to 10°C caused "cold coma" (loss of equilibrium) in young larvae. The authors argue that these effects lengthen larval exposure to mortality sources and raise mortality risk. They conclude that thermal modification of dam discharge is a minimum necessary step for recovery, but that comprehensive control of [[Nonindigenous Species]] will likely also be needed.

## Background & Description

**Study species**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, Cyprinidae): a large-bodied cyprinid of the basin's big rivers.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*, Cyprinidae): a cyprinid that, after dam closure, completes its life cycle in only one Grand Canyon tributary.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae): described as extremely rare and functionally extirpated in the Grand Canyon system, with no evidence of reproduction.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*, Catostomidae): mainstem reproduction reduced or eliminated in parts of the basin.

**Historical context**
- After [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed in 1962, native fishes failed to reproduce in the 104 km Green River reach below the dam to the Yampa River confluence. Poisoning in 1962 likely hastened their disappearance from this reach.
- Colorado Squawfish, Roundtail Chub, and Bonytail were extirpated between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Lake Mead after dam closure in 1963. Most successful native reproduction in the [[Grand Canyon]] system now occurs only in tributaries.
- Dams altered temperature, discharge, and sediment transport, facilitated [[Nonindigenous Species]], and blocked migration routes.

**Study site and methods**
- Experiments were run at [[Bubbling Ponds State Hatchery]] near Cornville, Arizona, using pathogen-free artesian well water (18.6°C, pH 7.6, 366 µS/cm, dissolved oxygen 4.3–7.3 mg/L).
- Gametes came from wild fish (Humpback Chub from the [[Little Colorado River]], April 1993; Flannelmouth Sucker from near [[Lee's Ferry]], May 1995, at 12°C river temperature) and from hatchery broodstock (Colorado Squawfish, May 1993; Razorback Sucker from Dexter National Fish Hatchery, March 1994).
- Three temperatures were chosen: 10°C (cold tailwater), 14°C (mixed tributary-tailwater flows and backwaters), and 20°C (control and near-optimal for embryonic development).
- Three age groups (about 7, 14, and 41 days post-hatch) were tested to represent different times of entry into cold tailwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime and dam effects**
- Hypolimnial-release dams depress spring-summer water temperature. This inhibits spawning and embryonic development and reduces growth and swimming performance. Cold conditions in tailwaters create "essentially perpetual near-winter" conditions for drifting early life stages.
- Backwaters, eddies, and tailwater mixing zones are important rearing habitats for early life stages in the basin. Early life stages may drift, be carried by floods, or disperse from spawning streams into cold mainstem reaches.

**Mortality pathways**
- Delayed transformation prolongs exposure to food scarcity, hydrological disturbance, predation, and parasitism.
- Cold coma can increase predation risk and cause death through abrasion, entrainment in strong currents and turbulence, or burial in substrate.
- Cited field work suggests that Humpback Chub under 52 mm TL may never survive residence in the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.

**Management tension**
- Warmer dam releases would benefit native fishes but may also favor [[Nonindigenous Species]], possibly to the detriment of natives.
- Suggested complementary measures include high-magnitude floods, taxon-specific piscicides, segregation of native and nonnative fisheries, restored migration corridors, and public education. Hatchery production and instream flow acquisition are described as stopgap measures.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth is significantly reduced at low temperatures**
- Length, weight, and [[Specific Growth Rate]] (SGR) were significantly lower at 10 and 14°C than at 20°C for the suckers, with significant differences across temperatures at most sampling dates.
- Mean SGR of 7-day Humpback Chub at 10, 14, and 20°C was 0.8, 3.6, and 7.8. For 14-day Humpback Chub, SGR was 5.4, 5.7, and 10.2 at the same temperatures.
- For Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub, corrected statistics were nonsignificant. The authors attribute this to small sample sizes because only replicate means were available. They still concluded that the effect on all four species was similar.

**2. Larval-to-juvenile transformation is delayed**
- Reported transformation lengths: Razorback Sucker 27–30 mm; Flannelmouth Sucker 28–29 mm (rarely 31 mm); Humpback Chub 26–27 mm; Colorado Squawfish 25 mm (rarely 24 mm).
- Razorback Sucker (7-day group): first transformation at 20°C on day 57 (30% of sample), all by day 126. No fish at 14 or 10°C had transformed by day 126.
- Razorback Sucker (14-day group): 10% transformed at 20°C by day 51; no fish at 14 or 10°C had transformed by day 120.
- Flannelmouth Sucker (7-day group): first juveniles at 20°C on day 59 (70%); first juveniles at 14°C on day 91 (40%); none at 10°C.
- Flannelmouth Sucker (41-day group): juveniles appeared by day 35 at all temperatures (63% at 20°C, 63% at 14°C, 50% at 10°C), but only at 20°C did all fish transform by day 64.
- Humpback Chub (41-day group): mean TL at transformation was 28.6 mm at 20°C by day 14, 30.3 mm at 14°C by day 43, and 26.0 mm at 10°C by day 93 for some fish.
- Colorado Squawfish (14-day group): mean TL was 26.6 mm at 20°C by day 42, while fish at 14 and 10°C had not transformed by day 92 (mean TL 20.1 and 11.7 mm).
- The authors state that time to transformation appears to be a strict function of length.

**3. Cold shock causes loss of equilibrium**
- 5–7-day Humpback Chub moved from 20°C to 10°C entered cold coma. They remained immobile for about 90 min, took about 60 min more to regain swimming, and stayed lethargic. Transfer to 12°C caused lethargy without loss of position in the water column. Transfer to 14°C caused no obvious effects.
- 11–13-day Humpback Chub at 10°C also entered cold coma but recovered in 15 min.
- 7–9-day Razorback Sucker at 10°C had cold coma lasting 30–60 s. 7–8-day Flannelmouth Sucker recovered in 5–10 s.
- Older larvae showed only lethargy. No temperature-caused mortality occurred in any shock experiment over the 1–5 h observation periods.

**4. Statistical basis**
- Length and weight variances were not homogeneous (Levene test), so nonparametric methods were used. Kruskal-Wallis tests were followed by [[Bonferroni Correction]] (Holm procedure), with significance set at α = 0.05.
- Razorback and Flannelmouth Sucker data were pooled across replicates. Humpback Chub and Colorado Squawfish comparisons used replicate means due to lost individual data.
- Sample sizes per sampling date were usually N = 5–23 fish. Experiments ran 24–128 days in 2–3 replicate aquaria with 50–100 fish per group. The 41-day Flannelmouth Sucker group was not replicated.
- Cross-species comparisons were not made because of culturing differences.

**5. Conclusions and management recommendations**
- The authors reject the null hypothesis that early life growth is unaffected by temperature and conclude that low temperature affects both catostomids and cyprinids similarly.
- Delayed transformation likely raises cumulative larval mortality.
- Given the simulated population effects on Colorado Squawfish and the reduced growth of all four species in dam tailwaters, the authors state that "formidable challenges confront" recovery efforts.
- Thermal modification of dam discharge is required short of dam removal. Basin-wide measures are also needed, including routine high-magnitude floods, taxon-specific control of nonnative species, restored migration corridors, and public education.

**6. Data quality notes**
- Some Razorback Sucker showed a high incidence of spinal and cranial deformities, possibly from nutritional deficiency. Deformities were evenly distributed across tanks, so the authors do not believe they biased the experiments. Their transformation lengths (23.7–28.7 mm) differ from published ranges (27–30 mm).
- Colorado Squawfish growth stalled between days 42 and 59 during a costiasis outbreak.
