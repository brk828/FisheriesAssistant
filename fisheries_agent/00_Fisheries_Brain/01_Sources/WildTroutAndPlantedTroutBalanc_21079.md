# Wild Trout and Planted Trout: Balancing the Scale

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 draft by fish biologists from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[National Park Service]] reviews how Colorado's recreational trout fisheries have shifted from hatchery-based stocking toward management of native and wild trout. The authors trace stocking history in [[Rocky Mountain National Park]] (RMNP), describe the FWS cooperative wild trout programs and their three management priorities, and report angler and stream survey results since the 1984 regulation change. They conclude that ending non-native stocking and adopting restrictive regulations has helped native trout recover, while urban hatchery programs still provide valued low-cost recreation.

## Background & Description

**Scope of FWS programs.** The FWS has limited lands with recreational fishing but cooperates with several agencies across a range of habitats in Colorado. Through the FWS National Fish Hatchery System it runs propagation programs for threatened, endangered and native or non-native fish. Hatchery-reared fish are used in artificial habitats under the [[Sikes Act]].

**Urban catchable trout program.** This is a management-by-objective program with targets of an average catch rate of 0.5 to 0.8 trout per angler hour and a minimum return of 60% of hatchery-reared fish. Over 85% of catchable-sized trout stocked into urban lakes were reported caught. Urban ponds at a stocked site of 42 acres supported up to 50,000 angler days per year, roughly the same as the estimated annual angler use of all of RMNP.

**Stocking history in RMNP.**
- After the Yellowstone Lake and Estes Park hatcheries were established, up to 415,000 [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] fry were stocked annually into RMNP during the 1940s under the NPS native-fish policy.
- Rainbow trout were heavily stocked into roadside streams and lakes in the 1940s and 1950s. In 1946, suckers were removed from Bear Lake with rotenone before stocking. In 1957, 40,000 rainbow trout about 102 mm long were stocked into five kilometers of the [[Fall River]].
- A 1962 to 1971 schedule excluded stocking of lakes and streams where spawning occurred, but allowed non-native cutthroats in selected lakes without spawning habitat.
- Non-native stocking to maintain artificial recreational fisheries was discontinued by 1968, after which RMNP was managed for native fish and wild populations.
- Most RMNP lakes were probably originally fishless because of elevation and barriers to migration. Stocking probably began in the late 1800s, and several non-native species were established by the time the park was created in 1915.

**Management priorities.** Because of limited funding, FWS cooperative wild trout programs follow three priorities:
1. **Priority One:** Listed native species in native habitat that have not been manipulated other than by fishing. These are very rare, probably only one or two populations (Poudre River and possibly Columbine Creek). They are not managed as sport fisheries but supply genetic material for broodstock and research.
2. **Priority Two:** Native trout within their historic range, including early "coffee pot" transfers into fishless waters and interagency restoration projects. These maintain the species and provide sport fisheries.
3. **Priority Three:** Reproducing populations of exotic species (brook, brown, rainbow and Yellowstone cutthroat trout) that are not suitable for native restoration and are managed as exotic wild trout fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Greenback Cutthroat Trout.** Only two small populations were known to survive in 1973, when the subspecies was listed as endangered. It was downlisted to threatened in 1978. Recovery efforts combined habitat protection, captive breeding and expansion into suitable waters. Through 1993 greenbacks occupied 51 sites (100 hectares of lakes and ponds and 144 km of streams) in Colorado. Greenbacks performed well at lower, warmer elevations, and reached about 2.0 kg in weight in one introduction. They are very easy to catch, and up to 25% of fish in some areas showed jaw hook damage. Reproducing RMNP populations are managed as artificial-lure, catch-and-release fisheries.

**Colorado River Cutthroat Trout.** This species is not federally listed. Its only known RMNP population was lost by 1978 to competition from [[Brook Trout]]. Non-native cutthroats were removed from Timber Lake and Timber Creek in 1979 and from Bench Lake and Ptarmigan Creek in 1985. Surveys identified two stream and three lake populations considered pure within the Colorado River drainage, and one lake and one stream population in the South Platte drainage. Within RMNP these fish occupy 19 km of stream and 25.3 hectares of lake. Of these, 15% of stream habitat and 90% of lake habitat are open to at least catch-and-release fishing. [[Lake Nanita]] (13.8 hectares), stocked with the [[Trappers Lake Strain]] in 1933, is the largest known remaining pure population of that strain. It tested positive for enteric redmouth disease, which had not been reported to spread to [[Columbine Creek]], a stream population described as extremely important genetic stock.

**Exotic trout and altered fish communities.** Warmer streams, such as the Mailbox Junction reach of the [[Big Thompson River]], shifted from native suckers and brook trout to [[Brown Trout]]. In colder upper Colorado River habitats, brook trout remain more abundant than brown trout. White and longnose suckers are native to the South Platte drainage and should not be removed. In the high-velocity Mailbox Junction habitat, the authors suggest that trout dominated and displaced native suckers. In lower Fall River habitats that favor suckers, both suckers and brown trout returned to their 1973 population levels following the Lawn Lake flood and the 1984 regulation change.

**Lake fisheries.** By 1960, regular stocking of RMNP lakes was generally limited to a single stocking per year. By 1967, about 40% (59 lakes) were known to maintain fish populations through natural reproduction and stocking. After non-native stocking ended in 1968, fish disappeared from 12 lakes, and 47 lakes (31%) were known to maintain populations. Of these 47, 16 hold pure native cutthroat stocks (greenback or Colorado River), and 25 support hybridized populations of native cutthroats and non-native species, which are scheduled for conversion to pure native fisheries. The authors note that large native trout populations also support raptors, otters and watchable wildlife during spawning runs.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking ended and native management began.** Non-native stocking in RMNP was discontinued by 1968. Fishless alpine lakes were allowed to revert to their original ecological role, though the decision was unpopular with some anglers.
- **Regulation changes since 1984.** The former eight-fish limit was replaced in 1984 with a two-fish limit for brown, rainbow and non-native cutthroat trout over 250 mm. Up to 18 brook trout could be kept per day, with at least ten under eight inches. Native cutthroats are catch-and-release only, with artificial lures and barbless hooks required.
- **High release rates.** Anglers released less than 20% of brook trout caught in 1984 but over 80% by 1990. Release rates were high across RMNP, including Hidden Valley, where the brook trout rule was intended to reduce competition with native fish.
- **Angler success rose.** Reported catch rates increased steadily from 1987 in both the South Platte and Colorado drainages. The authors attribute this to larger sport fish populations and angler recycling of fish.
- **Brown trout increase.** In the Mailbox Junction reach (91.5 m section), brown trout 200 to 249 mm long rose from three in 1971 to 27 in 1993. Brown trout over 250 mm remained at two in both years.
- **Greenback recovery.** Nineteen greenback populations are considered stable, 17 of them in the South Platte drainage. The delisting goal is 20 stable populations, with at least five in the Arkansas drainage. Twenty-nine greenback waters in Colorado are open to catch-and-release fishing. The majority of stable reproducing greenback populations are within RMNP.
- **Leadville restoration.** The greenback project above the [[Leadville National Fish Hatchery]], completed in 1991, created four new greenback lake fisheries and several kilometers of stream fisheries. Greenbacks are reproducing in Rock Creek, which should add at least one reproducing population for delisting in the Arkansas drainage.
- **Partnership and recognition.** [[Colorado Trout Unlimited]] supported greenback restoration and sponsored the bill that made the Colorado River cutthroat trout the official Colorado State Fish in 1994.
- **Balanced recommendation.** The authors argue that stocked urban and commercial fee waters serve families and boat and bait anglers and reduce pressure on RMNP wild trout waters. They conclude that RMNP alpine lakes should be preserved as native habitat and sanctuary for native invertebrates and amphibians.
