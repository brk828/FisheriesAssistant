# Thermal and Respiratory Tolerance of the Unarmored Threespine Stickleback

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Feldmeth and Baskin (1976) investigated thermal and respiratory tolerance of the endangered [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni*) from the headwaters of the [[Santa Clara River]] in northern Los Angeles County. The researchers determined the [[Critical Thermal Maximum]] (CTM) at different acclimation temperatures and characterized the species' oxygen consumption and gill ventilation responses across a gradient of dissolved oxygen concentrations. Key findings show strong physiological plasticity to temperature (CTM ranging from 30.5°C to 34.6°C depending on acclimation) and a critical oxygen threshold of approximately 2.0 ppm, below which respiratory function rapidly deteriorates. These data provide baseline physiological tolerances essential for habitat assessment and conservation planning for this endangered subspecies.

## Background & Description

The [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni* Hubbs) is an endangered fish subspecies restricted to the headwaters of the Santa Clara River in northern Los Angeles County, California. The habitat is characterized as a small, intermittent, shallow stream flowing through arid desert scrub and coastal sage scrub terrestrial communities. The aquatic environment consists of:

- Shallow pools subject to significant warming
- Meandering sand and silt-bottomed stream channels
- Emergent aquatic vegetation and willow-bordered banks
- Weed-choked areas prone to low dissolved oxygen

Because this environment can expose fish to both thermal stress and hypoxic conditions, the study was designed to quantify tolerance limits for both stressors, framed explicitly around conservation and habitat management needs for this endangered taxon.

Prior comparative work by [[Lewis et al. 1972]] found that the nominate form, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, in England favors open, well-oxygenated waters with sand/gravel substrates, and that this species is likely less tolerant of low oxygen than the [[Nine-Spined Stickleback]] (*Pungitius pungitius*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Desert intermittent stream ecosystem, prone to extreme seasonal temperature fluctuations (hot summers, cold winters) typical of arid Southern California terrain.
- **Thermal regime:** Shallow pools can become quite warm during summer months; acclimation temperatures tested in the lab (8.0°C, 13.0°C, 15.6°C, 18.6°C, 22.7°C) were selected to bracket natural seasonal extremes.
- **Oxygen regime:** Field measurements showed a mean daytime dissolved oxygen concentration of 7.18 ppm in the upper Santa Clara River — well above the species' critical oxygen threshold. However, isolated stagnant pools and nighttime conditions (absence of photosynthetic oxygen production) may produce localized hypoxic conditions.
- **Behavioral ecology:** Field observations indicate sticklebacks show strong preference for flowing water and are entirely absent from ponds lacking flow, consistent with physiological data suggesting a preference for moving, well-oxygenated habitats.
- **Comparative physiology:** The species appears physiologically intermediate between stagnant-water-tolerant fishes (e.g., carps) and cold, well-oxygenated mountain stream specialists (e.g., [[Salmonids]]).
- **Comparative CTM context:** Citing [[Brett 1956]], salmonids have the lowest CTMs among North American freshwater fishes, centrarchids and cyprinids are intermediate, and ictalurids plus *Gambusia affinis* (a poeciliid) exhibit the highest CTMs — situating the unarmored stickleback's thermal tolerance within this broader taxonomic framework.
- **Ontogenetic comparisons:** Unlike [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), where juveniles occupy warmer shallow waters than adults (Lowe & Heath 1969), and [[Grayling]] (*Thymallus arcticus*), where fry show significantly higher CTM than adults (Eriksen, in press), no size-related CTM difference or habitat segregation by age was detected in the unarmored stickleback.

## Key Findings

### Thermal Tolerance (CTM)
- CTM strongly influenced by acclimation temperature.
- Acclimated to **8.0°C** → CTM = **30.5°C**
- Acclimated to **22.7°C** (approximating warmest summer conditions) → CTM = **34.6°C**
- CTM determined as the temperature at which opercular ventilatory movement ceased, using a heating rate of 1°C per 4 minutes (following [[Fry 1957]] methodology).
- Fish were acclimated for 23 days prior to testing to allow full metabolic adjustment.

### Effect of Body Size on CTM
- Two size classes acclimated to a constant 18.6°C for three weeks.
- **No significant difference** in CTM was found between small and large fish (small fish mean CTM = 33.49°C; large fish mean CTM = 33.6°C).
- This contrasts with other species (e.g., grayling) where ontogenetic differences in thermal tolerance correlate with habitat segregation; no such separation is known for this stickleback.

### Oxygen Consumption & Critical Oxygen Concentration
- Respiratory rate is **independent of oxygen concentration** above ~2.0 ppm dissolved O₂ (homeostatic regulation of oxygen uptake).
- Below **2.0 ppm**, oxygen consumption **rapidly declines** — termed the "level of no excess activity" (sensu [[Fry 1971]]), approximating the lethal oxygen threshold.
- A slight increase in respiratory rate was observed just above the critical threshold, likely reflecting increased energetic cost of ventilation at low oxygen (consistent with [[Beamish 1964]]).
- Tested oxygen concentrations: 0.97, 1.91, 3.60, 5.25, 7.04, and 7.33 ppm; oxygen consumption measured as mg O₂/g fish/hour.

### Gill Ventilation Rate
- Ventilation rate increases sharply as dissolved oxygen decreases, to maintain respiratory independence.
- At **7.4 ppm O₂**: mean ventilation rate = **104.6 movements/minute**
- At **1.32 ppm O₂**: mean ventilation rate rises to **230.7 movements/minute**
- Below ~1.32 ppm, ventilation rate **rapidly decreases**, indicating respiratory failure/collapse near the lethal limit.
- Tested concentrations: 0.60, 1.32, 2.94, 4.80, 5.90, 6.40, and 7.40 ppm.

### Behavioral Correlation
- Findings align with [[Jones 1952]], who observed that *Gasterosteus aculeatus* actively avoids water with dissolved oxygen below 2.0 ppm, immediately reversing or backing out of such conditions — corroborating the physiological critical oxygen threshold identified in this study.

### Conservation Implications
- The relatively high critical oxygen concentration (2.0 ppm) suggests an ecological preference for flowing, well-oxygenated water.
- Field dissolved oxygen levels in the upper Santa Clara River (mean 7.18 ppm) are generally well above the tolerance threshold, but isolated stagnant pools and low-flow nighttime conditions could pose localized risk.
- Data support the use of physiological tolerance limits in habitat appraisal and refugia selection for future translocation/conservation efforts involving this endangered subspecies.
