# The Name of Biodiversity: Preservation Versus Sportfishing in the West

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This Bassmaster Magazine feature (January 1994, by Robert Montgomery) examines a conflict between conservation preservationists and state sportfishing agencies over the stocking of [[Non-Native Species]]. Preservationists, working through the [[Endangered Species Act]] and the [[Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]], seek to end stocking of introduced fish, especially [[Largemouth Bass]], in western waters. They focus on the [[Colorado River Basin]], home to four endangered fishes. State fisheries chiefs argue that most western fisheries are human-made, that native fish support little angling, and that restricting stocking would damage recreational fishing and the states' management authority. The article covers federal policy, funding threats, and competing views on the meaning of [[Biodiversity]].

## Background & Description

The article opens with the claim that some preservationists would drain western reservoirs such as [[Lake Powell]] and remove non-native fish. Federal fish and wildlife officials are quoted as saying that "all bass west of the Rockies are history." The author notes that bass are "naturalized" residents of 49 states.

Three policy vehicles are identified:

- **[[Endangered Species Act]]:** Due for reauthorization, and described as the main legal tool for halting stocking. Its 20-year history is noted.
- **[[Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] (1990):** Passed to address accidental introductions such as the [[Zebra Mussel]] infestation in the [[Great Lakes]]. Critics fear it could be used against intentional stocking.
- **[[Wallop-Breaux Fund]]:** A federal fund that allocates money to states for sportfish management. A November 1992 [[Clinton Wildlife Transition Team]] report recommended withdrawing these funds from non-native fish stocking.

Multiple state and federal voices appear. State fisheries chiefs include Bruce Schmidt (Utah), Phil Durocher (Texas), Joe Janisch (Arizona), Eddie Kochman and Don Horak (Colorado). Federal staff quoted include Chris Dlugosz, Lee Mills, and an assistant regional director in Region VI. Advocacy groups include the [[National Wildlife Federation]], the [[Sport Fishing Institute]], and the [[American Fisheries Society]]. The Clinton appointee Mollie Beattie, nominated to direct the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], is portrayed by critics as a preservationist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key environmental points made in the article:

- **Altered systems:** About 75 percent of Colorado's fisheries habitat is man-made, according to Kochman. Man-made lakes and reservoirs nationwide total more than 10 million acres.
- **Introduced species:** More than 100 exotic plant and animal species have been introduced into the Great Lakes, intentionally and accidentally.
- **Native decline:** The main cause of native western fish decline is habitat loss. Historically, the [[Colorado River Basin]] was a large, free-flowing, turbid river that flooded each spring, creating breeding and nursery habitat. Its altered flow now favors warm-water species.
- **Endangered fishes:** The [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] are the four species endangered in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
- **Sea lamprey control:** Sea lampreys decimate sportfish in [[Lake Champlain]]. Control chemicals drew preservationist objections over possible harm to invertebrates. Permits took eight years to obtain, according to angler Bill Jacobus.

Ecological views differ. Preservationists hold that genuine biodiversity exists only in unaltered ecosystems. Managers argue that diversity, stability, and productivity can require introduced species.

## Key Findings

**Scale of stocking and angling (as reported by state officials):**

- Nearly every state stocks non-native species for recreational fishing, including Florida bass in Texas, salmon in the Great Lakes, brown trout in Arkansas, and stripers in Oklahoma.
- Colorado has six native species with potential as sportfish (cutthroat trout, channel catfish, bullhead, green sunfish, orange-spotted sunfish, and whitefish). Natives account for 1 percent of recreational angling in Colorado.
- Utah has four native game fish species, two of which occur in only one lake. Schmidt estimates 5 percent of Utah anglers fish for natives.
- Janisch states that 2 percent of angler effort in Arizona targets natives, out of a total of 6.7 million angler-days per year.
- Roughly 75 million anglers and boaters are cited as a major constituency.

**Policy developments:**

- The 1988 agreement among Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming with the Secretary of the Interior established the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]]. Under this agreement the states gave up some stocking rights.
- Colorado is analyzing stocking procedures under new federal guidelines. Kochman says the state retains final decision authority, but the FWS could challenge stockings under the Endangered Species Act.
- Horak worries the Aquatic Nuisance Act could be used to stop intentional stocking of bass and trout. The Act defines aquatic nuisance as a non-indigenous species that threatens native diversity or dependent recreational activities.
- The FWS is preparing an "intentional introductions" options paper to clarify state rights under the new law.
- The Clinton Wildlife Transition Team recommended a uniform federal policy against federal funding for non-native fish introductions. Schmidt notes that withdrawal of Wallop-Breaux money for warm-water stocking has been discussed, though not yet proposed formally.

**Competing positions:**

- Schmidt and Kochman both state that they do not stock fish where endangered species are present, while maintaining that warm-water predators are beneficial in altered habitats.
- The [[National Wildlife Federation]] supports strengthening the Endangered Species Act to make it ecosystem-oriented, add multispecies recovery plans, set deadlines, and offer incentives to private landowners.
- Sport Fishing Institute officials warn that overapplying the Endangered Species Act could push the broad middle of anglers and boaters into opposition.
- Developers and industry are accused of using non-native species as scapegoats to divert attention from habitat loss.

**Concluding view:** In a guest editorial for *Fisheries Magazine*, SFI's Gil Radonski and Andrew Loftus write, "Biodiversity is a process, not a status." They call for philosophies that let managed and natural aquatic ecosystems coexist, which will require "patience, understanding and, above all, definition."
