# Intuitive Toxicology: Expert and Lay Judgments of Chemical Risks

#toxicology #risk-perception #risk-assessment #survey-research

## Executive Summary

This survey study compares how professional [[Toxicologists]] and the lay public judge chemical risks. The authors treat lay people as "intuitive toxicologists" and test whether public views differ from expert views in ways that could drive conflict over chemical regulation. The main survey was mailed to members of the [[Society of Toxicology]] and to a Portland, Oregon public sample. Two additional datasets, an [[EPA Baseline Survey]] and a [[New Jersey Study]], were used to check the findings.

The study finds large expert-public differences in dose-response understanding and in attitudes toward chemicals. It finds little difference in trust in [[Animal Toxicity Studies]], and toxicologists are themselves sharply divided on that question. Industry-affiliated toxicologists hold more favorable views of chemicals than academic or regulatory colleagues. The authors argue that risk controversies stem partly from real limits in [[Risk Assessment]] science, not only from public misconceptions.

## Background & Description

**Framing.** Humans have long judged food, water and air by sight, taste and smell. Toxicology and risk assessment were developed because these senses are inadequate for chemical hazards. Even so, human health risks are inferred from animal and bacterial data through extrapolation and judgment. The authors note that the [[National Academy Of Sciences]] (1983) acknowledged this subjectivity but did not fully examine its implications.

**Study objective.** The study compares the cognitive models, assumptions and inference methods of lay "intuitive toxicological theories" with those of scientific toxicology. The goal was to structure discussion and communication about chemical risk.

**Questionnaire.** The instrument had four core categories, plus a miscellaneous Category 5:
- Category 1: Dose-response sensitivity
- Category 2: Trust in animal and bacterial studies
- Category 3: Attitudes toward chemicals
- Category 4: Attitudes toward risk reduction

Most items were agree/disagree statements, each built around a hypothesis. The central guiding principle was [[The Dose Makes The Poison]], attributed to [[Paracelsus]].

**Expert sample.** The sample was drawn from the 1988 [[Society of Toxicology]] membership directory. Only US full members were eligible. Members were grouped as academic, industrial or regulatory, and 120 names were randomly drawn from each group (360 total). Of 360 questionnaires mailed in October 1988, 170 were returned, a 47% response rate. Respondents were highly educated (91% PhD, 2.4% MD) and 84.6% male.

**Public sample.** Households were drawn from the Portland, Oregon area, screened for income of at least $20,000. Of 1,100 households mailed, about 975 were deliverable and 262 usable questionnaires returned (about 27%). Respondents were unrepresentative of Portland: more male, better educated and higher income. Only 9.9% of the population had graduate training, versus 23.15% of the sample.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is a social-science and risk-communication study. It contains no aquatic-ecosystem, species or habitat data. Water appears only as a risk context: one scenario concerns a chemical found in a city's drinking water at 30 parts per million, and the public sample was asked whether filtration that reduces but does not eliminate the chemical reduces danger.

The broader setting is an environmental policy debate. The authors cite conflicts over [[Nuclear Power]], pesticides, benzene and formaldehyde regulation, and the use of [[Animal Toxicity Studies]] to predict human harm. They also note that the lay public feels increasingly vulnerable to technological risk.

## Key Findings

**1. Dose-response understanding differs sharply.** Toxicologists were much more likely than the public to recognize that dose and exposure mediate toxicity. The public more often equated any exposure with harm. Examples include:
- Pesticides: the public more often agreed that the fact of exposure, not the amount, is the critical concern.
- Drinking water: the public more often agreed that reducing a contaminant's concentration does not reduce danger.
- Carcinogens: the public more often agreed that there is no safe level of exposure.

Prescription drugs were an exception. The public recognized dose dependence for drugs more readily than for chemicals in general.

**2. Trust in animal studies is low in both groups.** About 41% of experts and 46% of the public disagreed that animal reactions reliably predict human reactions. Very few in either group agreed that proper animal studies identify all harmful effects (2b) or allow accurate dose predictions for humans (2c). The two groups diverged on a carcinogenicity finding:
- Toxicologists' agreement that animal carcinogenicity implies human cancer fell from 55.4% (item 2a) to 40.6% (item 2d).
- The public's agreement rose from 43.7% to 69.4%.

Toxicologists did not become more confident when an animal test was positive, which the authors read as a sign that they do not treat positive findings as more credible than negative ones. The public did.

**3. Attitudes toward chemicals are much more negative among the public.** Most public respondents (sample percentages as reported):
- 83.9% agreed that society has perceived only the tip of the chemical-risk iceberg.
- 88.4% agreed that contamination is greater now than ever before.
- 68.7% disagreed that people are unnecessarily frightened about small pesticide amounts.
- 87.1% disagreed that people worry unnecessarily about chemicals.
- 61.6% agreed it can never be too expensive to reduce chemical risks.

About 40% said they do everything they can to avoid chemical contact.

**4. Shared motivation.** The strongest point of agreement concerned wanting to understand chemical risks: 94.6% of toxicologists and 93.4% of the public agreed they should know as much as they can about chemicals in order to control the risks.

**5. Specific contrasts.**
- Natural versus synthetic chemicals: the public has much more confidence in natural chemicals.
- Microorganisms as predictors: 89.6% of toxicologists disagreed that microbial reactions reliably indicate human reactions. Among the public, 34.9% answered "don't know."
- Pesticides and birth defects (item 5h, a hypothetical community case): 48.5% of the public agreed the pesticides were very likely the cause, versus 5.5% of toxicologists.
- Trivial lifetime risk (a 1-in-10,000,000 lifetime cancer risk): the public was less willing to dismiss it than toxicologists were, especially when the risk came from chemicals rather than prescription drugs.

**6. Demographics matter, but group status matters most.** Regressions showed that group status (public versus toxicologist) was by far the strongest predictor of responses. Education, race and gender explained smaller amounts of variance. Comparisons of college-educated white male laypeople with white male toxicologists still showed large differences.

**7. Affiliation effects among toxicologists.** Industry toxicologists held the most favorable views of chemicals.
- On the carcinogenicity item (2d), 22.0% of industry toxicologists agreed, versus 48.9% of academics and 52.7% of regulatory toxicologists.
- Industry toxicologists were more likely to say humans are less vulnerable than animals.
- Industry toxicologists most often disagreed that pesticides caused the malformations in item 5h.

The authors cite a 1980s study of 136 occupational health specialists (Lynn). Only 27% of industry respondents agreed that a substance shown to cause tumors in animals should be considered a carcinogen posing human risk, versus 69% of government and 52% of university respondents. Eighty percent of industry respondents agreed that thresholds exist, versus 61% of academics and 37% of government employees. An alternative explanation, that industry toxicologists work with less hazardous chemicals, is not ruled out. However, 96.4% of all toxicologists disagreed that "most chemicals cause cancer," and the authors favor a sociopolitical interpretation.

**8. Gender and education effects in the public sample.**
- Women were consistently more concerned about chemical risks and less favorable toward chemical benefits.
- College-educated respondents were more favorable toward chemicals and more likely to see dose as mediating risk. They were also somewhat closer to toxicologists.
- Women had 26 toxicologist counterparts only. The toxicologist subsample of women (n = 26) was too small to measure gender effects reliably.

**9. Attitude structure.** Among public respondents, the correlation between "dose-response sensitivity" and "favorable attitude toward chemicals" was r = .68. Dose sensitivity correlated with lower concern about reducing risks (r = .47). Attitudes toward chemical risk and toward risk reduction correlated at r = .85. Trust in animal studies did not correlate significantly with any other scale. Regression predictions were moderate: attitude toward chemicals R = .49 (education, gender, scientific training); dose-response sensitivity R = .40 (health, education, age); trust in animal testing R = .22 (weak).

**10. Vulnerability beliefs link trust and fear.** Toxicologists who distrusted animal tests and believed humans are less vulnerable than animals were the most likely to say the public is unnecessarily frightened about small pesticide amounts. The authors call vulnerability perception a key mediating factor.

**11. Replication across datasets.**
- EPA Baseline Survey: 3,129 telephone interviews in six communities (Albuquerque, Cincinnati, Durham, Middlesex County, Racine, Richmond). The overall response rate was 59.1%. Responses differed even more from toxicologists than the Portland sample did. For example, 73.3% agreed that exposure to a cancer-causing chemical makes later cancer likely, versus 34.4% in Portland and 8.4% of toxicologists. About 63% endorsed the all-or-none statement about exposure, versus 36.1% in Portland and 4.2% of toxicologists. Only 47% disagreed that chemicals improved health more than they harmed it, versus 33.9% in Portland and 3.0% of toxicologists.
- New Jersey Study: a telephone survey of 500 residents in 31 communities (November 1987). 78.4% strongly agreed that they would not drink water containing even a tiny amount of a cancer-producing substance. 84.9% agreed that no one really knows how big hazardous-waste risks are.

**12. Contagion explanation.** The authors suggest that lay views reflect a "law of contagion" (Rozin et al.), in which any contact transfers harm. This all-or-none model of contamination differs from the scientist's dose-based model. Rachel Carson's analogies between carcinogens and infectious disease may also have contributed.

**13. Implications for risk communication.**
- Experts should clarify terms such as "toxic" and "carcinogenic," and their relationship to exposure.
- The authors argue that risk assessment is "too fragile, too indirect" to prevail in adversarial public debate. Persistent expert disagreement about animal-test validity feeds public distrust.
- Risk assessment has been oversold. Toxicologists should acknowledge subjective judgments and uncertainty, and protect the science from misuse. The authors endorse Graham et al.'s call for "modest expectations" about what science can deliver in chemical regulation.

**14. Limitations noted by the authors.** The Portland public sample had a low response rate (about 27%) and was unrepresentative of its community. The toxicologist sample was US-only and drawn from one society. The EPA telephone survey had no "don't know" option displayed, so its "don't know" frequency is not comparable to the written surveys.
