# Recent Stream Trenching in Semi-arid Southwestern New Mexico

#geomorphology #hydrology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This journal page combines a set of short bibliographic notices with a full research article by J. L. Rich in the *American Journal of Science* (Fourth Series, Vol. XXXII, No. 190, October 1911). The notices cover a book on marriage and totemism, the *Smithsonian Physical Tables*, Turin Academy prizes, a [[Lepidoptera]] catalogue, [[Bureau of American Ethnology]] bulletins, the first issue of the *Sarawak Museum Journal*, and a Rhodesia Museum report.

The main article argues that recent stream trenching in the temporary streams of the [[Silver City Quadrangle]], New Mexico, was caused by the removal of vegetation cover through excessive cattle grazing. Rich supports this with field observations, settler testimony, and physiographic evidence. He concludes that the trenching is recent, that it corresponds in time with overgrazing, and that it should be widespread in regions whose normal climate supports luxuriant vegetation.

## Background & Description

**Bibliographic notices (pages 1 to 2 of the source):**
- *Marriage, Totemism and Religion: An Answer to Critics* by Lord Avebury (London and New York, 1911). It is based on his 1870 work *Origin of Civilization*, and it addresses primitive marriage, totemism, witchcraft, magic, and religion.
- *Smithsonian Physical Tables*, fifth revised edition, prepared by F. E. Fowle (Washington, 1910; 318 pages, 335 tables). It is a major revision of the 1896 tables and is organized into four series: meteorological, geographical, physical, and mathematical.
- Royal Academy of Sciences of Turin, Vallauri prizes. Each prize is 26,000 Italian livres, awarded without regard to nationality, for Latin literature (1911 to 1914) and the physical sciences (1915 to 1918).
- *Catalogue of the Lepidoptera Phalaenae in the British Museum*, Vol. X (London, 1911).
- Bureau of American Ethnology bulletins, including Bulletin 43 (J. R. Swanton on Lower Mississippi Valley tribes) and Bulletin 50 (J. W. Fewkes on the Navajo National Monument, Arizona).
- *Sarawak Museum Journal*, Vol. I, No. 1 (Sarawak, February 1911; 201 pages), with ethnological papers, three papers on insects, and a list of Sarawak minerals.
- Rhodesia Museum Eighth Annual Report (Bulawayo, 1909), with appendices on gold-bearing rocks, minerals, and the batrachians and reptiles of Rhodesia.

**The research article:**
Rich describes the Silver City quadrangle as divided into a mountainous region and a desert plain built from mountain gravels, with about one-third of the area covered by these desert accumulations. The climate is semi-arid. Average rainfall is near 18 inches in the mountains and less on the desert, and it falls mostly as summer showers from mid-June to late September. Because there are no permanent streams outside the higher mountains, Rich uses "streams" to mean temporary channels that flow only during or just after storms.

The case study is Cane Spring Canyon. Its headwaters drain about seven square miles of rocky hills, and the channel flows about 5 miles across a dissected piedmont gravel fan at an average slope of 120 feet per mile before joining the Mangas, roughly 15 miles above the Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Valley morphology:**
- Valley walls are partly cemented desert conglomerate, 50 to 150 feet high.
- The valley floor is an alluvial flat of fine sandy loam with gravel lenses. Pebbles reach 5 to 8 inches, with 2 to 3 inches as a fair average for the coarser gravel.
- The recent trench is 50 to 200 feet wide and 2 to 15 feet deep, with vertical alluvial walls.
- The older valley was one-eighth to one-quarter mile wide and locally over 150 feet deep.

**Interpreted history:** The valley was first cut into the piedmont fan, then aggraded under arid or semi-arid conditions with no permanent main-channel stream. Sediment came largely from side tributaries, as shown by alluvial fans. A later phase of renewed cutting produced the trenches, which are still working upstream by headwater recession and sapping.

**Flood evidence:** Coarse gravel with boulders up to 2 feet in diameter lies on top of the fine alluvium, and some of it is very recent. Rich argues that transporting these boulders required more water than the fine fill, which indicates that peak floods have grown larger in recent times than during deposition.

**Vegetation and grazing:**
- Settlers such as Mr. MacMillan, a Mangas Valley rancher resident since 1876, described grass about knee high and thick enough to be cut for hay, with more timber than now.
- Under overstocking, cattle reduced vegetation to almost nothing in places, and grazing may have exterminated much of the grass.
- A fenced area at Fort Bayard military reservation, with very slight grazing, still has thick knee-high grass.

**Regional scope:** Rich reports similar trenching across New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming. He predicts it should occur mainly where normal vegetation would be dense enough to conserve rainfall, and it should be weak in more arid regions. The extreme southeastern corner of the quadrangle, which is drier, shows less trenching.

## Key Findings

1. **Trenching is recent and vegetation-linked.** Rich concludes that the trenches formed from increased flood violence caused by the removal of vegetation cover through overgrazing, and not from rainfall change alone. He compares run-off with cover against run-off on bare ground. Under bare ground, the same rain produces rapid floods that cut valleys and move large boulders.
2. **Timing supports the hypothesis.** Settlers report few valley-bottom trenches in early days. Trenching followed heavy floods. The deep Mangas trenches formed within the last 20 to 30 years. A street-level gully in Silver City, at least 20 feet deep and about 100 feet wide, began about 15 years earlier during a severe storm and has deepened every year.
3. **Floods changed character.** Earlier storms produced slower run-off that lasted two or three days. Current storms deliver water in one flood, and floods have grown more frequent and violent since overstocking.
4. **Grazing effects are fast.** Rich notes that the speed of physiographic change from overstocking is striking. He suggests that continued processes could alter this landscape within a few centuries.
5. **Protected areas recover.** A watershed fenced by MacMillan is gradually filling its trenches, which supports the view that vegetation controls the process.
6. **Testable prediction.** Rich proposes that comparing semi-arid areas with more arid areas, such as southern New Mexico and Arizona, would test the principle that trenching occurs mainly where normal vegetation is luxuriant.
