# Parasitism and Body Condition in Humpback Chub from the Colorado River and Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

This study compares [[Parasite Infection]] and body condition in juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) from two adjacent reaches of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona: the cold, clear, less productive mainstem and the warmer, more productive [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). Juveniles were sampled in 1996–1999 and examined for two introduced parasites, the [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) and the [[Anchor Worm]] (*Lernaea cyprinacea*).

Fish from the Colorado River had higher condition factor (K) and abdominal fat index (AFI) than LCR fish, and infected fish were generally in poorer condition. The authors argue that the LCR, although it is the species' essential spawning and recruitment habitat, is a less favorable environment for juvenile growth, and that the Colorado River may act as a partial refuge from exotic parasites.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a large-bodied, endemic minnow native to the Colorado River basin. The Grand Canyon population is the largest remaining population of the species. The LCR is the only place in Grand Canyon where humpback chub regularly spawn, and it is the source of nearly all new recruits into the Grand Canyon population.

Two introduced parasites were the focus of the study:

- **Bothriocephalus acheilognathi** (Asian tapeworm), a cestode that embeds in the intestine and can cause damage to host tissue at high intensities.
- **Lernaea cyprinacea** (anchor worm), a copepod that attaches to the head, fins, and body. It causes lesions and fibrosis, and it is probably lethal or pathogenic at high infection intensities.

Juvenile fish were examined for length, weight, infection status, and two condition indices: Fulton-type [[Condition Factor]] (K) and the [[Abdominal Fat Index]] (AFI).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and productivity contrasts.** The LCR is a warmer, turbid stream. The Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] is colder and clearer, because of [[Hypolimnetic Releases]] from the reservoir. Despite its warmth, the LCR is much less productive than the Colorado River (Haury 1981; Oberlin et al. 1999). Higher water temperature increases metabolic demand in poikilotherms, so the warmer LCR may force juvenile fish to spend more energy on metabolism and less on storage.

**Nonnative species.** Several [[Nonnative Fishes]] occur in the LCR and compete with humpback chub for food and habitat. These include the [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Plains Killifish]], and young [[Common Carp]]. Most were introduced in the late 1800s. Humpback chub did not evolve with these competitors, and their diets and habitats overlap.

**Parasite life cycles and flood effects.** *Lernaea cyprinacea* free-swimming stages depend on [[Copepod]] hosts. Spring floods in 1998 and 1999 probably flushed these stages out of the LCR, while low discharge in 1996 and 1997 let the river warm earlier and extended the parasite's growing season. *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* requires water temperatures in a particular range to complete its life cycle, and the LCR's warmth favors it.

## Key Findings

**Condition and body size**

- Mean K was higher in the Colorado River (0.735) than in the LCR (0.622) (p < 0.0001). This difference held in every year except one.
- Mean AFI was higher in Colorado River fish (2.02) than in LCR fish (0.48) (p < 0.0001). Annual means in the Colorado River ranged from 1.62 (1996) to 2.95 (1999). In the LCR, they ranged from 0.19 (1996) to 1.00 (1999).
- Across both rivers, infected fish were lighter than uninfected fish, and mean K was higher in uninfected fish (p < 0.0252 for the combined analysis).
- In the Colorado River, infected fish were smaller and lighter than uninfected fish. In the LCR, there was no length or weight difference between infected and uninfected fish.
- Unparasitized LCR fish were smaller and had lower K and AFI than unparasitized Colorado River fish.
- Length and weight were positively correlated with AFI in both rivers combined and in the LCR (p < 0.0219).

**Lernaea cyprinacea**

- LCR humpback chub had a mean of 1.73 *L. cyprinacea* per infected fish. About 12% of LCR fish were infected.
- In the Colorado River, only 3.5% of fish were infected, and each infected fish carried only one parasite.
- Annual LCR prevalence ranged from 5.3% in some years. The maximum count on a single LCR juvenile was seven parasites.
- In 1998 and 1999 no Colorado River fish were infected. Infection rates were 5.9% in 1996 and 16.7% in 1997.
- Parasite intensity was negatively correlated with K and AFI in the Colorado River and in the combined analysis (p < 0.0072 for K). It was not significantly correlated with condition in the LCR.

**Bothriocephalus acheilognathi**

- About 50.7% of LCR humpback chub were infected, a significantly higher proportion than in the Colorado River (p = 0.0209).
- Parasite intensity was not significantly correlated with any measured body variable.
- Infected LCR fish were no longer or heavier than uninfected fish.

**Overall conclusions**

- The authors conclude that juvenile humpback chub in the Colorado River are healthier than those in the LCR. They have higher K, more body fat, and lower infection by both parasites.
- The LCR has poorer productivity, more nonnative competitors, and more parasites. Together these push juveniles toward a lower-energy budget and possibly higher mortality. Heavily infected fish in the LCR are likely to suffer high mortality, according to the data of Hansen (2004).
- The Colorado River appears to be a refuge from exotic parasites, but warming of its water could erode this benefit.
- The LCR remains essential for spawning and recruitment, so it must be protected even though juveniles may benefit from moving to the Colorado River as they grow.
- The authors describe the situation as a trade-off between a warm, productive-poor stream with high parasite load and a cold, productive stream with low parasite risk.
- The study shows the unavoidable consequences of introduced species and argues for managing all available habitats while considering both biotic and abiotic factors in endangered species management.
