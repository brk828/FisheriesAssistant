# Pan Fishes of New York State: Perches, Basses, Drum and Sunfishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Serranidae #Sciaenidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of three articles from the *N. Y. State Conservationist*, the New York State Conservation Department's magazine (1965), plus an identification leaflet on the Bluegill and Redbreast Sunfish. The articles, written by Edward C. Raney, cover the "pan fishes" of the state. The first covers [[Yellow Perch]], [[White Perch]], [[White Bass]] and [[Freshwater Drum]]. The second covers [[Rock Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]] and the sunfishes of the family Centrarchidae. The articles combine identification keys, range records, life histories, growth and spawning data, food habits and angling notes. The author's central management message is that these species are underutilized, should be fished hard, and should not be introduced into new waters without careful evaluation.

## Background & Description

Pan fishes are described informally as fish that are easily caught and good to eat. The four species in the first article are spiny-rayed fishes, all of which are important commercial or sport fishes, especially in [[Lake Erie]] and Lake Ontario. Their taxonomic placement is often misunderstood:

- **Percidae (perch family):** [[Yellow Perch]], related to the walleye, blue pike and sauger.
- **Serranidae (sea bass family):** [[White Perch]], [[White Bass]] and the striped bass. The white perch is not a true perch.
- **Sciaenidae (drum family):** [[Freshwater Drum]], the only member of its family living entirely in fresh water in North America.
- **Centrarchidae (sunfish family):** Sunfishes, [[Rock Bass]] and black basses. The family is North American, with 25 known species, all but one (the Sacramento perch) native east of the Rocky Mountains.

Key identification features include the black vertical bars and slim body of the yellow perch, the number of anal fin soft rays in the white bass (12 or 13) versus the white perch (nine or ten), and the large, circular otoliths of the freshwater drum, known as "lucky stones."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** All four first-article species are warm-water fishes. Yellow perch prefer 70°F (adults) or 75°F (fingerlings) in laboratory tests, with a maximum tolerated temperature of 92°F. Pumpkinseed prefer 88–89°F, and bluegill prefer 90°F. Most sunfishes become quiet below 50°F, and the redbreast forms dense wintering schools below 40°F.
- **Oxygen:** Yellow perch tolerate winter low-oxygen conditions better than [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Walleye]].
- **Habitat preference:** Yellow perch favor weedy shallows. White perch do best where water reaches 75°F or more. White bass prefer clear lakes and large rivers and avoid silty and polluted waters. Freshwater drum occupy large rivers and lakes, often silty ones. Sunfishes generally prefer quiet, weedy waters, though the longear sunfish is found in sluggish streams and is sensitive to silt.
- **Range change:** Yellow perch and white perch have extended their ranges over the last 20 years. White perch moved westward through the Mohawk and Erie Canal, reached Lake Erie by 1953, and entered Lake Ontario from the Oswego River.
- **Predation and competition:** Overpopulation of yellow perch and white perch can cause stunting and depletion of native forage fish and trout. Young sunfish compete for space and food, and stunting is common where large predatory fishes are scarce.

## Key Findings

### Yellow Perch
- Yellow perch are widely distributed, but in trout ponds they can be disastrous, and they are a "deplorable nuisance" in Adirondack ponds.
- Growth varies with food, competition and water size. A stunted six-year-old from Gilbert Lake weighed 1.75 ounces on average. Perch of the same age from Bradley Brook Reservoir weighed almost six times as much.
- Under good conditions, perch reach about 12 inches and nearly one pound in nine or ten years. The New York maximum age recorded is 12 years. The all-time record is a 4¼-pound specimen from the Delaware River (1865).
- Males mature at about two years in Lake Mendota, and in Lake Erie at about seven inches. Spawning occurs in April and early May at 44–54°F. A single female may deposit up to 48,000 eggs. Eggs hatch in about 48 hours at 63°F, and adults do not guard them.
- Adult perch are mostly piscivorous. Walleye in Oneida Lake contained perch in 53 of 460 stomachs (young walleye).

### White Perch
- Range covers the Atlantic Coastal Plain from the Maritime Provinces to South Carolina, in salt, brackish and fresh water.
- Maximum size in estuaries approaches six pounds. Oneida Lake growth data show average total lengths of 3.5, 7.5, 8.7, 9.6, 10.1 and 10.6 inches at the end of years one to six.
- The New York maximum age is 15 years; in Maine it is 17.
- Females produce as many as 40,000 eggs. Egg diameter averages 0.79 mm unfertilized and 0.92 mm after water hardening. Eggs hatch in about 48 hours at 63°F.
- Stunting and harmful effects on other fishes occur where white perch multiply rapidly in fresh water.

### White Bass
- The species is short-lived, seldom exceeding seven years. It can produce strong "bumper" year classes, as in Oneida Lake in 1954.
- Mature males ripen earlier than females. Spawning occurs from April through June at 58–75°F, usually in water two to four feet deep over firm bottom.
- Egg counts in 14 ripe females ranged from 241,000 to 933,000.
- Tagged fish in Lake Texoma, Oklahoma, moved from under 50 yards to 200 miles, with an average of 10.6 miles. The fastest travel was 6.9 miles per day in a tributary.
- Adults feed on [[Daphnia]] in Lake Mendota and on young yellow perch and emerald shiners in other waters.

### Freshwater Drum
- Large drums feed on the bottom, crushing mollusks with pharyngeal teeth. They also eat crayfish, fishes and insects, with mayflies and amphipods important in Lake Erie.
- The largest recorded drum was 27.2 inches long and weighed 7 pounds 14 ounces, at age 17 in Lake Erie.
- Females aged five to eight produce 43,000 to 508,000 eggs. Eggs are about 1 mm in diameter and float at the surface.
- Flesh quality varies. Smaller fish (one to two pounds) are better, and some lakes produce muddy-tasting fish.

### Rock Bass and Crappies
- Rock bass are identified by the red eye and five or more anal spines. They reach about 14 inches and two pounds under ideal conditions, with a maximum age of about 11 years in nature.
- Female rock bass of 4–8 ounces produce about 3,000 eggs; those of 16–20 ounces produce 8,500.
- The black crappie has seven or eight dorsal spines. Maximum weight is two to four pounds, and the maximum age is at least eight winters.
- The white crappie has six dorsal spines and is replacing the black crappie in many New York lakes. A 13-year-old white crappie weighing 3 pounds 9 ounces from Iowa was exceptional.
- White crappie eggs range from about 2,000 in a fish just under six inches to 325,000 in a 13-inch fish.

### Other Sunfishes
- **Pumpkinseed:** The most widely distributed sunfish in New York and the only native Centrarchid in the Adirondacks. Dwarf populations at Beaver River Flow and Irving Pond never exceeded about 4 inches. Pumpkinseed are ranked second after the rock bass as a pan fish.
- **Bluegill:** Reaches nearly one foot and nearly two pounds. A five-inch female can produce 6,000 eggs, and a nine-inch bluegill almost 50,000. Bluegill were absent from the Adirondacks in the 1929–1933 surveys.
- **Redbreast Sunfish:** Reaches 12 inches and several pounds in lowland waters, with a maximum age of six years. Nest diameter is 12–16 inches.
- **Longear Sunfish:** Usually two to four inches long, with a slow growth rate of about four inches in five years. Found in New York only in Oak Orchard and Johnson creeks, west of Rochester, and in Oneida Lake.
- **Green Sunfish:** Probably not native to New York. Adults are usually six or seven inches, with a maximum of about ten inches.
- **Warmouth:** Maximum length eight to ten inches. Females produce 4,500 to 63,000 eggs. Unlike most relatives, it does not become dominant or stunted.
- **Bluespotted and Banded Sunfishes:** Small species that rarely exceed four inches. Both are limited to southeastern New York and the Coastal Plain.
- **Mud Sunfish:** Usually less than six inches. It is nocturnal, has smooth cycloid scales, and is limited to the Hackensack River drainage in New York.

### Management and Conservation Recommendations
- Natural reproduction is so high that stocking is unnecessary except in unusual cases or new reservoirs.
- Natural death rates of 25–40% per year are reported for perch populations.
- Species are underutilized and should be fished hard. Sunfish stocks can withstand year-round intensive angling.
- Introductions should be avoided. The author specifically warns against further introductions of white perch, stocking of the black and white crappie in small farm ponds, and introduction of rock bass into upper Adirondack waters.
- Hybridization is documented in sunfishes. The pumpkinseed × redbreast hybrid was collected at 26 localities in New York.
