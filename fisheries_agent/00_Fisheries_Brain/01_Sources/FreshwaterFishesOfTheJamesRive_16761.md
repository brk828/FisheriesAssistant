# James River Basin Fish Fauna: Annotated Checklist and Distribution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by Edward C. Raney of Cornell University, provides a historical review and annotated checklist of the fishes of the [[James River]] Basin in Virginia. No systematic survey had been made of the James or of any Virginia river. Earlier data came largely from [[Cope]] (1869), [[Jordan]] (1890), and Fowler's collections, supplemented by 1930s–1940s U.S. National Museum collections and the author's own field work. The fauna is classified under 21 families, 52 genera and 73 species, plus one species represented by two subspecies (74 kinds in total), and 14 further species are listed as probably present. The fauna reflects a mix of marine-derived, widespread Atlantic, northern, and southern elements, with a sharp faunal break between the James and the [[Roanoke River]] to the south.

## Background & Description

**Sources and history.** Much of the distribution data dated from studies more than sixty years old. Major contributors included:

- Cope (1869) and Jordan (1890), who collected in the mountains and upper Piedmont.
- Fowler (1918–1945), who reported isolated collections and re-examined Cope's material.
- Schultz and Reid (1937–1938), who made 52 collections across Virginia, 7 of them in the James system.
- The author's own collections in 1940, 1941, 1946, 1947 and 1948, plus material from Pettit, Hobbs and Hoffman.

In total, 108 collections were made in Virginia, 20 of them in the James system. Collections by Schultz, Raney, Pettit, Hobbs and Hoffman totalled 57 and served as the primary source of recent distributional data.

**Scope of the checklist.** The checklist covers:

- Lampreys, sturgeons, gars and bowfin.
- Herrings, which include several anadromous species.
- Salmonids, suckers, minnows (the largest family contributing 21 species), and catfishes (six species).
- Killifishes, livebearers, pirateperch, temperate basses and perches.
- Sunfishes and basses, the second-largest family in the basin with 12 species.
- Sculpins, sticklebacks and silversides.

Each species account gives distribution, abundance, habitat, spawning behaviour and economic value where known. Key identification problems are flagged, such as the separation of *Nocomis micropogon* from *N. leptocephalus* and of *Notropis rubellus* from *N. amoenus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiographic regions.** The James rises in the Allegheny Mountains, crosses the Great Valley, cuts through the Blue Ridge, and then flows across the Piedmont to the [[Fall Line]] at Richmond. Below the Fall Line lies the [[Coastal Plain]]. The fauna is best known in the **mountain region**, with alternating riffles, pools and gravel or bedrock bottoms. The **Piedmont** has sand-bottomed, often turbid streams, and has been poorly studied. The **Coastal Plain** is sluggish with silt or sand bottoms, is difficult to sample with seines, and is poorly known.

**Anadromous and estuarine links.** [[Chesapeake Bay]] supplies many species, both anadromous spawners and marine wanderers. Among the anadromous species are the [[Sea Lamprey]], [[Atlantic Sturgeon]], [[American Shad]], [[Alewife]] and [[Glut Herring]]. Among the marine or brackish species that enter fresh water are [[Striped Bass]], [[White Perch]], [[Mummichog]] and [[Fourspine Stickleback]]. [[American Eel]] migrates to a spawning ground south of Bermuda and enters coastal streams as juveniles.

**Pollution.** Parts of the upper James and the reaches below large cities are badly polluted, so the original fish distribution cannot be fully reconstructed.

**Faunal origins.** The author identifies a marine-derived element, a widespread Atlantic element, and a northern montane element (e.g., [[Thoburnia rhothoeca]], *Chrosomus oreas*, and *Cottus* species). A distinct break occurs between the James to the north and the Roanoke and Chowan rivers to the south. The [[Dismal Swamp]] has not acted as a barrier to typical Coastal Plain species, but many Piedmont and mountain species have not crossed it. Only two of the 74 kinds appear to be endemic: an undescribed *Boleosoma nigrum* subspecies, and *Boleosoma longimanum*, whose reported occurrence in the Potomac is probably erroneous.

**Introductions.** Several species were introduced, including [[Northern Smallmouth Bass]] (from the Ohio River), [[Southern Channel Catfish]] (from the Mississippi drainage), rock bass, and the rainbow trout, which is stocked. The status of the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] in the James is uncertain.

## Key Findings

1. **Checklist and status.** The James River fauna comprises 73 species (74 kinds) in 21 families, with 14 additional species expected on hypothetical grounds. Several records are doubtful or erroneous, including *Moxostoma cervinum*, *Notropis atherinoides*, *Notropis cerasinus*, *Notropis procne longiceps*, *Hadropterus crassus*, and *Boleosoma nigrum effulgens*. Some earlier records were based on misidentified specimens.

2. **Commercially important anadromous fishes.** Atlantic sturgeon was once abundant: 108,900 pounds were taken in the James in 1880, but numbers have "seriously declined in recent years." American shad, alewife and glut herring spawn in the lower and middle river.

3. **Recent collecting effort.** Recent collections (57 total) found the following frequencies of selected species:
   - Common white sucker: 12 of 57 stations.
   - Common hog sucker: 14 of 57.
   - Rustyside sucker: 32 of 57 (abundant in mountain riffles).
   - Eastern common shiner: 32 of 57.
   - Fantail darter: 29 of 57 (probably the most common darter in the upper James).
   - Carolina chub: 28 of 57.
   - Eastern blacknose dace: 26 of 57.
   - Eastern brook trout: 6 of 16 Johns Creek stations and 4 of 12 Craig Creek stations (Burton and Odum, 1945).

4. **Taxonomic revisions and open questions.** Several taxa need further work, including the subspecies status of *Campostoma anomalum*, the Johnny darter (*Boleosoma nigrum*) populations of the mountains, the spottail shiner (*Notropis hudsonius saludanus*), and the eastern *Moxostoma* group. The author states that "the Atlantic Coastal species of *Moxostoma* are in need of revision."

5. **Key identification issue.** *Nocomis micropogon* (river chub) and *N. leptocephalus* (Carolina chub) were long confused. The author provides a dichotomous key using scale counts, head proportions and nuptial tubercle placement.

6. **Economic and ecological roles.** Many minnows serve as forage and bait. The catfish fishery was described as more important than the shad or striped bass fishery in the James (Menzel, 1945). Gizzard shad is important as forage but of little value as food. Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) is valued for consuming mosquito larvae.

7. **Research priorities.** The author calls for more collecting in the Piedmont and Coastal Plain, which are "worked very little" and "poorly known," and notes that many hypothetical species should attract "young zoologists."
