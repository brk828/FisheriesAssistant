# Relations Between Maximum-Annual Discharge and Relative Abundance of Age-0 Colorado Squawfish and Other Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], tests whether [[Maximum-Annual Discharge]] in the upper [[Colorado River]] is related to the relative abundance of larval and post-larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] and six sympatric fish species. Data came from [[Larval Sampling]] and [[Post-Larval Sampling]] collected 1982–1988. Colorado squawfish is one of several endangered fishes in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], and the authors frame discharge management as a possible recovery tool.

The main results are:
- Larval Colorado squawfish frequency-of-zero catch was positively correlated with maximum-annual discharge across the river, but only one reach-level correlation was reported as significant.
- Post-larval Colorado squawfish catch rates in reach 1 showed a parabolic response, with the highest catches after maximum flows of 30,000–40,000 cfs.
- Most introduced species tended to decline in relative abundance in high-discharge years, though the relationships were generally weaker.

The authors conclude that regulating maximum-annual discharge may not be an important way to suppress non-native fishes while benefiting Colorado squawfish. They recommend maximum-annual flows of 30,000–40,000 cfs and call for more data.

## Background & Description

Three native fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin were listed as endangered when the report was written: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*). The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was extremely rare and a candidate for listing. The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (USFWS 1987) directs management, including water management, toward recovery.

Seasonal discharge is thought to affect Colorado squawfish reproduction. Earlier work reported that:
- Age-0 abundance in the [[Green River]] in fall was negatively related to mid-to-late summer discharge (Jones and Tyus 1985).
- Abundance was lowest in 1983 and 1984, years of unusually high basin-wide runoff.
- Larvae were relatively abundant in the [[Yampa River]], an important spawning area, in those same years (Haynes et al. 1985).

The authors proposed that high spring runoff helped reproduction in the Yampa, but high summer flows downstream in the Green may have reduced survival of young that moved from the Yampa as larvae.

**Study area.** The study covered the 185-mile reach of the Colorado River from the Green River confluence (RM 0) to Palisade, Colorado. For larval analyses, the river was split into three reaches:
- **Reach A:** low-gradient area downstream from [[Moab]] (RM 0–65).
- **Reach B:** moderate-to-high-gradient reach from Moab to [[Westwater Canyon]] (RM 65–110). Westwater Canyon was not sampled.
- **Reach C:** moderate-gradient reach from Westwater Canyon to the upper Grand Valley near Grand Junction (RM 110–185), including the deepwater [[Black Rocks]] area.

For post-larval analyses, the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) reach 1 covered RM 0–110 and reach 2 covered part of reach C (RM 140–170).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Larval fishes were summarized as frequency-of-zero catch (F₀). Post-larval fishes were summarized as geometric-mean [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (C/E, fish per m² seined), calculated from backwater seine hauls using a loge((C/E)+1) transformation. Seine mesh was 6 mm in 1982 and 3 mm in 1983–1988. Post-larval surveys were made once in 1982, twice in 1983, three times in 1984 and 1985, and once in each of 1986–1988. When several surveys occurred in a year, the October sample was used. Relations to maximum-annual discharge, measured at the USGS gage at the Colorado-Utah border, were tested with the [[Pearson Correlation Coefficient]].

**Sample sizes.** Larval samples numbered 153 (1982), 221 (1983), 191 (1984), 226 (1985) and 48 (1988).

**Community composition.**
- Larvae: [[Red Shiner]] was most common over the study period, followed by [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Gila Spp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], Colorado squawfish and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. White sucker, plains killifish, green sunfish, mosquitofish, common carp and channel catfish were rare.
- Post-larvae: Red shiner was again most abundant, followed by bluehead sucker, unidentified suckers, roundtail chub, fathead minnow, sand shiner, speckled dace, Colorado squawfish and flannelmouth sucker. Common carp, channel catfish, green sunfish and largemouth bass were in small numbers.

**Discharge context.** Maximum-annual flows in 1983 and 1984 were the highest ever recorded, about 7–8 times the average streamflow of the previous 10 years. The authors note that discharge is correlated with temperature regime, sediment dynamics and summer streamflow, and they could not separate which factor drives the responses.

**Introduced species and flooding.** The authors cite [[Minckley and Meffe 1987]], who found that flood events exceeding average discharge by about 100 times or more removed introduced fishes from canyon-bound Arizona stream reaches, while native fishes persisted. Smaller floods reduced but did not eliminate non-native populations. Many authors have argued that moderated flow regimes have helped [[Introduced Species]] establish in the Colorado River.

## Key Findings

**1. Larval Colorado squawfish.**
- Frequency-of-zero catch was positively correlated with maximum-annual discharge in all three reaches and the river as a whole, meaning fewer samples contained larvae in high-flow years.
- Sample size was small (4–5 years). Only one reported correlation was significant (r = 0.988, P < 0.05). The others were relatively high (r > 0.65).
- Colorado squawfish larvae occurred in more than 30% of collections in a reach in only one year. They never exceeded 20% of all collections in any year.

**2. Post-larval Colorado squawfish.**
- Geometric-mean C/E was not linearly related to discharge in reach 1 (r = −0.240 for mean C/E). The response was parabolic, with the highest catch rates preceded by maximum flows of 30,000–40,000 cfs. Low catches occurred in both high- and low-flow years.
- When the record-high years 1983–1984 were excluded, reach 1 C/E correlated with maximum discharge at r = 0.98 (P < 0.01).
- Reach 2 showed a weak negative relation (r = −0.658).

**3. Other species, larval stage.**
- Positive F₀ correlations with discharge were found for [[Gila Spp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Sand Shiner]].
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] was the only species whose larvae were most common in high-discharge years (negative correlations).
- Speckled dace showed only a slight, inconsistent trend.

**4. Other species, post-larval stage.**
- Relative abundance of most post-larval species was lower in high-discharge years. Several relations were strong but not significant because of small sample sizes.
- Red shiner had the strongest relation in reach 1 (r = −0.889, significant at P < 0.05). Sand shiner in reach 2 was significant (r = −0.801). Fathead minnow and Gila spp. were also negatively related.
- Unlike Colorado squawfish, introduced species often peaked in low-flow years.

**5. Management conclusions.**
- Regulating maximum-annual discharge may not be an important way to reduce the reproductive success of introduced fishes while increasing that of Colorado squawfish.
- Water management to increase Colorado squawfish reproduction should aim for maximum-annual flows of 30,000–40,000 cfs, measured at the Colorado-Utah border. The necessary duration of such flows is not yet known.
- Peak flows may also benefit introduced fishes, though less than lower flows would.
- Enhancing undesirable non-native species may be an unavoidable side effect, which highlights the need to learn which non-natives harm Colorado squawfish.
- A rapid reduction in maximum-annual flows, possibly through upstream reservoir storage, might speed seasonal warming, promote earlier spawning and lengthen the first-year growing season. This would follow the hypothesis of Kaeding and Osmundson (1988) that faster early growth could raise survival and recruitment.

**6. Limitations and next steps.** The authors state that the observed relations need more years of data to become statistically robust, that the underlying causal mechanism is unknown, and that continued collection and analysis are required.
