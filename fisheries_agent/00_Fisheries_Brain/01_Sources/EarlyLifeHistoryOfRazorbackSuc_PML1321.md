# Early Life History of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study, published as a final report in January 1986 for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region) by Langhorst and Harsh of Arizona State University, examined why [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae disappear from [[Lake Mohave]] before recruiting to the adult population. Adults spawned successfully from January to April, and larvae were abundant from February to mid-April, but no juveniles or small adults had been recorded since the 1950s. The authors tested four objectives: predation, larval food habits relative to food availability, larval dispersal, and larval survival in predator-free habitats.

Larvae grew to more than 20 mm in a predator-free backwater and in cages, but did not grow in the open lake. The study found that predation by [[Green Sunfish]] occurred, and that a flood breach let predatory fishes invade the backwater, where the larval population then declined. The authors propose that larvae move offshore into deeper, benthic water and are transported down-reservoir, with predation and current-driven loss as the main fates. The hypothesis is explicitly untested.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large [[Catostomidae]] fish, was once common in the [[Colorado River]] system of southwestern North America. Its range and numbers have declined across the native range, from Wyoming to Sonora, Mexico. The only substantial remaining population in 1985 was in Lake Mohave, a Colorado River reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border. That population consisted of large, old individuals, with no evidence of successful recruitment since the 1950s.

Adults spawned in [[Arizona Bay]], [[Hammerhead Cove]], and the Six Mile Area. Arizona Bay and Hammerhead Cove were the primary study sites. A natural backwater adjacent to Arizona Bay, separated from the lake by a gravel spit, was used for isolated rearing. The backwater had maximum depths of 1.2 to 3.7 m and surface areas of 0.85 to 2.1 ha, and it was stocked in January 1985 with 30 female and 150 male adults from Arizona Bay. Fishes that originally inhabited the backwater were removed by rotenone in autumn 1984.

Key study items:
- Spawning was first observed on 8 January 1985 in Arizona Bay, at a water temperature of 11.5 °C. More than 1,000 individuals were counted on optimal viewing days.
- Spawning in Hammerhead Cove began about a week later, on 15 January. The cove held fewer adults (roughly 10 to 60), and adults there were depleted by sampling for hatchery propagation on 27 to 30 January.
- Wave heights above about 8 cm usually stopped spawning. The authors judged this a more important cue than time of day.
- First larvae appeared on 9 February in Hammerhead Cove and on 17 February in Arizona Bay. Larvae in Arizona Bay were relatively scarce despite abundant spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake conditions.** Zooplankton densities in Lake Mohave ranged from under 100 to more than 7,500 individuals/m³. Cladocerans ([[Daphnia]] and *Bosmina*) dominated, with adult copepods making up most of the rest. In the Arizona Bay backwater, rotifers dominated, followed by *Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, and copepods, and total densities were generally lower (maximum about 1,000/m³). Mean plankton sizes were similar in both systems, except that copepods and *Daphnia* were slightly larger in the backwater.

**Larval habitat.** Larvae in Hammerhead Cove were collected in shallow littoral zones (under 3 m) over cobble-gravel to fine sand, often among organic debris from saltcedar (*Tamarix*). They were associated with submerged shoreline vegetation but were not restricted to it. A few were taken at 5 to 7 m. In the backwater, small larvae stayed in shallow margins, while larvae over 15 mm moved toward deeper, offshore water.

**Predators.** Introduced fishes present in nearshore habitats included [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Common Carp]].

**Flood breach.** On 4 April 1985, a rising lake level (196.90 m, rising to 197.02 m) and westerly winds breached the spit between the lake and the backwater. Green sunfish, bluegill, threadfin shad, channel catfish, largemouth bass, and common carp invaded the backwater. By May, lake elevation had fallen to 196.60 m, and the backwater was again connected to the lake. Larvae could no longer be collected by then. The authors note that some larvae may have emigrated, but no such activity was seen, and they attribute the decline at least in part to predation. When the backwater was resampled in October 1986, only introduced species were present.

**Hydrology.** Using conservative estimates for cross-sectional area (about 19,500 m²) and discharge through the lake, the authors estimated a current velocity of about 2.6 cm/s. Extending the calculation to cross-sectional areas of 5,000 to 40,000 m² and discharges of 100 to 1,000 m³/s gives velocities of about 1.0 to 25 cm/s. The authors consider these plausible and suggest that velocities could be higher where deep currents are confined.

## Key Findings

**1. Spawning and larval timing.** Adults spawned from early January to late April. Larvae were abundant from early February through mid-April, and they disappeared from Hammerhead Cove and, presumably, the lake surface by mid-April. Mean total length (TL) of lake larvae was 10.6 mm from early February to early April, with no increase in mean or maximum length. The largest specimen was 12.2 mm. The authors estimated the maximum age of the largest specimens at only a few weeks.

**2. Growth in protected habitats.** Larvae reared in the backwater reached more than 16 mm by 28 April, and the largest was 20.3 mm. Caged larvae from Hammerhead Cove survived 2 to 3 weeks with about 80% survival and grew to 11.5 mm. Other caged larvae increased by up to 3.1 mm, nearly 30% of initial size. Eighteen-millimeter larvae moved from the backwater to a cage in Hammerhead Cove survived more than 3 weeks and reached up to 34 mm TL. The authors conclude that growth of larval razorback sucker is not precluded by physical or chemical constraints in Lake Mohave, contrary to the view of [[Bozek et al. 1984]]. The reasons for disappearance at about 10 to 12 mm TL remain unknown.

**3. Food habits.** Most larvae had empty stomachs, probably because mouths were not yet fully functional. The most frequent stomach contents were amorphous organic matter, followed by cladocerans (*Bosmina*, *Daphnia*, and *Macrothrix*), rotifers, and copepods. Larvae selected *Daphnia* but took individuals about half the average size available in the plankton. Larger larvae (over 12 mm) had fewer empty stomachs and a more diverse diet, including chironomid larvae, the largest prey items. The authors suggest that benthic invertebrates may contribute more to larval nutrition than occurrence data indicate. Larvae used limnetic, benthic, and macrophyte habitats.

**4. Predation.** Among 81 fish from gill nets in Hammerhead Cove, sucker larvae were found only in green sunfish. Frequency of occurrence was low (2.7%), and mean larvae per predator was 2.0. In a feeding experiment, 77 to 119 mm green sunfish consumed 10 larvae within 0.25 hours. Larvae were recognizable at 2 hours, amorphous by 4 hours, and largely digested by 6 hours. They were undetectable by the final sampling interval, so stomach samples may underestimate predation. The authors advise caution when interpreting previous predation estimates. In the backwater after the breach, 37% of 52 green sunfish contained larvae, with a mean of 3.9 ± 2.0 larvae per fish (Table 5). The text gives a mean of 19 larvae, which conflicts with the table and should be checked against the source. Green sunfish were the only fish species found to consume sucker larvae during this period.

**5. Dispersal.** The single larva captured in 22 net-hours of larval trawling (13 mm TL) suggested that larvae were either unavailable in open water or too scarce to sample. The authors conclude that larvae may be below 15 m or near the bottom, and they propose that the larvae are benthic and move offshore to deeper water. Two possible fates are predation by [[Threadfin Shad]] (a probable major predator offshore) and down-reservoir transport toward [[Davis Dam]].

**6. Recruitment context.** More than 1,000 razorback suckers have been taken from Lake Mohave in the past decade, and none, apart from larvae, has been smaller than about 400 mm TL. The smallest individual was estimated at more than 20 years old. A similar population exists in Senator Wash Reservoir, California, which also shows no recruitment. Since the mid-1970s, however, at least a dozen fish under 400 mm have been taken from canals fed by the Colorado River below Davis Dam.

**7. Proposed hypothesis.** The authors propose that larvae move offshore and are then transported down-reservoir and either eaten or flushed through Davis Dam. Local currents near Hammerhead Cove could trap larvae there. The authors explicitly note that the hypothesis is untested and that alternative explanations exist.

**8. Recommendations for further study.**
- Determine the dispersal of larvae below Davis Dam during peak larval abundance.
- Measure the depth, direction, and magnitude of down-reservoir currents, and their role in dispersing larvae from known spawning areas.
- Expand predation research with digestion-rate experiments using threadfin shad and rainbow trout, and continue close sampling of predators during larval periods.
- Monitor the Arizona Bay backwater in 1986 (restocked with adults in December 1985), focusing on larval growth and feeding.
- Refine cage experiments to improve survival and growth measurements in Lake Mohave.
- Examine preserved larvae to determine mouth and anus dimensions at the switch from yolk to active feeding.
