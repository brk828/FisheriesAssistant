# Farewell to the Nineteenth Century: The Breaching of Edwards Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anadromous-fish

## Executive Summary

This narrative essay, written in the style of [[John McPhee]], follows a canoe trip down the [[Kennebec River]] in Maine and the 1999 removal of [[Edwards Dam]] in [[Augusta Maine]]. The breaching of the dam on July 1, 1999 was the first time a major river dam was ordered out of existence by the federal government while its owner was left holding a renewal application. The text frames the event as a restoration of anadromous fish access to historic spawning grounds, while also covering the history of the river, the politics and financing of the removal, and broader debates about dams across the United States.

## Background & Description

**The river and the dam.** The Kennebec flows south from [[Moosehead Lake]] and was the central thoroughfare of Maine. The first English settlement on the river was established before the Pilgrims' arrival, and [[Fort Western]] was built at the [[Fall Line]] in 1754. The Augusta Water Power Company blocked the river in 1837, and the impoundment covered [[Six-Mile Falls]]. Edwards Dam, a barrage roughly 300 yards across, was named after the Edwards Manufacturing Company, which bought it in the 1880s. The dam generated electricity from 1913 and, at the time of removal, produced 3.5 megawatts.

**Historical fish runs.** Before the dam, about a million [[American Shad]] reportedly came up the Kennebec in 1837 before the Augusta dam stopped them. Atlantic salmon were speared by the Kennebec peoples below upriver falls. A [[Fish Ladder]] at the Augusta dam was lost in a flood in 1838 and was not replaced. In the eighteenth century, Kennebec salmon were reportedly so abundant that employers had to limit how often they were fed salmon.

**Decline and recovery.** Log drives on the Kennebec began in Colonial times and ended in 1976. By the 1940s and 1950s the river was polluted with industrial toxins and community waste, and office windows near the State Capitol were kept shut in summer. Following the [[Clean Water Act]] of 1972, the river steadily improved.

**The removal.** Before the breach, the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] ordered the owner to shut down turbines and deconstruct the dam, reasoning that "needed and appropriate" fishways would cost three times as much as removal. The 1986 amendment to the Federal Power Act required the commission to give "equal consideration" to wildlife, recreation, and environmental quality when licensing dams. Financing came from several sources: the State of Maine, the [[Bath Iron Works]] (about $2.5 million), and the Kennebec Hydro Developers Group (nearly $5 million), in exchange for fish-passage postponements of up to fifteen years at their own dams. The text reports that more than $7 million was provided in total, with about $1 million left over for fish restoration programs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biological rationale.** [[Lewis Flagg]], director of the Stock Enhancement Division of Maine's Department of Marine Resources, listed seven benefits of removing any dam: (1) removing obstructions to migration, (2) restoring natural habitat, (3) restoring natural flow variation, (4) ending siltation of spawning and feeding habitat in the former pool, (5) removing debris, (6) eliminating unnatural downstream temperatures, and (7) removing turbines that kill juvenile fish.

**Projected fish returns.** Flagg expected as many as 725,000 shad to spawn north of Augusta in roughly fifteen years. A sport-fishing economic study projected $48 million in economic activity for the river from sport fishermen soon after the dam's removal. Striped bass and a large Atlantic salmon were seen near the dam on the day of the breach.

**Freshwater mussels and other species.** Conservationists raised concerns about the [[Yellow Lampmussel]] and the [[Tidewater Mucket]]. On the day of the breach, volunteers from Nokomis High School rescued lampmussels and muckets, while lamprey larvae were seen in the muck. Some commentators argued that removing the dam might allow [[Carp]] and other unwanted species into tributaries.

**Post-breach landscape.** Newly exposed river reaches showed filamentous algae in thick mats, and the restored rapids had been submerged for 162 years. Birds observed included mallards, song sparrows, a wood thrush, a bald eagle, a cedar waxwing, and a spotted sandpiper; beavers and a cormorant were also seen. Rock-filled log-drive cribs remained standing in the river.

**Broader dam landscape.** The text cites about 66,000 US river dams at least five feet high, with about half of American dams expected to be around fifty years old by 2020. Other cases cited include:

- [[Condit Dam]] on the White Salmon River, Washington, where fishways were required.
- The [[Snake River Dams]] (four dams), where the Army Corps of Engineers' report was due in 2000 and removal was estimated at $1 billion.
- [[Embrey Dam]] on the Rappahannock River, Virginia, where removal was estimated at $4 million versus $10.2 (units as printed) for fish passage.
- [[Savage Rapids Dam]] on the Rogue River, Oregon, and Price-Stubb Dam on the Colorado River, relevant to the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
- Fossil Creek, Arizona, cited as a conflict between hydropower and the [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- Malibu Creek, California, where a historic steelhead run was blocked by a dam now filled with silt.
- Wisconsin, which has removed 49 dams, and [[Grand Coulee Dam]] on the Columbia River, the largest producer of hydropower cited (6,000 megawatts).

## Key Findings

- **Federal precedent:** The Edwards Dam breach was the first time a major river dam was ordered removed by the federal government while the owner retained the license application, a development the text presents as a "new era of dam removal."
- **Cost comparison:** Removal was ordered because fishways were judged to cost about three times as much; the owner's exit was negotiated through a settlement involving the State of Maine and several parties.
- **Restoration scope:** The Kennebec's first 15 river miles above Augusta were described as the potential for spawning habitat for millions of migrating fish, though the author notes that the Kennebec watershed still contains at least 100 dams.
- **Economic and political framing:** Proponents of removal cited fish restoration, tourism, and river health; critics raised concerns about hydropower's lower carbon emissions, lost industrial heritage, and the trade-offs behind the settlement.
- **Scale of the issue:** The essay argues that dam removal is a growing national trend, although it notes that only a small fraction of American dams had been removed; over 200 small-river dams had been removed since 1960.
- **Historical continuity:** The author ties the event to Revolutionary War history, colonial trade, and nineteenth-century industry, presenting the removal as a symbolic break with the economic and ecological legacy of that era.
