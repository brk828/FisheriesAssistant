# Recharge to Ground-Water from Floods in a Typical Desert Wash, Pinal County, Arizona

#hydrology #groundwater-recharge #ephemeral-streams

## Executive Summary

This 1941 hydrological study by H. M. Babcock and E. M. Cushing, of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], examines how flash floods in [[Queen Creek]], a typical large desert wash in [[Pinal County]], [[Arizona]], recharge the underlying [[Alluvium]] aquifer. The work supported a proposed flood-control dam at or above [[Black Point Dam Site]], which would protect irrigated lands downstream while controlling the water released for ground-water storage. Using two gaging stations and 11 seepage runs, the authors found that about half of the measured flood runoff over two years infiltrated into the channel and flood-plain. Average infiltration over the wetted area was about 1.08 feet per day. The study concludes that controlled release could push recharge toward the full flow, with a suggested safe release of about 500 second-feet, or up to 1,000 second-feet with artificial spreading devices.

This document is hydrological rather than biological. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or other aquatic species data.

## Background & Description

Queen Creek rises near the mining town of Superior, crosses the outwash plain at Black Point about three miles north of [[Florence Junction]], and flows southwest toward Chandler, where it spreads across the lowlands and disappears. The stream flow is almost entirely storm water of the "quick, flashy" type typical of Southwestern deserts. In most years it is dry most of the time.

Historically, floods spread harmlessly across the desert floor. Development of irrigated cropland, supplied by [[Salt River]] water or wells, changed this. Floods now damage crops and canals. The proposed remedy was a dam to store storm water.

The study's aims were to:
- Estimate how reservoir control would affect normal ground-water recharge downstream.
- Determine the release rate that would maximize recharge.
- Assess whether [[Water-Spreading]] could increase recharge.

The work was part of a wider [[Ground-Water Resources of Arizona]] program run with the Arizona State Water Commissioner and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Field work began on August 5, 1939, under L. C. Penny. R. B. Morrison studied the geology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and sediments**
- Upstream, the creek crosses relatively impermeable schists, granites and volcanic rocks, and loses little water to deep storage.
- Older alluvium forms a piedmont plain and a stream terrace. Both are moderately to poorly sorted, and a [[Caliche]] layer 6 to 10 feet thick in the gravel retards water penetration.
- Recent alluvium in the present flood-plain consists of sand, gravel and silt. Sediments become finer downstream. Channel material is reworked by clear-water flows, becoming better sorted and more permeable.
- Downstream, a silt mantle one to several feet thick covers the flood-plain and limits infiltration.

**Recharge pattern**
- Queen Creek is influent (losing) throughout its course below the Black Point dam site.
- Minor floods infiltrate within a short distance. Larger floods lose water progressively as channel deposits become finer.
- Water that is not absorbed spreads over the silt-covered lower plain and is lost by [[Evaporation and Transpiration]].

**Water-level response**
- Wells on the desert plain near the creek rose noticeably in early spring 1941 due to winter and spring runoff recharge.
- Wells farther from the creek showed little rise.

## Key Findings

**1. Flood-loss measurements between gaging stations**
- Two gaging stations were used: the [[Upper Gaging-Station]], about three-quarters of a mile below Black Point, and the [[Ellsworth Bridge Gaging-Station]], about 20 miles downstream. The upper station was established in August 1939 and the Ellsworth Bridge station somewhat later.
- The first year was exceptionally dry and the second exceptionally wet, allowing comparison of seepage loss under contrasting climates.
- Table 1 summarizes 17 floods from February 12, 1940, to March 12–18, 1941. Peak flows at the upper station ranged from a few second-feet to 13,000 second-feet.
- Over the two years, 64,300 acre-feet passed the Upper Gaging-Station and 32,100 acre-feet passed the Ellsworth Bridge station. About one-half of the runoff was therefore recharged to ground-water.

**2. Infiltration rates during floods**
- The computed average infiltration over the wetted area ranged from 0.14 to 2.09 feet per day and averaged 1.08 feet per day.
- Summer-type flash floods had lower rates and a wider range than winter-type floods. The authors attributed this to shorter duration and higher silt load in summer floods, and noted that measurement of the summer floods was less accurate.
- Silt-laden water reduces infiltration, as earlier [[Santa Ana River]] spreading work by Mitchelson and Muckel had shown.

**3. Seepage-run measurements (clear-water flows)**
- Eleven seepage runs were made between January 24 and March 29, 1941, when heavy rainfall produced continuous, comparatively clear flow past the upper station.
- During these runs, clear-water infiltration averaged more than four feet per day. This was higher than the flood average of about one foot per day.
- The lower flood rate was attributed to silt load early in floods and to spreading over impermeable silty areas outside the channel.
- Infiltration rates increased over time during continuous clear flow. For a 2.1-mile reach between the upper station and the highway bridge, the rate was 6.18 feet per day on January 29, 6.53 on January 30, and 6.82 on January 31, 1941. It was 5.98 feet per day on March 23 and 6.84 on March 25.
- The authors interpreted this as clear water carrying away fine material and leaving coarser, more permeable beds.

**4. Pool-drawdown check**
- Declines in water levels in pools left after floods gave a rate range from 2.76 to 0.16 feet per day, with an initial average of 0.91 foot per day. This is close to the 1.08 feet per day flood average, supporting the stream-gaging coefficients.

**5. Evaporation from the stream bed**
- Moisture loss from containers of stream-bed material matched a Colorado sunken-pan [[Evaporation]] measurement for the first 36 hours after a flood. It then decreased rapidly. This indicates that only a modest amount of water is needed to satisfy the bed's moisture deficit before recharge begins.

**6. Conclusions and management recommendations**
- Recharge to ground-water is the main source of desert aquifer recharge in the Southwest and occurs by infiltration through stream beds where they cross desert plains.
- A flood-control dam at or above Black Point could release low-silt water at a nearly uniform rate and extend flows over a much longer period. The authors judged that under controlled release the channel would not seal with silt and would instead be partly cleaned of fine material.
- The largest release from the upper station that would produce no flow past the Ellsworth Bridge station is about 500 second-feet, assuming most silt has settled in the reservoir. This could possibly rise to 1,000 second-feet with artificial spreading devices in the channel.
- Arizona's developable surface-water and much of its ground-water are already in use, so artificial recharge, as used in [[Southern California]], could recover water otherwise lost to evaporation.
