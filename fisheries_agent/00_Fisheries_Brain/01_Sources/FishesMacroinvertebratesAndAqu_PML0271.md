# Fishes, Macroinvertebrates, and Aquatic Habitats of the Purgatoire River in Piñon Canyon, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes the physical habitat, water chemistry, fish assemblages and benthic macroinvertebrates of a relatively undisturbed canyon reach of the [[Purgatoire River]] within the [[Piñon Canyon Maneuver Site]] in southeastern [[Colorado]]. Sampling ran from 1983 through 1989, with intensive work in 1987–1989 at 15 river sites and 51 tributary sites. The fish fauna is depauperate, with 11 native species in five families and no non-native species captured. The authors attribute the assemblage's persistence to generalized habitat, trophic and reproductive traits suited to harsh plains conditions. A marked decline in [[Red Shiner]] abundance between 1983 and 1987 is a key unexplained result.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** A reach of the Purgatoire River, a seventh-order tributary of the [[Arkansas River]], plus 10 adjacent ephemeral, intermittent or perennial tributaries. The reach lies in the southwestern Tablelands ecoregion and has a semi-arid climate with mean annual precipitation of about 30 cm.
- **Setting:** The canyon is cut into Dakota sandstone, 60 to 1,800 m wide and up to 150 m deep. Vegetation ranges from pinyon-juniper uplands to shortgrass prairie, with willow and cottonwood riparian zones often invaded by tamarisk.
- **Flow management:** Since January 1975, discharge has been regulated by [[Trinidad Dam]], a hypolimnial-release reservoir about 32 km upstream.
- **Rationale:** Few prior studies exist for this area. Plains streams in the region are rarely undisturbed, owing to channelization, damming, mining, overgrazing, irrigation, and introduced species. The authors present this reach as a rare remnant for ecological study.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were collected with seines (5.0- and 6.5-mm mesh), baited minnow traps, a monofilament gill net and a small trap net, with passive gear set overnight for about 18 h. Benthic invertebrates were collected with Surber samples (three 0.09 m² samples per riffle) and dip-net samples. Habitat was measured along transects during low flow in September and October 1987.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Streamflow is perennial but highly variable. Unpredictable floods during April through September reached 1,207 m³/s (the largest on record), against a summer baseflow of about 0.85 m³/s. Instantaneous flood flows ran 1.5 to 23 times the daily mean discharge. Post-dam records (1975–1988) showed mean monthly flows higher in 11 of 12 months and mean annual discharge more than double the pre-dam period. The authors attribute this to wetter conditions, not the dam. Floods were as extreme after dam closure as before, and were mostly produced by intense local rains downstream of the dam. Baseflow in the canyon appears to come mainly from groundwater and irrigation return flows.

**Physical habitat.** River habitat was dominated by long, silt-bottomed pools with large boulders, separated by short cobble riffles.
- Pools: 84 to 359 m long, 11.7 to 20.5 m mean width, 0.40 to 0.89 m mean depth, maximum depths 1.1 to more than 2.7 m.
- Riffles: 17 to 112 m long, 9.3 to 18.2 m mean width, 0.16 to 0.29 m mean depth. Cobble made up about 73% of riffle substrate points.
- Fish cover was sparse at river sites, and no site had large wood debris. Tributary pools had more abundant aquatic and riparian vegetation.

**Water chemistry and temperature.** Conditions were within ranges tolerable for plains stream fishes, including in isolated tributary pools during midsummer. Water temperatures reached 31.0 °C upstream and 34.5 °C downstream. Dissolved oxygen was never low enough to stress fish. Suspended sediment was high (medians 212 to 470 mg/l), and metals from historical mining were probably not bioavailable.

**Land use.** Army tracked-vehicle training reduced vegetation cover on about 40% of the maneuver site's uplands during 1985–1987, while bare ground decreased on the 60% of uplands not used for training. Training has apparently not increased sediment yield beyond levels caused by earlier grazing.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish fauna is depauperate.** Eleven native species in five families were found. Six cyprinids dominated: [[Central Stoneroller]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Flathead Chub]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Longnose Dace]]. The remainder included [[White Sucker]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Plains Killifish]] and [[Green Sunfish]].

2. **No non-native fishes were captured** in 1983 or 1987–1989. This is notable because several non-natives occur upstream in [[Trinidad Lake]] (for example [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Walleye]]), and common carp were recorded about 20 km downstream in 1981. The authors suggest severe summer floods within constraining canyon walls prevent establishment of fishes not adapted to harsh flows.

3. **Three faunal associations were identified** through multivariate clustering:
   - *Main river fishes* (red shiner, flathead chub, sand shiner, longnose dace, channel catfish) were captured at 92–100% of river sites but only 0–72% of intermittent tributary sites.
   - *Small perennial stream fishes* (central stoneroller, white sucker, plains killifish) were captured at 67–100% of Van Bremer Arroyo sites.
   - *Generalist fishes* (fathead minnow, green sunfish, and black bullhead) occurred across several habitats.

4. **Red shiner decline.** Abundance declined markedly from 1983 to 1987, despite favorable flow regimes. In 1983, age-0 fish (20–30 mm) made up 43% (SE 6.4) of red shiners sampled. Only three red shiners under 30 mm were captured during 1987–1989. The authors suggest reduced reproductive success, but flood records did not clearly support the flood-elimination hypothesis, and the cause remains unresolved.

5. **Unexpected spawning site.** Central stonerollers, usually associated with lotic habitat, were relatively abundant in intermittent tributary pools. Individuals of 35–40 mm captured in June 1988 in a Bent Canyon pool suggest spawning in pools that may lack running water for more than a year.

6. **Benthic macroinvertebrates.** A total of 42 taxa were found: 27 at river sites and 27 at tributary sites. Richness per site was 7 to 19 at river sites and 11 to 15 in tributary pools. Densities ranged from 237 to 7,446 organisms/m². Collector-gatherers (*Choroterpes mexicanus*, *Microcylloepus* sp.) and collector-filterers (*Cheumatopsyche*, *Hydropsyche*, Simuliidae) together made up about 96% (SE 0.95) of river fauna by number. No shredders were found at river sites. Their scarcity and that of primary producers were attributed to high turbidity, scouring floods and limited woody riparian vegetation. This pattern is consistent with the [[River Continuum Concept]].

7. **Mixed biogeographic origins.** The macroinvertebrate fauna combines southwestern, eastern, Rocky Mountain and widespread North American elements. Eastern dragonflies such as *Erpetogomphus designatus*, a new state record, reached the reach via the Arkansas River. The stonefly-like megalopteran *Corydalus cognatus* has no other recorded site east of the Rocky Mountains in Colorado.

8. **Persistence and conservation value.** The authors conclude that the depauperate fish assemblage is well adapted to harsh plains conditions through generalized habitat, trophic and reproductive traits. The reach, with its unique cobble riffles and absence of non-natives over a seven-year period, is recommended for further study as one of the few remaining relatively undisturbed [[Great Plains]] stream ecosystems.
