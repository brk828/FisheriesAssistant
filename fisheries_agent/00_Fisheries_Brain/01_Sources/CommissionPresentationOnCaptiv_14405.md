# Arizona Captive Propagation Rule Petition Review

#herpetology #reptiles #wildlife-policy

## Executive Summary

This internal memo from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] Chief of Law Enforcement, Tim Wade, to Assistant Director Bruce Taubert (WMD) on December 8, 1994, presents the Department's staff position against a petition by Michael B. Peugnet and the [[Arizona Herpetological Association]] (AHA). The petition sought a new [[Live Wildlife Rule]] (Article 4) allowing commercial captive propagation of native Arizona amphibians and reptiles. The memo argues that commercializing native wildlife would increase harvest pressure, drive illegal collecting, add habitat damage, and burden an already strained Department budget. It recommends against the petition and reaffirms the Commission's earlier restrictive policy.

## Background & Description

The Live Wildlife Rules were substantially revised in 1989 after two years of review, mediation, public hearings, and regulatory and legislative scrutiny. During that process the Commission directed the Department not to expand commercial use of wildlife under Article 4. Only historically permitted activities, such as trapping and minnow and waterdog sales, and activities that help control exotic species, such as carp and buffalofish sales, were allowed.

Under [[R12-4-404A]], a person may take wildlife for which the Commission has set a live bag limit. For reptiles in this case, the taken animals may only be possessed, transported, or placed for personal use. Under Part B, such animals and their progeny may be disposed of only by gift, as directed by the Department, or by release if not removed from the area of capture. Export is permitted only as a gift or as directed by the Department.

The memo quotes the 1989 explanatory statement, which describes [[R12-4-404]] native-species rules as more restrictive than [[R12-4-405]] import-trade rules. The stated purpose was to prevent the take or propagation of Arizona wildlife for commercial purposes, because unrestricted activity could lead to over-exploitation.

The petition's main provisions are:
- Licensed breeders would be allowed to sell captive-bred reptiles.
- Only animals under a specified maximum length could be sold, to limit sales of wild-taken juveniles.
- Licensed veterinarians would mark breeding stock with [[PIT Tag]] (Passive Integrated Transponder) devices to separate captive-bred animals from wild-caught adults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memo links commercialization to several resource risks:

- **Harvest pressure:** Personal-use harvest can usually be managed with bag and possession limits. Financial incentives, however, encourage take that exceeds legal limits.
- **Juvenile vulnerability:** Reptile births and hatchings in Arizona are highly seasonal, and juveniles of most petitioned species make up a large share of animals seen in the wild during those periods. The Department reports that 40 percent of the [[Desert Rosy Boa]] its herpetologist observed in the wild were under the proposed maximum size limit. The memo notes this figure would likely be higher if surveys targeted juvenile periods.
- **Habitat degradation:** Collection has been associated with widespread habitat loss and degradation for at least two of the listed species. The memo cites habitat loss as probably the single most important threat to biodiversity, globally and locally, and stresses the fragility of Arizona's habitats and microhabitats.
- **Collector and buyer overlap:** The memo states that buyers and collectors overlap little. Most buyers do not collect, and most collectors do not stop collecting because they enjoy it. Both groups are growing.

## Key Findings

1. **Captive breeding has not reduced wild pressure.** The memo cites several cases:
   - In a year-long [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] undercover operation, 25 individuals were cited or arrested. Eight held California commercial breeding licenses. Of 750 reptiles seized, most came from those eight commercial breeders. Violations included selling snakes over the legal length, reselling wild-caught native snakes as captive stock, and inadequate record keeping.
   - The [[Gray-Banded Kingsnake]] has been bred in captivity for about 20 years, yet wild collection is probably more intense than ever.
   - Captive breeding of the [[Desert Rosy Boa]] has grown exponentially, with dozens of breeders, while Department law enforcement reports unprecedented collecting of wild rosy boas.

2. **The size limit is weakly protective.** The proposed length cap is meant to make illegal sales of wild juveniles unprofitable. The memo argues that juveniles of several species are commonly seen in the wild, including [[Gopher Snake]] and [[Common Kingsnake]], so the cap would not prevent illegal take.

3. **PIT tagging is impractical and poorly specified.** The memo expects only a few veterinarians in Phoenix and Tucson to afford PIT readers, forcing rural breeders to travel. It notes that tags would need standardization to avoid multiple readers, and it questions the petition's vague "expertise" requirement for veterinarians. It also observes that the Commission does not regulate veterinary medicine. If the rule passes, the memo says an identification system at least as strict as proposed would be essential.

4. **Administrative and enforcement costs are high.** The Department already struggles to administer its current special licenses. Another license with large administrative and enforcement demands would strain the budget. Wildlife Managers would need to take on enforcement, which raises the question of which responsibilities they would drop.

5. **Recommendation:** The memo recommends against the petition. It frames the Department's mission as sustaining wildlife populations and recreation, not providing financial opportunities through commercial use of native wildlife.
