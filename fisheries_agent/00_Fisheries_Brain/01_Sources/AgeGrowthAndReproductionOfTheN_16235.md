# Age, Growth and Reproduction of the Northern Mottled Sculpin (*Cottus bairdi bairdi*) in Mt. Vernon Creek, Wisconsin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This Milwaukee Public Museum Occasional Paper (Number 2, January 1969) by [[Gerald M. Ludwig]] and [[Carroll R. Norden]] describes the age, growth and reproductive biology of the [[Northern Mottled Sculpin]] in [[Mt. Vernon Creek]], a small tributary of the [[Sugar River]] in southern Wisconsin. Fish were collected over one year (December 1966 to November 1967) using seines, dip nets and electrofishing. The study compares its findings with published work on other subspecies of *Cottus bairdi* across North America, and is presented as a Master of Science thesis contribution.

## Background & Description

- The mottled sculpin (*Cottus bairdi*) occurs across much of central and north-eastern United States and southern Canada. Several subspecies are recognized, including the northern mottled sculpin (*C. b. bairdi*), the [[Great Lakes Mottled Sculpin]] (*C. b. kumlieni*), the Utah sculpin (*C. b. semiscaber*), the Rocky Mountain mottled sculpin (*C. b. punctulatus*) and *C. b. shasta*.
- The authors note the species tends to remain in cold headwaters and shows no apparent migratory habit, suggesting life-history traits may vary across its range.
- Prior life-history work on *C. b. bairdi* came from New York (Koster, 1936) and Michigan (Hann, 1927), with spawning and food-habit studies from several other authors. Before this study, the subspecies had not been investigated in the Mississippi drainage beyond its distribution and food habits.
- Identification of sex relied on external examination: males above 45 mm SL have a genital papilla (100% reliability on a check of 413 fish). Mature females show distended abdomens, and nuptial males develop dusky coloration with cream or red fin edges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study stream.** Mt. Vernon Creek is 9 miles long, averages 12 ft wide, drops 210 ft (160 ft within four miles of its source), has a mean gradient of 23.3 ft per mile, and drains 28 square miles mostly used for corn, hay and pasture. Its main tributary is Fryer's Feeder (3.4 miles long). The upper reach has riffles and pools; the lower reach is sandy, meandering and silt-bottomed.

**Study area.** A two-mile section around the village of Mt. Vernon was sampled across five stations (A to E). Station B was fed by a large spring and was heavily vegetated with [[Elodea Canadensis]] and *Nasturtium* sp. Station D was well shaded; Station E was shallow riffle with rubble and gravel.

**Temperature.** Water temperature stayed near 45°F from November to April, usually warmer than air owing to numerous springs. The minimum recorded was 33°F (March 27, 1967) and the maximum 63°F (July 15, 1967).

**Habitat use.** Sculpins were found throughout the creek, most abundant in rubble with moderate current and nearly as common in vegetation, particularly Elodea. They were scarce over bare silt or sand and difficult to catch except by electrofishing in packed humus with sheltering holes.

**Fish associates.** Sculpins were the most abundant fish. Associates included [[White Sucker]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brook Stickleback]] and [[Redside Dace]]. The [[American Brook Lamprey]] was common only during its brief spring run. Becker (1959) noted sculpins were inhabiting waters too warm for trout, questioning their status as a trout indicator.

## Key Findings

**Spawning**
- Spawning occurred from April 1 to May 3, 1967, at water temperatures of 48° to 57°F. This was earlier and longer than reported elsewhere, possibly due to unseasonably warm weather in late March.
- Of 431 females collected on May 3, only 37 were gravid; 394 were spent.
- Nests (18 measured) were mostly under flat rocks or in crevices among placed gravel, with some in firm humus tunnels (openings of about 4 cm). Average nest depth was 22 cm in 31 cm water, about 85 cm from the nearest bank in a stream averaging 430 cm wide. The use of humus tunnels was not reported in other studies.
- Practically all nests held one adult male; no nest contained more than one mature male among 74 observed. Males guarded eggs from onset of spawning until fry left in late May.

**Sex ratio and size**
- The male proportion stayed near 50% from hatching until about six months before first spawning, then fell to a minimum of 19.6% for age group II just before spawning.
- Age group III males made up 71.2% of fish 35 months old, significantly above 50%.
- On May 3, 1967 (73 males, 247 females), males averaged 59.1 mm SL and females 53 mm; the difference was reported as 95% significant.

**Fecundity and eggs**
- Females averaged 328 eggs (39 females, 41–71 mm SL); maximum 635 (two fish of 64 and 71 mm), minimum 111 (41 mm fish).
- Egg diameter averaged 1.88 mm (range 1.50–2.06 mm), smaller than previously reported.
- Fecundity equations: F = -648.4 + 16.75 L (r = 0.655) and log F = -2.7381 + 2.9615 log L (r = 0.684); the authors judged both equally valid.
- Egg clusters averaged 1205 eggs (18 dyed clusters; range 224 to 2871) and 3.3 egg masses per cluster (range 1–5), suggesting multiple females per nest.

**Incubation and early life**
- Eyed eggs appeared April 30; first prolarvae on May 17; larvae left nests from late May. Laboratory incubation took 27 days at 52–55°F.
- Newly hatched larvae averaged 5.6 mm SL (5.9 mm TL); yolk sac was absorbed by about day 14 at 6.7 mm.

**Growth**
- Mean SL was 35.9 mm in fall (age group 0), 35.2 mm at one year, 54.5 mm at two years and 72.6 mm at three years (spring collections).
- Growth was rapid in the first 6.5 months and slowed in age groups II and III. Males were significantly longer than females by 6.5 months.
- At three years, spawning males averaged 74.5 mm and females 67.9 mm.

**Length–weight relationship** (955 fish; sexes combined)
- Combined: log W = -4.48065 + 2.96359 log L.
- Relative weight peaked just before spawning (log W = -4.76263 + 3.1413 log L) and dropped sharply after spawning (log W = -4.43412 + 2.8947 log L), attributed to release of gametes and male energy loss during nest care.

**Overall conclusions**
- Sex ratios and egg production in Mt. Vernon Creek departed from published values for other subspecies (ratio not 1:1; more and smaller eggs than in *C. b. bairdi*, *C. b. kumlieni* or *C. b. punctulatus*, but fewer than *C. b. semiscaber*).
- Possible explanations for the male deficit in age group II include competition for nest sites and high post-spawning female mortality; these are hypotheses, not tested results.
