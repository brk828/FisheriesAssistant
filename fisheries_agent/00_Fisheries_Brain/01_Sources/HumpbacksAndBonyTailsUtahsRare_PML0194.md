# Utah's Rare Fish: Humpbacks and Bony Tails

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit article, written by [[Charles R. Berry Jr.]], describes Utah's rare native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species share unusual traits such as humped backs, bony tails, spines and large body size. The article traces their historical importance as food for early settlers and explorers, documents their decline, and reviews the conservation framework, led by the [[Endangered Species Act]], that is intended to protect them.

## Background & Description

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest member of the minnow family ([[Cyprinidae]]). Historical specimens reached about 45 kg (100 lb), but the largest recently taken weighed about 9 kg (20 lb). It is a piscivorous top predator. Age figures given: 1-year-olds about 100 mm, 6-year-olds about 400 mm, 11-year-olds about 600 mm.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Has a bony keel behind the head supported by vertebral extensions. Up to 1 m (3 ft) and 7 kg (lb figure garbled in source) have been captured. It is a non-predatory bottom feeder that eats algae and insect larvae.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): Broad hump behind the head, large fins, and a streamlined body. Probably never common, but widely distributed.
- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): Similar to the humpback chub but with a less pronounced hump. Described as the most streamlined native fish.
- **Spinedace** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*): Grows to about 7 cm (3 in). Historically used as bait ("shiner"). Silvery with dark specklings.
- **Woundfin** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Minnow with spined fins, a zoological rarity in the minnow family. Found only in the Virgin River.
- **Virgin River Roundtail Chub** (*Gila robusta seminuda*): Subspecies of the Colorado River roundtail chub. Adults reach about 20 cm (8 in).
- **Least Chub** (*Iotichthys phlegethontis*): Seldom exceeds 6 cm (2.5 in). A mosquito larvae predator tolerant of broad temperature ranges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The Colorado River historically contained fewer than ten fish species, a low diversity attributed to extreme conditions.
- Elevation drops about 3.2 km (2 mi) over a 2,700 km (1,700 mi) journey from Wyoming to the Gulf of California, producing highly turbulent water.
- Spring flows may be a thousand times greater than dry-weather flows.
- The river carried the equivalent of 0.3 m (1 ft) of topsoil from a 405 km² (156 mi²) area in one year.
- The fishes' adaptations (humps, streamlined bodies, large tails, keels) are believed to help them maintain equilibrium and swim upstream in turbulent water.
- Dams, dewatering for irrigation, and introduced nonnative fishes have altered the physical, chemical and biological conditions of the river.
- The least chub is threatened by competition from introduced species, irrigation water use and [[Geothermal Exploration]] in the west desert.
- The Virgin River is a silt-laden, warm-water stream in southern Utah and southeastern Nevada.

## Key Findings

- Historically the squawfish was the most prized native fish, sold commercially around 1900, and was called "white salmon" or "Colorado salmon" by settlers.
- Explorers William Ashley (1825) and John Wesley Powell's expedition relied on native fish, including squawfish, for food.
- Squawfish populations have declined drastically since the early 1900s and are now confined to parts of the upper Colorado basin in small numbers. It is listed as endangered.
- Razorback suckers have become rare, with a few individuals found in the Green River in Utah and the Colorado River in western Colorado and southeastern Utah.
- Spinedace populations are shrinking, now confined to the North and East Forks of the Virgin River near [[Zion National Park]].
- Woundfin is restricted to a few Virgin River areas and is endangered.
- Virgin River roundtail chub is known from very few specimens; it is found only in the lower Virgin River in Utah.
- Least chub, once common across the Bonneville Basin, now survives in isolated springs and marshes in the Snake Valley.
- Causes of decline are not fully understood but are linked to dams, irrigation, habitat change and nonnative species.
- Conservation legislation progressed from the 1966 Endangered Species Preservation Act to the 1969 Endangered Species Conservation Act and the 1973 [[Endangered Species Act]], which requires state laws to meet Section Six criteria for federal funding.
- The [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] leads federal efforts. The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources' Nongame Section coordinates Utah's rare fish conservation.
- The [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] at [[Utah State University]] has studied Colorado fishes for nearly 17 years and published about two dozen articles.
