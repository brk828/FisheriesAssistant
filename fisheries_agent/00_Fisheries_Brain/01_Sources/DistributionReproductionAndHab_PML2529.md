# Distribution, reproduction and habitat use of the razorback sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study by [[Harold M. Tyus]] assesses the distribution, spawning, growth and habitat use of the rare [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Green River]] of Utah and Colorado, 1979–85. Field work combined electrofishing, seining and trammel netting with [[Radiotelemetry]] of tagged fish. The species is sparse and unevenly distributed, concentrated in flatwater reaches of the Upper Green River, and absent from [[Whitewater Canyons]]. Ripe adults and larvae confirm spawning in spring, yet recruitment appears absent, and the author concludes the population will decline as old individuals die unless causes are identified.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and is among its rarest fishes. It was once abundant and widely distributed, but its riverine existence is now mainly confined to the Upper Colorado River Basin, including the Green and Upper Colorado mainstems. Relict individuals persist in lower Colorado River reservoirs, where they are presumed non-reproducing. The species was proposed for federal listing as endangered, but that proposal was withdrawn (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1980). It is protected in Utah and listed as endangered by the State of Colorado (1980).

Sampling was conducted under two programs:
- **Standardized program** (April–November, 1979–81): basin-wide sampling across six homogeneous reaches selected by random numbers, using boat electrofishing, seines, trammel nets and wire traps.
- **Spring program** (April–June, 1983–85): shoreline electrofishing in the pre-runoff period, extended 40 km upstream to the Yampa confluence.

Individuals were tagged with Carlin dangler tags, and selected fish were implanted with radiotransmitters (AVM fish modules, 40 kHz range) in 1980, 1981 and 1985. Habitat variables recorded at telemetry locations included depth, mean column velocity and substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** The species is widely but patchily distributed in the mainstem Green River and lower Duchesne and Yampa rivers, with its center of distribution in the Upper Green River between the Duchesne and Yampa mouths. It was absent from Split Mountain, Desolation and Gray canyons.
- **Habitat:** Fish used shoreline runs in early spring, deeper shoreline and main-channel runs and eddies in the nonbreeding season, and midchannel sand bars in summer (depths generally 0.6–3.4 m; bar depths often under 2 m; velocities about 0.5 m/s on bars).
- **Hydrology and temperature:** During the 1984 spawning period, Jensen flows ranged from 13,800 to 38,500 cfs (mean 26,100 cfs), peaking May 18. Spawning-associated water temperatures ranged from 10.5 to 18 °C, averaging about 15 °C. Ripe females averaged 16 °C.
- **Competitors and predators:** Numerous exotic predators occur in the system, though the author considers predation an unlikely sole explanation given their long presence.
- **Impoundment:** [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure, roughly 20 years before the study, is proposed as a possible driver of reproductive failure through habitat alteration.

## Key Findings

**Abundance and catch comparison**
- Only 5 razorback were caught in the Standardized program, versus 1,776 flannelmouth suckers (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and 278 [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- Razorback:flannelmouth ratio was 0.0028 (0.28%) overall and 0.004 by electrofishing catch rate; razorback catch was 1.8% of the squawfish catch.
- A 1.5-month concentrated effort in 1984 yielded 100 razorback, with an electrofishing rate of 0.72 fish/h (88% of the squawfish rate).
- Totals from 250 adult razorback captured across the study underpin the distribution analysis.

**Spawning**
- 64 razorback in breeding condition were collected May 3–June 15, 1981–85; 34 had strippable sex products, 26 ripe fish were used for spawning-preference analysis.
- Three suspected spawning areas were identified: Island and Echo Parks (Green River, including the lower Yampa), the Jensen area (Ashley Creek to Split Mountain, which yielded 22 of 34 ripe fish), and the lower Duchesne River.
- Ripe males averaged 500 mm TL (n=20) and females 539 mm (n=14), a significant difference (P=0.01); the male:female ratio on spawning grounds was 1.45:1.
- Spawning occurred over sand and gravel substrates, with ripe fish mostly over coarse sand.
- Larvae (10.6–13.6 mm) were collected downstream of all three reaches in 1984; about 31% of 42 samples (1,085 specimens) contained razorback larvae. Razorback appeared to spawn earlier than other catostomids.

**Movement and growth**
- Of 45 razorback captured, 39 were recaptures; 18 were recaptured after one to six years.
- Mean growth was 1.65 mm/year and not significant (P=0.02), suggesting no significant growth in this population.
- Breeding-season and nonbreeding-season recaptures averaged 10.3 km apart, whereas seasonal cross-period recaptures averaged 69.1 km, indicating sedentary behavior with long spawning migrations.
- A radiotagged fish moved about 6 km into the Duchesne River, then into the Green during June flooding, and later moved upstream about 11 km before contact was lost.

**Size and age**
- Captured fish ranged from 426 to 583 mm TL (mean 520 mm, 1,458 g).
- Green River razorback were smaller than upper and lower Colorado populations reported by other authors (e.g., lower basin males to 640 mm).
- A 505 mm specimen showed 12+ vertebral annuli; a 560 mm fish was estimated at about 20 years by otolith.
- No small (about 400 mm TL) size classes were found, indicating no successful recruitment.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The author concludes the razorback is very rare in the Green River but possibly more abundant there than elsewhere in the Upper Colorado Basin.
- Without recruitment, the population will decline as older individuals die, and the species may survive only in hatcheries, where genetic integrity could be lost.
- Immediate measures are urged to ensure survival, but funding for data collection is difficult without federal protection.
- Causes of recruitment failure remain unidentified; habitat alteration after dam closure is the leading hypothesis.
