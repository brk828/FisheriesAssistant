# A Survey of Fishing in 1959 in 1,000 Ponds Stocked by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service circular (Fish and Wildlife Circular 86, May 1960), prepared by [[Willis King]] of the Branch of Fishery Management Services, evaluates the [[Farm Pond Stocking Program]] run by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]]. Interviews with owners of a random sample of 1,000 ponds in 25 States, conducted in January 1960, cover fishing use in 1959 for ponds stocked with warm-water fish (mainly [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]], plus [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]]) in 1957.

The survey concludes that the program provides substantial recreation at low cost. Owners rated 82 percent of ponds as providing excellent or satisfactory fishing. The authors estimate more than 20 million fisherman-days in 1959 and at least 5 million anglers, at under 5 cents of Government cost per fisherman-day. The authors also note that fishing success could improve with better management guidance.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The survey was designed to answer how much recreational fishing national fish hatcheries provide by supplying warm-water fish for farm and ranch ponds. Since World War II, the Fish and Wildlife Service had supplied warm-water fish to stock 30,000 to 40,000 ponds annually. Bureau records for 1957 (the key year) show 43,720,912 bass and sunfish and 1,015,698 channel catfish and bullheads, a total of 44,737,610 fish, consigned to the pond program. This was about two-thirds of the largemouth bass, bluegill and redear sunfish, and all of the catfish, produced at the Bureau's warm-water hatcheries that year.

**Sample design.** The 1,000 ponds represented 1 in 40 (2.5 percent) of ponds stocked in fiscal year 1956-57. Ponds were selected randomly using a systematic interval, with an alternate list (about 10 percent of the sample) used when owners could not be reached. Ponds from 0.5 to 10 acres were included (mean area 1.7 acres). Smaller ponds were reported only from Illinois, Indiana and Iowa, which may bias returns. Pacific Coast and northern States were omitted. Interviews were conducted by hatchery and fishery management personnel using a single form (Budget Bureau No. 42-5923). Stocking rates were 50 to 100 bass per acre and 100 to 1,000 bluegill (including redear sunfish) per acre, with fish released as small fingerlings.

**Limitations.** Fish population data could not be collected because of the January timing and available time. Accurate catch records existed for only 13 owners, so most catch figures are estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pond purposes:** Water for livestock (80 percent) and fishing (70 percent) were the dominant reasons for building ponds. Irrigation was cited by 13 percent and swimming by 9 percent.
- **Management problems:** Among the 142 ponds rated poor, the most common complaints were too many small bluegills, excessive mud and silt, and the presence of wild fish. Aquatic weeds were a problem in only three ponds, probably reflecting the youth of the ponds.
- **Wild fish:** About 30 percent of ponds contained wild fish, suggesting that eradication before stocking is not always successful or that fish enter later.
- **Overstocking and fertility:** Owners often added more fish than the Government recommended. Insufficient fertilization, combined with this, frequently led to poor growth and poor fishing.
- **Balance:** Bluegills tend to overpopulate a pond to the detriment of both species, so adequate bass numbers are required to maintain a [[Bass-Bluegill Balance]].
- **Catfish:** Channel catfish and bullheads were reported in 200 to 210 ponds and were considered a desirable addition under some conditions.

## Key Findings

**Ownership, use and quality of fishing**
- Of 1,000 ponds, 958 provided some fishing in 1959. Fishing was enjoyed by family (93 percent of reports), friends (83 percent), tenants (19 percent) and others (10 percent).
- Eighty-two percent of owners required permission before the public could fish.
- Anglers were 52 percent men, 23 percent women and 25 percent children (15 years and younger). The share of women and children exceeded typical public-water figures, which the report links to proximity, safety and lower cost.
- Owners rated fishing 20.2 percent excellent, 61.4 percent satisfactory and 14.2 percent poor (4.2 percent reported none). Owners judged quality against local alternatives.
- Reasons for poor fishing (119 ponds reported): too many small bluegills (16.0 percent), silt or muddy water (12.6 percent), presence of wild fish (11.8 percent), low fertility (10.1 percent), too little fishing (9.2 percent).

**Fishing effort**
- Persons fishing: 27,246 in the 958 ponds, or 28 per pond and 16 per acre.
- Fisherman-days: 103,854 in the 1,000 ponds, or 108 per fishing pond and 64 per acre.
- Average fisherman-days per angler were 4, compared with 9.5 days per year for the typical fisherman in the 1955 National Survey of Fishing and Hunting.

**Catch**
- Bass: 75,431 fish, averaging 54 per acre from 824 ponds (46 per acre across all fishing ponds).
- Bluegill and other sunfish: 370,794 fish, averaging 276 per acre from 789 ponds (228 per acre prorated to the 958 fishing ponds).
- Catfish and others: 19,621 fish from 210 ponds, averaging 93 per acre.
- Total catch for all fishing ponds: 465,846 fish.
- The bass catch was high for a first year, though the authors warn that early harvest may be too heavy to keep ponds in balance.

**Hatchery fish and stocking**
- At least 99 percent of owners believed fish arrived in good condition; 97 percent believed they survived.
- Twenty-one percent of owners added fish from other sources, often through friends, and the authors note that indiscriminate stocking is a common practice.

**Assistance**
- Financial help from the U.S. Department of Agriculture went to 715 of 1,000 owners (71 percent). Technical guidance was reported by 84 to 85 percent of owners, mostly from the [[Soil Conservation Service]].

**Economic and national significance**
- Based on an estimated 40-fold projection, the 1957 stocking produced about 1,089,840 fishermen in 1959 (over 1 million). Five years is taken as the average productive life, so ponds stocked 1953 to 1957 supported at least 5 million anglers.
- Estimated fisherman-days: at least 4,154,160 from the 1957 ponds, and more than 20 million (20,770,800) from ponds stocked 1953 to 1957.
- These anglers are about 25 percent of freshwater anglers (about 20 million in 1959, per license sales) and account for about 5.7 percent of freshwater fishing effort (359,494,000 fisherman-days in 1959, estimated from the [[National Survey of Fishing and Hunting]] with the same growth rate).
- Hatchery and distribution cost was about $16 per acre, or under 5 cents per fisherman-day.
- At the 1955 national rate of about $4 per day, pond anglers would have spent an estimated $80 million in 1959.

**Regional highlights**
- Satisfactory-or-better fishing ratings: Region 2, 89 percent; Region 3, 72 percent; Region 4, 79 percent; Region 5, 77 percent.
- The Southwest (Region 5) reported only about half the fisherman-days per acre of other regions, probably due to population density and accessibility.
- Children made up 47 percent of pond anglers in the Northeast and 22 percent in the Southeast. Women were lowest in the Northeast and highest in the Southeast.
- The bass catch was considerably higher in southern regions, and Region 4 led in catch per acre for both bass and sunfish.
- Only 51 percent of Northeast owners accepted Federal financial help, compared with 72 percent overall, though the Northeast had the smallest sample.

**Comparisons with other studies**
- Holloway (1951) found one of three ponds in 612 Southeastern ponds had a successful bass-bluegill balance, though the studies are not directly comparable.
- The 64 fisherman-days per acre here compare with 65 fisherman-days per acre at nine Texas reservoirs (1958) and 162 trips per acre annually at Alabama's managed State lakes (Byrd, 1959).

**Management implications**
- The authors conclude the program is making a substantial contribution to recreation at relatively low cost.
- Catfish have a place alongside the bass-bluegill combination, and may be used alone in some ponds or stocked with trout.
- Results could improve if more owners used management guidance, particularly on fertilization, stocking rates and bluegill control.
