# Imperial Native Fish Habitat Reconstruction: Design Workshop Final Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation design workshop report (July 2005) presents a redesign of the [[DU2 Ponds]] at [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (INWR) in Arizona. The original four-cell pond complex failed as a [[Native Fish Sanctuary]] for [[Razorback Sucker]]: stocked fish declined from about 658 fingerlings to an estimated 25, while non-native [[Warmouth Sunfish]] rose to an estimated 17,000. Hypoxia and non-native fish were the main identified causes, both traced to site design flaws. The expert workshop proposed expanding the system to six hydrologically isolated ponds (about 40–50 new acres of backwater habitat), groundwater-fed water delivery, deeper thermal refuge, hummocks, gravel spawning beds, and a salt-tolerant riparian strategy. The estimated construction cost is $3,106,000, explicitly described as rough and not suitable for budgeting.

## Background & Description

The DU2 Ponds were initially built under the 1997 [[Biological Opinion]] requiring Reclamation to create or restore at least 300 acres of protected native fish habitat in the [[Lower Colorado River]] floodplain. Construction was completed in autumn 2002. The site was treated with the piscicide [[Rotenone]] in October and December 2002 and stocked with about 658 fingerling razorback suckers in March 2003. Warmouth sunfish persisted despite multiple treatments.

The original 96-acre site comprised four cells with about 35 acres of water (ponds 4–14 acres), maximum depth of 6 feet, and a single inlet and outlet per pond. Surrounding terraces were intended for [[Native Riparian Forest]] but were largely unsuccessful because of high soil salinity (electrical conductivity of 29–40 mmhos/cm in sampled areas, versus a survival threshold of roughly 3 mmhos/cm).

A one-day expert panel met on December 1, 2004 and decided the ponds would not be connected to the river through a semi-permeable rock structure (as at [[High Levee Pond]] and Beal Lake), leaving groundwater or pumped river water as delivery options. The workshop team, including staff from Reclamation, USFWS, USGS and Arizona State University, prioritized four design goals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key problems documented at the original site:

- **Hypoxia and temperature:** Low dissolved oxygen and high water temperatures in all ponds caused fish kills. A timed well introduced water between midnight and 6 a.m. in summer, which saved fish only in Pond 1.
- **Hydrologic connection to farm fields:** Ponds could not hold their intended elevation without inundating adjacent fields. Lower levels let aquatic vegetation expand, consume oxygen at night, and leave shallow fish exposed to avian predators.
- **Excess objectives:** Too many goals in a limited area reduced success.
- **Groundwater uncertainty:** Groundwater hydrology is poorly understood; construction may encounter the water table, and river exchange is unknown.
- **Invasive salt cedar:** Dense [[Salt Cedar]] stands surround the site and resprout from root collars.

The design also addresses habitat for marsh birds, including [[Western Least Bittern]] and potentially [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], and aims to support invertebrate prey and mosquito-larva predators.

## Key Findings

**Design goals (prioritized):**
1. *Highest:* Reconstruct ponds as backwaters with physical, chemical and biological conditions supporting native LCR fishes.
2. *Medium:* Expand surface area and/or marsh habitat within the 96 acres; design features easily maintained with a target life of at least 50 years.
3. *Lower:* Increase knowledge of managed habitat for LCR fishes.

**Core design features:**
- **Six ponds**, ranging about 9–17 acres, with independent inlets and outlets. Hydrologic connections between ponds are severed to limit non-native fish movement and allow isolated renovation.
- **Depth ratios:** 20% under 5 ft, 60% between 5 and 10 ft, and 20% between 10 and 12 ft (roughly), with maximum depths of 12–18 ft depending on pond.
- **Groundwater supply** chosen over river water to reduce non-native fish, eggs and larvae introduction and to exploit possible selenium filtration by alluvial substrate. An additional well provides redundancy.
- **Inflows placed in deep water (>10 ft)** with aerated injection to raise dissolved oxygen; cooler groundwater creates thermal refuge.
- **Fish collection kettles** (about 10 × 10 ft) at deepest points near outlets to concentrate fish during draining and reduce rotenone volumes.
- **Hummocks:** 18 planted mounds (about 20 × 100 ft, tops within 12 inches of the surface) for emergent vegetation such as California bulrush, with Pond 5 holding no hummocks and Pond 6 testing eleven hummocks experimentally.
- **Gravel spawning beds** covering about 5% of each pond, lined to block plant colonization, with gravel of 3/8 to 3 inches.
- **Boat ramps and staging areas** at each pond, plus an integrated drainage system that prevents salt buildup and diverts farm runoff.

**Pond-specific layouts (surface area):** Pond 1 (about 8.9 acres, partly described in the source), Pond 2 (13.1 acres), Pond 3 (14.3 acres), Pond 4 (10.2 acres, modeled on [[High Levee Pond]] conceptual features, maximum depth 18 ft), Pond 5 (17.2 acres), Pond 6 (9.2 acres, maximum depth 14 ft, hummocks oriented east–west for shading).

**Monitoring program:**
- Water quality (dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, temperature, depth, ORP, turbidity) at three permanent sites per pond; quarterly in cooler months and at least twice monthly when temperatures exceed 30°C.
- Fish surveys: one-hour summer collections for large fish and at least one cooler-season sampling for health and population size; renovation triggered by non-native detection.

**Excavated material:** High-salinity spoil will be reused in roads, berms and hummocks rather than hauled off-refuge, and the salt-cedar area will be root-plowed, chipped and removed.

**Cost and O&M:** The total construction estimate is $3,106,000, excluding contingencies, design, permitting and management. Annual O&M items include pump power ($10,000–$20,000/yr), pump replacement, periodic renovation with [[Rotenone]] (about $100/gal), monitoring, and spare parts. Recommended for planning only.

**Environmental compliance** will be handled by INWR under NEPA, ARPA, ESA Section 7, and Clean Water Act Sections 401 and 404.

**Open issues:** Groundwater depth, clay lens presence, and river connection are unknown; test wells are proposed. A filtered irrigation-pump alternative with fish screens is under evaluation at another site.
