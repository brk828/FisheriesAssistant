# The Warmwater Game Fishes of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game publication, written by retired Supervising Fisheries Biologist Brian Curtis (a revision of a 1949 article), describes the warmwater game fishes of inland California. Native game fish were scarce in these waters; apart from the [[Sacramento Perch]], all the black basses, sunfishes, catfishes and related species were introduced from east of the Rockies, mostly after 1870. The note covers the species list, an artificial identification key, life histories (especially spawning and nest guarding), introduction histories, and principles of [[Fisheries Management]] for warmwater fisheries.

## Background & Description

The text announces 16 species in three families, but its lists actually enumerate 17: five catfishes (Ameiuridae), one perch (Percidae) and eleven sunfishes (Centrarchidae). The species are arranged by evolutionary order, but the identification key groups them by conspicuous anatomy rather than genealogy.

- **Catfishes (Ameiuridae):** [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Catfish]], Yellow Bullhead, Brown Bullhead and Black Bullhead. All are scaleless with barbels and an adipose fin.
- **Perch (Percidae):** [[Yellow Perch]], the only member of its family in California.
- **Sunfishes (Centrarchidae):** three black basses ([[Smallmouth Black Bass]], [[Redeye Black Bass]], [[Spotted Black Bass]]), [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], Pumpkinseed, [[Sacramento Perch]], [[Warmouth]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Black Crappie]].

The key relies on combinations of features (fin ray counts, scale counts, spine numbers, body shape, mouth size) because color varies too much to be reliable. The author notes that the key does not work for marine species or for fishes absent from California. Related species excluded from the key, including [[Carp]], goldfish and the freshwater viviparous perch, are described as confusable but distinct.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native and introduced fauna.** Salmon and steelhead used the cooler tributaries. The only native warmwater game fish was the Sacramento perch, confined mostly to the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River System]]. Most other species were introduced in the late 1800s and early 1900s, often from Illinois and the Middle West. Several historical introductions are recorded, including largemouth and smallmouth bass in 1874 (Napa River and Alameda Creek), white catfish in 1874 (San Joaquin River), and green sunfish, bluegill, black and white crappie, warmouth and yellow perch in 1891 (Lake Cuyamaca). The spotted bass was brought in by air in 1933. A rock bass record from 1874 was judged no longer valid.

**Temperature and habitat.** Largemouth bass do best where water reaches at least 80°F for several months. Smallmouth bass tolerate cooler water and faster current, favoring clear, boulder-strewn streams and clear lakes. Both species tend to become lethargic below 50°F and move into shallows as water warms, spawning when it exceeds 60°F. Bluegill and green sunfish spawn later, from about 75°F. Crappies spawn from May to July at 64–68°F. Yellow perch are the coldest-tolerant species, spawning at 45–55°F. The catfishes do best in warm to moderate waters.

**Species interactions.** Bass and bluegill are described as one of the best combinations in small waters. Carp are called inimical to all game fish. Non-native sunfishes, catfishes and carp are proposed as the cause of the Sacramento perch decline, because they eat exposed eggs. Crappies compete with bass for food and can outnumber them in lakes where both occur.

## Key Findings

**Nest guarding and reproduction (black basses):**
- The male clears a saucer-shaped nest (a "redd") of 4–40 hours' work, in water 3–6 feet deep, and defends it.
- A two-pound female carries about 6,000 eggs, and ten thousand eggs in one nest is "not unusual."
- Hatching takes about two days at 72°F and five days at 66°F.
- Anglers taking guarding males, or the parent being scared off by a boat, can expose eggs to predation.

**Sacramento perch spawning (Clear Lake, June 15, 1947):**
- Murphy (1948) observed about 50 fish spawning at 75°F near a rock breakwater, with no nest building and no guarding.
- Up to 84,000 eggs were found in a single 10.5-inch female.
- Murphy hypothesized that introduced sunfishes, catfishes and carp, which attack unguarded nests, caused the population decline.
- Growth at Clear Lake: 3.5 inches at year one, 7 inches at year two, 8 inches at year three, 9 inches at year four. Spawning starts in the second year.

**Size records:**
- Largemouth bass reached 22 pounds (vs. 14 pounds for smallmouth), in the warm south. Five pounds (largemouth) and three pounds (smallmouth) are considered highly satisfactory.
- Channel catfish have been known to reach 35 pounds. White catfish of eight pounds are not rare. The three bullheads rarely exceed 13 inches.
- Bluegill up to 2.5 pounds have been caught; half a pound is considered respectable. Warmouth rarely exceed 10 inches.

**Identification:**
- Largemouth and smallmouth bass differ in pyloric caeca: branched in largemouth, mostly unbranched in smallmouth. This is described as infallible at all ages.
- Black and white crappie are separated by the length of the dorsal fin base relative to the distance from its front to the eye.
- Bluegill and green sunfish hybridize in nature. The hybrids are sterile and 81–95% male.

**Introduction status:**
- The spotted bass has become established in the Cosumnes River.
- The channel catfish is common in the Sutter Bypass and lower Sacramento River.
- The white catfish is the most abundant catfish in the state, with over 50% of the catfish catch.
- The yellow bullhead is known only from the Colorado River drainage.
- The pumpkinseed is so rare that it "can for all practical purposes be disregarded" (two records before 1948, plus a 1948 planting in Irvine Lake).
- The yellow perch was failing in the Central Valleys but has re-entered from Oregon and is abundant in Copco Reservoir. The author opposes its further spread.

**Management principles:**
- The "biotic potential" principle: warmwater fish biomass stays near a lake's carrying capacity. Heavy angling is said to make it impossible to fish a water to depletion.
- Stocking for maintenance is considered unnecessary and undesirable once warmwater fish are established. Stocking is justified for seed stock, replacement after catastrophe, rectification of imbalances, and farm ponds.
- Much of the warmwater fish crop goes unharvested, as shown in Norris Reservoir (Tennessee Valley Authority). The first remedy is removing restrictive regulations, particularly closed seasons, with size and bag limits often undesirable.
- Watershed erosion control and preventive measures for power and irrigation projects are considered among the best improvement work.
- No introductions should be made without careful investigation and the ability to control and eradicate the exotic.
