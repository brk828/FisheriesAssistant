# Feeding Habits of the Montana Grayling in Ford Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study analyzes the diet of [[Montana Grayling]] (*Thymallus montanus*) introduced into [[Ford Lake]], a small landlocked lake in northern Michigan, after stocking in fall 1936. The work was motivated by the probable extinction of the native [[Michigan Grayling]] (*Thymallus tricolor*) and the ongoing decline of Montana grayling in their native waters. Fish were sampled on May 18 and October 18 to 20, 1937, and their stomach contents were tabulated by taxonomic order. The authors found that the diet contained an unexpectedly large share of predacious invertebrates, especially [[Odonata]] nymphs (mainly *Enallagma*), [[Notonecta Insulata]] backswimmers, and [[Dytiscidae]] water beetles. Because no earlier feeding data existed for lake-dwelling Montana grayling, the authors could not determine whether this diet was normal. They concluded that the question of selection versus availability remains open until food availability in the lake is measured.

## Background & Description

The Montana grayling and the Michigan grayling are members of the genus *Thymallus* in the [[Salmonidae]]. Fisheries investigators had long sought the causes of the Michigan grayling's disappearance and the recent annual decline of the Montana grayling. Both declines were attributed to increasing human activity and to the spread of trout, which may harm grayling through competition, predation, or both. The author notes a contrast with the [[European Grayling]] (*Thymallus thymallus*), which persists in large numbers despite human pressure and coexistence with salmonids, and is regarded by some anglers as a "weed fish."

Earlier accounts of grayling diet were sparse and general:

- **[[Thaddeus Norris]] (1883)** reported that the Michigan grayling took flies and their larvae as its only food.
- **[[Parker]] (1889)** found large amounts of vegetable matter, including oat grains, in stomachs. He suggested that plants might be a normal part of the diet.
- **[[Milner]] (1874)** recorded beetles, neuropterans, and odonate nymphs, along with white cedar leaves that he thought were taken by mistake.
- **[[Henshall]] (1907)** stated only that Montana grayling eat insects and their larvae.
- **[[Heckel and Kner]] (1858)** and **[[von Siebold]] (1863)** described the European grayling as eating worms, snails, insect larvae, fish spawn, fry, minnows, and seasonally available terrestrial insects.
- **[[Svetvidov]] (1931)** reported that black grayling in [[Lake Baikal]] feed on amphipods, caddisflies, and adult winged insects, while white grayling feed mainly on fish and amphipods.

**Study lake.** Ford Lake lies in rolling, sandy country of jack pine and aspen, in [[Pigeon River State Forest]], and receives little surface drainage. Its surface area is 10.66 acres. The deep bottom is pulpy peat, and the shoals (about 175 feet wide) are sand on the north, east, and south sides, with a marl belt of similar width on the peat side.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Physical and chemical conditions were recorded during an August 1, 1932 survey:

- Maximum depth: 10 m; slope 20 to 30 degrees
- Secchi disc reading: 5.5 m
- pH at all levels: 8.2
- Thermocline between the 5 and 6 m levels
- Dissolved oxygen: 9.4 ppm at the surface, 8.6 ppm at the bottom

**Sampling methods.** Two gear types were used. A single haul of a 100-foot bag seine (water 2 to 8 feet deep, sparsely weeded shoal) gave the May sample. Seining in shoal water gave the October evening and morning samples. [[Gill Net Sampling]] gave the two October night-series samples. Fish were examined for [[Stomach Content Analysis]], with food counted, measured by volume, and reported as frequency of occurrence.

**Dominant prey groups:**

- **Aquatic Diptera (midges):** More than half the May diet. The May stomachs held midge larvae and pupae (Chironomidae and Ceratopogonidae), and two stomachs held [[Chaoborus Punctipennis]] larvae.
- **Odonata nymphs:** Second place in both May and October. They were mostly *Enallagma carunculatum* and *E. hageni*, with four *Tetragoneuria* nymphs in May.
- **Amphipoda:** All identified as [[Hyalella Knickerbockeri]]. Scuds were the leading October food by a margin of 0.7 percent over odonates.
- **Aquatic Coleoptera:** Mostly minute adult [[Bidessus]] dytiscid beetles (usually under 2 mm), along with terrestrial Scarabeidae (chiefly *Aphodius*).
- **Aquatic Hemiptera:** Backswimmers, including *Plea striola* and *Notonecta insulata*, plus one *Belostoma* and one water strider.
- **[[Cladocera]] (water fleas):** Absent from May but present in October.
- **Terrestrial insects:** About 16 percent of the food in the October 18 evening sample, which suggests surface feeding.

The authors noted that aquatic hemipterans, Hydracarina, and mayflies are seldom eaten by fish. Their musky odor and stinging ability may explain this. Yet the Ford Lake grayling took them in quantity.

## Key Findings

- **Sample sizes and sizes of fish:**
  - May 18, 1937: 50 fish, 67 to 111 mm standard length (average 89 mm), seine.
  - October 18, 1937 evening: 11 fish, 148 to 176 mm (average 165 mm), seine.
  - October 19, 1937 morning: 8 fish, 147 to 179 mm (average 163 mm), seine.
  - October 19, 1937, 10:15 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.: 7 fish, 151 to 183 mm (average 163 mm), gill net.
  - October 19 evening to October 20, 1937, 1:00 p.m.: 6 fish, 148 to 165 mm (average 159 mm), gill net.
- **Diet composition:** Predacious invertebrates made up a large share of the diet in every collection. Odonate nymphs (mainly *Enallagma*) formed nearly half the food volume in the October 18 evening sample. One stomach held 85 nymphs.
- **Seasonal shift:** Aquatic Diptera dominated the May diet but were a small share of the October diet. Cladocera were absent in May and common in October. The authors suggested this reflects a seasonal decline in midges or a change in diet linked to fish size.
- **Winter specimen (February 27, 1938):** A 196 mm, 100 g fish caught through the ice on a stonefly nymph bait contained ten scuds, remains of six [[Anax Junius]] dragonfly nymphs (98.6 percent of volume), and one midge larva. It supported the predatory pattern seen in the autumn samples.
- **Unresolved question:** It is not known whether the diet reflects selection or the local availability of prey. The authors planned to measure food organisms in the lake and to calculate the fish's condition factor to test the adequacy of the food supply.
- **Incidental observations:** A statoblast of the polyzoan *Cristatella mucedo* appeared in one stomach. Earlier accounts of plant material in grayling stomachs were not confirmed in this sample.
- **Significance:** The results give the first detailed data on the feeding of lake-dwelling Montana grayling. They offer a baseline for judging how the species adapts to a new lake after stocking, which bears on the broader question of why grayling decline where trout are present.
