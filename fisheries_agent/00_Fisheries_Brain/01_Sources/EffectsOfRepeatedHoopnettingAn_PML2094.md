# Repeated Hoopnetting and Handling Effects on Bonytail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether repeated [[Hoop Netting]] and handling reduce the growth of the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). In a 0.04 ha pond at the [[Rocky Mountain Research Station]] in [[Flagstaff, Arizona]], 327 hatchery-reared fish were [[PIT Tag|PIT-tagged]], released, and sampled with hoop nets during four three-day sampling events between September 2003 and June 2004. Fish recaptured more often grew significantly less in both length and weight than fish never recaptured. The authors conclude that the cumulative effects of repeated capture and handling, rather than PIT tagging itself, caused the growth reductions. They recommend further work on physiological, reproductive, and population-level impacts before routine sampling of this endangered species is considered safe.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub is an endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River basin. The authors note that sampling and handling are essential for estimating population characteristics, but that sampling practices should minimize impacts on fish communities, especially for threatened species. Researchers generally assume handled fish survive and behave normally after release. Prior work has documented injury from electrofishing and stress from confinement and time out of water. Hoop netting is widely regarded as a low-impact passive capture method, but its effects on fish had received little study.

Repeated handling (weighing, measuring, tagging) is often necessary in [[Mark-Recapture]] studies used to estimate population size, growth, and movement. Stress effects are cumulative, and handling can reduce growth and condition, cause abnormal physiological or behavioral responses, and produce delayed mortality. The study objectives were to determine how repeated hoop netting and handling affect hatchery-reared bonytail chub, and to measure the time needed to process fish in the field so that time-consuming steps could be identified and reduced.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Study population:** 327 bonytail chub were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags in July 2003 and stocked in the pond.
- **Sampling:** Six unbaited hoop nets (0.9 m diameter × 1.54 m long, 9.5 mm mesh) were set overnight (16–22 h) on four three-day events: two in fall (September–October 2003) and two in spring (June 2004).
- **Processing:** Fish were measured (total length and fork length), weighed, scanned for PIT tags, and released. Untagged fish received a second PIT tag. Processing time was timed for each step.
- **Controls:** Fish never recaptured served as controls. Growth was calculated by subtracting initial size at stocking from final size.
- **Statistics:** Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) compared growth among recapture groups, and regression tested the relationship between percentage growth and number of recaptures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hoop nets were effective at catching bonytail chub, with a mean of 7.9 fish per overnight net set (SE 1.1) across 79 net sets. The pond was drained in June 2004 and all remaining fish were measured, weighed, and scanned.

Mortality was low but not zero. Of the 327 fish, 26 died during the study: 12 had been handled at least once before dying, and 14 died without being recaptured. Of the 12 handled fish that later died, seven were handled once, four were handled twice within one three-day period, and one was handled three times over a two-day period. The authors suggest that mortality was largely independent of handling, although the sample is small.

Handling time was dominated by fish removal from the water. Initial processing (lifting the net, placing fish in a bucket, transporting them to the processing area, and holding them) averaged 424–436 s, or 86–89% of total handling time. Total handling time from net lift to release averaged 479 s for untagged fish and 505 s for tagged fish. Scanning for PIT tags took 55–69 s, and roughly 48–58% of that time was spent on tag insertion. Even with streamlined procedures, fish spent considerable time out of their natural environment.

## Key Findings

- **Growth declined with repeated handling.** Fish recaptured multiple times grew less in length (P < 0.001) and weight (P < 0.001) than fish that were never recaptured. The decline was consistent across successive recapture groups.
- **Fish handled five times grew less than controls.** Fish never recaptured grew an average of 10.9 mm in length (SE 0.69) over the 11-month period. Fish recaptured five times grew less, with a reported mean of about 5.7 mm (SE 0.82; partially legible in the source).
- **Weight gain was reduced by about 17 percentage points.** Fish never recaptured gained 29.7% of their initial weight (SE 3.X, partly legible), whereas fish recaptured five times gained only 12.8% (SE 5.84). Fish recaptured five times also grew significantly less than controls in weight (ANCOVA F = 5.33, df = 5,273, P < 0.001).
- **Growth reduction was attributed to handling, not tags.** All fish received PIT tags, so the authors reason that the number of recaptures was the only difference among groups. This is consistent with earlier studies finding minimal effects of PIT tags on fish, including [[Childs]] (2002) and [[Ward]] (2003), who found that normal handling and tagging did not impair swimming performance.
- **Fish handled six to eight times were excluded.** Only five or fewer fish were handled six, seven, or eight times, so these were removed from analysis. Over 95% of fish were recaptured five or fewer times.
- **Sampling time is a major stressor.** Most handling time was spent on the net lift and holding step rather than on measurement or tagging. This suggests that reducing time out of water could reduce stress.
- **Population-level effects remain unknown.** The authors did not measure reproductive success, disease resistance, or size-related differences in handling, all of which could affect population outcomes. They call for research on [[Physiological Stress]] and [[Reproductive Success]] under repeated hoop netting, and for comparisons with other non-lethal gears such as [[Trammel Net]]s.
- **Management implication.** Because the endangered bonytail chub is sensitive to cumulative handling, the authors recommend minimizing the number of recaptures and handling time, and selecting sampling methods with lower stress impacts, especially where fish must be sampled repeatedly.
