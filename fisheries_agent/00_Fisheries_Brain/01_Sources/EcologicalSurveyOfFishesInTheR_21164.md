# Ecological Survey of Fishes in the Raisin River Drainage, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 survey by Smith, Taylor and Grimshaw documents the fish fauna of the [[Raisin River]] drainage in southeastern Michigan. The basin has been heavily altered by industrial, urban and agricultural change. Sampling at 160 localities between June 15 and October 31, 1978 yielded 50,345 specimens, and 240 habitat samples were analyzed. The study aimed to map fish distribution and abundance, assess threatened species, and compare present populations with older collections to track historical changes in water and habitat quality.

The authors found that siltation from agricultural runoff and pollution from municipalities are the main drivers of decline. Clean-water species are being lost, while silt-tolerant species are holding steady or increasing. The sole remaining Michigan population of the threatened [[Notropis Photogenis]] survives in a short stretch of the upper river.

## Background & Description

The Raisin River basin is the thirteenth largest in Michigan. It drains more than 1,000 square miles across Lenawee, Hillsdale, Jackson, Washtenaw and Monroe Counties. Its major tributaries are the Saline River, Bear Creek and Wolf Creek. The region lies in the southeast of the state, which the authors describe as the area most modified by human activity. Many native fishes in Michigan are restricted to it.

The authors report a known fauna of 84 species in the introduction, but 81 species in the results section. Of these, 67 were sampled in the 1978 survey. Three species were collected for the first time in the basin: [[Lepomis Humilis]], [[Oncorhynchus Tshawytscha]] and [[Erimyzon Oblongus]]. The 70 species sampled represent 35 genera in 15 families. Four listed species, [[Coregonus Artedii]], [[Moxostoma Valenciennesi]], [[Notropis Photogenis]] and [[Ammocrypta Pellucida]], have been recorded from the drainage.

The stated objectives were to:
1. Document distribution and abundance of fishes, and the environmental characters that determine habitat suitability.
2. Assess the status of threatened species and of species whose Michigan status depends on Raisin populations.
3. Compare present fish status with older collections to assess historical trends in water and habitat quality.

Records from the [[Museum of Zoology]], University of Michigan were used as the baseline for earlier collections, which span about 60 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and analysis.** Fish were collected with seines chosen for habitat type. Standard habitat dimensions, substrate diversity, water transparency and current were measured. Specimens were identified against comparative collections and voucher specimens were catalogued. Ecological associations were analyzed by [[Factor Analysis]] (Morrison 1967). This method combined species abundances with environmental variables to produce three ordination factors.

**Species not taken in 1978.** Two groups of previously recorded species were absent from the survey:
- Possibly extirpated species, such as [[Hybopsis Amblops]] (bigeye chub), which needs fast, clean water and has disappeared from the Raisin, Huron and Rouge basins. [[Notropis Anogenus]] (pugnose shiner) was last recorded in 1964 and was not found despite effort. [[Ammocrypta Pellucida]] (eastern sand darter) persists in the Huron but not in the Raisin.
- Peripheral or hard-to-sample species, including lampreys, [[Coregonus Artedii]] (cisco), and records of [[Salvelinus Fontinalis]] (brook trout) from poorly documented introductions. Some species, such as [[Osmerus Mordax]] (rainbow smelt) and [[Pomoxis Annularis]] (white crappie), are rare incursions from [[Lake Erie]].

**Diversity patterns.** Species per site ranged from 0 to 26. Four Saline River localities below Milan yielded zero species. Diversity peaked in two areas: the Raisin above Tecumseh and the lower Raisin between Dundee and Monroe. The average sample held about 12 species. Richness at sites of 14 or more species was typically high in the northwestern, large-water sections, or high in the southeastern lower river with large volume, despite silt and pollution.

**Abundance classes.**
- **Abundant (13 species):** each had more than 1,000 specimens, together making up about two-thirds of the sample. The group includes [[Pimephales Notatus]], described as possibly the most abundant inland species in Michigan. Most of these are stream fishes, except brook silverside and blacknose shiner, which are commonest in lakes.
- **Common (37 species):** mostly sunfishes, darters and catfishes, plus suckers, minnows, sculpins, pike, pickerel and mudminnow. Together they make up about 25% of individuals. A few, including [[Notropis Heterodon]], [[Nocomis Micropogon]] and [[Etheostoma Spectabile]], were found at few localities and may be declining. [[Etheostoma Spectabile]] was found in only 31 samples.
- **Restricted (7 species):** including [[Notropis Photogenis]], found in only four samples at three localities, and [[Notropis Stramineus]] (sand shiner), taken in six samples but yielding 497 individuals.
- **Rare (13 species):** each represented by 1 to 11 individuals. Seven are common elsewhere in Michigan. The other six are special cases, discussed below.

**Habitat associations.** Factor I runs from lake and large-water fishes to small creek species. Factor II runs from slow, muddy-water species such as [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] and [[Cyprinus Carpio]] to clear-riffle species such as [[Cottus Bairdi]] and [[Noturus Flavus]]. Factor III runs from deep, varied-substrate river species to small creek and marsh species associated with mud. The combined factors separate lake-margin species, shallow-stream species, and deep-river species. Silt-tolerant and silt-intolerant forms fall apart along Factor II.

**Distribution patterns.** The authors identify several recurring patterns:
- *Lake pattern*: [[Etheostoma Exile]] (Iowa darter), concentrated in the northwestern lake district.
- *River pattern*: [[Nocomis Micropogon]] and [[Notropis Photogenis]].
- *Upland creek pattern*: [[Etheostoma Caeruleum]] and [[Cottus Bairdi]].
- *Floodplain creek pattern*: [[Ericymba Buccata]] and [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]].
- *General pattern*: [[Pimephales Notatus]].

[[Macon Creek]] held populations of several darters, including the least darter and [[Etheostoma Spectabile]]. Historical records from the Raisin were compared with 1978 collections. The authors also noted hybridization between [[Notropis Cornutus]] and [[Notropis Chrysocephalus]] at some localities.

## Key Findings

**Threatened and rare species**
- [[Notropis Photogenis]] (silver shiner) is the only known remaining Michigan habitat for this threatened species. It was found in four samples at three localities on a six-mile stretch of the upper Raisin below Manchester. It requires clean water, silt-free substrate and deep riffles in moderately large streams. Life-history data came from 37 scale-annulus specimens: young-of-year were 49 to 62 mm in August, fish of 80 to 90 mm were in their second year, and the sample held 21 males and 15 females. Its diet is more terrestrial than that of potential cyprinid competitors, and the authors attribute its extirpation from the lower river to pollution below Adrian, Blissfield and Dundee.
- [[Noturus Miurus]] (brindled madtom) was found in 12 specimens from four samples. It is the closest local relative of the endangered northern madtom and is considered a good indicator of water quality that deserves intensive study.
- [[Moxostoma Duquesnei]] (black redhorse) was taken in seven specimens from five samples. It needs clear water with gravel or rubble riffles and appears to be declining.
- [[Erimyzon Oblongus]] (creek chubsucker) was taken at two localities. It is one of the rarest species in Michigan, known from nine localities in the southern counties. Its preference for small muddy creeks suggests it may not be declining.
- [[Phoxinus Erythrogaster]] (southern redbelly dace) is a rare headwater species known from fewer than a dozen localities in Lenawee and Washtenaw Counties.
- [[Lepomis Humilis]] (orangespotted sunfish), a southwestern species, has spread into the southeast corner in recent years. Its spread indicates changing aquatic habitat.

**Silt-tolerant and expanding species**
- [[Ericymba Buccata]] (silverjaw minnow) was found in 39 habitat samples (33 localities, 609 specimens). It thrives in small, sandy-bottomed streams where few other species live.

**Main threats**
- Siltation from agricultural runoff is identified as the dominant recent driver of change. Spring floods carry silt from plowed fields, and it settles during spawning season. Siltation covers gravel and rubble, which reduces substrate heterogeneity. The mechanism, either loss of spawning substrate or of benthic food, is unresolved.
- Municipal pollution is clearly visible in the species richness data. About one-third of the fauna is lost below Clinton and Tecumseh. About two-thirds of the remaining species cannot survive below Adrian. Some recovery occurs near Blissfield, and Dundee supports a rich fauna dominated by silt-tolerant species.
- The most severe problem is below Milan, where waters that should hold 20 to 25 species are apparently clear but completely fishless. The authors suspect a toxic agent that also threatens other aquatic life.

**Diet**
- Stomach analysis of [[Notropis Photogenis]] showed terrestrial insects in 71% of specimens and aquatic insects in 30%. Coleoptera, adult Diptera and Hymenoptera were the most common terrestrial groups. The authors interpret this as drift feeding. Chironomidae were the most frequent aquatic invertebrates.

**Zoogeography and historical trends**
- The fauna are postglacial colonists from the Kankakee and Ohio basins over roughly 14,000 years, following periglacial drainage and stream captures. Warm-water fishes are moving north naturally, while local extinction of clear-water fishes is unnatural.
- The authors note that no rare or extinct northern species exist in the drainage. Most rare species are southern forms near the northern edge of their ranges.
- The authors caution that species richness must be adjusted for habitat volume before it can indicate water quality. Intermediate pollution may leave species counts unchanged, because some tolerant species benefit.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Rare and possibly endangered species are clean-water forms near the northern edges of their ranges. Silt-tolerant species appear to be increasing.
- Climate and substrate appear to be the dominant factors governing distribution and abundance.
- Preserving endangered inland fishes in southeastern Michigan requires attention to the same water-quality conditions that matter to human users of the resource.
