# Spawning, Movement and Population Structure of Flannelmouth Sucker in the Paria River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 University of Arizona master's thesis examines the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in the [[Paria River]], a turbid, flash-flood-prone tributary entering the [[Colorado River]] about 25 km below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The study combined seine, hoop-net and PIT-tag sampling (January 1992 to August 1993) with habitat measurements to document spawning timing and microhabitat, use of the [[Confluence Area]] across flows, population demographics, growth, and recruitment. The author concludes that the Paria functions mainly as a spawning ground for fish from the Glen Canyon reach and downstream populations, that no juvenile recruitment has been observed there for about 12 years, and that adult-sized fish continue to enter the population.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Flannelmouth sucker, a large native catostomid and the only endemic "big river" fish still common throughout Grand Canyon. Eggs are the largest of Colorado Basin catostomids (3.8-3.9 mm). It is a non-guarding, open-substrate, lithophilic broadcast spawner with demersal, initially adhesive eggs.
- **Conservation status:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed it as candidate category 2 (USFWS 1993). Its range has been drastically reduced, and hybridization with introduced white sucker in the upper basin is a threat.
- **Related natives:** [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]]. Several were extirpated or nearly so; the razorback has hybridized with flannelmouth where they co-occur.
- **Diet:** Upper basin stomachs were dominated by algae and debris. In Grand Canyon, amphipods (*Gammarus lacustris*) and midge larvae (Chironomidae) were also important.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Paria drains about 3650 km² from springs below Bryce Canyon National Park, Utah, and flows about 88 km across the Navajo sandstone, Kayenta, Moenave and Chinle formations. The last 16 km are low gradient (<1.0%). Gypsiferous geology gives it high sulfate and mineral content, and its phosphate and nitrate concentrations were the highest in the Grand Canyon region.

**Hydrology and temperature.**
- Base flow is 3.5–35.3 cfs, with the river perennial at its mouth in most years because of springs.
- Instantaneous flows during the study ranged from 2.5 to 1790 cfs. Flash floods (>50 cfs) were common, and mean daily flows exceeded 100 cfs on 21 days in 1992 and 43 days in 1993.
- Water temperatures ranged from 0 to 34°C.
- Colorado River flows during the study ranged from 5,000 to 20,000 cfs, with temperatures of roughly 6–12°C.
- Suspended sediment averaged 114,000 ppm at the mouth, with flood peaks as high as 780,000 mg/L.

**Confluence dynamics.** High Colorado flows (16,000–20,000 cfs) inundated the lower Paria about 160 m upstream, and the Colorado cut a new channel 80 m upstream of the confluence in summer 1993.

**Other fishes.** Only speckled dace occurred upstream of Bushhead Falls (~16 km up). Incidental catches included bluehead sucker (n = 3), rainbow trout (n = 24) and common carp (n = 1). A single moribund striped bass was found at the mouth in September 1992.

## Key Findings

**Population size and demographics**
- 1,063 flannelmouth suckers were caught (January 1992 to August 1993); mean total length (TL) was 495 mm.
- Females (mean 524 mm, n = 455) were larger (p < 0.001) than males (mean 470 mm, n = 522).
- Fish at the confluence (mean 501 mm, n = 787) were larger than upstream spawners (mean 476 mm, n = 246).
- Spawners in 1993 averaged 478 mm (n = 246), 53 mm longer (p < 0.001) than the 1981 spawners (425 mm, n = 286). No post-larval fish under about 373–379 mm were caught in 1992 or 1993.
- Upstream sex ratio was about 2 males to 1 female. The confluence was slightly female-biased during the run, and males dominated after spawning.
- No fish over 580 mm were caught upstream. Twenty-one fish of 580–620 mm were caught at the confluence, some showing traits suggesting hybridization with razorback sucker.

**Spawning timing and conditions**
- Ripe fish were first caught on March 20, 1992 and February 13, 1993 (males first; females first on March 1). Females were ripe only between March 28 and April 10, 1993, and no ripe females were caught after April 26.
- Spawning peaked on April 8–10, 1993, at 8–20°C; females were not ripe until minimum stream temperatures reached about 6°C.
- In 1992, spawners left abruptly before a cold front and flash flood on March 27.
- Spawning occurred in shallow water (mostly under 25 cm) over sand and gravel, in the lower 10 km of the Paria. Reach 1 had no spawning observed.
- Condition factor peaked March 27–April 1 (males 1.03, females 1.05).

**Spawning habitat selection**
- Spawning sites at 2.8 and 6.0 km had mean substrate of 17 and 23 mm, and mean column velocities of 0.40 and 0.57 m/s respectively.
- Spawning fish used slower velocities than availability (mean column 0.48 vs 0.87 m/s; bottom 0.38 vs 0.57 m/s), with bottom velocity the more useful measure.
- Depth at spawning sites did not differ from availability in mean (p = 0.29), but distributions differed (K-S test). The author judged spawning in water deeper than 25 cm unlikely.
- No spawning was observed in the confluence area, which had 100% fine sand or silt substrate.

**Confluence use and sampling**
- The confluence was used year-round but erratically in daylight. Seventy of 93 daytime hauls (78%) yielded no fish; mean daytime CPUE was 0.65 fish/100 m².
- Night catches were higher (mean 2.3 fish/100 m²; p < 0.001); only 11 of 56 night hauls (20%) were empty.
- The highest individual CPUEs (5–25 fish/100 m²) occurred at Colorado flows of 10,000–14,000 cfs; a Kruskal-Wallis test found no significant flow effect.
- Night catches moved into shallow slack water, possibly reflecting reduced disturbance and feeding in fine sediments.

**Movement and recapture**
- Cumulative PIT-tag recapture was 7% at study end; per-trip recapture ranged 11–17% over the last four trips.
- Among 77 fish tagged in the Paria, no measurable growth was observed.
- Recaptures from downstream included fish tagged in the [[Little Colorado River]] and as far as Kanab Creek (228–229 km), some recaptured during spawning in the Paria. Seven fish tagged in the Paria were later recovered in the Little Colorado River.

**Growth and longevity**
- Floy-tagged adults grow at about 7.3 mm/year. Sub-adults under about 407 mm grew non-linearly, following a second-order fit.
- The author estimates a maximum age near 30 years, based on extrapolation from recaptures and a size of 400 mm at maturity.
- Scale-based aging was considered to underestimate maximum age.

**Recruitment**
- No young-of-year were seen in 1993. In 1992, only a few juveniles (22–45 mm TL) were caught, the last YOY observations occurred in June, and no YOY were reared in the Paria beyond June in 1991–1993.
- The author suggests either downstream nursery areas with fish returning at about 380–400 mm, or dispersal of downstream fish to the Paria at maturity, possibly through olfactory imprinting.
- Recruitment failure in any single year may matter little given the species' long lifespan.

**Other fishes and historical context**
- Speckled dace were present year-round at temperatures from near 0 to 34°C, with CPUE varying more across sampling events than across reaches.
- Historically, flannelmouth recruited into Glen Canyon into the early 1980s despite exotic predators (channel catfish, carp, centrarchids), suggesting exotics alone do not explain the decline.
- Hypolimnetic releases from Glen Canyon Dam (closed March 1963) cooled the river to 8–9°C at the tailrace, which the author links to early-life-stage survival problems.

**Summary conclusions**
- The Paria is a seasonal spawning ground for flannelmouth from the Glen Canyon reach and downstream populations. Spawning ascends at least 10 km upstream.
- Spawning occurs in shallow water with moderate bottom velocities and clean gravel, timed to temperature and flow. Flash floods may reduce spawning success.
- Adult-sized fish continue to enter the population, but no juvenile recruitment has been documented locally for about 12 years, and the population may be aging.
