# Inventory of Crayfish and Spiny Softshell Turtle Populations in the Yuma Division of the Lower Colorado River

#aquatic-ecology #freshwater-ecology #cambaridae #trionychidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 report, prepared for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Regional Office) by the California Department of Fish and Game, inventories two aquatic species in the Yuma Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]]: the crayfish [[Procambarus Clarkii]] and the [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]]. The work was prompted by flood control proposals for the division, which raised concern about fish and wildlife values. Sampling in August and September 1981 used baited traps, minnow seines and [[Hoop Net]] sets, and the study also reviewed the [[Habitat Evaluation Procedures]] (HEP) used in a 1980 assessment.

No crayfish were captured in the Yuma Division during the inventory, though their presence was inferred from observations and predator stomach contents. Softshell turtles were widespread, with a localized concentration near the [[Gila River Confluence]]. The authors concluded that the 1980 HEP was not a valid assessment of softshell turtle habitat and should not be used to represent pre-project values for this species.

## Background & Description

The Yuma Division extends about 21 river miles from Laguna Dam to Morelos Dam. Historically, flows averaged about 20,000 cubic feet per second (cfs), with natural high flows near 100,000 cfs. After [[Laguna Dam]] was closed in 1907, typical discharge fell to roughly 400 cfs, with occasional increases to 4,000 cfs. Sediment deposition, vegetation encroachment and development reduced the flood control capacity of the levees. Operation of [[Lake Powell]] (25 million acre-feet at operational level) also moderated downstream floods.

The 1980 HEP was conducted by a multi-agency team for the [[Flood Control Levee]] project, using habitat ratings based largely on team members' visual impressions and a very limited literature base. High flows and reduced funding prevented adequate sampling, so the team initiated supplemental work on selected species.

The objectives were to determine the pre-project distribution and relative abundance of crayfish and spiny softshell turtles. These species were chosen as habitat indicators, important members of the aquatic community, and species about which little local life-history information existed. The report aims to support impact prediction, mitigation planning, environmental compliance documents and post-project comparisons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The river channel is a major water conveyance system delivering irrigation water to the Yuma Valley and Mexico. Agricultural drains, treated waste water and the [[Gila River]] help maintain flows during low mainstream periods. Channelized and riprapped banks are mechanically kept clear of vegetation. Submerged plants are sparse, and [[Saltcedar]] and [[Cottonwood and Willow]] riparian zones dominate the shoreline. Emergent plants such as cattail and bulrush form narrow bands, and tree roots and snags provide habitat diversity.

**Sampling methods.**
- *Crayfish:* [[Baited Crayfish Trap]] sets (76 cm long, 30.5 cm diameter, welded wire with funnel entrances), at 0.5 river-mile intervals in water 0.5–1.5 m deep, fished three consecutive nights.
- *Fish and crayfish verification:* 62 random seine hauls (7.6 m by 1.2 m, 0.6 cm mesh) in probable crayfish habitat.
- *Softshell turtles:* Baited hoop nets (4.6 m long, 1.1 m throat, 2.5 cm mesh), with a single net-night per site for distribution and 6 to 8 consecutive nights at two intensively sampled stations for relative abundance. Turtles were marked with color-coded tags for recapture.

**Environmental conditions.** Surface water temperatures ranged from 25 to 33°C during crayfish sampling. The area below the Gila River confluence was typically the coolest, by about 1.0°C. Hoop net water temperatures ranged from 30 to 37°C during most of the summer.

**Predation and competition.** A 1981 survey after high water in 1979–1980 found exceptional recruitment of [[Sunfish and Centrarchids]] and catfish. Fish were concentrated in the main channel and backwaters, creating a high standing stock of predators.

## Key Findings

**Crayfish (Procambarus clarkii)**
- Total effort: 5,112 trap-hours (213 trap-nights) across representative main channel and backwater habitats. No crayfish were captured or observed in the Yuma Division.
- Seine hauls (62 hauls) also captured no crayfish. Authors suggest either low density or inadequate sampling methods.
- A verification effort at West Pond captured 8 crayfish (5 females, 3 males) in 216 trap-hours (9 trap-nights). Efficiency was lower than expected, and mesh size may have excluded crayfish with cephalothorax lengths under 30 mm.
- Incidental catch included juvenile fish: 15 channel catfish, tilapia, bluegill and carp. The presence of young channel catfish suggested fair to good recruitment.
- Authors argue that crayfish are probably present in adequate numbers to be an important food item for fish, softshell turtles, bullfrogs and the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]].
- Possible reasons for the negative results include seasonal burrowing behavior, predation by abundant fish, and high water during the 1979–1980 breeding period.

**Spiny softshell turtle (Apalone spinifera / Trionyx spiniferus)**
- Distribution phase: 46 turtles captured in 651 hoop net-hours (May, August and September 1981). Turtles occurred between RM 24.5 and 27.1 in the lower section and RM 34.1 and 38.0 in the upper section.
- Relative abundance phase: 58 turtles captured in 1,286 hoop net-hours at two stations (RM 24.5 and RM 34.2).
- Combined: 104 turtles in 1,937 hoop net-hours, an overall catch rate of 0.05 turtles per hour. The highest rate, 0.32 turtles per hour, occurred near the Gila River confluence (RM 34.2).
- Catch per unit effort at RM 34.2 was more than seven times higher than at RM 24.5.
- Population estimates (95% confidence, p = 0.05): 10 ± 56 turtles at RM 24.5 and 72 ± 32 turtles at RM 34.2. The authors note the wide interval at RM 24.5 reflects a small sample size.
- Sex ratio: 2.7 males to 1 female in the distribution sample (n = 46). In the relative abundance sample (n = 58), the ratio was approximately 1:1, but the proportion of females increased with consecutive nights.
- Size: females ranged from 15 to 25 cm and 30 to 40 cm carapace length, weighing 0.30 to 4.5 kg. Males were mostly 10 to 20 cm carapace length, weighing 0.15 to 0.85 kg. Carapace-to-plastron length ratio was 1.39 ± 0.07, not significantly different between sexes.
- Movement: only two recaptures; neither moved more than 50 m from release sites in one day.
- Habitat: turtles were most common near backwaters with mud/silt bottoms, points and sandbars adjacent to the main channel. These appear to be feeding and resting zones. Sandy banks were used for nesting.

**Habitat Evaluation Procedures (HEP)**
- The 1980 HEP ratings for softshell turtle habitat (averaged from 1 to 9 scale points) ranged from 4.0 to 6.0. Backwater sites rated 1.0 to 2.0 points higher than mainstream sites.
- The HEP suggested only a slight habitat difference between RM 24.5 and RM 34.2, while actual abundance differed more than seven-fold.
- The authors conclude the HEP is not sensitive enough for evaluating project impacts or mitigation requirements, and it omitted a "forage availability" factor.

**Recommendations**
- Maintain nesting, resting and feeding habitat components at regular intervals along the river to retain the existing softshell turtle population.
- Conduct additional surveys using different sampling techniques and seasons, plus movement studies, before project design decisions.
- Interpret habitat requirements for crayfish through specific studies before developing project alternatives.
