# Fish Tags and Tagging in Quebec Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-tagging #fisheries-management #Acipenseridae #Clupeidae #Anguillidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by Vadim D. Vladykov (Quebec Department of Fisheries) reports on a 12-year fish tagging program (1945–1956) conducted primarily in the [[St. Lawrence River]], during which 19,310 fish across 13 species were tagged (more than 25,000 tagged since 1938). The study is methodological rather than morphological, comparing three generations of fish tag design — the **cattle ear tag**, the **ring-shaped strap tag**, and the **split ring and plate tag** — and their relative performance across species with differing size, skin toughness, and behavior. Key findings include exceptionally long tag retention (10–11 years in sturgeon), minimal corrosion of monel/nickel/stainless components in salt water, and large-scale migratory movements documented for [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] (892 miles) and [[American Shad]] (1,650 miles). The work remains foundational for understanding early 20th-century anadromous fish migration research methodology in the [[Northwest Atlantic]].

## Background & Description

While the paper does not describe fish morphology in detail, it documents tag-attachment anatomy relevant to species-specific handling:

- **Sturgeon (Lake and Atlantic)**: Tags fastened through skin and cartilage at the **front edge of the dorsal fin** — reflecting the tough, cartilaginous dermal structure of [[Acipenseridae]].
- **Channel Catfish, Eel, Shad, Smelt, Striped Bass, Tomcod, Trout**: Tags placed on the **lower jaw**, indicating reliance on jaw bone/cartilage as an attachment site across teleost taxa.
- **Speckled Trout**: Early cattle-ear-style tags on the lower jaw caused **bone reabsorption** due to metal pressure, indicating this jaw bone experiences active osteolytic remodeling under mechanical stress — tags fell off within months.
- One recaptured [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] grew from 28 to 36 inches (fork length) and 4 to 11 lbs within ~11 months, providing rare direct growth-rate data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Predominantly the [[St. Lawrence River]] and its estuary, with recoveries extending into the wider Northwest Atlantic coastal zone.
- **Migratory Behavior** (documented via tag recovery distances):
  - [[Atlantic Sturgeon]]: tagged at Isle-aux-Coudres, recovered at Fox Point, Nova Scotia — **892 miles**, the longest confirmed sturgeon migration in the study.
  - [[American Shad]]: tagged at Isle Verte, recovered at Old Point Comfort, Virginia (Chesapeake Bay entrance) — **1,650 miles**, the greatest distance recorded for any species in this study.
  - [[Lake Sturgeon]]: more localized, movements not exceeding ~130 miles.
  - [[Channel Catfish]]: limited movement in the St. Lawrence region, not exceeding 95 miles.
  - [[Striped Bass]]: maximum travel ~180 miles within the St. Lawrence system.
  - [[Rainbow Smelt]] and [[Atlantic Tomcod]]: shorter-range movements of up to 90 and 135 miles, respectively.
  - [[American Eel]]: adult females recaptured up to 200 miles from tagging sites; notably far from their true spawning grounds in the **Sargasso Sea**, though Quebec commercial fisheries do not extend east of Rivière-du-Loup.
- **Seasonal Physiological Stress**: [[Atlantic Tomcod]] caught via ice-fishing during winter spawning season showed <1% tag return, likely due to combined stress of full gonads and abrupt temperature shock (32°F water vs. ~70°F fishing cabin). Summer/autumn-caught tomcod survived tagging far better (10–20% returns).

## Key Findings

**Tag Types & Design Evolution**
- **Cattle ear tag** (1938–1940): Monel strap tags adapted from livestock tagging; discarded due to poor growth accommodation and tissue reabsorption issues (esp. speckled trout jaw).
- **Ring-shaped strap tag** (from 1940): Monel/nickel, ¾" and 1" diameter, resembling the "Heincke ring." Suitable for large, tough-skinned fish (sturgeon) and used on jaw or dorsal fin cartilage. Thin gauge (1/64 inch) risked cutting tissue.
- **Split ring and plate tag** (from 1950, designed by Vladykov): Stainless steel piano-wire split ring (¾", ⅜", or ⅞" diameter) with a laminated vinylite plate (0.02 inch thick, later thickened 3×) bearing agency inscription and ID code. Non-cutting round wire edge; superior for smaller/lighter fish. Experimental spot-welding of ring ends was being tested to prevent accidental opening in nets.

**Comparative Tag Return Rates by Species** (strap tag vs. split ring, percent returned):
| Species | Strap Tag Return | Split Ring Return |
|---|---|---|
| [[Lake Sturgeon]] | 6.7% | — |
| [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] | 25.9% | — |
| [[American Eel]] | up to 11% | 6% (3% in some data) |
| [[Channel Catfish]] | 11% | 3% |
| [[American Shad]] | lower | higher |
| [[Striped Bass]] | lower | higher |

- Split ring tags generally outperformed strap tags in smaller/juvenile fish (smelt, tomcod, trout); strap tags outperformed in tough-skinned or large fish (sturgeon, eel, catfish).

**Longevity & Corrosion Resistance**
- Monel/nickel strap tags resisted salt-water corrosion for **4–8 years** in shad, eel, striped bass, and channel catfish with no significant degradation.
- Sturgeon recaptured after **10–11 years** still carried strap tags; wear was attributed mainly to abrasion against the sea bottom, not corrosion.
- Stainless steel split rings showed **no corrosion** even after 4 years in salt water, contradicting earlier claims (Calhoun, Fry, and Hughes 1951) that monel/nickel tags were unsuitable due to corrosion.

**Migration Distance Records**
- Maximum distance recorded: [[American Shad]] — **1,650 miles** (Quebec to Chesapeake Bay, Virginia).
- Second-longest: [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] — **892 miles** (Quebec to Nova Scotia).
- These findings underscored tagging as **the only reliable method** at the time for elucidating long-distance migratory behavior in anadromous Quebec commercial fishes.

**Methodological Caveats**
- Return rates are **not strictly comparable across species** due to differing fishing intensity, gear type, fish size at tagging, and seasonal handling stress.
- Tag placement site (jaw vs. dorsal fin/cartilage) was tailored to species-specific skeletal robustness and skin toughness.
