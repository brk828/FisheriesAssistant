# Differentiation in Fishes of the American West: Causal Hypotheses and Tests

## Executive Summary
This study by DeMarais, Minckley, and Douglas examines the confusing, non-geographically-directional ("mosaic") patterns of morphological and meristic variation in the [[Gila Robusta]] complex — a group of cyprinid fishes ([[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Intermedia]], and [[Gila Grahami]]) inhabiting the Gila River basin of Arizona and New Mexico. Rather than accepting single-cause explanations (e.g., pure ecotypy, hybridization, or vicariance) common in prior taxonomic treatments, the authors developed and statistically tested **three competing evolutionary models** simultaneously using Mantel matrix-correlation methods. The central finding is that **historical vicariance events, particularly drainage configurations dating to the mid-Miocene**, best explain the extant mosaic of morphological diversity — a result reinforced by a secondary, independent phylogenetic (Wagner tree/branch-length) test. Models invoking [[Ecotypy]] and [[Hybridization]]/intergradation showed comparatively weak explanatory power.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomic Focus:** The "Gila robusta complex" is treated in this paper as a single taxonomic entity encompassing three historically contentious nominal forms:
  - *Gila robusta* (Baird and Girard)
  - *Gila intermedia* (Girard)
  - *Gila grahami* (Baird and Girard) — Note: variously treated across the literature as full species, subspecies, or ecological races.
- **Family:** [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows), a family well known in western North American freshwater systems for producing taxonomically difficult, highly variable lineages.
- **Historical Problem:** Western fish taxa exhibiting discordant morphological/meristic variation have historically been either:
  1. **"Split"** into a multitude of nominal taxa (e.g., Jordan et al., 1930), or
  2. **"Lumped"** into single polytypic species with variants dismissed as ecological/geographic noise.
  Neither approach has proven satisfying, motivating the multi-hypothesis testing framework used here.
- **Study Region:** The Gila River basin, part of the Basin and Range province of Arizona/New Mexico, characterized by:
  - Northwest-southeast trending mountain ranges and parallel valleys.
  - Elevations ranging from near sea level (west) to peaks >3000 m msl.
  - Northern tributaries descending from the Colorado Plateau via the Mogollon Rim (~1700 m msl).
  - Complex Miocene-to-Recent geologic history including mountain building, aridification, and drainage reorganization (see [[Vicariance Biogeography]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Aquatic Connectivity:** Unlike geologically stable, groundwater-rich terrestrial systems, western aquatic systems are described as having connectivity that is "at best transitory, at worst illusory," driving discordant, non-clinal patterns of variation across drainages.
- **Sub-basins Sampled:** Santa Cruz (n=4 sites), upper San Pedro (n=4), lower San Pedro (n=2), Gila (n=4), Verde (n=7), Salt (n=6), Agua Fria (n=3) — total 33 sites, 1106 adult specimens (SL > 50 mm; ≥25 specimens/site).
- **Comparative/outgroup material:** Bill Williams and Little Colorado watersheds (Arizona) and Rio Yaqui (Sonora, Mexico).
- **Environmental Gradients Modeled:**
  - **Stream flow/size** (ranked 0–3 based on historical base flow records: 0 = no flow; 1 = <0.28 m³/sec; 2 = 0.28–1.42 m³/sec; 3 = >1.42 m³/sec).
  - **Elevation** (proxy for thermal regime; cooler at higher elevation).
  - **Barrier/isolation status** (0 = no barrier; 1 = partial isolation via seasonal dry reaches/steep gradients; 2 = absolute isolation, e.g., above waterfalls or within the historically disconnected Santa Cruz basin).
  - **Paleodrainage configuration** across five epochs: Oligocene, early Miocene, mid-Miocene, Pliocene, and Pleistocene, based on reconstructions from Nations et al. (1985).
- **Key Environmental/Geological Driver:** Progressive Miocene-to-Recent aridification and episodic mountain building fragmented once-continuous aquatic systems, causing lakes, springs, and rivers to disappear, isolate in intermontane basins, or dwindle into disjunct channel segments — later re-integrated piecemeal into composite modern drainages (relevant to [[Drainage Evolution]] and [[Historical Biogeography]]).

## Key Findings

### Study Design: Three Competing Models
1. **Model I — Ecotypy/Ecophenotypy:** Predicts body shape/meristic variation driven by localized environmental gradients (e.g., small tributary vs. large river conditions). Tested via correlation with elevation (ALT) and stream flow (STRF) matrices.
   - Historical roots in botany ([[Turesson 1922]] hawkweed ecotypes; Clausen et al. 1948 yarrow; Antonovics & Bradshaw 1970 mine-tailing plants).
   - First applied to fishes/Gila by Hubbs (1940, 1941), Miller (1946, "ecological subspecies"), and Rinne (1976).
2. **Model II — Hybridization/Intergradation:** Predicts "problematic" populations represent hybrids from secondary contact between parental *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia* lineages, even absent current sympatry. Tested via correlation with a stream-barrier/isolation matrix (STRB).
   - Draws on Mayr's (1982) distinction between smooth intergradation vs. abrupt, highly variable hybrid zones.
3. **Model III — Vicariance:** Posits all three Gila forms as independent lineages with historically overlapping/syntopic ranges, subsequently fragmented and passively re-mingled by tectonic/geological processes, producing the extant morphological mosaic. Tested via correlation with five paleodrainage matrices (OLG, EM, MM, PL, PLST).

### Analytical Methods
- **Morphometric/meristic data:** 26 morphometric measurements + 7 meristic counts (gill rakers, fin ray counts, lateral line scales) per specimen, following Hubbs and Lagler (1974).
- **Statistical treatments:** PCA, canonical variate analysis (CVA), UPGMA clustering, sheared principal component analysis (Bookstein et al. 1985; Rohlf & Bookstein 1987) to derive size-free shape residuals.
- **Matrix congruence tests:** Two-way and three-way [[Mantel Test]] correlations between phenotypic matrices (shape [SH], meristics [MER]) and environmental/historical matrices (ALT, STRF, STRB, paleodrainage epochs).
- **Secondary independent test:** Wagner trees built separately from meristic and morphometric data (homogeneous-subsets coding, Archie 1985); rank-correlation of phylogenetic sequence position against branch length, following Thorpe (1984) — testing whether more recently-derived populations show shorter (less anagenetic) branches, diagnostic of historical (vicariant) vs. ecotypic causation.

### Primary Results
- **Vicariance (Model III) was the strongest supported hypothesis**, particularly congruence with **mid-Miocene paleodrainage configuration**, which showed statistically significant correlation with morphological/meristic mosaic patterns.
- **Ecotypy (Model I) and Hybridization (Model II) showed little explanatory power** in the matrix correlation tests, despite ecotypy being the more commonly invoked explanation in prior literature for similar western fish variation patterns.
- The independent phylogenetic branch-length test (Thorpe's method) was used to corroborate the historical (vicariant) signal over an ecological/ecotypic one.

### Broader Significance
- Findings support a growing body of evidence that [[Vicariance Biogeography]] can operate at **local/regional spatial scales**, not just continental/global scales as classically applied (Rosen 1974, 1979; Brooks 1988).
- This is presented as one of the first regional/local studies to directly link extant fish phenotypic diversity to reconstructed **ancient hydrography** (paleodrainage patterns), rather than relying solely on phylogenetic pattern without explicit historical drainage geometry.
- Broader implication: many western North American river systems are **not** single continuous evolutionary units but rather **geological composites** of multiple ancient drainage systems (Minckley et al., 1986), and their fish faunas were passively transferred or actively dispersed among these fragmenting/reintegrating systems — producing morphological patterns that defy simple clinal or single-cause explanations.
- Relevant taxa for further cross-reference: [[Gila Cypha]] (related congener from lower Colorado basin, Miller 1946), broader [[Colorado River Basin]] fish fauna, and general theory of [[Ecotypy]] vs. [[Ecophenotype]] distinctions (Mayr 1942; Dobzhansky 1970; Quinn 1978).

### Notable Conceptual Distinctions Made
- **Ecotype vs. Ecophenotype:** Ecotype implies a heritable genetic component to habitat-correlated variation; ecophenotype does not (Mayr 1942). The paper treats both under a unified "ecotypy" label for analytical simplicity.
- **Intergradation vs. Hybrid Zone:** Following Mayr (1982) — intergradation shows smooth, low-variance transitions between forms; hybrid zones show abrupt transitions with highly variable intermediate populations.
