# Dams in New Hampshire and Their Effects on Fish and Game

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 report from the [[New Hampshire Fish And Game Department]] (Management and Research Division) assesses how dams affect fish and wildlife in New Hampshire. It concludes that some dams have been highly destructive while many have been beneficial. The authors argue that dam proposals should be judged only after their effects on all natural resources have been analyzed, and they recommend that dam builders account for damage to fish and game in their cost estimates. The report includes a technical appendix on the [[Androscoggin River]] (Errol-Pontook region) and survey summaries for the [[Contoocook Watershed]] and [[Lower Connecticut River Basin]], drawn from surveys conducted during 1951 to 1954.

## Background & Description

- **Scale:** New Hampshire has over two thousand dams of many shapes and sizes, in varying states of repair. Nearly a thousand have been used for power or storage.
- **Small dams** (impounding less than 1,000 surface acres) were built historically for saws, grist mills, log drives, drinking water, recreation, and farm ponds. They generally added fish and game habitat. Some acted as barriers to [[Atlantic Salmon]] and [[Trout]] migration or warmed cold-water streams into warm-water ponds. Since the report's date, the Fish and Game Department must approve any obstruction placed in fish streams.
- **Large dams** (on major tributaries or impounding more than 1,000 surface acres) serve flood control, industrial, and hydroelectric purposes. They have produced both the greatest benefits and the greatest damage to fish and game.
- **Flood control dams:** Four were built by the [[US Army Corps Of Engineers]]: Franklin Falls Reservoir (Franklin), [[Blackwater Reservoir]] (Webster), Surry Mountain Reservoir (Gilsum), and MacDowell Reservoir (West Peterborough). These dams normally hold no water; their gates close only during abnormal runoff.
- **Survey background:** A presidential directive of October 9, 1954 placed New England–New York waterways under investigation. The [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] and state agencies surveyed wildlife in flood control and power dam areas. Biologists reported that the time allotted (four years) was too short, so state-wide completion was not possible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migratory fish losses.** Dams built on New Hampshire's three major river systems ended runs of ocean fishes, including [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[American Shad]], and [[Alewife]].

**Habitat inundation.** Important game country was flooded by impoundments such as Lake Francis, the Connecticut Lakes, Umbagog Lake, and the Franklin Pierce (Jackman) Reservoirs.

**Water level fluctuation.** Large fluctuations, especially during or after spawning, may eradicate year classes of fish. Documented or expected annual fluctuations were:
- [[Lake Francis]]: possible 50 feet
- First Connecticut Lake: possible 30 feet
- New Littleton dam: expected 40-foot drawdown

By contrast, nine of the fifteen New Hampshire lakes over 1,000 acres had annual fluctuations under 10 feet. Some of these are outstanding salmonoid lakes.

**Drawdown as management.** Studies at [[Bow Lake]] found it overpopulated with undesirable fishes. Periodic late-summer [[Drawdown]] would remove escape cover for young competitor fish, making them easier prey for game fish. The report states that too-stable water levels can be detrimental.

**Waterfowl and furbearers.** Most deep impoundments make poor waterfowl and furbearer habitat. The Pontook and Errol dams on the Androscoggin, however, created marshland that may be the best waterfowl and furbearer habitat in the state.

**Economic value.** Fishing and hunting bring at least $22,000,000 annually to New Hampshire. A 1952 study found over half a million dollars spent yearly in the Androscoggin watershed by licensed hunters and anglers.

## Key Findings

### Flood Control Dams
- The four Corps of Engineers flood control dams have, for the most part, been beneficial. Feared habitat destruction from flood-time impoundment did not materialize. Blackwater and MacDowell sites hold excellent game habitat.
- Animals are driven to higher ground during floods, and some small mammals likely perish, as happens in any river bottom during floods.
- One case of real harm occurred below the Blackwater dam when gates closed so completely that the stream dried and trout were stranded. The report recommends a specified minimum flow.
- Permanent pools behind flood control dams (at Otter Brook, Hopkinton-Everett, and Surry Mountain) can help or harm depending on site. Warming trout water and flooding game country are harmful. Impounding warm-water streams can benefit sportsmen.

### Androscoggin River (Errol-Pontook Plans)
Three power development plans were evaluated:
- **Plan 1 ("High Errol")**: Three dams. Largest losses. Land loss was 1.3 square miles (as stated). The report judges it undesirable. Daily fluctuation is about 3 feet, with peaking releases.
- **Plan 2 ("Low Errol")**: Two dams. Smaller losses, with less loss of deer yards and waterfowl gains from the Errol impoundment. Seasonal fluctuation is 10 feet at Errol and 20 to 25 feet at Pontook. Described as a compromise.
- **Plan 3 ("High Pontook")**: One dam. Greatest land loss under the three plans, but it creates a deep, stable lake suitable for salmonids and offers log transport and recreation. The report calls it the best compromise for fish and wildlife.
- Fishing data (complete year): Pontook still water showed 2,726 hours and 1,603 fish caught (warm water). The Androscoggin reach between Bog Brook and Errol dam showed 1,514 hours and about 500 fish (salmonoids). Losing the 12 to 13 miles of salmonoid stream is the greatest fisheries loss.
- Fishways are recommended on all dams under any power plan. Minimum flow below Errol dam must protect the salmonoid population.

### Wildlife Impacts (Errol-Pontook)
- Table data show habitat losses for upland game, woodcock, deer yards (1,275 acres, including 600 acres of known deer yards), and waterfowl.
- Trapper income was about $2,591.52 in gross muskrat income and $552.50 for otter-related catch, with a bobcat bounty of $120.00 in Errol.
- Muskrat, mink, otter, beaver, raccoon, moose, and bear were all recorded, with varying abundance.

### Contoocook Watershed (Merrimack Basin)
- About 111,15x fish were removed by an estimated anglers, who spent approximately $172,117.00, with nonresidents contributing slightly under half.
- Warm-water ponds were the most preferred angling resource, followed by trout streams. The Contoocook River itself is little used, mainly due to pollution.
- Site recommendations:
  - [[Hopkinton-Everett Site]]: Temporary flooding causes no permanent damage. Management should be conducted under a Corps license, and a conservation pool should be decided only after acreage estimates.
  - [[Blackwater Site]]: Little damage was observed. The Fish and Game Department strongly opposes a permanent pool, especially with fluctuating power-related levels. The report recommends no permanent pool.
  - [[Riverhill Site]]: Little damage expected. A multipurpose conservation pool with a low-level outlet is suggested.
  - [[Roby Site]]: A conservation pool is not recommended because warming, silting, and trash fish introduction would harm trout fisheries.

### Lower Connecticut River Basin
- Anglers spent an estimated $3,300 during the composite year. Trout streams are used mainly in May. Large bass–pickerel lakes provide year-round angling.
- Small game hunters (5,952 trips reported) spent about $28,200. Deer hunters made about 1,500 trips and spent about $43,400. Approximately 20 deer were taken.
- The estimated deer population was about 700, a density of 3.5 deer per square mile.
- Beaver: about 60 animals, 72 dams, 16 active colonies. Estimated beaver value was $80.
- Muskrats are relatively abundant and mainly live in bank burrows. Severe water fluctuations from the Vernon Power Dam may threaten young muskrats.
- Waterfowl habitat ratings: 20% excellent (94.50 acres), 18% good (12 acres), 16% fair (22 acres), and 6% poor (1,035.4 acres).
- Recommendations for [[Otter Brook]] include a stabilized minimum flow, avoiding siltation during construction, and no channel straightening.
- For [[Honey Hill]], flood control operation would cause no significant trout losses. A dead storage pool, however, would likely eliminate valuable trout fisheries.

### Summary Recommendations
1. Dam construction should proceed only after analysis of effects on all natural resources.
2. Dam builders should include damage costs to fish and game in cost estimates, with funds used for compensatory management.
3. Where damage is unavoidable, sportsmen and businesses that benefit should help finance dam projects that create new recreation and business.
