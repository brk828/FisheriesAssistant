# Otoliths and Other Fish Remains from the Timms Point Silt (Early Pleistocene) at San Pedro, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoichthyology #otoliths

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by [[John E. Fitch]], published in the [[Los Angeles County Museum of Natural History]] *Contributions in Science* (No. 146), identifies fish remains recovered from the [[Timms Point Silt]] exposed near San Pedro, California. Over 1,000 pounds of fossiliferous matrix yielded 2,601 otoliths, 121 vertebrae, 68 teeth, 2 dermal denticles and 1 fin spine. These came from a minimum of 62 species (53 bony fishes and 9 elasmobranchs) in 54 genera and 30 families. The assemblage includes nine deep-sea species from the [[Myctophidae]] and [[Melamphaidae]], which are rarely captured in water shallower than 1,000 feet, and six "northern" forms no longer recorded near the locality. The author uses this fauna to infer deposition at depths of about 25 to 100 fathoms under cooler water than today.

## Background & Description

The study rests on material from repeated sampling of an exposure near the type section, processed by wet screening and sorting as described in an earlier paper (Fitch, 1966). Additional specimens came from [[George P. Kanakoff]] and museum volunteers, [[Charles R. Wright]], and the [[Zinsmeister Collection]]. Otoliths (sagittae) are the main diagnostic element, and the paper includes length ranges and estimated adult sagitta sizes for each taxon.

Stratigraphically, the Timms Point silt lies after the [[Lomita Marl]] and before the [[San Pedro Sand]]. The Lomita marl had been regarded as early Pleistocene, but the author's otolith evidence and molluscan work suggest it is the youngest marine Pliocene in the area, which would make the Timms Point silt the oldest Pleistocene unit of the region. A potassium-argon date of 3.04 million years B.P. was reported for the Lomita marl, and the author notes that dating methods for the younger terraces are doubtful.

The systematic account covers each species with its range, size records, depth preferences, fossil occurrences and material count. Representative entries include:

- **Heterodontus francisci** (horn shark): 1 tooth; abundant in rocky subtidal areas.
- **Carcharodon carcharias** (white shark): 2 teeth.
- **Galeorhinus zyopterus** (soupfin shark): 9 teeth.
- **Squalus acanthias** (spiny dogfish): 19 teeth.
- **Clupea pallasi** (Pacific herring): 5 otoliths.
- **Engraulis mordax** (northern anchovy): 4 otoliths.
- **Stenobrachius leucopsarus** (northern lampfish): 35 otoliths.
- **Merluccius productus** (Pacific hake): 22 otoliths.
- **Lyopsetta exilis** (slender sole): 219 otoliths.
- **Citharichthys spp.** (sanddabs, undetermined): 267 otoliths.
- **Sebastodes spp.** (rockfishes, undetermined): 464 otoliths.
- **Cottidae** (sculpins, undetermined): 419 otoliths.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depth and temperature.** [[Valentine (1961)]] inferred from molluscan assemblages that the silt formed at about 25 to 100 fathoms, in waters cooler than today at least seasonally. The author argues that the fish fauna supports this. Of the 53 teleost species, 9 are mesopelagic myctophids and melamphaids. Beach seining (451 hauls, 3 years, to about 10 to 12 feet) captured only 10 of the species. Otter trawling in Santa Monica Bay (60 to 600 feet) captured 27 of the 53+ Timms Point species.

**Northern and cool-water taxa.** Six species (*Ammodytes hexapterus*, *Atheresthes stomias*, *Lyconectes aleutensis*, *Malacocottus zonurus*, *Microgadus proximus* and *Theragra chalcogramma*) have not been recorded south of about Monterey during modern times. Figure 4 maps present-day distributions of 51 of the 53 teleosts.

**Mechanisms of deep-sea otolith deposition.** The author lists five ways deep-sea otoliths can reach shallow deposits: predator transport, mass mortality with currents or scavengers, nocturnal diel migration near submarine canyons and steep shelves, spawning migrations, and tectonic uplift or faulting of deeper beds.

**Taphonomy and preservation.** Many cottid otoliths were badly eroded, possibly by predator digestion. Fossil deposits are death assemblages, so inferences about living communities remain speculative.

**Conservation.** Many [[Timms Point Silt]] exposures recorded by earlier workers had disappeared, and the author warns that the remaining outcrops face development. Salvage is urged before the remaining material is lost.

## Key Findings

- The Timms Point fauna comprises a minimum of 62 species (53 teleosts, 9 elasmobranchs) in 54 genera and 30 families.
- Nine myctophid and melamphaid species (typically found deeper than 1,000 feet) indicate offshore or deep-water influence on the deposit.
- Six "northern" species have no modern records near the locality, which supports cooler conditions during deposition.
- The fish evidence agrees with a depositional depth of about 25 to 100 fathoms and cooler water, as proposed by Valentine (1961).
- The Lomita marl may be the youngest Pliocene rather than early Pleistocene, which would make the Timms Point silt the oldest Pleistocene of the Los Angeles region. No direct age estimate is available for the silt itself.
- The most abundant taxa were cottids, sanddabs, rockfishes, slender sole and goby-family remains. Several taxa were recovered only in small numbers (for example, one Dover sole and one arrowtooth flounder otolith).
- The author notes that the remaining exposures are at risk, so the fossil record may be limited to this collection.
- Conclusions about life associations cannot be drawn from death assemblages without additional evidence.
