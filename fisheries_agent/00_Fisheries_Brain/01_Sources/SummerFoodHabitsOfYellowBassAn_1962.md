# Summer Food Habits of Yellow Bass and Black Bullheads in Clear Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Moronidae #Ictaluridae #limnology

## Executive Summary
This 1960–1961 study examines the summer diet and feeding periodicity of [[Yellow Bass]] (*Roccus mississippiensis*) and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*) in [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]], a shallow eutrophic lake. Young-of-the-year yellow bass shifted diet from entomostracans (copepods, cladocerans) to immature insects (chironomids) once they reached approximately 50 mm total length. Adult yellow bass diets were dominated by immature insects (60% frequency) and crustaceans (81% frequency), with cladocerans—particularly *Daphnia* sp.—consumed in extremely large numbers, suggesting active selective feeding. A historically significant finding is the sharp decline in [[Forage Fish]] utilization by yellow bass over time (86% in 1943 → 24% in 1952 → 2% in 1960), a trend linked to reduced yellow bass body size and growth rates. In contrast, black bullheads showed a comparatively higher and increasing reliance on small fish (9% in 1960 vs. <1% in 1951). Diurnal sampling revealed peak feeding activity between 4–8 PM, immediately preceding peak nocturnal [[Gill Net]] catch rates (6 PM–2 AM).

## Background & Description
*Note: This paper focuses on diet/feeding ecology rather than detailed morphology; size-class data relevant to feeding shifts are summarized below.*

- **Yellow Bass (YOY):** Diet shift threshold occurs at **~50 mm total length**, when immature insects (chironomids) become a significant dietary component alongside entomostracans.
- **Yellow Bass (Adult, 1960 sample):** Total length range of 3–7.9 inches sampled; forage fish consumption was rare and restricted almost exclusively to the largest size classes (3 of 4 fish that ate forage fish were 7–7.9 inches).
- **Historical Size Trend:** Adult yellow bass sampled in 1952 were notably larger (7–10.9 inches) than the 1960 cohort (3–7.9 inches), correlating with documented reduced growth rates in recent decades.
- **Black Bullhead:** Total length range contracted from **8–12 inches (1951)** to **7.9–9.9 inches (1960)**, indicating a decrease in average adult size over the study period, paralleling patterns seen in [[Yellow Bass]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]] is a shallow, eutrophic lake (~3,643 acres, max depth ~20 ft). Littoral substrate is sand/gravel; deeper zones are silt. Dominant vegetation includes emergent [[Scirpus Validus]] (giant bulrush) and submergent *Potamogeton* spp.
- **Benthic Fauna Base:** [[Chironomidae]] larvae were the dominant benthic organism at 8 of 9 sampling sites in 1960, a shift from earlier decades when *[[Hyalella]]* sp. was co-dominant (Pearcy, 1953). Overall benthic fauna biovolume declined relative to earlier studies (1953, 1958).
- **Trophic Role — Yellow Bass:**
  - YOY: Primary predator of entomostracans (copepods, *Leptodora*, *Daphnia*, Sididae), transitioning to insectivory (chironomids) with growth.
  - Adults: Generalist feeder on crustaceans (especially cladocerans) and immature insects; selective for *Daphnia* sp. over the larger *Leptodora* sp.; minimal piscivory in 1960 relative to historical baselines.
  - Spatial variation: Cladoceran consumption frequency was higher in shallow water (82%) vs. deep water (64%).
- **Trophic Role — Black Bullhead:**
  - Generalist benthic/omnivorous feeder consuming crustaceans (57%), plant material (42%), chironomids (28%), and small fish (9%).
  - Greater reliance on piscivory than yellow bass and increasing utilization of small fish through the summer season (0% June → 6% July → 18% August).
  - Crustaceans and chironomids consumed more frequently in shallow water than deep water locations.
- **Seasonal Dynamics:** Cladoceran frequency in yellow bass diets declined from June to August 1960, despite relatively stable cladoceran abundance in the water column (per [[Small 1961]] plankton study), indicating a behavioral shift in prey selection rather than prey availability. Copepod, *Hyalella*, and immature insect frequencies peaked in July, suggesting increased benthic/vegetation-associated foraging mid-summer.
- **Diel Feeding Pattern:** Peak stomach fullness/feeding activity occurred 4–8 PM; highest mean chironomid and *Daphnia* counts per stomach at 6 PM; *Leptodora* sp. peaked at 2 AM. Peak [[Gill Net]] catch rates (indicative of activity/movement) occurred 6 PM–2 AM, trailing the feeding peak.

## Key Findings
1. **Ontogenetic Diet Shift:** Yellow bass YOY shift from an entomostracan-dominated diet to significant insect consumption at ~50 mm TL, consistent with prior findings at North Twin Lake ([[Kutkuhn 1955]]).
2. **Long-Term Decline in Piscivory:** Forage fish frequency in adult yellow bass stomachs collapsed from 86% (1943) to 24% (1952) to just 2% (1960), coinciding with a historical decrease in yellow bass body size — a pattern the author links to reduced growth rates.
3. **Inverse Crustacean Trend:** Crustacean consumption frequency in yellow bass rose over the same period (7% in 1943 to 81% in 1960), suggesting a dietary substitution of crustaceans for forage fish over ~17 years.
4. **Selective Feeding Behavior:** Individual yellow bass were found to consume extraordinary quantities of *Daphnia* sp. (up to 3,772 individuals in one stomach), indicating strong prey selectivity even when forage fish are available in normal densities.
5. **Species Dietary Divergence:** The primary ecological difference between co-occurring [[Yellow Bass]] and [[Black Bullhead]] is the bullhead's substantially greater reliance on small forage fish, despite normal YOY game fish populations being available to both species.
6. **Diurnal Feeding Chronology:** Maximum feeding activity (4–8 PM) precedes the period of maximum nocturnal catch/activity (6 PM–2 AM), a pattern corroborated by both 1960–61 and historical (1957–58) data.
7. **Bullhead Decline Parallel:** Similar to yellow bass, black bullheads exhibited decreased average body size and decreased chironomid consumption frequency (53% in 1951 → 28% in 1960) between study periods, alongside increased piscivory.
8. **Benthic Community Shift:** A long-term shift in dominant benthic fauna from *Hyalella* sp. to [[Chironomidae]] larvae was noted across multiple decades of study at [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]], alongside an overall decline in benthic biovolume.
