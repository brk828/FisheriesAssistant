# The Ecology of Typha latifolia in Imperial Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report from the [[Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] (University of Arizona, Tucson) examines whether [[Typha latifolia]] (broadleaf cattail) supports the sport fishery of [[Imperial Reservoir]], a eutrophic, partly brackish reservoir on the [[Lower Colorado River]] in Arizona and California. Sampling ran from September 1969 to September 1972 (39 visits). The study measured the production of Typha, [[Najas marina]], and planktonic and benthic algae, and assessed allochthonous inputs, detritus distribution, decomposition, dissolved organics, and fish use of Typha beds.

Typha is the largest energy-fixing component of the reservoir, producing more than twice the energy of all other components combined. Its fibrous blades decompose slowly, so most of this energy reaches fish through detrital and dissolved-organic pathways, with only limited direct use. Typha beds also provide spawning, nursery, and cover habitat for [[Largemouth Bass]] and other centrarchids. The author recommends retaining emergent vegetation and shallow-water areas when the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] modifies the river.

## Background & Description

- **Typha latifolia** is a perennial emergent plant found in semi-permanent fresh and brackish waters worldwide. It forms dense monospecific stands in water shallower than about 0.2 m, spreads rhizomatically, and is very persistent once established. Its blades commonly reach 3 to 5 m.
- **Najas marina** (spiny naiad) is a submerged vascular plant that dominates summer and fall submerged weedbeds in the study lakes.
- **Fish species** named in the study include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Red Sunfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Common Carp]]. Most sport fish at the reservoir are carnivores.
- **Invertebrates** include [[Asian Clam]] (Corbicula fluminea), [[Chironomid Midge Larvae]], and snails (Physa sp.).
- **Motivation:** Typha had been little studied in relation to fisheries. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] proposed channelization, [[Phreatophyte]] removal, and controlled-flow lakes along the lower river, which could destroy large Typha stands. Typha covers an estimated 60% of the vegetated shoreline in the reservoir.
- **Study area:** Imperial Reservoir was formed in 1938 when Imperial Dam was completed. It covers 7,300 acres (the hectare figure is partly garbled in the scan; roughly 2,950 ha). Its original storage of 35,000 acre-feet has been reduced by [[Siltation]], and it is now used mainly for diversion. Eight representative lakes were studied in the Arizona portion of the reservoir, most under 3 m deep. The reservoir lies in the upper half of the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] and is a major southwestern recreational fishery, averaging more than 0.5 fish per man-hour. Of 208,414 recorded visits to the refuge, about 25% were by fishermen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** The desert area receives about 8 cm (3 in) of rain per year against about 195 cm (77 in) of evaporation. Water levels are controlled by the [[Colorado River]] dam system, with fluctuations of roughly 0.5 m per year. Isolated lakes are highly brackish, with conductivity averaging about 1,500 µmhos/cm, roughly 50% higher than the main river (1,000 to 1,400 µmhos/cm). Alkalinity ranged from 135 to 165 mg/L as CaCO3, and pH was generally 7.5 to 8.0.

**Temperature.** Water temperature peaked at 32.2 °C (90 °F) in August and dropped to about 7 °C (46 °F) in January, following air temperature. No permanent stratification was found, and the lakes are holomictic because of wind. Dense Typha beds shaded and cooled the water by 1 to 1.5 °C at midday in spring.

**Substrate.** Most lake bottoms are silt and detritus, with rock near cliffs and sandbars near lake entrances. Submerged mesquite and ironwood trees remain in most lakes.

**Energy sources.** Typha, Najas, algae, and allochthonous material are the main potential energy sources for the fishery.

**Allochthonous inputs.** Inflowing seston and dissolved organics were small and of minor importance. Seston averaged 0.055 g/m³ in the main river and 0.0025 g/m³ in a minor channel that had passed through Typha, and net seston was about 22 times greater in the river than in the backwaters. Typha beds therefore act as settling traps for river particles.

**Eutrophication and siltation.** Typha beds trap sediment and litter, accelerating [[Eutrophication]] of shallow areas. Within a year a new sandbar can be colonized by Typha, and in the following spring it can build into an island.

## Key Findings

### Primary production

- **Typha blades:** Annual net production averaged 1.80 kg/m² ash-free dry weight (1.84 in 1970 and 1.77 in 1971). Blade turnover averaged 1.12 times the fall standing crop. Late-fall mean standing crop was 1.82 kg/m² dry weight in 1970 and 1.75 kg/m² in 1971. Blade ash content was 9.4%.
- **Najas marina:** Standing biomass in October was 0.43 kg/m² ash-free dry weight. The growing season is about 3 months (mid-June to mid-September), and net production averaged about 105 days of growth. Roughly 25 to 40% of new Typha blades died and fell before October.
- **Submerged community and algae:** Gross primary production averaged 5.53 g O₂/m²/day (±0.77) for the reservoir. Lake 7 averaged 6.30 and lake 8 averaged 4.75 g O₂/m²/day, a difference significant at the 5% level (Student's t). Net production of the entire submerged community was 0.73 kg/m²/year. Algae alone contributed about 0.29 kg/m²/year.
- **Comparison:** Typha production is more than twice that of all other components combined. The author estimates Typha beds produce well over two-thirds of total fixed energy in the backwaters.

### Energy transfer and decomposition

- Typha is not directly eaten by fish. Its energy reaches the food web as detritus and dissolved organics.
- **Detritus distribution:** Most Typha detritus is retained in or within a few meters of the beds. Organic content of sediments fell off sharply with distance from the bed edge. Very little Typha detritus is exported to open water.
- **Decomposition:** Crude fiber in blades in Typha beds dropped from 43% to 32% in one year, compared with 43% to 40% along rocky shores. Lake 7 showed less than 10% fiber loss, and the fastest first-year breakdown was about 25%. Blades were still recognizable after one year. Protein rose from 4.75% to nearly 8% as bacteria colonized the detritus.
- **Dissolved organics:** Extraction tests showed that at least 3.5% of blade material is readily soluble phenolic compounds and at least 4% is soluble carbohydrate. Stagnant conditions (November 22 and December 14, 1969) raised phenolics to about 0.5 mg/L in the beds, while carbohydrates were lower than normal.
- **Consumers:** Midge larvae (Chironomidae) in Typha beds had eaten Typha detritus, while those from open water had not. Clams and snails in litter bags had grown to about twice the mesh size. Stomach contents of five sport fish species from the Typha-open water ecotone commonly contained clams, snails, and midge larvae.
- **Nutritional quality:** Typha blades are nearly half crude fiber and about 4.75% protein, versus about 14% protein in *Najas guadalupensis* according to [[Boyd]] (1970b). Najas breaks down much faster, so it enters detrital chains more readily, and its available food energy is relatively more important than its gross production suggests.

### Fish use of Typha

- **Telemetry:** Three largemouth bass were tracked for 67 hours in summer and fall 1971. Of 63 initial locations, 43% were in or along Typha beds, and 36% of tracking time was spent there. Typha was the preferred habitat, followed by Najas, dead trees, and rocks. Tracking signals were lost when fish entered Typha, so these figures underestimate Typha use.
- **Movement:** Bass remained in restricted areas within one or two lakes, and the greatest distance covered was about 600 m.
- **Juvenile habitat:** Typha is the only major refuge for juvenile bass for 6 to 8 weeks in spring. Within the [[Ecotone]] it serves as the main nursery area for juvenile fish (Weaver, 1971).
- **Spawning:** Largemouth bass spawn in April at about 15.5 °C (60 °F). Other centrarchids spawn in May and June, near 21 °C (70 °F), when Najas appears. A temperature plateau near 18 °C (65 °F) separates the two spawning periods by 6 to 8 weeks, allowing predator and prey populations to remain balanced.
- **Substrate:** Largemouth bass do not spawn on silt. Typha edges supply firm substrate that is absent from the silt-bottomed lake 8.

### Conservation and management implications

- The author concludes that a shallow system with abundant emergent vegetation is more favorable for a sport fishery than steep-sided lakes deeper than 3 m. Lakes averaging 3 m deep were more productive than those averaging 1 m, with gross production of 6.30 and 4.75 g O₂/m²/day.
- Removing Typha and shallow areas to reduce evaporation would cut a large energy source and fish habitat. Typha transpires up to three times more water than open water under similar conditions, but the author argues that its ecological value outweighs this cost.
- Typha beds also support [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (an endangered species), furbearing mammals, and shorebirds, and they strongly contribute to the reservoir's aesthetic value.
