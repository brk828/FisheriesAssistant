# A Key to the British Freshwater Leeches with Notes on their Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hirudinea

## Executive Summary

This is the second edition (1964) of Scientific Publication No. 14 from the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], a dichotomous identification key to the freshwater leeches of the British Isles, written by K. H. Mann with a coloured plate by E. V. Watson. The key covers roughly fifteen species across several families, and it includes notes on identification, cocoon morphology, life histories, ecology, and distribution. The second edition replaces vice-county distribution with maps based on 10 km national grid squares, following the [[Atlas of the British Flora]] format. The document also contains Association front matter (membership, laboratories, publications) and a list of the Association's Scientific Publications.

## Background & Description

The key was first published in 1954. Nomenclature of British leeches had previously relied on [[Harding 1910 Revision]], which used names later altered by Blanchard (1894). The second edition revises the list of accepted names, with a side-by-side comparison against Harding's names. Taxonomic changes include the recognition of *Protoclepsis tessellata* as *Theromyzon tessulatum*, *Herpobdella* corrected to *Erpobdella*, and the addition of *Batracobdella paludosa* and *Trocheta bykowskii* to the British list.

Leeches are described as organisms with an earthworm-like organization modified for parasitism. Key features include:

- **Body:** limited to 34 segments, each subdivided into three annuli (Glossiphoniidae) or five annuli (Hirudidae and Erpobdellidae).
- **Suckers:** anterior sucker formed from the first five segments and posterior sucker from the last seven.
- **Eyes:** variable in number and arrangement (one to five pairs, or eight to ten eyes in some groups), and central to identification.
- **Feeding structures:** a proboscis in Rhynchobdellae; toothed jaws in Hirudidae; muscular ridges without jaws in Erpobdellidae.
- **Reproduction:** median, unpaired male and female pores; the female pore lies posterior to the male pore.

Suborders and families covered in the key:

- **Rhynchobdellae:** Piscicolidae (*Piscicola geometra*) and Glossiphoniidae (*Theromyzon*, *Hemiclepsis*, *Glossiphonia*, *Batracobdella*, *Helobdella*).
- **Arhynchobdellae:** Gnathobdellidae (*Haemopis*, *Hirudo*) and Erpobdellidae (*Erpobdella*, *Dina*, *Trocheta*).

Collection methods favour gentle hand collection. Leeches are wiped off stones or reed bases with a fingertip, which the author found more effective than sweeping, sieving or trapping. Anaesthesia is achieved with soda water (about fifteen minutes) or chloroform for large specimens. Preservation requires gradual relaxation, for example by slowly raising alcohol concentration over about 30 minutes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Ecological notes are based on a quantitative survey in the Lake District and Berkshire (Mann 1955), supplemented by records from correspondents across Britain. Mann notes that recorded distributions are biased by the distribution of collectors.

Key ecological observations:

- **Piscicola geometra:** a fish parasite of cool, well-aerated water, found in fast streams and wave-washed lake shores. Not recorded from Scotland.
- **Theromyzon tessulatum:** a parasite of waterfowl, feeding from the nasal or buccal cavity. Found in all habitats except fast streams.
- **Hemiclepsis marginata:** a fish parasite that probably also attacks tadpoles. Thrives in small standing waters where Piscicola is rare.
- **Glossiphonia heteroclita:** feeds on gastropod molluscs and avoids strong currents. Not found in northern Scotland; no records from Norway, Sweden or Iceland.
- **Glossiphonia complanata:** also feeds on molluscs, is more common in running water, and is recorded from Scotland, Ireland and Iceland.
- **Helobdella stagnalis:** feeds on aquatic invertebrates, especially chironomid larvae. Optimal in productive hard-water lakes, where it is usually the most numerous leech.
- **Haemopis sanguisuga:** a macrophagous carnivore that eats earthworms and other tissues. Fairly common in Lake District shores and widely distributed in Britain.
- **Hirudo medicinalis:** requires mammalian blood as an adult. Harding (1910) considered it extinct in Britain, but it persists in small numbers in undeveloped areas.
- **Erpobdella testacea:** adapted to anaerobic conditions such as dense reed swamps and organically polluted waters. Sparsely distributed and not recorded from Scotland.
- **Erpobdella octoculata:** occurs in all habitat types, is the most numerous leech in running water and soft-water lakes, and is associated with moderate organic pollution.
- **Dina lineata:** in southern England confined to temporary ponds and receding lake margins. In Scotland it occupies the niche of *E. testacea*.
- **Trocheta subviridis:** amphibious, found in damp clayey garden soil, and devours earthworms. First recorded in the London Zoological Gardens in 1850.
- **Trocheta bykowskii:** breeds in moderately fast streams. Its first British record was in 1959; there are now eleven records from England, southern Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Cocoon types separate the families. Glossiphoniidae carry gelatinous cocoons under the parent, with young attaching to its ventral side. Piscicolidae cocoons are firm, shaggy, about 1 mm long, cemented to solid objects in winter. Hirudinidae cocoons are about 10 mm long, laid in summer above the water line. Erpobdellid cocoons are about 3–4 mm (*Erpobdella*), narrower in *Dina*, and 5–8 mm (*Trocheta*).

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** The key covers the British freshwater leeches in the suborders Rhynchobdellae and Arhynchobdellae, with a coloured plate and identification keys by eyes, body colour, genital pores, jaws and annulation.
- **Taxonomy:** The second edition updates nomenclature, correcting the Harding (1910) names and adding *Batracobdella paludosa* and *Trocheta bykowskii*. *Batracobdella verrucata* is included on a single dubious British record.
- **Identification difficulty:** The Glossiphonia/Batracobdella couplet is the most problematic. Glossiphonia has six pairs of crop diverticula and firm, muscular bodies; Batracobdella has seven pairs and a soft body.
- **Life histories:** Mann describes four types, from species completing their life cycle in less than one year (*Helobdella stagnalis*) to species requiring two to three full years (*Trocheta subviridis*, possibly *Haemopis* and *Hirudo*).
- **Adaptive life history:** *Erpobdella testacea* completes its life cycle in one year, which the author links to survival through summer oxygen minima.
- **Host relationships:** Fish parasites include *Piscicola geometra* and *Hemiclepsis marginata*; the bird parasite *Theromyzon tessulatum*; and snail feeders include the two *Glossiphonia* species.
- **Distribution maps:** The 10 km grid maps allow direct comparison with plant distributions in the [[Atlas of the British Flora]], and the maps show recorded occurrences only, not true absence.
- **Research gaps:** Life histories of *Glossiphonia heteroclita*, *Piscicola geometra*, *Hemiclepsis*, *Theromyzon*, *Batracobdella*, *Hirudo* and *Haemopis* remain incompletely studied.
- **Association context:** The document also lists the Freshwater Biological Association's Windermere and River Laboratories, membership terms, and about 50 Scientific Publications, including keys to other aquatic groups (e.g. Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Gastropoda, Cladocera).
