# Effects of Interim Flows from Glen Canyon Dam on Aquatic Resources: Diamond Creek to Lake Mead (Trip 5, May–June 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ColoradoRiver

## Executive Summary
This is the fifth quarterly trip report from a long-term monitoring program assessing the impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] interim flow regimes on aquatic resources in the lower [[Colorado River]], spanning from Diamond Creek (RM 225.7) to the Lake Mead inflow near Pearce Ferry (RM 280). Conducted May 25 – June 6, 1993, the survey combined trammel netting, gill netting, electrofishing, minnow trapping, seining, and snorkeling across five campsites. A total of 1,915 fish across 12 species were captured. Native species ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]]) constituted only ~2% of total catch, while the exotic [[Red Shiner]] dominated at 61%. No [[Humpback Chub]] or [[Razorback Sucker]] were detected. Tributaries, particularly [[Spencer Creek]] and [[Surprise Creek]], showed markedly higher fish densities than the mainstem, and were identified as important spawning and rearing habitat for both native and non-native species. Water quality, productivity, and river-stage monitoring were also conducted to contextualize biological findings within the interim flow experiment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Mainstem habitat gradient**: River conditions varied longitudinally — swift/deep runs near Bridge Canyon, lower-gradient cliff-lined reaches near Spencer Canyon, slow meandering lake-influenced reaches near Burnt Spring Canyon, and lentic, riparian-lined habitat near RM 265 strongly influenced by [[Lake Mead]] backwater effects.
- **Lake Mead inflow zone**: Characterized by inundated riparian vegetation, minimal current, and reduced turbidity due to sediment settling; sampled near [[Scorpion Island]] and Pearce Ferry.
- **Tributary importance**: [[Spencer Creek]] and [[Surprise Creek]] exhibited substantially higher fish densities than the mainstem or other tributaries, attributed to superior habitat quality and (for Spencer Creek) greater flow volume. These tributaries served as key spawning grounds for [[Common Carp]] and [[Red Shiner]].
- **Marginal tributaries**: [[Travertine Creek]] (high gradient, unstable substrate, high bedload, low invertebrate abundance), [[Bridge Canyon Creek]] (low flow, restricted access), and [[Lost Creek]] slough (heavily sedimented, largely dewatered, poor water quality) were assessed as having limited fisheries value.
- **Food web linkage**: Abundant tadpoles of canyon tree frogs and red-spotted toads in Spencer Canyon were identified as a potentially significant food resource for fish, especially ictalurids (e.g., [[Channel Catfish]]).
- **Species distribution shift**: Downstream near RM 265, community composition shifted — [[Green Sunfish]] appeared, and [[Striped Bass]] (originating from Lake Mead) increased in catch rate and size, reflecting lacustrine influence.
- **Reproductive timing**: [[Common Carp]] spawning in Spencer Creek appeared to span early March through late May, based on cross-trip comparison of ripe male/female ratios.

## Key Findings
- **Total catch**: 1,915 fish captured across 12 species (Table 4), dominated by exotic [[Red Shiner]] (1,163 individuals, 61%), [[Common Carp]] (356), [[Channel Catfish]] (168), and [[Western Mosquitofish]] (108).
- **Native species scarcity**: Only [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (27) and [[Speckled Dace]] (13) were native captures, together representing ~2% of total catch. No [[Humpback Chub]] or [[Razorback Sucker]] were found during this or any prior trip.
- **First mainstem rainbow trout**: [[Rainbow Trout]] were sampled in the main channel for the first time this trip (previously only one individual had been captured, in Spencer Creek).
- **Channel catfish increase / fathead minnow decline**: Long-term trend noted of increasing [[Channel Catfish]] abundance in the main channel with concurrent decline in [[Fathead Minnow]] frequency.
- **Tributary population estimate**: A multi-pass depletion electrofishing study was conducted in a 53-m reach of Spencer Creek (block-netted) to derive quantitative population estimates; preliminary results indicated very high fish densities dominated by [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Red Shiner]].
- **Spawning activity**: Large numbers of ripe male [[Common Carp]] but few ripe females in Spencer Creek suggested the spawning season was ending; young-of-year carp were found downstream of Spencer/Surprise confluences. [[Red Shiner]] spawning activity (ripe females, colorful males) was also concentrated in tributaries relative to the mainstem.
- **Lake Mead sampling**: Fewer fish captured than expected, especially centrarchids; [[Common Carp]] were the most abundant species sampled, with trammel nets outperforming gill nets and electrofishing.
- **Gear effectiveness**: Trammel nets and electrofishing were the primary gears (Table 3, 233 total samples); overall, net sets were more effective than electrofishing for adult fish, and trammel nets outperformed gill nets.
- **Water quality**: Standard parameters (temperature, pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen) were logged continuously via Hydrolab instruments in the mainstem, Spencer Creek, and Lost Creek; river temperatures were higher and turbidity lower than the previous trip, consistent with declining flow.
- **Management implications**: Rising [[Lake Mead]] levels (relative to 1992) shifted the inflow zone upstream, requiring adaptive sampling design. Recommendations included increased tributary sampling effort (especially Spencer and Surprise Creeks), reduced mainstem morning netting, additional drift sampling, and flow-related accessibility studies (velocity/depth/wetted perimeter transects) to identify potential fish-passage thresholds into tributaries under interim flow conditions.
