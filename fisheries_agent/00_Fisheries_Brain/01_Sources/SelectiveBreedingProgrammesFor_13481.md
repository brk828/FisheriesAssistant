# Selective Breeding Programmes for Medium-Sized Fish Farms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #cyprinidae #aquaculture-genetics

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Technical Paper (No. 352, 1995, 122 p.), written by Douglas Tave of Urania Unlimited (Coos Bay, Oregon, USA), is a practical manual for [[Extension Agents]] and aquaculturists on designing [[Selective Breeding]] programmes to improve cultured food fish on medium-sized farms, defined arbitrarily as about 2 ha of ponds. It explains basic [[Mendelian Genetics]] and quantitative genetics through [[Tilapia]] and [[Common Carp]] examples, then gives simple programmes that a farmer can run in one to two ponds. The author stresses that selective breeding is open-ended, yields small cumulative gains, and suits only farmers who are good managers, keep records, and plan long term. Management improvements are presented as the cheaper first step.

## Background & Description

The manual is organised into five chapters, a glossary and a recommended reading list:

- **Chapter 1, Introduction:** compares [[Selective Breeding]], [[Crossbreeding]] (hybridization), inbreeding, and newer methods: [[Chromosomal Manipulation]], [[Sex Reversal]] and [[Genetic Engineering]]. Selection is positioned as the method for creating superior brood fish, while crossbreeding targets production fish.
- **Chapter 2, Genetics:** covers [[Meiosis]], phenotype versus genotype, qualitative (Mendelian) and quantitative inheritance, and variance partitioning.
- **Chapter 3, Qualitative Phenotypes:** rules for creating true-breeding populations.
- **Chapter 4, Quantitative Phenotypes:** [[Individual Selection]], [[Family Selection]], cut-off values, selection indices and control populations.
- **Chapter 5, Simple Programmes:** one- or two-pond designs for growth rate, with pond sizing, record keeping and data sheets.

Key genetic concepts defined in the text include [[Heritability]] (h² = Va/Vp), the response to selection (R = S h²), [[Progeny Testing]], [[Independent Culling]] and [[Pleiotropy]]. Examples are drawn from [[Nile Tilapia]] (pink versus normally pigmented body colour, red colour), [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (black, bronze and gold colour under incomplete dominance), and [[Common Carp]] scale pattern (scaled, mirror, line and leather, controlled by S and N genes with dominant epistasis). Other species mentioned include [[Grass Carp]] (triploids legal in most US states only for triploid culture), [[Rainbow Trout]] (golden and palomino colour), [[Channel Catfish]], [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] (market prices of $6.05/kg for dark and $8.25/kg for rosy red fish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The manual treats the pond environment as a major source of phenotypic variance. Environmental influences include stocking density, feed quality and particle size, fertilization, algal blooms, dissolved oxygen, female age and size, spawning date, and pond-to-pond differences. The author warns that uncontrolled environmental differences can make selected fish appear superior when they are not genetically superior, and that an age difference of as little as 24 hours can give a lasting size advantage.

Pigment-dependent colours depend on diet: fish cannot synthesise pigments, so farmers add plant pigments to feed. Pleiotropic effects are highlighted, notably the negative effect of red colour on viability in Nile tilapia, where red fish had lower survival through incubation, sex reversal, fingerling production and grow-out than normally pigmented sibs (data from [[El Gamal 1987]]). Multiple-batch pond production, in which escape-prone fish survive repeated seining, is cited as a scenario where careless selection reduced growth rate in Indian carps.

## Key Findings

**Feasibility and farmer selection**
- Selection exploits heritable phenotypic variance; two prerequisites are variance in the trait and heritability of that variance.
- Medium-sized farm was assumed to be about 2 ha of ponds. Programmes can run in 1 to 150 ponds, and the number of ponds matters more than total farm area.
- Most farmers should not be encouraged to run selection. Suitable candidates are good managers who can keep records, commit ponds, labour and money, and plan 1 to 10 years ahead.
- Gains are small but cumulative, and genetic gain typically reaches production ponds after one generation.

**Qualitative phenotypes**
- Single-gene dominant traits can be fixed in one act of selection when the desired phenotype is homozygous (e.g., culling all normally pigmented Nile tilapia to fix pink).
- Where the desired dominant phenotype includes heterozygotes, only progeny testing fixes the allele. Progeny testing requires at least 20 offspring with no recessive phenotype to declare a homozygote.
- Heterozygous phenotypes cannot be made true-breeding. Bronze Mozambique tilapia can only be produced by mating black with gold fish each season.
- Non-heritable deformities cannot be removed by selection; the author suggests ignoring them unless frequency approaches 0.5%. Other guidance states a frequency under 1 in 250 is probably not a problem, while 1 in 100 to 200 warrants investigation.

**Quantitative phenotypes and heritability**
- Quantitative traits (length, weight, fecundity, feed conversion) are controlled by dozens to hundreds of genes and form continuous distributions.
- Mean length example: 30 fish measured, sum 3,120 mm, mean 104 mm.
- Heritability guidance: h² > 0.25 indicates good gains; h² < 0.15 indicates ineffective individual selection; h² > 0.3 is large.
- Worked example: h² = 0.26, selection differential 16 mm, predicted response 4.16 mm, next-generation mean 150.16 mm (from 146 mm).

**Selection programmes**
- Individual selection is simplest and cheapest. It works best when h² > 0.25, can be done in one or two ponds, and gives the largest select fish.
- Within-family selection suits low heritability or environmental effects felt at family level (such as spawning date or mother's age). It enables asynchronous spawning, and each family can occupy one pond.
- Between-family selection requires 25 to 50 families, each replicated in at least three ponds, so 75 to 150 ponds may be needed. It suits h² < 0.15, or traits requiring killing; its costs and inbreeding risk are high.
- Independent culling uses separate cut-offs for each trait. Selecting top 10% for two traits retains only 1% of fish. Retaining 15% of the population with two equal traits requires top 39% each (√0.15).
- Selection indices are recommended only for research stations or large hatcheries, and require importance factors.

**Practical design and record keeping**
- Target 100 to 200 select brood fish each generation, with at least 25 males and 25 females to limit inbreeding.
- Cut-offs are set from a random sample of 100 to 200 fish, with the phenotypic value at the desired percentile as the cut-off. Example: 250 fish sampled, top 20% cut-off at 183 mm.
- Single-pond ideas: stock fry in one pond and select on harvest, or use two-phase selection at fingerling and food-fish stages, with cut-offs of 35 to 50% and 10 to 20%.
- Pond size example: target 200 select brood fish, yielding a fingerling pond of 0.045 ha and a food-fish pond of 0.318 ha.
- Control populations are essential to separate genetic from environmental gain. Table 12 example: P0 mean 150 mm, F1 select 165 mm, F1 control 159 mm, giving environmental gain 9 mm, genetic gain 6 mm, or 3.8% per generation.
- Abandoning selection loses much of the gain, so programmes should be continued long term.
- Management improvements (fertilization, liming, density, feeding) should be applied first, since their effects are immediate. Selection should not begin if culture conditions are expected to change.
- Growth should be measured as length for practicality, with standard length preferred over total length, and selection on weight only when accurate batch weighing is possible.

**Concluding recommendations**
- Medium-sized farms should usually select for growth rate alone, or add at most one or two further traits.
- Tandem selection should not be used. Two traits suit independent or modified independent culling.
- Record keeping (spawning data, stocking, cut-offs, harvest measurements, control population) is central and is considered the least appreciated but most necessary element.
