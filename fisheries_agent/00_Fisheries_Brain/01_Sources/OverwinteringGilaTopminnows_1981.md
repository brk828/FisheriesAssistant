## Executive Summary

This 1981 husbandry report from Dexter National Fish Hatchery documents an applied conservation aquaculture study on overwintering the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), a warm-spring-adapted desert livebearer being propagated for restocking of threatened/endangered wild populations. Because the species evolved in thermally stable warm springs and lacks tolerance to cold seasonal fluctuations, hatchery managers observed severe winter die-off ("winter kill") each year, leaving only small, immature survivors that delayed spring reproduction and reduced the size/quality of fish available for stocking. The study compared three overwintering strategies — an indoor flow-through holding tank, a small flow-through pond, and a large semi-static rearing pond — to identify methods that minimize mortality and preserve larger, reproductively mature brood fish going into the spring spawning season.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis* — a small-bodied, livebearing [[Poeciliidae|poeciliid]] fish.
- **Size range at collection**: Juveniles/subadults up to ~1 inch total length were seined from the production pond in autumn.
- **Growth pattern**: Fish held over winter grew at a **slow rate**; by March, **well over 50% were sexually mature** in the best-performing (tank) treatment.
- **Condition indicator**: Survivors of natural winter kill in outdoor ponds were consistently the **smallest individuals**, suggesting size- or condition-dependent cold tolerance, with larger/older fish disproportionately succumbing to low temperatures.
- No detailed meristic, fin-ray, or coloration data were reported; the paper is a husbandry/management study rather than a taxonomic description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native habitat**: Warm, thermally stable desert springs within the Gila River drainage (Arizona/Sonora), characterized by minimal seasonal temperature fluctuation — a key driver of the species' poor cold tolerance.
- **Thermal physiology**: As a species that evolved and lives in warm springs and is never subjected to cold temperatures, *P. o. occidentalis* shows strong behavioral thermoregulation — actively seeking warm inflow water sources rather than tolerating ambient cold, and becoming inactive/hidden when water temperatures drop into the 40°F range.
- **Behavioral response to cold**: In flow-through systems, fish congregated near warm water inlets (well water at ~62–71°F) throughout winter and remained active; in the large semi-static pond without a strong warm-water source, fish became inactive and largely disappeared from observation below ~40°F, correlating with heavy mortality.
- **Parasite ecology**: Only incidental *[[Trichodina]]* sp. infestations were observed in the tank-held population; no significant parasite buildup occurred despite dense, low-flow holding conditions.
- **Conservation role**: The species is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as part of a captive propagation and restocking program for desert rare, threatened, and endangered fishes — situating this study within broader [[Desert Fish Conservation]] and endangered species recovery efforts in the southwestern United States.

## Key Findings

**Study design — three overwintering methods compared:**

1. **Small flow-through pond (0.1 surface acre, 2.5 ft deep)**
   - Stocked with 2,700 topminnows (up to 1 inch) on Sept. 29 at 64°F water temp.
   - Maintained with well water (62–71°F) at 30–50 gpm through winter.
   - Harvested early March; held in holding tank until production pond warmed above 60°F.

2. **Indoor holding tank (9' × 2.5' × 2')**
   - 4,750 fish removed from large rearing pond when pond cooled into low 60s°F.
   - Subset used for bioassays; **2,450 fish retained for overwintering**.
   - Fed No. 1 trout starter granule; low flow (~5 gpm) of well water (62–71°F).
   - Restocked into spawning-rearing pond March 16 (>60°F).
   - **Only 4 mortalities over 5 months** — best survival outcome.
   - Fish began feeding by day 2; no parasite buildup beyond occasional *Trichodina*.
   - Over 50% reached maturity by March.

3. **Large rearing pond (1.0 surface acre), minimal water exchange**
   - Fish density tracked qualitatively rather than exact counts.
   - Fish congregated near inflow until temps dropped into the 40s°F, then became largely unobservable.
   - **Estimated ≥50% population loss**, despite an unusually mild winter — demonstrating high inherent cold vulnerability of the species.

**Combined pond/tank harvest results:**
- Flow-through pond fish + tank-held bioassay fish were combined (4,656 total stocked into rearing pond) after a 2-week holding period.
- Only 494 fish unaccounted for (~10.6%), attributed mainly to loss in heavy filamentous algae during pond harvesting rather than true mortality.

**Conclusions:**
- Tank holding method was superior for survival and fish quality, using substantially less water, though requiring more daily labor (feeding, cleaning) and carrying higher risk from potential power/pump failure.
- Flow-through pond method was adequate but water-intensive; harvesting losses occurred due to heavy filamentous algae and rooted aquatic weeds.
- Best management practice for *P. occidentalis* is to **overwinter adults in temperature-controlled/regulated systems** rather than relying on ambient outdoor pond conditions, which produced substantial uncontrolled losses even under mild winter conditions.

**Management implication**: Controlled overwintering (especially tank-based) enables earlier spring spawning, larger and higher-quality brood/stocking fish, and directly supports the hatchery's production goal of **20,000 Gila Topminnows in 1981** for reintroduction/restocking efforts tied to [[Endangered Species Recovery]] programs.
