# Physical and Chemical Characteristics of the Hyporheic Zone of a Sonoran Desert Stream

#stream-ecology #limnology #hyporheic-zone

## Executive Summary

This 1990 study by [[H. Maurice Valett]], [[Stuart G. Fisher]], and [[Emily H. Stanley]] (Arizona State University, *Journal of the North American Benthological Society* 9(3):201-215) describes the physical and chemical conditions of the [[Hyporheic Zone]] in three sandy runs of [[Sycamore Creek]], an intermittent desert stream in [[Arizona]]. The authors found that the hyporheic zone holds more water than the surface channel, that its porosity is high, and that its organic matter is low and patchy. Interstitial water is enriched in inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus relative to surface water, and interstitial oxygen is strongly shaped by whether water is moving downward (downwelling) or upward (upwelling). The authors conclude that surface and hyporheic subsystems are tightly linked and should be studied together. The document contains no fish data; fish appear only in the literature review.

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The paper addresses two objectives: (1) to describe the physical and chemical conditions of the hyporheic zone of Sycamore Creek, and (2) to characterize the regions where the hyporheic zone and surface stream are linked. The authors define the hyporheic zone as the saturated sediments and interstitial spaces of alluvium beneath and lateral to the wetted perimeter where surface and subsurface water are actively exchanged. The definition is based on water flow, not on the distribution of interstitial biota.

**Literature context.** The authors note that hyporheic zones have been studied mainly as [[Zoobenthos]] habitat (e.g., [[Coleman and Hynes]] 1970; [[Stanford and Ward]] 1988) and as sites for fish reproduction. Cited fish-related work includes [[Pollard]] (1955), [[Hansen]] (1975) on [[Brown Trout]] redds, and [[Johnson]] (1980) on [[Salmon]] spawning gravels. Earlier work had shown that hyporheic water can be rich in dissolved organic carbon and nutrients, and that solutes are retained in hyporheic sediments ([[Bencala]] 1984).

**Study setting.** Sycamore Creek drains a 505 km² watershed (elevation 2164 to 427 m) and flows about 55 km from ephemeral headwaters to the [[Verde River]]. Its channel is "underfit," with wetted perimeters occupying only part of the channel, and the sediments are frequently reworked by [[Flash Floods]]. Within a 12 km transitional reach, runs make up 45% of stream length, riffles are about as abundant, and pools cover 10%. Three runs at about 650 m elevation were studied (Sites I, II, III).

**Methods (summary).**
- Morphometry was mapped along transects spaced 20 m apart, with sand depth measured by sounding.
- Sediment cores (72.5 cm³ plexiglass) estimated porosity, and sieving sorted sediment into four size classes (small <0.5 mm; medium 0.5-5.0 mm; large 5.0-9.5 mm; extra large >9.5 mm).
- Piezometer-type PVC wells sampled interstitial water from depths of 10-135 cm. Temperature, dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), ammonium (phenol-hypochlorite), soluble reactive phosphorus (molybdate-antimony), and nitrite/nitrate (cadmium reduction) were measured.
- Fluorescent dye injections identified upwelling and downwelling points and measured interstitial velocity.
- Organic matter was measured by [[Dichromate Oxidation]] in shallow cores and in deep pits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and exchange.** Surface flow in Sycamore Creek is intermittent and varies with season and storm intensity, but low discharge is typical for most of the year. The hyporheic zone therefore makes up a large share of the aquatic habitat. Exchange is patchy: Sites I and III were downwelling (recharge) zones, and Site II was an upwelling (discharge) zone. Flash floods scour and redeposit sand, so the volume and location of the hyporheic zone shift over time, which affects whole-stream metabolism ([[Grimm and Fisher]] 1984; [[Fisher et al.]] 1982).

**Thermal regime.** Sand surfaces reached about 50 °C in summer. Evaporative cooling is high in the dry Sonoran air but is reduced in the interstitial environment. Heat moves into lateral hyporheic zones by conduction. Hyporheic temperatures were higher than surface temperatures in summer, and the authors contrast this with northern temperate streams, where subsurface water is warmer mainly in winter ([[White et al.]] 1987).

**Nutrients and primary production.** Surface autotrophs in Sycamore Creek are nitrogen-limited ([[Grimm and Fisher]] 1986). Upwelling hyporheic water may relieve this limitation, and longitudinal nitrogen gradients created by algal uptake can shape surface algal communities. During dry months, hyporheic water emerges at the upstream ends of isolated surface segments.

**Organic matter and metabolism.** The hyporheic zone was previously shown to be metabolically important: apparently autotrophic reaches were heterotrophic once subsurface respiration was included ([[Grimm and Fisher]] 1984). Organic matter storage in deep sediments may act as a reservoir that affects oxygen demand, nutrient cycling, and stream metabolism.

## Key Findings

**1. Structure and porosity**
- The hyporheic zone of three runs averaged 63 cm in depth and consisted mainly of coarse sand or fine gravel (0.5-5.0 mm).
- Porosity was high at 19.3-23.3%.
- Study reaches held over 1000 m³ of sediment (Table 1 sediment volumes: 1194.5-1772.6 m³ per site).
- Interstitial water volume was 3-4 times surface water volume (the Results state "more than three times"; the Discussion states "nearly four times").

**2. Hydrologic exchange**
- Mean subsurface velocity was 0.62 mm/s (SE = 0.11, n = 16), ranging from 0.035 to 1.25 mm/s.
- Velocity was highest beneath the wetted perimeter and declined laterally.
- Surface water flux accounted for 95%, 96%, and 98% of total discharge at Sites I, II, and III.
- Estimated residence time of hyporheic water was 42-110 hours from velocity data but 3-8 hours from oxygen demand. The authors attribute the difference to incomplete mixing.
- Surface water residence time was 30-60 minutes at summer low flow and 2-5 minutes at winter high flows.

**3. Organic matter**
- Sediment organic matter averaged 0.08% by weight and declined with depth in shallow sediments.
- Small particles (<0.5 mm) made up under 3% of sediment mass but held about 25% of total organic matter. Medium particles held about 75%.
- Total benthic organic matter at the three sites was 5.7, 2.9, and 2.8 kg/m².
- Hyporheic organic matter averaged 0.67 kg/m² (n = 69, SE = 0.030) in the top 40 cm. The authors estimate that hyporheic sediments store more than three times the organic matter found on the benthic surface.
- Deep sediments showed non-monotonic profiles that the authors link to flood-driven sorting.

**4. Temperature**
- Temperature ranges were 8.5 °C (Site I), 9.0 °C (Site II), and 1.5 °C (Site III).
- Maximum interstitial temperature was 32.0 °C at Site II, recorded lateral to the channel, and temperature rose with depth beneath the wetted perimeter at all sites.

**5. Dissolved oxygen**
- Oxygen was high in downwelling zones and reduced in upwelling zones, with a complex pattern overall.
- Anoxic water was rare (2 of 105 samples). Low oxygen (<1 ppm) occurred in 64 of 105 samples.
- Regions below 0.5 mg/L made up 31% of cross-sectional area at Site I, 2% at Site II, and 51% at Site III.
- Oxygen increased with depth at Site II (r = 0.74, p < 0.0001), producing an inverted profile. It decreased with depth at Site III (r = 0.66, p < 0.0005).
- Biological oxygen demand averaged 0.753 g O₂ m⁻² hr⁻¹ (3.49 mg O₂/L·hr), so water entering with 10 mg/L oxygen would become anoxic within about three hours. Oxygen therefore depends on replenishment by hydrologic exchange.

**6. Nutrients**
- Hyporheic water was enriched relative to surface water: NH₄-N 269% (abstract; 268% in Results), NO₃-N 327%, and SRP 174% of surface values (n = 27 wells).
- Coefficients of variation were 204% for NH₄-N, 129% for NO₃-N, and under 60% for SRP.
- Surface total inorganic nitrogen (TIN) was highest at Site II (72 µg/L), where the upwelling zone also showed high surface N.
- At Site II, NO₃-N in the hyporheic zone averaged 240 µg/L (about six times surface concentration). Nitrate exceeded 500 µg/L in two of eight wells.
- Mean N:P atomic ratio was 4.50 in surface water and 8.97 in hyporheic water. Six of 27 wells exceeded 15, suggesting possible phosphorus limitation (Redfield 1958).

**7. Comparison with other streams**
- Hyporheic NH₄-N in three New Mexico headwater streams (205 µg/L) was much higher than in Sycamore Creek (72 µg/L). Hyporheic NO₃-N was similar in both (108 vs. 109 µg/L).
- The authors link the high NH₄-N in New Mexico to retentive geomorphology and widespread anaerobic conditions ([[Carr]] 1989).

**8. Conclusions**
- The hyporheic zone of Sycamore Creek is a dynamic component of the stream ecosystem. Under low surface flow, most stream water resides there, and during dry periods all water is subsurface.
- Flash floods, scouring, and redeposition drive much of the variation in sediment organic matter and oxygen.
- Surface and hyporheic subsystems are closely linked, so both must be considered in understanding stream ecosystem dynamics.
