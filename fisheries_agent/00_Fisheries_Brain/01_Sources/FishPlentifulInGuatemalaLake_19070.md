# Fish Population Assessment and Fisheries Development in Lake Atitlan, Guatemala

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Centrarchidae #fisheries-management #community-development

## Executive Summary

Two Oklahoma State University zoology professors, Dr. Troy C. Dorris and Dr. Robert C. Summerfelt, conducted a three-week study of fish populations in [[Lake Atitlan]], [[Guatemala]], in 1967 to address perceived fish shortages affecting the community of [[Santiago Atitlan]] (population 14,000). The investigation revealed that fish were abundant in the lake, but native fishermen were catching only 2-3 pounds per day due to primitive harvesting methods. The study demonstrated that modern fishing techniques, particularly [[Electrofishing]] and artificial lures, could dramatically increase harvest rates to 100-150 pounds per night. The researchers recommended introducing herbivorous species, stocking [[Bluegill Sunfish]], and establishing breeding enclosures for [[Tilapia]] to balance the lake's fish community structure.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Atitlan]] is a scenic, clear-water lake in Guatemala characterized by very blue water with high visibility. The lake had been stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in 1956. At the time of the study, the fishing industry was vital to Santiago Atitlan's economy, though fishermen were earning less than 50 cents per day from their catches. Native commercial fishermen used primitive methods consisting of worms for bait on 30-40 foot lines.

The study was sponsored by Ed Leroux and A. F. Boudreau of Tulsa and had two primary objectives: (1) determine if modern fish-harvesting methods could improve harvest of existing fish populations, and (2) determine if the fish population could be enlarged through management interventions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The OSU team found that [[Lake Atitlan]] supported abundant populations of carnivorous fish species, including [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Crappie]] (*Pomoxis* sp.). The largest bass caught by the research team weighed 5.5 pounds, though a spear fisherman caught a 10-pound specimen during their visit. The lake's productivity was described as "far greater than most lakes in the United States."

However, the fish community structure was imbalanced, with insufficient herbivorous species. [[Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis* sp.), a herbivorous cichlid already present in the lake, was experiencing reproductive suppression due to heavy predation from bass and crappie. The deeper areas of the lake were practically devoid of fish, suggesting potential for stocking cold-water species such as trout or whitefish.

The water clarity, while aesthetically appealing, posed challenges for daytime [[Electrofishing]] operations, as fish could easily detect the electrical rig. This necessitated nighttime fishing operations for optimal results.

## Key Findings

**Fish Population Status:**
- Fish populations in [[Lake Atitlan]] were abundant, contrary to local assumptions
- The perceived shortage was due to ineffective harvesting methods rather than actual fish scarcity
- [[Largemouth Bass]] introduced in 1956 had not depleted smaller fish as feared
- Bass and [[Crappie]] were present in large quantities throughout the lake

**Fishing Method Improvements:**
- Native fishermen using artificial shallow-running lures doubled or tripled their harvest compared to traditional worm-and-line methods
- [[Electrofishing]] demonstrated potential yields of 100-150 pounds per night with a three-man team
- Nighttime electrofishing was recommended due to water clarity making daytime operations detectable to fish
- Fish harvested through improved methods could be sold in Guatemala City (three hours away) for 30 cents per pound

**Management Recommendations:**
- Stock [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) to provide forage for bass and easy catches for native hook-and-line anglers
- Establish breeding enclosures for [[Tilapia]] to increase herbivore populations currently suppressed by bass and crappie predation
- Introduce trout or whitefish species to utilize deeper lake areas currently lacking fish populations
- Implement chemical fishing in shallow waters to facilitate bass harvest
- Develop a laboratory for continuing research and development programs

**Economic and Social Impact:**
- An agricultural cooperative in Santiago Atitlan purchased a generator to begin experimental [[Electrofishing]] operations
- Improved fishing methods could significantly increase fishermen's income from less than 50 cents per day
- Enhanced fish harvest would add protein to local diets
- The Guatemalan Department of Renewable Natural Resources expressed vital interest in developing [[Lake Atitlan]]

**Tourism Potential:**
- [[Lake Atitlan]] was identified as having unlimited potential as a fishing resort
- The north side featured resort areas with good housing accommodations and fine food
- The lake's beauty, blue clear water, and abundant fish populations made it attractive for sport fishing
