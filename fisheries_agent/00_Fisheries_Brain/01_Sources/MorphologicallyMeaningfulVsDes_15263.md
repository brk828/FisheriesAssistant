# Morphologically Meaningful vs. Descriptive Terminologies for Use by Taxonomists, with Comments on Interordinal Homologies of Male Genitalia

#entomology #morphology #taxonomy #insect-genitalia

## Executive Summary

This paper, by [[Charles D. Michener]] of the University of Kansas, was published in the Proceedings of the Tenth International Congress of Entomology (1956, vol. 1, pp. 583–588). It argues that taxonomists should adopt a uniform, morphologically meaningful terminology for insect structures wherever homologies are reasonably certain. It also warns that adopting such terms prematurely, before homologies are established, can cause instability. The paper's main example is the insect [[Ovipositor]] and [[Male Genitalia]], with particular attention to the [[Hymenoptera]]. The author concludes that noncommittal terms of broad application are preferable to terms built on uncertain homologies. The document also includes a short discussion section with comments from G. F. Ferris, G. Steyskal, and J. Smart.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** A discussion paper on the theory and practice of insect morphological terminology. No new specimen data are presented.
- **Central problem:** Entomologists need a shared vocabulary so they can understand one another. In practice, each insect group has developed its own system, often with the same term applied to non-homologous structures.
- **Two positions:**
  - *Descriptive or special terminologies* avoid change and need no homology analysis, but they confuse workers who move between groups.
  - *Morphologically meaningful terminologies* signal homologies across taxa and encourage broadly trained taxonomists, but they change as interpretations shift.
- **Wing venation precedent:** The author cites the uniform wing vein and cell terminology advocated by [[Comstock and Needham]] as a successful example of broad adoption.
- **Advantages of meaningful terms (as listed by the author):**
  - A. They make it easier for taxonomists to move between insect groups.
  - B. They encourage specialists to follow entomology as a whole.
  - C. They reduce the need to learn different names for comparable structures in unrelated groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This is a theoretical and methodological paper. It does not address habitat, ecology, or geographic distribution. Its context is the taxonomic and morphological study of insects, including:

- **Female ovipositor:** Standard works describe it as built from appendages of abdominal segments 8 and 9. Terms such as [[Valvifer]] and [[Valvula]] are applied across [[Thysanura]], [[Odonata]], and [[Hymenoptera]], so they serve the whole class even though their origin is debated.
- **Male genitalia:** Two views exist. One holds that these structures are homologous and of appendicular origin. The other holds that some orders derive them from primary phallic lobes. The author favors homologizing them across orders, noting that a two-segmented clasper, penis valves, and penis are unlikely to have arisen independently.
- **Homology debates:** Competing hypotheses, including [[Matsuda (1957)]]'s proposal that valvifers and valvulae are sternal rather than appendicular in origin, show why stable naming needs caution.

## Key Findings

1. **Terminology should balance usefulness and stability.** The author proposes two criteria: (1) usefulness across a wide range of systematic categories, meaning homologous structures share names, and (2) a high degree of stability. These criteria often conflict when homologies are uncertain.
2. **Use narrower names where homology is uncertain.** Names should be narrowed according to the probable accuracy of homologies, not according to taxonomic groupings. For example, the ovipositor terms *valvifer* and *valvula* are retained because their homology with walking-leg parts is not certain.
3. **Hymenopteran male genitalia terminology was premature.** The author's 1944 terminology, which used terms such as [[Gonocoxite]] and gonostylus to imply homology with the female second valvifer, is now judged premature. Interordinal homologies are not certainly established, and valvifers may be sternal rather than coxal in origin.
4. **Recommended approach.** Noncommittal terms of broad application, such as *valvifer* or "first segment of clasper" (basal) and "apical segment," are preferred over broader terms based on questionable homologies. Special terminologies may be kept until interordinal homologies are better understood.
5. **Historical note on Hymenoptera.** [[Snodgrass (1941)]] established a single terminology for male genitalic parts across the order, excluding the genus *Apis*, which presents major difficulties. The author notes that the word "paramere" has been applied loosely to nearly every paired male structure, which he calls unnecessary and confusing.
6. **Discussion comments.**
   - G. F. Ferris noted that morphology is known for perhaps fewer than 500 of roughly 10 million insect species, so it is too early for a fixed general terminology.
   - G. Steyskal observed that wing vein terminology remains open to serious question, especially for median veins in Diptera and reduced venation in some groups.
   - J. Smart raised the question of priority for morphological terms. Michener replied that he did not intend to propose a priority system.
