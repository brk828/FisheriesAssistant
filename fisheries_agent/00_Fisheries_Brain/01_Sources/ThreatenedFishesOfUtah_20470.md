# Threatened Fishes of Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 report, published in the *Proceedings of the Utah Academy of Sciences, Arts, and Letters* and prepared by the [[Utah Fishes Committee]] of the [[Bonneville Chapter]], catalogs the endangered, threatened and indeterminate-status fishes of Utah. It applies the definitions of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 and assesses each species by description, documentation of range and abundance, factors of decline, recent studies and recommendations. The report covers 13 fishes, about 40% of Utah's native fish fauna, and concludes that Utah's aquatic environments have been greatly altered, largely by human water use.

## Background & Description

The authors state that fish status must be judged at the scale of river systems rather than political boundaries, so a species rare only within Utah is treated as an endangered population rather than an endangered species. Each species account follows five categories: description, documentation, factors influencing decline, recent studies and recommendations. The report notes that cutthroat trout subspecies are difficult to tell apart, and that [[Robert Behnke]] of Colorado State University was the authority for their identification.

The species are grouped by drainage:

- **Cutthroat trout** (*Salmo clarki* subspecies): Colorado, Utah and Snake Valley forms.
- **[[Colorado River Fishes]]**: squawfish, bonytail chub, humpback chub and humpback sucker.
- **[[Virgin River Fishes]]**: Virgin River chub, woundfin and Virgin River spinedace.
- **[[Bonneville Basin Fishes]]**: least chub, June sucker and Webug sucker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many of these fishes are highly specialized and endemic to small areas or portions of larger drainages. Their specialization makes them less adaptable to altered conditions. Generalist and introduced species, such as [[Channel Catfish]], redside shiner and largemouth bass, often thrive in disturbed habitats where specialists decline, adding competitive pressure.

Major decimating factors across the species include:

- **Dam and reservoir construction**, which altered river flow and temperature and reduced habitat (e.g., [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]]).
- **Water diversion and irrigation demands**, which reduced habitat in the Gila drainage, Colorado River and Virgin River.
- **Hybridization** with introduced trout, notably [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].
- **Competition and predation** from introduced fishes.
- **Drought and winter kill**, implicated in the decline of June sucker in [[Utah Lake]].

The Colorado and Virgin River systems are described as "endangered" environments, with the four Colorado basin species making up 57% of the large-river species native to that drainage and the three Virgin River fishes making up 50% of the native fish fauna there. The authors note that the Bonneville basin, which contains the least chub, is poorly understood.

## Key Findings

**Status by species** (see the report's Table 1 for comparison with other agencies):

- **Endangered:** Colorado cutthroat trout, Utah cutthroat trout, Snake Valley cutthroat trout, Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, humpback chub, Virgin River chub, woundfin, least chub, humpback chub, humpback sucker.
- **Threatened:** Virgin River spinedace.
- **Indeterminate:** June sucker and Webug sucker, which are confused taxonomically.

**Cutthroat trout**
- Colorado cutthroat trout is known in Utah only from the Little West Fork of the Blacks Fork River, Summit County, plus three Wyoming and three Colorado streams.
- Utah cutthroat trout is known from Water Canyon and a tributary of Reservoir Canyon (Washington County), an isolated tributary of Little Cottonwood Creek, Birch Creek near Beaver, and Thomas Fork of Bear River, Wyoming.
- Snake Valley cutthroat trout was recently recognized as a separate subspecies; a population was identified in the Deep Creek Range headwaters of Trout Creek, Utah.

**Colorado River fishes**
- Colorado squawfish is "presently the stronghold" in Utah, with the most consistent reproduction on the Green River below Ouray, including Desolation Canyon and Canyonlands area.
- Bonytail chub, once common, was found by Holden (1973) to be very rare, with no confirmed recent reproduction; the report considers it the most endangered Colorado basin species.
- Humpback chub persists in Desolation Canyon, where reproduction is likely, and is declining in Lake Powell.
- Humpback sucker: Holden (1973) found only 53 individuals in four summers of upper-basin collecting. Over 3,500 juveniles and 6,000–7,000 squawfish were reported hatched at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery.

**Virgin River fishes**
- Virgin River chub was abundant in Utah in the late 1960s, but Cross and Deacon (1973) did not find it above the mouth of Beaver Dam Wash, Arizona, or in Utah except at La Verkin Spring. Causes of the decline are unknown.
- Woundfin is now found only in the Virgin River below La Verkin Spring, and its Utah populations showed no appreciable historic decrease.
- Virgin River spinedace declined sharply after introduced redside shiners increased and largemouth bass moved upstream from Gunlock Reservoir.

**Bonneville basin fishes**
- Least chub was originally common in lowland streams, springs and ponds, but is now found in only a few springs in Snake Valley, and loss of habitat through water diversion is the main cause of decline.
- June and Webug sucker status remains unresolved. Tanner (1936) believed June sucker extinct after drought and 1934–35 winter kill, but recent Chasmistes-like fish from Utah Lake appear to be hybrids of Utah and June suckers.

**Recommendations**
- Conduct thorough searches for the three cutthroat subspecies, especially on the south slope of the Uinta Range, where oil shale development may threaten the Colorado form.
- Monitor Utah populations of the four Colorado River species, prioritizing the Desolation Canyon area and the Green River spawning sites.
- Support research on population status before life history studies, using this priority order: (1) least chub distribution and abundance; (2) bonytail chub population status and reproduction; (3) cause of Virgin River chub decline; (4) taxonomic analysis of Utah Lake suckers.
- Avoid further alteration of the Colorado and Virgin River systems, and halt the spread of introduced species in the Virgin River.
- Consider proposed dam construction and blockage of La Verkin Spring, which could endanger the remaining native fish.

**Recent studies cited**
- [[Holden]] (1973) documented distribution and abundance of the four Colorado River species.
- Cross and Deacon (1973) and Deacon and Minckley (1973) studied the Virgin River fishes.
- Current work includes Stalnaker and Klar's biochemical genetics of cutthroat trout, the Woundfin Recovery Team and BLM habitat surveys.
