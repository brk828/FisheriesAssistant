# Conservation Status of the North American Fish Fauna in Fresh Water

## Executive Summary
This synthesis by Williams & Miller (1990) assesses the conservation status of the entire North American freshwater fish fauna (Canada, the United States including Alaska, and Mexico north of the Isthmus, per the range of [[Cyprinidae]]). Of **1033 native species in 51 families**, **292 species (28%)** fall into IUCN threat categories: 74 Endangered (7%), 85 Vulnerable (8%), 101 Rare (10%), 5 Indeterminate (<1%), and 27 Extinct (3%). The paper identifies habitat degradation from dams and water diversion, introduction of non-native species, and increasing aridity in the western U.S.A. and northern Mexico as primary drivers of decline. The U.S. [[Endangered Species Act]] is highlighted as the most effective — though still insufficient — conservation tool in the region, with Canada and Mexico lacking comparable legislative protections.

## Background & Description
This is a faunal status/conservation review rather than a morphological study; no anatomical descriptions of individual species are provided. However, taxonomic diversity spans major freshwater fish lineages, including:
- **[[Cyprinidae]]** (minnows) — 272 species, the most diverse family, with 75 (28%) listed as threatened/extinct.
- **[[Catostomidae]]** (suckers) — 68 species, 24 (35%) listed.
- **[[Ictaluridae]]** (catfishes) — 47 species, 19 (40%) listed.
- **[[Salmonidae]]** (salmonids) — 47 species, 17 (36%) listed, including four extinct [[Coregonus]] species.
- **[[Cyprinodontidae]]** (pupfishes) — 65 species, 33 (51%) listed — among the highest proportional threat levels.
- **[[Goodeidae]]** (goodeids, Mexican livebearers) — 38 species, 11 (29%) listed.
- **[[Poeciliidae]]** (livebearers) — 63 species, 20 (32%) listed.
- **[[Percidae]]** (darters and perches) — 146 species, 49 (30%) listed.
- Notably robust/unthreatened families with 0% listed species include [[Lepisosteidae]], [[Amiidae]], [[Clupeidae]], [[Esocidae]], and several marine-affiliated families (e.g. [[Carangidae]], [[Haemulidae]]).
- Highest percentage-threatened families: [[Polyodontidae]] (100%, 1 species Rare), [[Rivulidae]] (100%, 1 species Rare), [[Acipenseridae]] (63%), [[Cyprinodontidae]] (51%), [[Amblyopsidae]] (50%), [[Characidae]] (50%).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Geographic distribution**: Alaska and Canada hold only 19% of the North American fish fauna despite covering over half the continental landmass; the Mississippi River basin harbors the bulk of cyprinid, catostomid, ictalurid, and centrarchid diversity due to its geologic stability.
- **Western aridland fishes**: Western U.S.A. and northern Mexico host a disproportionately high number of narrowly endemic species that have persisted through volcanism, mountain building, and long-term aridification (see [[Miller & Smith 1986]]).
- **Threat drivers**:
  - Dam construction and water diversion favored over free-flowing river maintenance (western U.S. water policy).
  - Non-native species introductions causing severe native fish declines in [[Florida]], western U.S.A., and Mexico.
  - Entire ecosystems (e.g., [[Colorado River]] and [[Rio Grande]] systems) considered endangered due to cumulative habitat alteration.
- **Refugia of intact native fish communities**:
  - **[[Aravaipa Creek]]** (Gila River system, southwestern U.S.A./northwestern Mexico) — retains seven native fish species, including two federally threatened.
  - **[[La Media Luna]]** spring/lagoon complex (Rio Verde system, central Mexico) — supports six rare fish species and three endemic crustaceans, including [[Cichlasoma minckleyi]].
- **Human-caused extinctions**: The first documented anthropogenic fish extinctions in North America were the whiteline topminnow (*[[Fundulus albolineatus]]*) and Parras characodon (*[[Characodon garmani]]*), both extirpated around 1900. Extinction rate has increased dramatically since 1950 (27 species + 13 subspecies extinct per Miller et al. 1989).

## Key Findings
1. **1033 native freshwater species** in **51 families** occur in North America; **292 (28%)** are classified as Endangered, Vulnerable, Rare, Indeterminate, or Extinct under IUCN criteria.
2. Breakdown of at-risk categories: 74 Endangered (7%), 85 Vulnerable (8%), 101 Rare (10%), 5 Indeterminate (<1%), 27 Extinct (3%).
3. The number of North American fish species/subspecies requiring special management increased **45% over the preceding 10 years** (per Williams et al., 1989).
4. Of 102 U.S. species classified as Endangered or Vulnerable, only **54 (53%)** are formally protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] — indicating a significant protection gap.
5. In the past decade, only **seven fish species** previously listed as endangered/threatened in the U.S. improved sufficiently to change status — underscoring limited recovery success despite legal protections.
6. **Canada** and **Mexico** lack equivalent species-protection legislation; Canada relies on the non-binding [[COSEWIC]] status reporting system, and Mexico's most complete rare-species list stems from Williams et al. (1989), with a fuller monograph in preparation.
7. Conservation recommendations include: expanded natural history surveys (especially Canada/Mexico), genetics-informed reintroduction strategies (e.g., the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] case demonstrating failure from low genetic variability in source populations, per Vrijenhoek et al. 1985), increased funding/staffing for listing and recovery programs, new legislation in Canada/Mexico, protection of intact refugial habitats, and broader human population/resource-consumption policy reform.
8. The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 is characterized as the most powerful available conservation tool and a model globally, but is described as providing only "temporary respite" rather than durable solutions, given the ever-growing list of protected species and rare recoveries.
