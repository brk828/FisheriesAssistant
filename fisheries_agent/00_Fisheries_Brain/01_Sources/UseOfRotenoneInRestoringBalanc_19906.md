# Use of Rotenone in Restoring Balance to Overcrowded Fish Populations in Alabama Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates [[Partial Poisoning]] with [[Rotenone]] as a management tool for correcting overcrowded fish populations in five of the 18 [[Alabama Public Fishing Lakes]] managed by the [[Alabama Department of Conservation]]. Overcrowding, mainly of intermediate-sized [[Bluegill]] and [[Redear Sunfish]], was usually corrected with three to seven [[Marginal Poisoning]] treatments, or a combination of marginal and [[Sectional Poisoning]], conducted mostly in September and October. Angler harvest was higher in balanced years (193.4 lb/acre) than in overcrowded years (132.7 lb/acre), and the lakes sustained an average harvest of 174.5 lb/acre during 5 to 8 years of continuous public fishing. The authors conclude that prompt partial poisoning is effective and economical, but that [[Bullheads]] and [[Crappie]] add crowding that partial poisoning does not readily correct.

## Background & Description

**Program context.** The [[Alabama Public Fishing Lakes]] program began in 1948. At the time of the study, the Department owned and managed 18 lakes, 32 to 250 acres each, totaling 1,444 acres. Lakes were sited for suitable topography, subsoil, and water supply, in areas of the State with inadequate fishing waters.

**Study lakes.** Five lakes were studied, all open to continuous fishing for 5 to 8 years:
- Coffee Lake (80 acres)
- Barbour Lake (75 acres)
- Crenshaw Lake (53 acres)
- Pike Lake (45 acres)
- Cullman Lake (32 acres)

**Stocking and management.** Lakes were treated two or three times with 5 to 10 ppm of 5% rotenone before impoundment to remove native fish. Hatchery sunfish (2 to 3 inches) were stocked at 1,000 to 1,500 per acre (about 85% [[Bluegill]], 15% [[Redear Sunfish]]). One- to two-inch [[Largemouth Bass]] were stocked at 100 to 150 per acre the following spring. Lakes were fertilized annually (an average of 12 applications of 100 lb of 8-8-2 per acre, or equivalent). No size limits applied; the creel limit was 20 sunfish and 6 bass per day. Resident managers recorded daily angler numbers and catches, and [[Seining]] assessed population balance during spawning seasons.

**Definitions used.**
- *Balanced population:* satisfactory annual catches and adequate annual reproduction of bass and sunfish.
- *Overcrowded sunfish population:* a seine haul (14-inch-mesh, 50 ft by 6 ft) averaging more than 35 intermediate-sized sunfish (3-, 4-, and 5-inch groups), and/or inadequate sunfish fry in fine-mesh seining.
- *Az value:* the percentage of total population weight in harvestable-sized fish. Balance requires Az values between 33 and 90 ([[Swingle Balance Index]]).

**Harvestable size thresholds.** Minimum harvestable weights were 0.1 lb (sunfish), 0.4 lb (bass), 0.26 lb (crappie), and 0.3 lb (bullheads).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of overcrowding.** The authors identified several interacting factors:
- Competitive species that survived pre-stocking treatment, migrated over the spillway in high water, or were introduced by anglers, including [[Bullheads]], [[Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Golden Shiner]].
- Heavy aquatic vegetation, which increases cover and reduces predation. Regular fertilization largely prevented this in most State lakes, but [[Pithophora]], a branched green alga, contributed to imbalance in Barbour Lake.
- [[Fish Kill]] events from dissolved-oxygen deficiency or unknown disease, and loss of fish over the spillway.
- Heavy [[Angler Harvest]] pressure, which rapidly removes harvestable fish and releases food for intermediate-sized fish, causing accelerated growth and reproduction and further crowding.

**Regional comparison.** Byrd (1959) reported an annual average of 162.2 fishermen per acre harvesting 174.2 lb/acre across 12 State lakes (841 acres total), five of which had been partially poisoned. In one lake (Crenshaw), an eight-year average of 212 trips and 249 lb per acre showed unusually heavy fishing pressure.

**Alternatives.** Earlier workers suggested restocking after whole-lake poisoning or draining. The authors note that draining and restocking would have cost at least 18 months of fishing. Swingle, Prather, and Lawrence (1953) found sectional and marginal poisoning effective, but seining was too time-consuming for general use.

**Timing.** Treatments were applied between 8:00 a.m. and noon on clear, calm days to concentrate fish in shallow water and limit movement of rotenone into deep water.

## Key Findings

**1. Harvest rose after balance was restored.**
- Balanced years: 161 trips/acre, 193.4 lb/acre (abstract: 193.4 lb/acre).
- Overcrowded years: 146 trips/acre, 132.7 lb/acre.
- Five- to eight-year open period: 156 trips/acre, 174.5 lb/acre.

**2. Treatment methods.**
- *Marginal poisoning:* 2 lb of 5% rotenone per 100 yards of shoreline, applied 20 to 30 ft offshore in water under 3 to 4 ft deep. Three to seven applications were needed, spaced 3 to 5 days apart.
- *Sectional poisoning:* approximately 1 ppm emulsifiable rotenone pumped through a weighted perforated hose, applied to areas of 2.5 to 8.0 acres in Barbour Lake and 3.0 to 4.0 acres in Pike Lake.
- *Detoxification:* [[Potassium Permanganate]] at about 2 ppm, applied roughly one hour after treatment, reduced bass kill in sectional treatments.
- *Restocking:* fingerling bass were restocked at 100 per acre when spring seining showed adequate bluegill spawning but poor bass spawning (range 25 to 200 per acre).

**3. Timing and kill requirements.** Success was greatest for partial poisoning between September and November, a period when sunfish do not spawn in Alabama. Restoring balance in lakes without crappie required killing 80 to 150 lb/acre of intermediate-sized sunfish.

**4. Lake-specific results (Tables 1 and 2 summarized).**

| Lake (acres) | Treatment and kill | Harvest response |
|---|---|---|
| Coffee (80) | June 1954: 34 lb/acre; Sept–Oct 1954: 93 lb/acre (six marginal treatments in each series) | 64.4 lb/acre (1954) to 174.1 (1955) and 203.4 (1956); balance held for 4 years |
| Barbour (75) | Sectional, Oct 1954: 231 lb/acre sunfish, 9 lb bass; marginal, 1957: 108 lb/acre sunfish, 10 lb bass | 71.3 to 130.7 lb/acre (1955); 109.7 to 157.1 lb/acre (1958) |
| Crenshaw (53) | Marginal, 1954: 83 lb/acre; 1955: 143 lb/acre; 1956: 5 lb/acre (failed; crappie in deep water) | 164.5 to 268.9 lb/acre (1955); 289 lb/acre in 1956 before crowding returned |
| Pike (45) | Marginal, Sept 1955: 115 lb/acre sunfish, about 22 lb bass; sectional, Oct 1955: 134 lb/acre sunfish, 25 lb bass | Decline after bass kill; recovery only after the second year |
| Cullman (32) | 1952: 55 lb/acre; 1953: 100 lb/acre; 1957: 10 lb/acre; 1958: 97 lb/acre | 82.6 to 118.4 lb/acre (1954); did not return to balance by 1959 |

**5. Failure modes.**
- Marginal treatment in Barbour Lake (September 1954) failed because few sunfish were in shallow water.
- Crenshaw Lake relapsed after fishermen introduced [[Crappie]] and reproduction began in 1956.
- In Pike Lake, sudden gusty winds moved rotenone into deeper water, killing most adult bass.
- In Cullman Lake, crappie introduced by anglers contributed to 1958 crowding.

**6. Conclusions.** Partial poisoning with rotenone is an effective and economical way to restore balance to bass-bluegill-redear sunfish populations overcrowded with intermediate-sized sunfish. [[Bullheads]] and [[Crappie]] augment crowding and cause management problems that partial poisoning cannot readily correct.
