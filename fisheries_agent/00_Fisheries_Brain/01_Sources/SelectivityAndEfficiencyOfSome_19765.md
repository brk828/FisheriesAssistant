# Selectivity and Efficiency of Commercial Fishing Devices in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluated the selectivity and efficiency of several commercial fishing devices in two [[Salt River Reservoirs]], [[Apache Lake]] and [[Roosevelt Lake]], in central Arizona. Research ran from 1966 to 1968 and drew on more than 500 net nights. The authors sought to determine whether commercial harvest could be developed alongside recreational fishing in artificial reservoirs, a use that is often neglected. The main target was [[Buffalofishes]], with [[Common Carp]] as a secondary commercial species. Among the devices tested, the 51-mm inner-wall [[Trammel Nets]] was the most efficient for commercial species, and [[Electrofishing]] was the least selective and least efficient.

## Background & Description

Commercial fishing is often overlooked in U.S. artificial reservoirs, even though multiple-use management is now common. Contributing factors include limited data on fish size, quality, and abundance; little knowledge of harvest techniques; and conflicts between recreational anglers and commercial operators.

The study was funded by a Grant-in-Aid from the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries (under P.L. 88-309) to the Arizona Game and Fish Department. Commercially exploitable species in the lakes were buffalofishes (*Ictiobus* spp.) and carp (*Cyprinus carpio*). Dominant sport fishes were [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), and various [[Sunfishes]] (*Lepomis* spp.). At the time, the limited commercial fishery could not meet demand in the Phoenix metropolitan area, which imported buffalofishes from adjacent states.

**Methods tested:**
- **Nylon experimental gill nets** (flag type): nine mesh sizes from 13 to 127 mm, fished 1 to 2 m below the surface.
- **Nylon trammel nets**: outer walls of 254 mm, inner walls of 51 or 64 mm, 2.4 m deep, fished with corklines or leadlines.
- **Electrofishing**: a 230-volt, 2,650-watt generator on a boat, operated at night with two 75-watt floodlights.
- **Other methods** (not formally evaluated): seines, hoop and fyke nets, rotenone, and trawling.

All nets were set overnight. Catches were standardized as fish per 30.4 m (100 ft) of net per 12-hour "net night."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal movement of buffalofishes:** Catches peaked in May–June and fell to a low in September–October. In spring, fish moved into shallows formed by inflowing streams. Males arrived first and made up as much as 90% of the early-May catch. Females joined late in May, producing the largest catches of the year. Later, fish dispersed to deep water, and autumn declines were attributed to reduced movement or retreat to deeper areas.
- **Catch variation with temperature:** Commercial fishermen reported up to 150 fish per night in warmer periods, with catches falling on cooler nights.
- **Shallow-water methods:** Seines and traps were ineffective because the lakes lacked suitably shallow water.
- **Net fouling:** Algae and other material accumulated on nets within two or three days, reducing efficiency by making nets more visible. Commercial fishermen typically washed gill nets weekly in summer.
- **Size and species availability:** Small buffalofishes were largely absent from the lakes, so they were not caught in smaller meshes. Carp over 10 kg were common in the downstream Canyon and Saguaro lakes before those were drained in the late 1960s, but large carp were absent from Apache and Roosevelt lakes, for reasons unknown.
- **Population form:** The Roosevelt Lake buffalofish population was a single species (an aberrant form of *I. cyprinellus*), while Apache Lake supported a multi-species population (*I. cyprinellus*, *I. niger*, and *I. bubalus*). Catch patterns in the two lakes were similar.

## Key Findings

**Buffalofishes**
- The 51-mm inner-wall trammel net was the most efficient device for buffalofishes. It caught more than 43% of buffalofishes taken at Roosevelt Lake and 35% at Apache Lake.
- The 64-mm trammel net and the 76-mm gill net were about equally effective for buffalofishes, but each was less than half as efficient as the 51-mm trammel net.
- Electrofishing was relatively ineffective for buffalofishes. Fish escaped laterally or passed through the electrical field before being immobilized, and were stunned only when trapped in narrow, shallow bays.

**Common Carp**
- Carp were caught most often in 38-mm gill nets, followed closely by 32-mm nets. Their serrated dorsal and anal spines readily snag in netting, which makes them highly susceptible to entanglement.
- Carp were caught in every mesh size except the 64-mm trammel net.
- Carp were moderately susceptible to electrofishing year-round, especially in spring and early summer when they gather in shallows.

**Sport Fishes**
- Largemouth bass were caught in the same gill-net meshes as carp and at about the same efficiency. They formed a larger share of autumn–winter gill-net catches than carp did, a trend that reversed in spring and summer.
- Largemouth bass were considerably more susceptible to electrofishing than the other large fish present, especially in spring.
- Channel catfish and black crappie made up a small share of the catch in every device. Catfish were more abundant in nets set on or near the bottom, and black crappie were mostly taken in trammel nets, with catches rising during stormy weather.
- Sunfishes (*Lepomis* spp.) were caught mainly in small-mesh nets set near shallows, or by shoreline electrofishing.

**Overall Efficiency**
- The 51-mm inner-wall trammel net was the most efficient device for commercial species, averaging 1.8 commercial fish per net night per 30.4 m of net, with 0.7 sport fish per unit.
- The 64-mm trammel net caught the fewest sport fish (0.1 per unit) but was less than half as efficient for commercial species as the 51-mm trammel net.
- The 76-mm gill net averaged 0.9 commercial fish and 0.3 sport fish per unit.
- Electrofishing was the least selective method. It required at least 0.5 hour of effort per usable commercial fish, which made it far less efficient than entanglement devices.

**Interpretation Caveats**
- The two trammel nets differed markedly in buffalofish catch. The authors considered the 51-mm versus 64-mm inner-wall difference an unlikely explanation. They suggested net age and color (one was older and brownish, the other new and whitish) and unrecognized design differences as possible causes.
- Net-to-net differences reflect both true selectivity and less visible factors affecting "fishability," such as fouling and species body shape, spines, and movement.
- Each net type and mesh size samples a different sub-population within a lake, so catch composition depends on the gear used.
