# Relationship of Herbivore Growth to Rate of Gross Photosynthesis in Microcosms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tilapia

## Executive Summary

This reprinted paper by [[William J. McConnell]] (*Limnology and Oceanography*, Vol. 10, No. 4, October 1965, pp. 539–543) tests how the growth of a herbivorous fish relates to the rate of [[Gross Photosynthesis]] in experimental [[Microcosm Experiments]]. The study uses [[Tilapia Mossambica]] introduced into replicate tanks once a phytoplankton bloom had developed. Across 16 experiments, the growth of the fish increased with photosynthetic rate, but the response was curvilinear: a given gain in primary production produced a much larger gain in fish growth at higher productivity levels than at lower ones. The author links this to [[Trophic Efficiency]] and to the difference between [[Oligotrophic Lakes]] and [[Eutrophic Lakes]].

## Background & Description

The experiments used [[Tilapia Mossambica]] averaging about 12 mm total length and 35 mg live weight. Fish were stocked when a noticeable [[Phytoplankton]] bloom appeared, with 5 to 12 days between inoculation, fertilization and fish introduction. Most 310-liter tanks were adjusted to five fish, while certain tanks were reduced to three, either by starvation or deliberately, to avoid metabolite accumulation. High-density tanks (experiments 9, 10 and 11) were used to examine [[Intraspecific Competition]].

Key methods:
- **Light:** Tanks sat in direct sunlight. Experiments ran either April–June or July–September. Each tank had a transparent plastic cover, washed regularly to limit dust.
- **Aeration and chemistry:** Gentle aeration gave roughly equal mixing in every tank. Carbon dioxide was added to the air supply to keep pH between 7.0 and 8.0. The target was mostly met, though values ranged from 6.5 to 10.0.
- **Temperature:** Well water flowing through a metal jacket moderated temperature. Diurnal ranges were held to about 3°C. Spring experiments were always above 21°C and reached 29°C in the last two weeks. Summer experiments ranged from 27 to 32°C.
- **Measurements:** Gross photosynthesis and community respiration were calculated from the [[Diel Oxygen Pulse]] method, with diffusion correction following McConnell (1962). Fish were weighed live after blotting. Growth was measured as the cube root of mean weight gain, and fish were tracked to the upper inflection point of a [[Sigmoid Growth Curve]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames herbivore growth within the energy flow from primary producers to consumers. Several processes are discussed as possible sources of the observed pattern:

- **Food competition:** Individual growth rates in the densest tanks were apparently independent of population density, which the author attributes to food being unlimited under these conditions.
- **Decomposers and settled plankton:** Settled phytoplankton may not have been eaten by *T. mossambica*. Bacteria and other decomposers may have used the most nutritious settled material first, so initial competition may have occurred between decomposers and macroconsumers rather than among fish.
- **Algal community:** The most divergent points (experiments A and 8) differed mainly in algal composition. Experiment 8 was dominated by [[Aufwuchs]] of blue-green algae for the first 45 days, followed by larger forms such as *Oocystis* sp. Tank A consisted of very small coccoid green algae throughout.
- **Social effects:** The author notes that possible effects of schooling or crowding could not be excluded, but no clear evidence for them was found.

## Key Findings

- **Curvilinear productivity response:** Fish growth rose with gross photosynthesis, but the slope was steeper at higher production. A daily rise in gross photosynthesis from 1 to 2 g O₂/m² produced a fish-growth increase only 14% of that obtained by the same increment in a system at 4 g O₂/m² per day (Fig. 1).
- **Implication for lakes:** The author suggests that equal increases in primary production in eutrophic lakes yield significantly greater secondary production than in oligotrophic lakes. A narrow range of photosynthesis may therefore correspond to a wider range of secondary productivity.
- **Density independence:** Individual growth in the highest-density microcosms was not reduced relative to less dense ones. In experiment 10, growth appeared independent of population density.
- **Prolonged growth depression:** In experiment 11, growth remained depressed for longer than in experiment 10, so the duration of rapid growth may reflect direct competition.
- **Unexplained scatter:** Some points deviated from the photosynthesis–growth relationship by more than the combined standard error. Temperature, density, fry handling and handling frequency were examined, but no clear cause was found.
- **Methodological caveat:** Several experiments lacked periodic weights, so their growth curves were assumed to resemble those of tanks with similar density and productivity.
