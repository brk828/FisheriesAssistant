## Executive Summary

This 1979 study by H. H. Costa examined the food habits and 24-hour feeding chronology of [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) in [[Lake Washington]], Seattle, over nine months (April–December 1973). Based on 549 stomach analyses, the diet was numerically dominated by the mysid shrimp [[Acanthomysis awatchensis]], but by volume dominated by the sculpin [[Cottus asper]], with [[Chironomid Larvae and Pupae]] as the third most important item. Feeding activity closely tracked diurnal activity patterns, peaking just before dusk, with a calculated daily ration of ~1.4% of body weight. No significant dietary differences were found between sexes or size classes, though females consumed larger prey fish than males.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), family Percidae — a widely distributed species valued for food and sport fishing.
- **Study Site**: [[Lake Washington]] — a mesotrophic lake covering 87.6 km², 29.6 km long, 0.6–2.4 km wide; second largest lake in Washington State. Sampling occurred at north, south, and central sites, with intensive 24-hour studies conducted at "North Point" (depth 2–8 m).
- **Co-occurring fish community**: 28 fish species recorded in the lake, most commonly juvenile [[Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), [[Peamouth]] (*Mylocheilus caurinus*), Yellow Perch, [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*), and [[Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*).
- **Methods**:
  - Horizontal gill nets (primary gear); fyke nets attempted but unsuccessful for live capture.
  - Two 24-hour gill-net sampling periods (July 17/18 and August 22/23, 1973), nets cleared every ~3 hours; water temp 18–22°C.
  - Monthly seasonal collections April–December 1973 across multiple lake sites.
  - Stomach fullness scored using [[Bâlu 1961 Fullness Index]] (0 = empty to 10 = distended).
  - Diet composition assessed by number, volume (water displacement), and occurrence.
  - Daily ration estimated via weight of stomach contents per gram of body weight across diel cycle, following methods of [[Keast and Welsh 1968]] and [[Mathur 1973]].
- **Sample**: 549 perch, 100–350 mm total length; 387 males, 164 females (males considerably more abundant in catch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic role**: Yellow Perch functions as an opportunistic, visually-feeding predator combining benthic probing (for cottids, chironomids, snails) with open-water interception of mysid shrimp near the lake bottom.
- **Prey base**:
  - [[Cottus asper]] (prickly sculpin) — dominant by volume (62.3%), key prey especially July–August.
  - [[Acanthomysis awatchensis]] (mysid shrimp) — dominant by number (69.1%), especially May–June.
  - [[Chironomid Larvae and Pupae]] — third most important, peaking in October.
  - Minor/occasional items: Trichoptera larvae, [[Gasterosteus aculeatus]] (threespine stickleback), Mollusca (*Physa gyrina*, *Gyraulus* sp.), Hirudinea (leeches), and rare copepods/amphipods (only in juvenile perch <100 mm).
- **Seasonal feeding pattern**: Minimal feeding/empty stomachs observed in spring during spawning; intensive feeding resumed late May–June, potentially linked to rising water temperatures and prey availability.
- **Diurnal activity**: Yellow Perch are diurnally active, consistent with prior findings in [[Lake Mendota]] (Wisconsin) and [[Lake of the Woods]]/[[Clear Lake]] (Iowa). Activity and feeding peaked between 10 AM–7 PM, with cumulative catch peaks 1–4 PM, aligning with maximum stomach fullness before dusk.
- **Interspecific context**: Yellow Perch had the second-highest catch-per-effort in gill nets after [[Peamouth]], suggesting a successful, well-supported population; the dusk feeding peak may reduce competitive overlap with co-occurring species.
- **Comparative geography**: Diet composition contrasts with other studied populations — [[Seaburg and Moyle 1964]] (Minnesota lakes: darters, minnows, dragonfly nymphs, chironomids) and [[Keast and Welsh 1968]] (Lake Opinicon, Ontario: cladocerans, amphipods, isopods, dragonfly nymphs, chironomids) — underscoring strong habitat-driven dietary plasticity in this species.

## Key Findings

- **Diet composition (whole study, n=549)**:
  | Food Item | % Number | % Volume | % Occurrence |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | *Cottus asper* | 8.05 | 62.29 | 28.94 |
  | *Gasterosteus aculeatus* | 0.06 | 1.58 | 0.87 |
  | *Acanthomysis awatchensis* | 69.09 | 22.06 | 27.54 |
  | *Chironomus* spp. | 17.03 | 11.95 | 38.59 |
  | Trichoptera larvae | 4.19 | 1.30 | 0.87 |
  | Mollusca | 0.46 | 0.79 | 2.28 |
  | Hirudinea | 0.23 | 0.002 | 0.87 |

- **Sex-based differences**: Females contained more fish prey by volume (mean 1.54) than males (mean 0.63); females also ingested larger prey fish (>8.3 mm) than males, which never contained prey of that size.
- **Size-class patterns**: No major dietary distinction across the 100–350 mm size range; even smallest perch (100–149 mm) consumed fish prey (albeit smaller-sized), while only 2 perch <100 mm examined contained solely cladocerans/amphipods.
- **Seasonal peaks**:
  - Fish (*Cottus*) prey peaked July–August.
  - Mysid shrimp peaked May–June.
  - Chironomid pupae/larvae peaked October.
- **Daily feeding chronology**:
  - Single major feeding peak at dusk (~7 PM), contrasting with the bimodal (dusk + morning) pattern reported for Lake Opinicon perch by Keast & Welsh (1968).
  - Feeding began around noon, intensified toward dusk, then ceased with darkness; night/early-morning stomach contents were in advanced digestion.
  - Prey-specific diel timing: *Cottus asper* consumed mainly 4–10 PM; *Acanthomysis* peaked ~4 PM; chironomids/trichopterans important 4–10 PM.
- **Daily ration**: Calculated at ~0.014 g/g body weight (1.4% of wet body weight/day) — lower than the 2–4%/day reported by Keast & Welsh (1968) and Mathur (1973) for other fish, but consistent with Seaburg & Moyle's (1964) summer-based estimate of 1–2% for Minnesota lake fishes.
- **Catch-per-unit-effort (24-hr gill net study, July 19/20)**: Yellow Perch and Peamouth were the two dominant species captured; Yellow Perch catch peaked 7 AM–1 PM and dropped to near zero after midnight.
- **Management/ecological implication**: High relative abundance and successful establishment of Yellow Perch in Lake Washington indicate ample prey resource availability, with dusk-restricted feeding potentially minimizing interspecific competition with cohabiting fish species such as [[Peamouth]] and [[Squawfish]].
