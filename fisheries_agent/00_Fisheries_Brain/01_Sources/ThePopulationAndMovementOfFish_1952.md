# The Population and Movement of Fish in Prickly Pear Creek, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #Cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 2-year (1949–1950) mark-recapture study by [[Frank A. Stefanich]] investigated fish population dynamics and movement patterns in [[Prickly Pear Creek]], Montana, a tributary of the [[Missouri River]]. Using electrofishing and tagging techniques, researchers sampled six fixed stream sections repeatedly across shocking periods in both years. [[Brown Trout]] dominated the salmonid community (54–60% of trout by number, ~73–74% by weight), followed by [[Rainbow Trout]], with minor contributions from [[Eastern Brook Trout]] and a single [[Cutthroat Trout]]. Trout constituted approximately 79% of the total fish population by number in both years. Total fish biomass ranged from 58.87 to 241.10 lbs/acre across sampling periods, with sharp seasonal declines driven by sucker emigration. Tag-recapture data revealed strong site fidelity for most species (especially eastern brook trout, mountain whitefish, and suckers), but documented individual long-distance movements of brown trout, rainbow trout, and suckers—predominantly downstream, including migrations into the Missouri River itself (up to 27 miles for one brown trout).

## Background & Description

**Study System:** [[Prickly Pear Creek]] is a 23-mile stream flowing northeasterly into the [[Missouri River]] near Craig, Montana, draining approximately 394 square miles of grassland with open ponderosa pine stands. Elevation ranges from 4,250 ft (headwaters) to 3,300 ft (mouth), with a gradient of ~41 ft/mile. The lower 13 miles were selected for study, with stream widths of 15–60 ft and pool depths up to 8 ft; average pool-to-pool spacing was ~260 ft. Maximum recorded temperature was 67°F, with diel fluctuations up to 16°F.

**Sampling Design:** Six permanent 600-ft "regular sections" were established at intervals along the study reach (Section 1 at 2.2 road miles from the mouth; subsequent sections at 1.4 and then 2.3-mile intervals). Each section was subdivided into four 150-ft units, netted off, and electrofished using a 500-watt AC generator (voltages up to 240V; typically 160–190V). In 1950, 11 additional randomly-selected 300-ft sections were sampled to assess movement beyond the fixed study sections, following [[Snedecor]] (1946) randomization methods.

**Marking Methods:** Fish were anesthetized in 0.5% urethane solution ([[Gerking 1949]] protocol), measured (total length to 0.1 in), and weighed (to 0.01 lb). Trout were jaw-tagged using serially numbered monel-metal strap tags (styles 1005 and 18M, sizes keyed to fish length: No. 1 for 3.5–5.5", No. 3 for 5.6–14.0", No. 4 for 14.1–18.0", No. 18M for >18.1"). Non-trout species were fin-clipped or marked with Petersen disk tags.

**Hatchery Plantings:** Rainbow trout were stocked in experimental Sections 4 and 6: 2,955 fingerlings (Sept. 1949), 904 yearlings (avg. 6.7", May 1950), and ~4,000 fingerlings (Sept. 1950), each lot distinctively fin-clipped.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species Assemblage:** The fish community included wild [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] (plus hatchery-planted rainbow trout and rainbow x cutthroat hybrids), [[Eastern Brook Trout]], a single [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], two sucker species ([[Longnose Sucker]] and [[Western White Sucker]], analyzed jointly as "suckers"), occasional [[Carp]], [[American Burbot]], and abundant [[Freshwater Sculpin]] (too numerous to census systematically and excluded from population tabulations).

- **Seasonal Patterns:** Sucker abundance peaked in spring/early summer and collapsed sharply by late summer/fall, suggesting a mass seasonal emigration—partially confirmed by tag recoveries of suckers in the Missouri River. Carp were present only in June–August. Burbot occurred in nearly all sampling periods but in low numbers. Mountain whitefish numbers were consistently higher in June than later in the season, and were lower overall in 1950 than 1949.

- **Interannual Variation:** Total trout biomass declined from an average of 63.99 lbs/acre (1949) to 45.13 lbs/acre (1950), a statistically significant year-over-year difference attributed to unknown causes (overwinter mortality, angling pressure, or emigration to the Missouri River). Total fish biomass (excluding sculpins) ranged 81.75–241.10 lbs/acre in 1949 versus 58.87–148.04 lbs/acre in 1950.

- **Comparative Context:** Brown trout biomass in Prickly Pear Creek (September avg. 37.5 lbs/acre) exceeded that reported by [[Shuck 1945]] for [[Crystal Creek]], New York (13.85 lbs/acre). Total fish biomass was lower than the Root River system, Minnesota ([[Smith et al. 1949]]: 251.1 lbs/acre) but higher than most Michigan streams reported by [[Shetter & Hazzard 1939]] (1.4–40.0 lbs/acre trout), and fell within the range of New Hampshire primitive streams studied by [[Hoover 1938]] (29.7–158.4 lbs/acre).

## Key Findings

### Population Composition
- Brown trout comprised 60% of trout by number in 1949, 54% in 1950; by weight, 74% (1949) and 73% (1950).
- Trout represented ~79% of total fish numbers in both years; by weight, 58% (1949) and 47% (1950).
- Of 34 adult suckers sampled for species ID, 29 were longnose suckers and 5 were western white suckers (~85%/15% split).
- Eastern brook trout contributed <4% of trout numbers and <1.6% of trout weight in 1949, rising to 7.2% of numbers in 1950.
- Only one cutthroat trout was captured across the entire study (7th shocking period).

### Hatchery Plantings Contribution
- September 1949 rainbow plant: <2% of total trout captured in any 1950 sampling period.
- May 1950 rainbow plant: 20.8% of trout numbers in the immediately following sampling period, declining to 7.0% and then 1.7% in subsequent periods.
- September 1950 rainbow plant: 35.6% of trout numbers but only 4.5% of trout weight in the following sampling period (reflecting small fingerling size).

### Standing Crop / Biomass
- Total fish biomass (excl. sculpins) ranged 58.87–241.10 lbs/acre across all sampling periods.
- Trout biomass averaged 63.99 lbs/acre (1949) vs. 45.13 lbs/acre (1950) — a significant decline.
- Sharp biomass declines were driven primarily by sucker disappearance (1949: between 1st–2nd shocking; 1950: between 6th–7th shocking).

### Movement & Site Fidelity (Tag-Recapture)
- **Brown Trout:** 944 tagged; 398 of 844 available fish (47%) recaptured at least once; 10 individuals recaptured six times. Movement was predominantly downstream; one individual moved 2.3 miles downstream between Sept–Nov 1949. Of 29 brown trout found outside their tagging section, 25 moved downstream, 4 upstream. Creel census recorded one brown trout moving ~27 miles downstream into the Missouri River over ~10 months.
- **Rainbow Trout:** 638 tagged; 242 of 528 available (46%) recaptured at least once; one fish recaptured six times. Documented movements ranged up to 6.9 miles (both upstream and downstream) between sections. Of 22 rainbow trout found outside their tagging section, 15 moved downstream and 7 upstream. Rainbow trout showed more inter-section movement than brown trout but lower overwinter recapture rates.
- **Eastern Brook Trout:** Highly sedentary — all 15 recaptured individuals (of 76 available) were found in their original tagging section.
- **Mountain Whitefish:** Only 7 of 155 tagged/available fish recaptured, all within original sections.
- **Suckers:** 767 tagged; 35 of 752 available (4.7%) recaptured, one individual four times. Only 2 of these moved between regular sections (both downstream, 6.9 miles each, with one movement approaching ~1 mile/day). Random-section sampling recovered 14 additional suckers: 6 moved upstream (avg. 1.3 mi, max 2.3 mi) and 7 moved downstream (avg. 2.3 mi, max 9.1 mi, including one moving 5.0 miles in 6 days).
- **Carp & Burbot:** No tagged individuals of either species were recovered in any subsequent sampling.

### Random Section Sampling (1950)
- Of 24 recovered tagged brown trout, 6 were found outside regular sections (all downstream; avg. 700 ft, max 1,900 ft moved).
- Of 5 recovered tagged rainbow trout, 3 were outside regular sections (avg. 780 ft downstream).
- One mountain whitefish moved 1,500 ft downstream from its tagging section.

### Creel Census Findings
- 36 marked brown trout caught by anglers; of 27 with reliable location data, 13 showed no movement, 1 moved slightly upstream, 13 moved downstream (avg. 5.2 mi, max 27 mi — into the Missouri River).
- 44 rainbow trout caught by anglers; of 32 with location data, 16 showed no movement, 5 moved upstream (avg. 4.4 mi), 9 moved downstream (avg. 0.3 mi).
- 7 marked brook trout caught; only 1 moved (2.3 mi upstream).
- 14 suckers caught; of 10 with location data, 2 stationary, 2 moved upstream (avg. 1.4 mi, max 2.4 mi), 6 moved downstream (avg. 2.2 mi, max 5.6 mi), including 2 recovered in the Missouri River.
- Creel census was identified as the most effective method for documenting long-distance fish movement, outperforming both regular and random section electrofishing for this purpose.

### General Movement Trend
- Across all species and methods, the dominant directional trend for fish moving outside their tagging section was **downstream**, with suckers showing the most pronounced seasonal mass emigration pattern, some reaching the [[Missouri River]].
