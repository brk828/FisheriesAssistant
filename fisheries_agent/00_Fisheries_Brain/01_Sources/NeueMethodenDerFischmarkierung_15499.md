# New Methods of Fish Marking

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 article by Dr. K. Müller, of the [[Limnologische Flussstation Schlitz]] (Max Planck Society hydrobiological institute), reviews the history of fish marking and presents new approaches for studying fish migration. Marking has long clarified the migration routes of anadromous fish such as [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[European Salmon]], and of commercially important marine fish. The author argues that biologists now also need to know how fish orient during migration and how they behave on the way. Conventional marks cannot answer these questions, because recapture is left to chance. The article therefore describes a miniature transmitter that can be attached to fish and tracked by a receiver, allowing continuous contact with individual animals. The method was still in development at the time, with a detection range of 80 to 100 m.

## Background & Description

The article is a reprint from *Die Umschau in Wissenschaft und Technik* (Issue 14, 1963). It is written in German and includes two figures: a numbered light-metal marking plate (Bild 1) and a miniature transmitter (Bild 2).

**Species named in the text:**
- Migratory fish: [[Pacific Salmon]], [[European Salmon]], sturgeon ("Stör"), and "marine" migrants (unspecified).
- Economically important marine fish: [[Herring]], Atlantic cod ("Dorsch", "Kabeljau"), haddock ("Schellfisch"), and tuna ("Thunfisch").
- Freshwater fish targeted for future study: [[Carp]] and [[Pike]].

**Historical marking methods described:**
1. **Group marking:** clipping a pelvic fin, or attaching coloured glass beads. The author notes that pelvic fins regenerate within one to two years depending on the fish's age, and that the clip does not impair swimming. Group marks were still occasionally used to tell apart stocks in fish farms.
2. **Individual marking:** numbered metal or plastic tags, sewn into the muscle below the dorsal fin with plastic thread and knotted. The article shows a tag used by the author (Bild 1).
3. **Internal X-ray marking:** A. Lindroth inserted metal tags with X-ray contrast material into the body cavity of juvenile salmon. Every upstream-migrating salmon was caught, X-rayed and identified. This gave information on time spent at sea and growth rate, but the method was complex and expensive, and the author doubted it could be used on a large scale.

**Patent-based device:** O. H. Graf von Schlitz, gen. von Görtz, patent dated 5 March 1957, designed a pressure-resistant "Harpune" capsule for large fish and marine mammals. It records water depth, temperature and heart rate. After a set time the capsule detaches, floats to the surface, and can be located by a radio beacon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author frames the problem around three questions that go beyond earlier ones on route, duration and growth:
- **Orientation:** how fish find their way on migration.
- **Factors influencing orientation:** the environmental cues that affect navigation.
- **Migratory behaviour:** how fish act during the journey.

Earlier work by [[Hasler]] and colleagues, and by Carlin, Hoar, Ricker, Scheer and Braemer, had substantially expanded knowledge of fish orientation mechanisms. Hasler, for example, studied the movement of a lake fish population by attaching table-tennis balls to fish with perlon threads and watching them drift at the surface.

The main limitation of conventional tagging is that it depends on chance recapture. Methods that keep "contact" with an individual animal are needed for behavioural work.

Water propagation was a key design factor. The transmitter operates at about 60 kHz (long-wave), described as the most suitable frequency for underwater transmission.

## Key Findings

- **Migration research has moved from route and growth to orientation and behaviour.** Route and duration questions remain, but the author argues that orientation mechanisms and behaviour during migration now require a tracking method.
- **Individual tagging methods have evolved:** from group marks (fin clipping, coloured beads) to numbered metal or plastic tags, and from there to internal X-ray-identifiable tags. The X-ray method is costly and complex, and its wider use is doubtful.
- **A new miniature transmitter was developed** in collaboration with Dipl.-Ing. Schwarzbeck (Heidelberg) and the Eichhoff works laboratory in Schlitz. Features reported:
  - Single-stage transistor transmitter
  - Long-wave transmission at about 60 kHz
  - Attachable to smaller fish, especially freshwater species
  - Standard transistor receiver with amplified long-wave reception
  - Detection of tagged fish at **80 to 100 m** in the development stage described
- **Planned applications:** the author hoped to study movement of [[Carp]] and [[Pike]], including daily (day-night) rhythms and seasonal changes in activity.
- **Harpoon capsule concept:** the patented device (1957) records depth, temperature and heart rate, and is designed to float to the surface and be located by radio beacon. It is aimed at large fish and marine mammals.
- **Article status:** the text reports the method as a development step with planned research, not as completed results. No sample sizes, population estimates or quantitative tracking data are given.
