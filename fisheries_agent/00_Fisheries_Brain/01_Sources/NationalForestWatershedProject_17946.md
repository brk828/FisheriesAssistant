# National Forest Watershed Projects in Arizona

#forestry #hydrology #watershed-management

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by [[Fred H. Kennedy]], Regional Forester of the [[U.S. Forest Service]] Southwestern Region, reports on watershed pilot projects in the national forests of Arizona. It was delivered at the Third Annual Watershed Symposium, "Progress in Watershed Management," held in Phoenix on September 21, 1959. The central premise is that water is the most important single product of southwestern National Forests, and that forest management must balance timber, forage, wildlife, recreation, and water through [[Multiple Use and Sustained Yield]]. The main pilot effort is the [[Beaver Creek Watershed Project]] in the [[Coconino National Forest]], which tests how vegetation treatments affect water yield, soil movement, and timber growth, and what those treatments cost.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by quoting [[Dwight D. Eisenhower]]'s July 31, 1953 message to Congress, which affirmed federal responsibility for managing public lands for timber, minerals, grazing, fish and wildlife, recreation, and water supply. Kennedy frames [[Multiple Use and Sustained Yield]] as the twin conservation objectives of national forest management.

Key regional figures given in the paper:

- National Forests supply about 63% of the timber used by sawmills in New Mexico and Arizona.
- Over 5,000,000 recreational visits were made to southwestern National Forests in the previous year.
- The forests contain over 2,000 miles of fishing streams and 65 lakes, receiving more than 500,000 fishermen visits each year.
- About 46,000 big game animals, mostly deer, are harvested yearly.
- Permitted grazing covers over 220,000 cattle and 147,000 sheep.

The Beaver Creek project lies in the Coconino National Forest, covers more than a quarter million acres (276,000 acres in the soil survey), and is formed where the [[Wet Beaver Creek]] and [[Dry Beaver Creek]] converge about six miles above their confluence with the [[Verde River]]. The Verde supplies [[Salt River Valley]] irrigation and domestic and industrial water, and receives an estimated 10% of its flow from Beaver Creek about a mile above Camp Verde. The watershed rises to the [[Mogollon Rim]], where about six feet of snow falls in an average winter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Vegetation zones descend from high to low elevation as follows:

- **Ponderosa pine:** about 100,000 acres in the upper intermediate drainages.
- **Pinyon-juniper woodland:** precipitation about 15 inches annually.
- **Grassland and desert shrub:** precipitation about 12 inches or less on average.
- **Spruce-fir (White Mountains, Apache National Forest):** found only above about 9,000 feet, receiving an estimated 30 or more inches of rain and snowmelt a year. The paper notes these high areas are limited in Arizona but produce the most water per acre.

Average runoff from Beaver Creek is estimated at 50,000 acre-feet per year, a little more than 2 inches of surface precipitation. The paper states that long-term accurate measurements are not available.

A standard [[Soil Survey]] of the 276,000-acre watershed identified about 50 soil types, grouped into 8 management classes. Some soils absorb water rapidly with low erosion hazard, while others absorb water slowly and erode rapidly. Kennedy suspects that watersheds dominated by a single soil type differ markedly in water yield, but states that much remains to be learned about soil-plant-water relationships.

## Key Findings

**Objectives and approach**
- The Beaver Creek project aims to determine what can be done, how to do it, and what it costs to manage a watershed effectively.
- A specific goal is to reduce ponderosa pine stand density to about 80 square feet of basal area per acre, considered optimum for timber production.

**Ponderosa pine treatments (cost per acre)**
- Pre-commercial thinning: 3,500 acres at about $12/acre.
- Pruning: 840 acres at 28 cents per tree (a little over $8/acre).
- Oak and locust clump poisoning: 2,300 acres at $9.30/acre. The method has not yet succeeded on larger [[Gambel's Oak]].
- [[Watershed 7]] (2,300 acres near Stoneman Lake): a commercial sale removed defective and mature trees, pulpwood stems were poisoned at $6.00/acre, and total cost to reach optimum density was about $27/acre.

**Complete conversion to grass**
- [[Watershed 11]] (150 acres, fringe pine and alligator juniper zone) was fully converted to grassland in 1958 and completed in the reporting year. Slash was piled and burned, and the area was seeded with yellow sweet clover and wheatgrasses at seven pounds per acre.
- Cost to date was $74/acre, plus $13/acre for burning and follow-up seeding, plus an estimated $8/acre for four years of oak sprout control, for a total of $95/acre.
- The newly seeded area is fenced and heavily used by elk.

**Juniper control (48,000 acres planned)**
- Cabling with two bulldozers: 9,000 acres by June 30.
- Single "hula-dozer" follow-up: about 6,000 acres. Single machines also cleared 1,620 acres of heavier alligator juniper where cabling was impractical.
- Hand crews removed small missed trees on more than 5,000 acres.
- Average cost of the whole process was slightly under $11/acre.
- Burning of 2,000 acres of heavy debris is planned before reseeding.
- [[Fenuron]] (a chemical) showed promise against Utah and alligator juniper in a small trial and may be used more widely if successful.
- Grass seeding covered about 8,600 acres at $1.39/acre, with mixed results ranging from good to poor, which Kennedy attributes to conditions typical of pinyon-juniper.

**Monitoring and infrastructure**
- About 100 range transects measure effects on forage production.
- 200 seed traps, established by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] in cooperation with the Coconino National Forest, measure acorn production.
- Browse and game counts are underway, and the State of Arizona assigned a biologist to the project.
- All sub-watersheds have precision concrete flumes and recording stream gages (about $4,000 each). Two intermediate watersheds have larger [[U.S. Geological Survey]] gages (about $7,500 each), with two more planned on Wet and Dry Beaver.
- Four complete weather stations have been established.

**Progress after three years**
- A road network has been roughed out for access to gages and treatment areas.
- One-sixth (9,000 acres) of the planned pinyon-juniper work is complete, and 8,600 acres have been reseeded.
- Six percent (6,000 acres) of the planned ponderosa pine work is complete.
- Two intermediate watersheds and 14 sub-watersheds are designated for intensive study: 3 in Utah juniper, 3 in alligator juniper, and 8 in pine.

**Preliminary runoff (24-month period, fall 1957 to spring 1959)**

| Vegetation type | Precipitation (in.) | Runoff (in.) | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ponderosa pine | 51.4 | 10.6 | Average of two watersheds |
| Alligator juniper | 48.5 | 3.6 | One watershed |
| Utah juniper | 42.1 | 2.0 | Average of two watersheds |

**Planned work**
- Prescribed ground-fire burning tests in ponderosa pine to measure effects on water yield.
- Grazing studies in treated and untreated pine areas.

**Spruce-fir studies (Apache National Forest)**
- Willow Creek: three drainages of 288 to 515 acres. Two will be logged after calibration and compared with an untreated control drainage.
- [[Burro Mountain Watershed]]: a 730-acre area northwest of Big Lake testing harvest methods. Clear-cutting of one patch is complete, selective cutting of various intensities is underway, and more than half the work is done. Costs are running high during startup.
- A pulp mill being built in Snowflake, Arizona is expected to make smaller-diameter timber in this area usable.
