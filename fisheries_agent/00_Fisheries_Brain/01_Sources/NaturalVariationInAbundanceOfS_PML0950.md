# Natural Variation in Abundance of Salmonid Populations in Streams and Its Implications for Design of Impact Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Environmental Protection Agency]] project summary reports an extensive literature review of stock size and production in stream-dwelling [[Salmonidae]] populations. Its aims were to compile data on the magnitude of natural variation in population size, relate that variation to environmental conditions where possible, and recommend how this information should guide the design of studies measuring the effects of [[Nonpoint Source Pollution]]. The review draws on a partially annotated bibliography of 260 references.

Natural variation is large. Long-term studies spanning up to 15-20 years, along with spatial surveys, indicate that temporal and spatial variation may reach several orders of magnitude at the extremes and is at least sufficient to mask significant perturbations from NPS pollutants. Physical habitat characteristics, especially cover, along with [[Streamflow]], are the variables most closely linked to variability. The authors recommend prioritizing [[Stream Habitat Quality]] rating systems, developing [[Stream Classification]] schemes, studying basic stream processes, and using [[Paired Comparison Design]] in impact studies.

## Background & Description

Assessing damage from nonpoint source pollution to salmonid fisheries, which are the principal economically important fish affected by pollution in the western United States, requires a baseline understanding of natural abundance fluctuations. Analysts must separate natural variation from effects of human disturbance, such as those from [[Logging]] and [[Agricultural Practices]].

The review covers salmonids during their stream-resident phase. Species considered include:

- Anadromous Pacific salmon: [[Coho Salmon]], [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Pink Salmon]], and [[Chum Salmon]]
- Trouts and chars: [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Steelhead Trout]] (a form of *S. gairdneri*), [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Dolly Varden]]

Although the review began with West Coast North American studies, most quantitative data on resident salmonid variability came from other regions, so that information was included. Data on other fish species that live alongside salmonids were limited and were excluded, a gap the authors urge be addressed.

Two kinds of variability are considered:

- [[Spatial Variation]], studied at scales from microhabitat preference to differences within and between streams
- [[Temporal Variation]], occurring on diel, seasonal, or annual scales

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biomass range.** Standing stock biomass of stream salmonids varies greatly across time and space, from zero or just above to over 60 g/m². This range is large enough to hide major NPS-related perturbations.

**Physical habitat.** Physical characteristics of streams, notably cover in its various forms, are the factors most consistently associated with variation. [[Streamflow]] also influences both temporal and spatial patterns.

**Biological factors.** Food abundance and predation may sometimes affect abundance. However, their mechanisms are less clearly established, and the evidence for them is more equivocal than for physical habitat.

**Watershed analysis designs.** The review compares four designs for [[Watershed Stream Analysis]], classified by timing (before-after versus post-treatment) and by scale (intensive, on one or a few streams, versus extensive, on many streams):

- **Intensive Before-After:** Permits assessment of short-term recovery and needs no assumptions about initial conditions. However, it has no replication, so results form a case study, and it demands substantial investment and reliance on outside agencies.
- **Extensive Before-After (2-4 years):** Offers a broader geographic perspective and greater chance of catching extreme events. However, it is vulnerable to unusual weather, provides results that may not transfer elsewhere, and is difficult to maintain over time.
- **Intensive Post-Treatment (one watershed):** Is quick and relatively inexpensive. However, it provides no pre-treatment data, its control must be located upstream (risking downstream effects that confound analysis), and it offers no spatial perspective.
- **Extensive Post-Treatment (10 or more watersheds):** Gives wide spatial coverage, a long temporal perspective, and the ability to assess interactions between physical setting and treatment. It requires the least time (as little as 2 years) and is probably the most economical per unit of information. However, it lacks pre-treatment data and forces assumptions that control and treated sites were identical on average.

The authors conclude that no single design is optimal. The best approach combines post-treatment analysis with carefully designed process studies at one or more locations.

## Key Findings

1. **Natural variation is large and can mask impacts.** Temporal and spatial variation can reach several orders of magnitude in extremes, and even at minimum it is enough to hide significant NPS perturbations.
2. **Long-term data exist.** Studies running up to 15-20 years have documented temporal variation in abundance, and spatial surveys have documented variation between sites.
3. **Physical habitat is the strongest explanatory factor.** Cover and other physical features of salmonid habitat best describe both spatial and, to a lesser extent, temporal variability.
4. **Streamflow matters.** It is associated with both temporal and spatial variation and can help explain changes caused by flow fluctuations.
5. **Biological drivers are less certain.** Food abundance and predation may influence abundance, but the evidence is weaker than for physical factors.
6. **Habitat quality rating systems should be prioritized.** Assessment of physical features is the first priority for minimizing natural variation when evaluating NPS impacts. Such systems have been used mainly for spatial variation but show promise for temporal variation.
7. **Stream classification should be developed further.** The most useful systems come from geomorphology and biogeoclimatic perspectives, focusing on a stream's potential for biological production rather than its momentary condition.
8. **Basic process studies are needed.** Research on the physical and biological processes governing growth, mortality, and production would clarify natural variation.
9. **Paired comparisons improve impact studies.** Using paired comparisons in study design helps separate natural variation from pollutant effects and supports monitoring of recovery time.
10. **No single watershed design is best.** A combination of post-treatment analysis with intensive process studies at selected sites is recommended.
