# Relationships and Dynamics of Balanced and Unbalanced Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Alabama Agricultural Experiment Station bulletin (No. 274, June 1950), written by fish culturist H. S. Swingle, defines a "balanced" fish population as one that yields satisfactory harvestable crops year after year, and develops a set of quantitative indices to distinguish balanced from unbalanced populations. The indices are the [[F/C Ratio]], the [[Y/C Ratio]], the [[Ay Value]] (the share of biomass of harvestable size), and the [[E Value]], together with the A, I and S values computed within each forage species. The work draws on 55 balanced and 84 unbalanced populations sampled mainly by draining ponds and, in a few cases, by [[Rotenone Poisoning]]. The bulletin argues that populations must be understood as dynamic systems, not static species lists, if fisheries are to be managed effectively.

## Background & Description

The bulletin first reviews census methods used in fisheries research: draining and counting ponds (the most accurate, but impractical for natural waters), electrofishing (Haskell), cresol and copper sulphate treatments, mark-recapture, and [[Rotenone Poisoning]] (described as the most satisfactory method for most natural waters and large impoundments). It notes serious biases in these methods: rotenone-poisoned fish continued surfacing for about 4 days, and recovery of marked fish was incomplete (86 per cent in one study; 58.9 per cent of marked bluegills and 44.7 per cent of marked trout in another). Small fish were often under-recovered.

The author criticises the lack of standard reporting. Earlier workers grouped species into "game," "forage," "coarse," or "rough" fish, which the author considers artificial. He proposes instead functional groups based on feeding relationships.

The study covers 89 well-established pond populations, 2 to 80 years old, censused at the Alabama station over about 15 years. Most were censused by draining; four were poisoned with rotenone.

Key species categories:
- **"C" (carnivorous/piscivorous) species:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (all sizes), spotted bass, pickerel, gar, bowfin and crappie above 4 ounces.
- **"F" (forage) species:** [[Bluegill]], shellcrackers, green sunfish, warmouth, bullheads, channel and blue catfish under 2 and 8 lb respectively, [[Gizzard Shad]], golden shiner, carp, and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Balance mechanism:** High reproductive capacity means more young are produced than can grow to harvestable size. Predatory [[Largemouth Bass]] must control young forage fish. A balanced population needs at least one piscivorous species.
- **Bluegill-bass example:** Stocking 1,500 bluegills per acre in a fertilised pond, with about 1,000 surviving to 4 oz within a year, illustrates how delicate the balance is. Each pair can produce about 5,000 young in one summer.
- **Fertility control:** The weight of forage fish depends mainly on pond fertility. The weight of predators depends on the forage base. Reduced fertilisation lowered F/C ratios because forage declined faster than predators.
- **Harvest and interspecific competition:** Unharvested species such as [[Gizzard Shad]], carp, buffalo and suckers suppressed reproduction of forage species and stunted bass in several ponds.
- **Overcrowding:** Stocking bass at higher rates sharply reduced fish production by the second year (for example, F/C fell from 10.0 to 2.2 in one fry-stocking series).

## Key Findings

**Definitions and framework**
- A balanced population yields harvestable crops year after year; it requires natural reproduction, at least one piscivore, and a combination of small, intermediate and large fish.
- Unbalanced populations fail either through inefficient species combinations or, more often, through overcrowding.

**F/C ratio** (weight of forage fish ÷ weight of predators)
- Balanced range: 1.4 to 10.0; 94.8 per cent of balanced populations fell between 1.4 and 6.8.
- Unbalanced range: 0.06 to 65.1. Populations above 10.0 were all unbalanced.
- Most desirable: 3.0 to 6.0 (77 per cent of the best ponds), though 66 per cent of the poorest balanced ponds also fell here, so other indices are needed.
- A stocking-based example recommends F/C = 4 as a target and gives calculated stocking numbers for both bluegills and bass.
- A three-year pond series gave F/C values of 5.1 (year 1 on draining) and 4.6 (population on draining); repeated restocking gave 2.8 to 8.2 in the catch.

**Y/C ratio** (pounds of small forage fish available as prey per pound of predator)
- Balanced range: 0.02 to 4.8. Values 0.02 to 0.5 indicated severe overcrowding of predators.
- Most desirable range: 1.0 to 3.0. Values above 4.8 were always unbalanced.
- The ratio is dynamic but stays within narrow limits in balanced populations; each rise in small fish must be matched by a rise in predators.

**Ay value** (percentage of total weight in harvestable-size fish)
- Balanced range: 33 to 90. Most desirable: 60 to 85.
- Unbalanced populations: 0 to 40, with two exceptions.
- The author proposes Ay = 40 as the maximum allowable depletion and Ay = 33 as the threshold below which imbalance occurs.
- Hook-and-line fishing did not reduce Ay below 40 per cent in any experiment, but seining reduced it to 23.6 and 20.7 in two ponds, both of which became unbalanced.

**E value** (percentage of total population weight of a species)
- In a bluegill-bass system, the theoretical range for largemouth bass is 9.1 to 41.6 per cent; the desirable range is 14 to 25 per cent.
- Average of 26 balanced bluegill-largemouth bass populations: E largemouth bass = 22.8 per cent, E bluegill = 77.2 per cent.

**A, I and S values within species**
- Forage-fish groups are defined as large (harvestable), intermediate (too large for predators to eat, too small to harvest) and small (edible by predators).
- In bluegill-bass systems, A-values in balanced populations ranged from 18.2 to 99.6 (average 58.9). The minimum satisfactory value was 35.5; the desirable range was 60 to 80.
- S-values in balanced populations ranged from 0.4 to 80.9. The desirable range is 15 to 40; values above 60 indicate inefficiency.
- Intermediate-group weight (I-value) normally stayed below about 7 per cent of total weight.

**Correlations in dynamic populations (12 bluegill-bass ponds)**
- S-value vs. predator (C) weight: r = +0.701 (significant at 1.2 per cent).
- A-value vs. C weight: r = −0.646 (significant at 2.5 per cent).
- A-value vs. S-value: r = −0.782 (significant below 1 per cent).
- Corrected C-equivalent (5 lb small fish = 1 lb predator) gave r = −0.740 for A-value.

**Harvest effects**
- Harvesting adult forage fish increases small forage fish and predator weight. Failing to harvest adult forage fish (e.g., unharvested gizzard shad) suppresses reproduction; in one case the Y/C ratio fell to 0.3, and in another to 0.07, with predators unable to grow.
- Slow harvest of predators lowers the Y/C ratio and eventually the forage base. Rapid harvest of predators maintains Y/C and predator growth.
- Excessive removal of bass by seining caused imbalance.

**Management implications**
- Stocking should use calculated F/C and Ay targets. For example, a 400 lb/acre pond stocked to F/C = 4 requires about 1,280 bluegill fingerlings and 114 advanced bass fry or fingerlings.
- Ponds should be harvested for forage fish, with predators harvested at a moderate rate, to keep indices in the balanced range.
- The bulletin concludes that these standards can only be fully applied once adequate samples from other waters are measured.
