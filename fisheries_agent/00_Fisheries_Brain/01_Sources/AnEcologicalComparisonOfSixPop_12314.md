# Ecological Comparison of Six Populations of Brown Trout (Salmo trutta)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[James T. McFadden]] and [[Edwin L. Cooper]] (Pennsylvania State University), published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, compares fish populations in six Pennsylvania streams of the Susquehanna River drainage: three hard-water (limestone, fertile) and three soft-water (infertile) streams. Population density was estimated by mark-and-recapture electrofishing, with the [[Slimy Sculpin]] excluded. Standing crops of all fish ranged from 46 to 354 lb/acre, and brown trout standing crops from 13 to 137 lb/acre. Average annual survival of brown trout ranged from 0.189 to 0.554. Growth rate correlated significantly with [[Specific Conductance]], which the authors used as an index of water fertility. Recruitment was largely stable, apart from variations linked to floods and drought.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the study around a long-standing question in fisheries science: whether environmental fertility controls fish production, and through which pathways it acts on growth, mortality, standing crop and production. Most prior work had been done in lakes, where conductivity and total dissolved solids were linked to productivity (see [[Rawson]], [[Northcote and Larkin]], [[Moyle]], [[Carlander]]). This study applies the same logic to streams.

The six study streams were chosen as representative of Pennsylvania brown trout water:

- **Hard-water streams:** Cedar Run, Spring Creek and Spruce Creek, flowing through limestone valleys in the Ridge and Valley province, mostly surrounded by meadows and tilled land.
- **Soft-water streams:** Young Woman's Creek, Kettle Creek and Shaver Creek, in sandstone and shale terrain of the Allegheny Plateau or adjacent valleys. These are largely forested. Shaver Creek is heavily shaded.

Specific conductance (reciprocal megohms, adjusted to 25 °C) was used as a proxy for total dissolved solids and total alkalinity. Hard-water streams had conductivities of 280.9 to 411.5, and soft-water streams 36.4 to 43.5. The terms "hard water" and "fertile water" are treated as synonymous, as are "soft water" and "infertile water."

Nineteen fish species were recorded. Brown trout, brook trout and rainbow trout are salmonids, and the remainder are minnows, suckers, a madtom, a centrarchid, a percid and a sculpin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods overview**

- Stream widths were measured by tape, and conductivity was measured with a portable conductivity bridge in February–March 1960.
- Population estimates used mark-and-recapture electrofishing in 1958. Size-group estimates were partitioned among species according to second-run catch composition.
- Weights were calculated from a coefficient of condition (R = 1.60 for trout), and scale samples were used for age determination.
- Survival was estimated from age composition using the methods of Jackson (1939) and Ricker (1958).
- Growth was summarized as the average instantaneous daily growth rate from fry emergence to November of the fourth year.

**Population and recruitment**

- Trout in Spring Creek and Spruce Creek suffered reduced fingerling numbers in 1959, coinciding with severe winter floods that scoured the stream beds. Redds disappeared and no developing ova were found.
- Cedar Run had near-complete reproductive failure in 1958 and again in 1959. The unstable gravel substrate was suggested as a cause.
- Soft-water streams had low water in fall 1957 due to drought, and the 1958 year class was smaller there.
- Hatchery fish made up 22% of brown trout in Young Woman's Creek, 16% in Shaver Creek and under 2% elsewhere.

**Exploitation**

- Young Woman's Creek prohibits killing trout (fly fishing only), and it had the highest average survival.
- Cedar Run is private and lightly fished. Spruce Creek is private and more intensively fished. Spring and Kettle Creeks are public and popular. Shaver Creek is public but small.

**Comparative context**

Reported brown trout standing crops from elsewhere include 14 lb/acre in Crystal Creek, New York; 297 lb/acre (unfished) and 69 lb/acre (fished) in Convict Creek, California; 10 lb/acre in the Pigeon River, Michigan; and 47–68 lb/acre in Prickly Pear Creek, Montana. Reported annual survival rates include 0.20 for Michigan trout streams (Shetter and Hazzard, 1939), 0.15 in Convict Creek, and 0.28 then 0.17 in the Horokiwi, New Zealand.

## Key Findings

- **Standing crop:** Total standing crops ranged from 46 to 354 lb/acre. Brown trout standing crops ranged from 13 to 137 lb/acre. Spruce Creek had the highest trout standing crop (1,080 fish/acre, 137 lb/acre).
- **Dominance:** Excluding sculpins, brown trout were numerically dominant only in Cedar Run and Spruce Creek. By weight, trout dominated in Cedar Run, Spruce Creek and Young Woman's Creek. White suckers supported standing crops above 200 lb/acre in Spring and Spruce creeks.
- **Survival:** Annual survival of brown trout ranged from 0.189 (Kettle Creek) to 0.554 (Young Woman's Creek). The unweighted mean for all six streams was 0.358, for soft-water streams 0.392, and for hard-water streams 0.323. Survival was highly variable among streams and not consistently linked to water type.
- **Age structure:** Brown trout older than age-group IV were almost absent. The authors note that survival to ages reported in Norwegian waters (up to age XV) is practically nil in these Pennsylvania streams.
- **Recruitment:** Recruitment was fairly stable, except where drought or flood intervened. About 17% of cases suggested broods of one-third or less of usual strength.
- **Growth:** Growth was fastest in Cedar Run (0.673%/day) and Spring Creek (0.664%/day), and slowest in Shaver Creek (0.559%/day). Soft-water streams grew at nearly the same rate. Spruce Creek grew faster than the soft-water streams, which the authors attribute possibly to its high density.
- **Water fertility and growth:** Growth rate correlated strongly and significantly with specific conductance (r = 0.913, p < 0.05). The authors caution that the observations clustered at two extremes, so the relationship may not be linear.
- **Standing crop and fertility:** Conductivity correlated with total fish standing crop (r = 0.817, p < 0.05), but not significantly with trout standing crop or non-trout standing crop, possibly because of unmeasured factors such as selective fishing and temperature.
- **Competition:** Correlations between trout and non-trout standing crops were negative, the sign expected under competition. None approached significance, so the authors conclude that controlled experiments are needed.
- **Management implications:** Sizable trout populations occur in streams widely considered infertile. Water fertility may partly control growth and could be altered to increase production. Continued basic study of production-limiting factors is recommended.
