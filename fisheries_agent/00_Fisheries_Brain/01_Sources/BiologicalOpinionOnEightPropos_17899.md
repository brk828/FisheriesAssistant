# Biological Opinion on Eight Proposed Prescribed Burns, San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuges

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion, issued under [[Section 7 Consultation]] of the [[Endangered Species Act]], evaluates eight proposed prescribed burns on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (SBNWR) and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] (LCNWR) in Cochise County, Arizona. Six burns totaling 888 acres lie in the [[Black Draw]] drainage on the SBNWR, and two totaling 70 acres lie in the [[Leslie Creek]] drainage on the LCNWR. The listed fishes considered are the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]], the endangered [[Yaqui Chub]] and the threatened [[Beautiful Shiner]], with critical habitat for the chub and shiner on the SBNWR.

The Service concluded that the burns, as proposed, are not likely to jeopardize the fishes or to destroy or adversely modify designated critical habitat. It also concluded that the burns are not likely to adversely affect the [[Lesser Long-Nosed Bat]] or the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], and that the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]], [[Huachuca Water Umbel]] and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] are also not likely to be adversely affected. The main aquatic concern is [[Robertson Cienega]], the only fish-bearing area inside a burn perimeter. The opinion anticipates incidental take there only if burning causes a substantial water quality change that kills or sickens fish.

## Background & Description

The proposed action has three goals for the upland and floodplain burns: reducing fuel loads near riparian zones, setting back woody brush such as [[Mesquite]] and acacia, and restoring natural grasslands. The Robertson Cienega burn aims to set back living cattail (Typha) and bulrush (Scirpus), remove dead material, and maintain open water. Ignition uses drip torches. Fire control lines are existing roads, natural firebreaks, or mowed, disked or burned strips. [[Class A Foam]] may be used to build control lines but not within 50 feet of surface water. Water for fire control is limited to the sites listed in the project table.

The Yaqui topminnow is a small livebearing poeciliid. It is listed only in the United States portion of the Rio Yaqui basin, where its historic range included Whitewater and Black Draws. The chub is a medium-sized cyprinid, and the beautiful shiner is a small cyprinid that was extirpated from the United States and reintroduced to the SBNWR in 1990. Habitat loss from water diversion, downcutting, channelization, grazing and groundwater pumping drove the declines. Nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis), first found in the U.S. portion of the basin in 1979, are especially damaging to topminnows.

Yaqui topminnow occur on the SBNWR and LCNWR in more than a dozen spring and pond sites, and many populations are stocked in enhanced or artificial habitats as part of recovery. Yaqui chub occur in nine SBNWR sites, with only a few chubs caught in Robertson Cienega during 1994 monitoring. Beautiful shiner occur in five SBNWR ponds. The chub's taxonomy was revised in 1991, when the Rio Sonora, Rio Matape and downstream Yaqui chub were assigned to a separate species, Gila eremica. Gila purpurea is now treated as endemic to San Bernardino Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental baseline.** Human use of southeastern Arizona for centuries has altered vegetation and fire regimes. Mesquite, whitethorn acacia and burroweed have likely increased at the expense of desert grassland. Riparian and wetland areas on the SBNWR have declined from historical conditions, part of a regionwide trend. Baseline data from [[Kepner 1988]] reported 5.51 mg/l of [[Dissolved Oxygen]] at West Border Well, below the state standard of 6.0 mg/l. The source tends to be deoxygenated, and West Border Well is the water supply for Robertson Cienega.

**Listed-species status in the action area.**
- *Yaqui topminnow and chub:* the only affected population is in Robertson Cienega. Populations in Bathhouse Spring, Twin and Evil Twin Ponds, Oasis Pond, Black Draw and Leslie Creek are near burns and would be affected only if fire escapes the control lines.
- *Beautiful shiner:* occurs outside the burn perimeters. Its critical habitat includes Robertson Cienega, although shiners do not currently occur there.
- *Lesser long-nosed bat:* no known roosts near the burns. The Prospect burn has fewer than 2 agaves per acre, so its forage value is likely trivial.
- *Southwestern willow flycatcher:* uses the refuges only during migration, with no breeding records. Dense willow habitat does not occur in or next to the burns.

**Cumulative effects.** Expected regional growth around Douglas, Arizona and Agua Prieta, Sonora may bring pollution, more visitation and more resource use. Declining artesian well flows on the SBNWR are already documented. The opinion notes that the North American Free Trade Agreement could add growth pressure.

## Key Findings

**Predicted effects of burning.**
- Upland burns (four, 40 to 615 acres) are relatively flat and distant from riparian zones, so increased runoff and sedimentation are expected to be minimal. The West Mesa and Cemetery burns are of greatest concern because they lie closest to riparian areas.
- Sediment yields after fire drop to near preburn levels within 3 to 5 years. Because Black Draw is deeply incised, short-term sediment may help aggrade the channel and benefit listed fishes long term.
- Three floodplain burns (40, 65 and 20 acres) are also near riparian zones. Burns in similar settings increased herbaceous plants, and sacaton stands recovered within 2 to 3 years when left undisturbed.
- The Robertson Cienega burn carries the greatest concern. Its effects are judged small based on:
  - Burned cattail and rush produce little ash and energy, and water temperature changes are expected to be minor.
  - Bouffard's marsh study recorded nutrient spikes that returned to normal within 36 hours.
  - An Arizona wildfire study found no marked fish or macroinvertebrate change immediately after the fire.
  - At House Pond, a 10-minute cattail burn produced no dead fish one week later.
- Fire-fighting foam is toxic to rainbow trout and foam concentrates are toxic to fish, but use at 1 percent or less is nontoxic and the chance of foam reaching water is remote.

**Success criteria.** The burns are considered successful if at least 50 percent of the area is burned, and roughly 90 percent removal of some species is the hoped-for result. Unburned islands within each perimeter are encouraged.

**Effect determinations.** The burns are not likely to jeopardize the Yaqui topminnow, Yaqui chub or beautiful shiner, and are not likely to destroy or adversely modify designated critical habitat of the chub and shiner. Water quality changes are expected to last minutes to a few hours.

**Incidental take.** Take of Yaqui chub and topminnow is anticipated only if burning causes a substantial change, from a preburn baseline, in [[Dissolved Oxygen]], carbon dioxide, temperature or pH in the upper 20 centimeters of Robertson Cienega. No take of the beautiful shiner is anticipated. The opinion notes that fish deaths are hard to detect and attribute.

**Reasonable and prudent measures.**
1. Minimize mortality of Yaqui topminnow and chub.
2. Minimize destruction or modification of their habitat.
3. Keep complete, accurate records of fish populations and water quality in Robertson Cienega before, during and after burning.

**Key terms and conditions.**
- Class A foam is banned within 50 feet of surface water and anywhere in the Robertson Cienega drainage basin. [[Fusees]] start the Robertson Cienega burn.
- Fire must be kept out of riparian and aquatic habitats, with protecting lives and property as the only higher priority.
- Water may be drawn only from Table 1 sites, except in emergencies.
- The West Farm Field and Robertson Cienega burns must occur in different years. The Robertson Cienega burn is split, with pond islands and margins burned at different times where possible, at least one week apart.
- Burn critiques, postburn monitoring and a follow-up evaluation are due to the Arizona Ecological Services State Office by November 30, 1995.

**Conservation recommendations.**
- Develop a long-term fire management plan for both refuges, subject to programmatic consultation.
- Design burns with unburned areas inside the perimeter.
- Conduct detailed pre-, intra- and postburn water quality studies in Robertson Cienega, and publish the results.
- Inventory and photograph vegetation before and after each burn.
- Study whether changing cattail or bulrush cover affects chub, topminnow or shiner populations.
- Start no more than half the refuge burns in any one year.

**Reinitiation.** Consultation must be reopened if the incidental take limit is exceeded, new information reveals unforeseen effects, the action is modified in a way that causes new effects, or a new species is listed or critical habitat designated that may be affected.
