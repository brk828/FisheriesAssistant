## Executive Summary

This 1988 letter from Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Office of Endangered Species proposes an expansion of a native fish conservation program to newly constructed water features at the ASU Golf Course in Tempe, Arizona. Building on a successful 1986 stocking effort at the ASU Research Park (ASURP), the proposal requests approximately 1,500 juveniles each of [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] (now known as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]), and [[Bonytail]] from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Dexter National Fish Hatchery. The letter frames the golf course ponds as a novel, low-competition refuge habitat that would support research, captive broodstock development, and public outreach for three critically imperiled "big river" fishes of the Colorado River Basin.

## Background & Description

- **Precedent Program**: Native "big river" fishes were previously stocked at ASURP water features in 1986, described as achieving "tremendous success" for research and educational purposes.
- **New Opportunity**: The ASU Sun Devil Foundation was constructing an 18-hole championship golf course adjacent to the university, incorporating substantial water acreage suitable for fish habitat.
- **Proposed Species**: Three federally imperiled Colorado River Basin natives were proposed for stocking:
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus) — family [[Catostomidae]]
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius), now standardized as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] — family [[Leuciscidae]]
  - [[Bonytail]] (Gila elegans) — family [[Leuciscidae]]
- **Source of Stock**: Juveniles (~1,500 of each species) were requested from the **Dexter National Fish Hatchery** (Dexter, New Mexico), a key USFWS facility for endangered Southwestern fish propagation.
- **Timing Rationale**: Water features were newly filled at the time of writing; stocking was planned for **November 1988** to allow:
  - Colonization and development of algal, planktonic, and benthic communities
  - System stabilization
  - Completion of mechanical check-out of the water feature infrastructure

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Artificial Habitat as Refuge**: The golf course water features were expected to be initially devoid of non-native fishes, creating a temporary predator/competitor-free environment allowing native fish to grow and mature — a rare condition for these species in the wild.
- **Anticipated Invasion Dynamic**: Based on prior experience at [[ASU Research Park]], non-native fish species were expected to eventually invade the golf course ponds via connectivity to the **Salt River Project (SRP) canal system**, with some establishing reproducing populations over time.
- **Research Value of Invasion**: Rather than viewed purely as a negative, the anticipated non-native colonization was framed as a valuable opportunity to study **interspecific interactions between native and non-native fishes**, particularly given non-natives' implicated role in the historical decline of Colorado River Basin natives.
- **Recruitment Research Gap**: The letter explicitly notes that studying **recruitment dynamics** (survival from spawning to reproductive adulthood) in wild populations of these species has been "particularly difficult," positioning the controlled golf course environment as a proxy study site.
- **Habitat Connectivity Context**: The water features exist within a broader urban/suburban water conveyance network (SRP canals), linking artificial habitats to the regional hydrologic system — a pathway both for research access and for unintended non-native fish introduction.

## Key Findings

1. **Dual-Purpose Conservation Strategy**: The proposal integrates *ex situ*-like captive population management (broodstock development) with *in situ*-like ecological research (predator/competitor interactions, recruitment) in a semi-natural artificial habitat.
2. **Broodstock Contingency**: Survival of stocked juveniles to adulthood was expected to provide a supplementary broodstock reserve for the three endangered species, hedging against wild population declines.
3. **Comparative Research Framework**: The ASU Golf Course program was explicitly designed to run parallel to the existing ASURP program, enabling comparative data collection across two similar but distinct artificial habitats.
4. **Public Engagement Value**: The high public visibility and use of a golf course facility was identified as a strategic asset for raising public awareness of native fish conservation — considered "an integral aspect of species recoveries."
5. **Institutional Collaboration**: The letter cc's a wide network of stakeholders spanning USFWS (Phoenix, Dexter NFH), the Arizona Game and Fish Department (Nongame Branch, Fisheries Branch, Hatcheries), and multiple ASU personnel, indicating a coordinated multi-agency conservation effort typical of Colorado River Basin native fish recovery programs in the late 1980s.
6. **Species Conservation Status**: All three requested species — [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and [[Bonytail]] — were recognized as critically imperiled "big river" fishes native to the Colorado River Basin, reflecting the broader endangered species recovery context under USFWS's Office of Endangered Species.
