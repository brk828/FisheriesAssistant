# Changes in Minnow Populations in a Small Desert Stream Resulting from Naturally and Artificially Induced Factors

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracks population structure, size, condition and standing crop (biomass) of three minnow species in [[Seven-Springs Wash]], a small tributary of [[Cave Creek]] in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of central Arizona, from 1969 to 1971. Population changes were driven by two factors: a 1968 flood season and a series of experimental [[Rotenone Renovation]] treatments (1970 and 1971) intended to remove the dominant species before stocking two endangered Gila River cyprinids. Length, weight and biomass changes were consistent across years, but condition factor (K) did not follow parallel trends among species. Total fish biomass was dominated by one species, [[Longfin Dace]], which was itself strongly reduced after flooding and renovation.

## Background & Description

The author notes that no published data existed on fish production or biomass for the remaining low-elevation streams of the arid American Southwest. Most available biomass data came from eastern streams, lakes and reservoirs and concerned sport or commercial fisheries.

**Study site.** Seven-Springs Wash is about 2 km long, running from its confluence with Cave Creek (below an artificial concrete fall of 1.5–2.0 m) to its headspring. Physical characteristics:
- Mean depth: 19.6 cm
- Mean width: 3.32 m
- Total surface area: 0.55 ha
- Flow rate: 0.66 m³/min

The area is lightly disturbed, with grazing and tourism as the main human influences. A [[Forest Service]] picnic ground and an irrigation canal diverting 3.0 m³/min from the headspring are present.

**Study species.**
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*): a small stream fish that thrives in shallow, sandy habitats. The author considers it non-native to this stream, since early museum records lack it, contradicting an earlier account by [[Stout et al. 1970]].
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus osculus*): native, widespread in the western US, normally confined to reaches above 1,067 m elevation; it is a strong disperser with many local forms.
- [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*): endemic to the Gila River basin, a deep-bodied chub that occupies pools and marshes. It was formerly widespread but is now restricted to fewer than 10 localities in southern Arizona.

**Management context.** In March 1970, [[Arizona State University]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[Tonto National Forest]] began a program to establish two rare and endangered Gila River cyprinids in the wash: the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Their stocks, several hundred of each, came from [[Aravaipa Creek]], Pinal County.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and weather.** Mid-to-late summer 1968 brought 16.8 cm of rain, producing a major flood. The OCR text gives about "74 cm" of this falling in July, which is impossible given the total; the figure is probably 7.4 cm, and about "1t cm" in a 10-day period is probably 1.1 cm. Treat these two values as scan-affected. Increased streamflow and flooding were considered the main cause of reduced 1969 populations and of the altered age structure.

**Barrier effect.** The artificial waterfall at the lower end of the wash blocked recolonization from downstream. This was central to interpreting the 1970–1971 results, since survivors and fry could not be replaced by immigrants.

**Seasonal control.** Sampling dates were chosen to be temporally similar (spring) across years, which limited variation from seasonal population cycles, following [[Larimore 1955]].

**Gila chub habitat.** This species is largely confined to a small, constant-temperature headspring pool. It moves into the channel during high precipitation and runoff, but the headspring location gave it more protection from flooding than the other two species.

**Flood responses.** Based on earlier work in [[Sycamore Creek]] (Maricopa County), where flooding nearly eliminated longfin dace, the author suggests Agosia moves into stream margins during floods and is often displaced downstream. Speckled dace are reported to spawn in response to flooding in southern Arizona, and flash floods may kill very small fish-of-the-year.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- Specimens were collected by electrofishing, seine and dipnet in 1969–1971 and preserved in 10% formalin. Fish were weighed to the nearest 0.1 g and measured as standard length (SL) to the nearest mm.
- Many 1970 longfin dace were weighed in lots of 100, and about 40% of the 1971 longfin dace weights were estimated from length–weight relationships.
- Condition factor (K) was calculated for approximately 3,000 specimens across the three years, using a formula modified from [[Lagler 1952]]. Longfin dace from 1971 showed no significant differences in K by sex or age, so this was assumed for earlier years.
- Some 1969 data (from [[Stout et al. 1970]]) were converted from total length to SL using species-specific empirical factors. These data supplied length–weight reference values but could not provide biomass estimates.

**Size and population structure**
- Mean length of longfin dace was significantly lower in 1969 and after the second renovation (1971). Average weight was also lower in both periods, and the weight difference appears statistically significant given sample size.
- In 1969, 65% of longfin dace were below 45 mm SL, compared with only 10% in 1970 and about the same proportion in 1971.
- All speckled dace collected in 1969 and 1971 were below 57 mm SL; in 1970 only 44% were that small. The large share of small dace in 1969 and 1971 points to a younger population.
- Speckled dace sample sizes in 1969 and 1971 were too small (n = 13 and n = 7) for significance testing.
- Gila chub showed the same increase-then-decrease size pattern, with significant differences between 1969 and 1970, and between 1970 and 1971. Adults over 100 mm were scarce in 1971.

**Condition (K)**
- Mean K increased in all three species from 1970 to 1971.
- No consistent cross-species pattern in K was found. For longfin dace and Gila chub, K was inversely related to mean size; for speckled dace, it was directly related (1969–1970 data).
- The share of longfin dace in better-than-average condition (K ≥ 2.13 per the study's criterion) fell in 1971 relative to 1969 and 1970.

**Biomass and dominance**
- Longfin dace comprised about 95% of the total fish population and biomass in both years of the study.
- Speckled dace made up only 4–5% of total fish numbers and biomass.
- Longfin dace biomass fell to about 10% of 1970 levels in 1971 (value as reported). Speckled dace biomass fell to 0.1% of 1970 levels.
- Gila chub biomass increased by about 25% from 1970 to 1971 (OCR value; verify).

**Gila chub status**
- Only five specimens were found in summer 1971, and an attempt in May 1973 failed. The author considers the population "exceedingly rare, or extirpated" in Seven-Springs Wash.

**Interpretation**
- Smaller fish in 1969 were attributed mainly to the 1968 flood season, with reproduction after July rains possibly producing the young cohort sampled in spring 1969. Gear differences (a 6.4 mm seine versus a 3.0 mm dipnet) were judged not to explain the size shift.
- The 1970 to 1971 decline in size and biomass is best explained by increased reproduction in a less competitive environment, combined with flooding in September 1970 and the waterfall barrier. A May 1973 observation of few adult longfin dace and abundant fry after a record precipitation year supports this sequence.
- The author concludes that the renovation method did not eliminate longfin dace, and that flooding and the barrier strongly shaped the community. Each species responded individually to changing stream conditions, in line with [[Larimore 1955]].
- Comparison with [[Engstrom-Heg and Loeb 1971]] and [[Eschmeyer and Clark 1939]] shows that post-renovation responses vary: recolonization or spawning cycles, rather than simple growth, can drive size changes.
