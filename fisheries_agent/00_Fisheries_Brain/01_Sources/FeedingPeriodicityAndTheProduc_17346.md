# Feeding Periodicity and Daily Growth Increments in Steelhead Trout and Starry Flounder Otoliths

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tests whether [[Feeding Periodicity]] and [[Starvation]] affect the formation of [[Daily Growth Increments]] in the [[Otoliths]] of juvenile [[Steelhead Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Starry Flounder]] (*Platichthys stellatus*). Fish were reared for 64–76 days under feeding regimes ranging from thrice daily to once every three days, with starvation periods of up to 32 days in one tank. Both species continued to produce daily increments under all regimes and during starvation. Feeding frequency affected increment appearance and the incidence of subdaily increments in trout but not in flounder, which the author attributes to the higher [[Metabolic Rate]] of trout. Flounder increment deposition was far more variable, and a few individuals apparently stopped forming increments altogether. The study also reports the first record of daily increments in [[Vaterite]] (vateritic) otoliths.

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- **Steelhead Trout (*Salmo gairdneri*)**: Young-of-the-year fish from a hatchery, injected with an oxytetracycline mark and held in 200-L flow-through tanks. The fish were 7.58 cm standard length (SD = 0.66 cm) at marking.
- **Starry Flounder (*Platichthys stellatus*)**: Young-of-the-year fish collected from Bellingham Bay, Washington, roughly 7 months old, with a mean standard length of 5.36 cm (SD = 0.79 cm). They were held in 50-L aquaria and fed daily meals of live tubificid worms.

**Background concepts**

- Daily increments were first described in fish otoliths by Pannella (1971) and have since been confirmed in many species. They are now used to age larval fish, estimate daily growth rates, and assess life-history changes.
- The author notes that the controls on increment production are poorly understood. Proposed influences include an internal [[Circadian Rhythm]] entrained by a 24-hour light–dark cycle, [[Photoperiod]] as a [[Zeitgeber]], and diel temperature fluctuation. Evidence on photoperiod is conflicting.
- Before this work, no study had tested more than one nondaily feeding regime.

**Objectives**

1. Examine the role of feeding periodicity and starvation in daily increment production.
2. Compare the consistency of increment deposition between a species with a high metabolic rate (trout) and one with a low rate (flounder).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental conditions**

- **Trout:** Four 200-L tanks (30 fish in tanks 2–4, 18 in tank 1; 111 fish total). A seminatural photoperiod was maintained with an outdoor photocell. Water temperature started at 10°C and fell to 4.5°C by the end of the experiment. The diet was commercial trout food.
- **Flounder:** Three 50-L aquaria with 30 fish each (tanks 2–4), plus 25 fish in tank 1 (90 fish injected in total). Fish were held under a 14 h light : 10 h dark photoperiod at 16°C, which fell gradually to 10°C with no diel temperature fluctuation observed. Salinity was <5‰, matching the collection site.

**Feeding regimes**

| Tank | Trout | Flounder |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Once daily for 13 days, then starvation up to 32 days | Starvation from day 10 (sampled sequentially) |
| 2 | Once every 3 days, 64 days | Once every 3 days, 74–76 days |
| 3 | Once daily, 64 days | Once daily, 74–76 days |
| 4 | Thrice daily (8-h intervals), 64 days | Thrice daily, 74–76 days |

Food quantities were roughly equal across tanks. Excess food was removed 4 h after each feeding.

**Metabolic context**

The author cites standard metabolic rates for salmonids of about 75 mL O₂·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 10°C (sockeye salmon; [[Brett and Glass 1973]]). For pleuronectids of about 23 mL O₂·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 10°C ([[Platichthys flesus]]; [[Jorgensen and Mustafa 1980]]). The study's own fish were not measured for metabolic rate, so these values are representative only. The trout were also reared at lower temperatures, which the author argues would mask rather than enhance short-term metabolic responses.

**Natural feeding**

Rainbow trout often feed several times a day. Young flounder feed intertidally once or twice daily, depending on the tides. Periods of starvation probably occur in most wild individuals.

## Key Findings

**1. Daily increments form under all feeding regimes**

Both species produced daily increments when fed thrice daily, daily, or once every three days, and during starvation. Increment counts in trout did not differ significantly among feeding regimes (P > 0.05). Feeding frequency did not affect flounder counts either.

**Table 1. Mean daily increment counts by feeding regime**

| Species | Regime | Increment count (mean ± SE) | Days elapsed | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flounder | Once per 3 days (tank 2) | 73.5 ± 3.7 | 76 | 20 |
| Flounder | Once daily (tank 3) | 72.2 ± 2.1 | 75 | 27 |
| Flounder | Thrice daily (tank 4) | 67.6 ± 6.5 | 74 | 11 |
| Trout | Once per 3 days (tank 2) | 60.6 ± 1.1 | 64 | 29 |
| Trout | Once daily (tank 3) | 62.8 ± 0.6 | 64 | 28 |
| Trout | Thrice daily (tank 4) | 62.8 ± 1.0 | 64 | 28 |

- In trout, fish fed once every three days deposited significantly fewer increments than expected for daily production (Scheffé's test, P < 0.05). The shortfall was under 6%, so the author attributes it to poor resolution rather than missing increments.

**2. Starvation does not stop increment formation**

- Trout starved for up to 32 days and flounder starved for up to 26 days kept depositing daily increments (Figure 1).
- Regression of increment number against time gave slopes of 0.95 for trout (R² = 0.98) and 0.90 for flounder (R² = 0.82). Neither slope differed significantly from 1.0 at the 95% confidence level.
- Starvation began on day 13 for trout and day 10 for flounder.

**3. Feeding frequency changes trout microstructure but not flounder microstructure**

- Trout starved for several days showed narrower increments, becoming noticeable 0–4 days after starvation began. Contrast between adjacent increments also decreased.
- Subdaily increments in trout appeared to increase with feeding frequency. None were seen in starved fish. These were visual observations and were not counted.
- Trout fed once every three days showed a subtle 3-day pattern (one dark increment followed by two fainter ones) from 15 to 30 days after the start of the experiment. Increment width did not vary within the pattern.
- Flounder microstructure showed no observable effect of feeding periodicity. Some starved flounder produced narrow increments, but others did not, and no 3-day pattern was seen.

**4. Flounder increment deposition is far more variable**

- Flounder sequences were often interrupted by otolith checks (discontinuities), and increment width was irregular, especially near checks.
- Standard errors were much higher for flounder than for trout (Table 1), and subdaily increments contributed much more to flounder variance.
- One to six flounder from every feeding regime deposited far fewer than one increment per day on one or both sagittae. The author could not attribute these cases to counting error or otolith curvature.
- A small number of flounder apparently ceased increment formation at various times after tetracycline injection. SEM examination ruled out narrow increments as the cause. Otolith curvature explained some "lost" increments, but the remaining cases are unexplained. Similar anomalies were previously reported in juvenile and larval pleuronectids.

**5. Daily increments occur in vateritic otoliths**

- 27% of trout sagittae were vateritic ("crystalline"), a glasslike form of calcium carbonate, rather than the usual aragonite. Daily increments were still present in these otoliths (Figure 2).
- In sagittae that were partly aragonitic and partly vateritic, increment counts were similar in the two forms.
- Light refraction through vaterite produced visual artifacts, so counts of subdaily increments were unreliable.
- Tetracycline incorporation did not differ appreciably between the two minerals. This is the first report of daily increments in vateritic otoliths.

**6. Measurement reliability**

- In well-prepared sagittae, visual, photographic, and SEM counts did not differ significantly (P > 0.05).
- Narrow increments were hard to resolve visually. Etching for SEM was not possible on curved otoliths, especially in flounder.
- Each fish's counts were averaged across both sagittae. A count more than 30% below its pair was treated as anomalous and excluded. Anomalies were uncommon in trout but more frequent in flounder.

**7. Interpretation**

- The author proposes that the greater trout response reflects the faster assimilation of food in fish with higher metabolic rates. Changes in feeding are therefore reflected quickly in increment width and subdaily increments.
- Body fat reserves may let fish keep forming daily increments during starvation. Fish larvae lack these reserves and may stop forming increments, so [[Age Determination]] of starved early-stage larvae by increment counting may be unreliable. Starved [[Northern Anchovy]] larvae showed this pattern.
- The author concludes that increment number is not proportional to feeding frequency. Increments are therefore probably reliable for [[Age Determination]] and growth studies in wild fish, though the results cannot be extended to early-stage larvae.

**8. Methodology summary**

- Oxytetracycline (20 mg/mL) was injected intraperitoneally to create a fluorescent mark on the otolith, with the medial edge of the band corresponding to the day of injection.
- Otoliths were prepared by grinding and polishing to the midplane, with counts taken from the postrostrum. Counts were checked against [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]] images.
