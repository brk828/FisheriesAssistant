# Dissolved Oxygen and Temperature Profiles of Tenkiller Reservoir and Tailwaters as Potential Rainbow Trout Habitat

## Executive Summary
This 1953 study by Joe C. Finnell (Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory) evaluated whether [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] and its tailwaters on the Illinois River could serve as a viable habitat for introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), a species with no native occurrence in Oklahoma. Since most Oklahoma streams exceed the thermal tolerance of trout during summer, the study tested whether a deep reservoir with a stable [[Hypolimnion]] could supply cold, oxygenated tailwater analogous to the successful [[Norfork Dam]] tailwater fishery in Arkansas. Thermal and dissolved oxygen (DO) profiling revealed a pronounced summer [[Thermocline]] and a severely oxygen-depleted hypolimnion. Tailwater temperatures downstream of the dam were suitable for trout for at least seven miles, but dissolved oxygen — not temperature — was identified as the primary limiting factor for trout survival, especially once hydroelectric penstock discharge (which lacks the aeration benefits of the tunnel outlet) became the primary release mechanism.

## Background & Description
This paper is a limnological/habitat-suitability study rather than a morphological survey of fish; no biometric or anatomical data on rainbow trout are reported. Species-relevant biological notes extracted from the literature review include:
- **Species assessed**: *Salmo gairdneri* (rainbow trout), an artificially propagated and widely distributed exotic species.
- **Thermal physiology (per Embody 1927)**: Optimal feeding/growth range 55°–60° F; upper lethal/feeding limit ~77° F; lower limit for active feeding ~40° F.
- **Diet**: Filamentous algae may compose up to 15% of rainbow trout diet (per Metzelaar 1929); remainder inferred to be aquatic food organisms/invertebrates.
- **Stocking sizes**: 5,000 trout stocked in winter 1952–1953 — 600 individuals at 14–16 inches, remainder at 5–7 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Reservoir structure**: [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] is a deep (max depth 130 ft), narrow impoundment of the [[Illinois River]] (Oklahoma), 34 miles long at power pool, 12,500 acres, sheltered by the Cookson Hills, limiting wind-driven mixing at depth.
- **Thermal stratification**: Distinct **epilimnion** (0–20 ft, avg. 81.4°–84.8° F), **thermocline** (20–40 ft, dropping to 64.4° F), and **hypolimnion** (40–130+ ft, down to 51.8° F at 120 ft) at Station I during June–July 1953.
- **Dissolved oxygen stratification**: Surface DO averaged 8.1 ppm (range 7.0–9.4); DO fell sharply (~6 ppm) between 25–35 ft coinciding with the thermocline; hypolimnion was oxygen-deficient (<1.5 ppm) from 35–130 ft, reaching 0.0 ppm at 120–130 ft — a critical constraint despite favorable cold temperatures.
- **Upstream shallow station (Station II)**: Shallower (33 ft max), more wind-exposed site lacked a consistent thermocline on some dates, showing the interaction between basin morphometry and stratification stability.
- **Tailwater habitat (Stations III–V, 0–7 miles below dam)**: Water released ~120 ft below reservoir surface (from near-anoxic hypolimnetic water) was re-aerated via tunnel turbulence, a 6-ft outlet fall, and algal growth, restoring DO to ~10.3 ppm (>95% saturation) immediately below the dam (Station III), with temperatures averaging 55.4° F.
- **Downstream thermal gradient**: Temperature rose ~2.6° F per mile downstream (Figure 2 regression), reaching 66.3° F at Station IV (~4 mi) and 73.8° F at Station V (~7 mi, near Gore) — comparing favorably to pre-impoundment (1948) average river temperatures of 81.5° F at the same location.
- **Winter conditions (December 12, 1953)**: Reservoir was thermally unstratified (top-to-bottom variation only 0.4° F) with uniform 6.0 ppm DO; however, water released via the hydroelectric **penstock tunnel** (as opposed to the outlet tunnel) showed **no aeration effect**, because the penstock discharges water upward rather than through a cascading/turbulent outlet — foreshadowing summer oxygen deficits downstream once hydropower operations began.
- **Water chemistry**: Methyl orange alkalinity at Station I averaged 71 ppm at surface vs. 93 ppm near bottom; pH ranged 7.4–7.9 at surface, dropping to 6.8–7.0 near bottom, consistent with hypolimnetic anoxia/CO₂ accumulation. Notable H₂S odor detected at Station III tailwater, indicating anaerobic hypolimnetic chemistry in the outflow.

## Key Findings
1. **Thermal suitability confirmed**: The Illinois River tailwater below Tenkiller Dam remained within rainbow trout's tolerable thermal range (53.5°–77.3° F) for at least 7 miles downstream during summer 1953, a dramatic improvement over pre-impoundment river temperatures (72°–89° F, avg. 81.5° F).
2. **Dissolved oxygen — not temperature — is the critical limiting factor**: Reservoir hypolimnetic water was nearly anoxic (<0.5 ppm at release depth), and while the outlet tunnel's turbulence and fall fortuitously re-aerated this water to >95% saturation during initial low-flow (50 cfs) releases, the alternate **penstock tunnel** pathway used during hydroelectric generation provides no equivalent aeration, threatening to extend oxygen-deficient conditions further downstream.
3. **Reservoir morphometry drives stratification stability**: The narrow, deep, wind-sheltered basin near the dam produces a strong, stable thermocline and severe hypolimnetic oxygen depletion (<1.5 ppm from 35–130 ft) — contrasted with the shallower, more exposed upstream Station II, which showed weaker/inconsistent stratification.
4. **Successful analog cited**: [[Norfork Dam]] tailwaters in Arkansas were referenced as a comparable, already-successful cold tailwater trout fishery, motivating this Oklahoma feasibility assessment.
5. **Food resources likely non-limiting**: Abundant filamentous algae observed below the dam, and comparably high productivity to the pre-impoundment Illinois River, suggest food availability would not constrain trout growth.
6. **Management implications**: 5,000 rainbow trout (mixed size classes) were stocked in winter 1952–1953 as a put-and-take exotic species introduction; natural reproduction was deemed unlikely due to reservoir water-level fluctuations, implying any sustained fishery would require continual restocking.
7. **Seasonal variability**: Winter (December) sampling showed the reservoir fully mixed with uniform temperature and oxygen (6.0 ppm) throughout the water column, contrasting sharply with the strongly stratified summer condition — underscoring that summer stratification is the period of greatest management concern for tailwater trout habitat.
