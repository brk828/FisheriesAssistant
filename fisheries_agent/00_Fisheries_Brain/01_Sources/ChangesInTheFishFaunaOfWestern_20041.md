# Changes in the Fish Fauna of Western North America Correlated with Changes in Ocean Temperature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pacific-coast-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1948 paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]] argues that water temperature is the dominant control on the distribution of marine fishes along the [[Pacific Coast of North America]]. Hubbs uses fish distributions to infer past ocean temperatures, and treats the resulting correlation as a justified two-way method. He proposes that ocean temperatures fluctuated substantially during late Quaternary and historic time. Warm-water fish records from the 1850s, especially from the [[Pacific Railroad Survey]] at [[San Diego]] and [[Monterey Bay]], are taken as evidence that southern California waters were warmer than at any later period. The paper supports this with air and sea temperature records, and with documented short-term northward "strays" of southern species during warm years.

## Background & Description

Hubbs frames the study as a broad synthesis, limited to fishes, to western North America, and to the present and near-present. Emphasis is placed on annual and long-term surface temperature changes within historic time. The method is explicitly defended against critics who call the two-way use of data a "vicious circle." Hubbs considers it valid when used with caution, and cites a parallel analysis of the [[Great Basin]] desert basins (Hubbs and Miller, 1948).

The paper is a review with extensive figures on air temperatures at [[San Diego]] and [[San Francisco]] from the 1840s to 1948, and on sea temperatures at [[Scripps Pier]], La Jolla. It draws on fish records, oceanographic surveys by [[George F. McEwen]], and fishery and collecting reports. The author notes that the detailed data are presented "sketchily" and that much remains to be collated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Latitudinal transition and local reversals.** Fish faunas shift from Arctic-type to tropical-type assemblages from north to south along the coast. The change is sharpest at [[Point Conception]], separating subtropical southern California from subarctic central California. The pattern is reversed in northwestern Baja California. There, [[Upwelling]] produces offshore and inshore temperatures roughly 1 °C cooler than near San Diego during spring and summer. Recorded surface temperatures on 8 August 1948 were 15.1 °C near the upwelling focus south of the border, and 13.6 °C at Santo Tomás Anchorage. Warm exceptions occur in sheltered [[Todos Santos Bay]], where temperatures reached 22.6 °C. Central California species, including the reef seaperch *Amphigonopterus aurora*, and invertebrates such as the red abalone (*Haliotis rufescens*) and purple sea urchin (*Strongylocentrotus franciscanus*), reappear in these cool Baja upwelling areas.

**Upwelling and bay effects.** Along the Oregon and Washington coasts, upwelling lowers inshore temperatures and excludes Californian reef fishes such as *Gibbonsia montereyensis* and *G. metzi*, which reappear in British Columbia. Warmer bays host a more southern fauna than the open coast. [[Morro Bay]] is an example, with warm shallow mud flats. It supports the California killifish (*Fundulus parvipinnis*), a southern subspecies of the staghorn sculpin, and a northernmost population of a pipefish.

**Depth and seasonal control.** In regions where temperature is roughly uniform with depth, some intertidal species of central California occur in cooler infratidal water in southern California. Wide-ranging species, such as the ratfish (*Hydrolagus colliei*), tend to occur deeper toward the south. Seasonal movements are also temperature-linked. Marlin (*Tetrapturus mitsukurii*) appear off San Diego when surface temperatures approach 70 °F, and albacore (*Germo alalunga*) are sought at 60 to 65 °F.

**Single-season northward dispersals.** During warm years, pelagic and free-swimming southern species appear far north of their range. The most notable example is 1926, when [[Mola Mola]], [[Albacore]] and the siphonophore *Velella* reached British Columbia. Hubbs attributes these events mainly to warm offshore "blue water" populations moving closer to shore, rather than to long-distance regular migration. He notes that unlike the Atlantic, the Pacific Coast has no regular northward current comparable to the [[Gulf Stream]].

## Key Findings

**1. Temperature governs marine fish distribution.** Hubbs concludes that sea temperature determines much of the distribution of marine fishes along the Pacific Coast. The conclusion is supported by parallel relationships reported elsewhere in the world.

**2. Warm 1850s faunas at San Diego.** The Pacific Railroad Survey (1853–1857) collected about 30 species at San Diego. Six (about 20%) either no longer occur so far north or are now very rare. Notable records include five giant seahorses (*Hippocampus ingens*), four specimens of the tropical pipefish *Dermatostethus punctipinne* (never collected again), and two spadefish (*Chaetodipterus zonatus*), with the only later California record in 1931.

**3. Southern types at Monterey.** Of about 20 species reported at Monterey, five or six were southern forms (for example, sand bass, California sculpin, garibaldi, horned shark). Red crabs (*Pleuroncodes planiceps*) also washed ashore there in March 1859, hundreds of miles north of their usual range.

**4. Stable fauna at San Francisco.** About 100 species were reported there from 1853 to about 1860, and most remain common. Only about seven exceptions were noted, and the faunal change was slight.

**5. Persistence into 1880.** Jordan and Gilbert's 1880 survey showed a fauna from San Diego to San Francisco that was still slightly more southern than today, even though 1880 was an unusually cold year.

**6. Air temperature trends.** San Diego air temperatures were high from 1849 to about 1868, trended cooler until about 1910, and then showed moderate warming. The 1850s–1860s warm period is consistent with the fish evidence. Hubbs notes that the weather station was moved several times, but remained within about a half-mile radius from 1860 to 1940, which limits relocation bias.

**7. Air–sea temperature correlation.** Sea temperature at La Jolla and San Diego air temperature are closely correlated. The correlation coefficient between annual mean sea temperature and annual mean minimum air temperature (1916–1946) is 0.87. For 1940–1944, monthly comparisons reached 0.98. Mean sea temperature was 0.64 °C above mean air temperature from 1916–1939, and 0.10 °C below it from 1940–1947.

**8. Pleistocene and Postglacial displacements.** Northern and southern relicts show that isotherms and faunas were displaced during the Pleistocene and Postglacial periods. Relict populations in the upper [[Gulf of California]] (with a Panamaic element) and a specifically distinct trout in the Gila River system likely date from Pleistocene glacial-period southward shifts. Undifferentiated northern relicts in Baja California point to a late Pleistocene or early Recent southward shift. Hubbs suggests the warm Postglacial period as the likely time of northward displacement.

**9. Single-season events.** The warm years of 1926, 1931, 1936, 1941 and 1947 produced abundant southern strays north of their range. Hubbs judges these to be temporary events, not evidence of a long-term faunal shift. The 1926 event was linked to exceptionally high sea and air temperatures along temperate North America. The 1931 event did not extend north of the southern California coast.

**10. Limitations acknowledged.** Hubbs states that Pleistocene fish faunas are too poorly known for direct use, that water temperature data are insufficient to explain long-term changes, and that some discrepancies between early air and sea records remain unresolved.
