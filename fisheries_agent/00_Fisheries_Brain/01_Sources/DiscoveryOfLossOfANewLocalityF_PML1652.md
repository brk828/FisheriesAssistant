# Discovery of loss of a new locality for the Gila topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #mammalogy

## Executive Summary

This document combines two short natural history reports. The first, by [[Andrew T. Smith]], documents the terrestrial mammal fauna of [[Bodie, California]], a small area of about 8 km² in the [[Great Basin]] with 30 species, nearly one-tenth of the mammal species in North America north of Mexico. The second, by [[Randy McNatt]], reports the discovery in July 1978 of a new population of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] in [[San Pedro River]] tributary habitat in Arizona, and its extirpation within three months after an artesian well was modified and capped.

## Background & Description

**Part 1: Mammals of Bodie.** The author trapped at Bodie in fieldwork from 1946 to 1949 and as part of later studies. He recorded 30 species of terrestrial mammals in the 8 km² study area (Table 1 in the source). This is almost one-third of the 93 species listed for Nevada by Hall (1946). Eleven further species were reported from nearby localities that could occur at Bodie, including [[Spilogale Gracilis]], [[Mephitis Mephitis]] and [[Zapus Princeps]]. The author notes that "several species of *Eutamias*" were reported earlier, but only *E. minimus* was trapped.

**Part 2: Gila Topminnow.** The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is an endangered poeciliid that once occupied most of the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico. Its range is now limited to seven natural populations, all in Arizona, plus several experimental reintroduction sites. A second subspecies, *P. o. sonoriensis*, occurs in the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of southeastern Arizona and northwestern Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mammal assemblage.** The lagomorphs at Bodie are notable. The five species present make up almost one-third of the North American lagomorphs north of Mexico. The species segregate by habitat, yet the author saw four species within a single 1 ha area in 20 minutes. Several species that co-occur at Bodie are not normally found together. For example, [[Ochotona Princeps]], a typical alpine and subalpine species, was trapped at the same sites as the Great Basin species [[Perognathus Parvus]], [[Microdipodops Megacephalus]] and [[Dipodomys Panamintinus]].

The author argues that the current fauna is not an artifact of human activity, since historic photographs show little vegetation change. However, [[Ochotona Princeps]] and [[Neotoma Cinerea]] now use [[Mine Tailings]] and old mine buildings heavily. Predator control may have reduced densities of *Vulpes vulpes*, *V. macrotis* and *Felis concolor*. The fauna is thought to derive from the regional species pool of the [[Intermontane West]]. Because there are no major barriers between the [[Sierra Nevada]] and the Great Basin, dispersal could occur within species' physiological limits.

**Topminnow decline.** Decline of *Poeciliopsis* across its native range is attributed to [[Habitat Loss]] from diversions, impoundments and groundwater pumping, plus competition with the introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]]. The decline appears to have accelerated from the 1930s, after mosquitofish were introduced. The San Pedro River Basin had only one earlier record of *Poeciliopsis*, and none of the populations known as of 1973 occurred there.

**New locality.** The 1978 site was an artesian well outflow on the 111 Ranch, about 13 km southeast of Mammoth, Arizona, near the east bank of the normally dry [[San Pedro River]] channel. The outflow formed a marsh about 15 m long and 2 m wide, with a small stream running several hundred metres before joining the river. Topminnows were visually estimated at 500–1,000 individuals in the upper 15 m, and were also found in eddies and backwaters downstream.

## Key Findings

**Mammals of Bodie**

- 30 terrestrial mammal species were recorded in an 8 km² area, about 10% of the species in North America north of Mexico and about one-third of Nevada's 93 species.
- The five lagomorph species at Bodie are about one-third of those in North America north of Mexico.
- Co-occurrence of alpine and Great Basin forms at the same sites suggests a broad, mixed faunal origin, presumed to come from the Intermontane West species pool.
- The author judged that the current assemblage is not an artifact of human intervention, though mine tailings and buildings now support some species in high numbers.
- Coexistence mechanisms were considered likely to be many and not mutually exclusive, and would require detailed field experiments to test.

**Gila Topminnow**

- The July 1978 discovery added a new locality in the San Pedro River Basin, where few *Poeciliopsis* records existed.
- Between early September and early October 1978, changes to the well (irrigation connection, erosion, siltation to 5 cm or less, then capping) led to extirpation of this population.
- A late September 1978 collection found mosquitofish and topminnows in a ratio of about 10:1. The author notes that few sites are known where these two species are sympatric, suggesting *Gambusia* had not been present long enough to eliminate *Poeciliopsis*. Specimens were deposited in the Arizona State University fish collection.
- The well was on private land and therefore not protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The author regrets that the landowner was not contacted about retaining flow or salvaging part of a population that may have carried unique genetic material.
- Notification of the locality and the habitat threat was sent to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Endangered Species Office and the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
