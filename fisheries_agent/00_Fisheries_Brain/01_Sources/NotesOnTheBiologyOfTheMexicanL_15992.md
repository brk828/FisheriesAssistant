# Notes on the Biology of the Mexican Lampreys *Lampetra spadicea* and *L. geminis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports new field, interview, and museum-based information on two lampreys endemic to a small area of central Mexico: the parasitic [[Lampetra Spadicea]] and its nonparasitic derivative [[Lampetra Geminis]]. Surveys in November 1991 of the [[Rio Grande de Morelia]] and [[Rio Duero]] basins, supplemented by commercial fisher interviews at [[Lago de Chapala]] and examination of museum material, indicate that the parasitic species feeds mainly on blood, that nonparasitic adults do not feed, and that the spawning season of both species is longer than that reported for northern lampreys. The authors describe the species as endangered and note that their biology is poorly known.

## Background & Description

Both species are endemic to the highlands of central Mexico, more than 1000 km south of other North American lampreys, and are the only lampreys with a (barely) tropical distribution. Previous knowledge rested mainly on work by Alvarez del Villar (1966) and a few later studies. The authors describe their data as partly anecdotal, particularly for *L. spadicea*, which was not captured in its parasitic phase during the 1991 sampling. Nomenclature follows [[Mayden et al. 1992]], under which *Moxostoma austrinum* is now *Scartomyzon austrinus* and *Notropis calientis* is now *Hybopsis calientis*. Taxonomic status of the Morelia population of *L. geminis* is uncertain, so these lampreys are referred to as *L. cf. geminis*.

Methods included backpack electrofishing (pulsed DC), dip nets, and a small seine at each site for 15-60 minutes, with water temperature and conductivity measured by an electronic meter. Fishermen were interviewed, and museum specimens and literature were reviewed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding of the parasitic phase.** Commercial fishermen associated *L. spadicea* with catfish (*Ictalurus* sp., presumably *I. dugesi*), which reportedly reached 10-26 kg in the past but now rarely exceed 2-3 kg. Fishermen had not seen lampreys for 4-18 years. Lampreys attached to introduced [[Manatee]] (*Trichechus manatus*) stocked to control water hyacinth. Of 35 specimens examined by Alvarez del Villar (1966), most contained only blood; four also had scales from [[Scartomyzon Austrinus]], cyprinids, and possibly *Chirostoma*. The authors examined 19 parasitic-phase lampreys from Ajijic (UMMZ collections) and found mostly blood-like sludge, plus one 264-mm male containing a partially digested *Chirostoma* of about 30 mm standard length. Feeding is therefore largely hematophagous, consistent with other parasitic lampreys.

**Nonparasitic adults and ammocoetes.** Adult *L. geminis* and *L. cf. geminis* had empty digestive tracts. Ammocoetes are filter feeders, mainly on diatoms, with filamentous algae, ciliates, rotifers, and flagellates noted by earlier workers.

**Habitat.** *L. geminis* occurred in streams, spring ponds, and small rivers at 14-21 °C. Adults used pools, runs, and riffles over sand, gravel, and cobble, and were usually associated with cover such as undercut banks, woody debris, leaf packs, or large cobbles. Ammocoetes were buried in sand or silt in slow pool margins. Conductivity at Rio Duero sites ranged from 105 to 163 µS/cm and temperature from 16.0 to 20.9 °C. Habitats were rated "fair" to "good" for environmental quality and biotic integrity (Lyons et al., 1995).

**Co-occurring fauna.** Across six Rio Duero drainage sites, 13 additional fish species were found, with three to ten per site. The most frequent native fishes were *S. austrinus* (five sites), [[Zoogoneticus Quitzeoensis]] (five sites), and *Goodea atripinnis* (four sites). Five exotic species were recorded: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], *Poecilia mexicana*, *P. reticulata*, and *Oreochromis aureus*. Effects of exotics on lampreys are unknown, though trout are known to prey on nonparasitic lampreys.

**Predators.** The paedomorphic salamander *Ambystoma ordinarium* is a potential predator, but four stomachs examined contained no lampreys.

**Threats.** The species are regarded as endangered, with apparent scarcity and an uncertain future. Persistence of *L. spadicea* in the upper Rio Duero may depend on its ability to use small hosts in shallow lotic habitat, since migration to and from Lago de Chapala is no longer possible in some areas.

## Key Findings

- **Restricted range:** Both species are endemic to a small area of central Mexico, far south of other North American lampreys.
- **Parasitic feeding is mostly blood:** Most gut contents of *L. spadicea* consisted of blood, with occasional fish remains. This matches other parasitic lampreys.
- **Atypical host:** Lampreys attached to introduced manatees, which has not been reported for sirenians elsewhere.
- **Nonfeeding adults:** Adult *L. geminis* and *L. cf. geminis* have empty guts.
- **Extended spawning season:** Mature or spent adults were collected from October to June, and spawning was observed by early November in 1991. Earlier reports had indicated only December and January. The authors suggest that spawning may last six months or more.
- **Latitudinal pattern hypothesis:** Lamprey spawning seasons may lengthen toward lower latitudes, a pattern also seen in darters, minnows, and salamanders. The authors recommend future surveys be scheduled to test it.
- **Conservation concern:** Catfish hosts have declined sharply, lampreys have not been seen by local fishers for years, and exotic species are present at many sites.
