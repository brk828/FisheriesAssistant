# Science Missing in the "No Surprises" Policy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1997 draft white paper, written by consulting ecologist Dr. Shawn Smallwood for the Spirit of the Sage Council and the National Endangered Species Network, argues that the proposed [[No Surprises Policy]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] would weaken species protection by limiting the use of sound science and adaptive management once a [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (HCP) and [[Incidental Take Permit]] (ITP) are in place. The paper was prepared as a comment aid for scientists, small landholders and the public during the Federal Register comment period. It defines the scientific standards the author considers applicable to ESA biological assessments, explains why surprises are inherent to nature, science and mitigation, and proposes conditions that should be met before permits are issued.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Draft policy paper (June 1997), commissioned by the Spirit of the Sage Council.
- **Author:** Dr. Shawn Smallwood, consulting in the public interest (New Jersey and Davis, California offices).
- **Policy context:** The federal government agreed, under a settlement of a lawsuit (*Spirit of the Sage Council et al. v. Bruce Babbitt et al.*), to a full public review of the No Surprises policy as applied to HCPs and ITPs. The comment period was set to close in July 1997.
- **Legal framework:** Section 10 of the ESA requires that HCP/ITP actions not appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival and recovery of listed species, using the best scientific information. The paper also cites Section 4 (critical habitat), Section 2 (conservation of ecosystems) and the Supreme Court standard for scientific testimony from *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*.
- **Policy mechanism:** Under the proposed policy, the Services (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and National Marine Fisheries Service) would assure permit holders that they would only need to respond to unforeseen circumstances if the permit holder had followed a properly functioning HCP. The author argues this shifts conservation costs to the public.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sources of surprise identified by the author:**

- *Environmental stochasticity:* Coral reefs, tropical rain forests and midwest stream fish assemblages are shaped by periodic catastrophes that clear ecological space.
- *Inadequate scale:* Studies at different resolutions can yield different results, even at the same site and with the same data.
- *Improper experimental design:* Reserves are described as "not an experiment" because they lack controls, replication and interspersion.
- *Weak theory:* Key ecological terms such as [[Population Density]] are vaguely defined; the author cites a density-body mass relationship that he says resulted from circular logic.

**Incidental take and mitigation sources:**

- *Habitat fragmentation and cumulative impacts:* Described as the most certain impact of development and the primary cause of the biodiversity crisis. Populations are clustered and shift through time, so small preserves may not sustain them.
- *Failed translocation:* Translocation of animals and plant communities has achieved poor success. The author contends the [[Safe Harbor Policy]] further threatens conservation by allowing relocation without later responsibility.
- *Inadequate species accounts:* Reconnaissance surveys are often inadequate, and sighting records are treated as unreliable for proving absence.
- *Unquantified impact assessments:* Consultants usually assess impacts qualitatively, without confidence intervals or error rates.

**Scientific tools discussed:** [[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA), uncertainty and sensitivity analysis, GIS-based habitat mapping, ecosystem indicators, and peer review.

## Key Findings

1. **The No Surprises policy conflicts with the ESA.** The author argues the policy would have the Services conclude that HCPs are functioning properly, rely on faith in initial science, forgo adaptive management, and disregard the widely reported fact that surprises are inherent in species distributions and abundance.

2. **Science is provisional.** The paper draws on [[Karl Popper]] and [[Thomas Kuhn]] to describe science as hypothesis testing. It notes that sensory perception is limited, that scientists hold different world-views, and that observations are not independent, which means ecologists often violate the assumptions of their statistical tests.

3. **Scientific standards for ESA compliance.** The author lists conditions for adequate assessment:
   - Distribution and abundance estimates made at multiple spatial scales, with at least one generation of data for listed species.
   - Error rates and uncertainty ranges attached to all estimates, consistent with *Daubert* admissibility.
   - Critical habitat designated through mensurative or manipulative experiments with replication, not only expert opinion.
   - Ecosystem assessments conducted by ecologists using multi-scale indicators.
   - Adaptive management and mitigation implemented as experiments involving scientists.

4. **Population Viability Analysis is central.** The author describes PVA as the method for estimating time to extinction, probability of extinction and minimum viable population size. He states that smaller spatial areas increase extinction vulnerability, so reductions in habitat should be assumed to reduce populations proportionally unless analysis shows otherwise.

5. **Independent peer review is needed.** The author argues that peer review should be mandatory for HCP/ITP documents, that consultants hired by permit applicants have a conflict of interest, and that the Services and local government biologists should not act as independent reviewers because they issue permits.

6. **Reasons scientific standards are missing.** Consultants have financial incentives to minimize impacts, academics are seldom involved in compliance work, and consultants often use scientific methods selectively.

7. **Conclusion and recommendations.** The paper concludes that conservation of listed species is unlikely to succeed under the current HCP/ITP framework because of short timelines for data collection, inadequate mitigation ratios and funding, unassessed risk and uncertainty, and the absence of independent review. It calls for scientists to participate actively in the public comment process and for permit decisions to meet modern scientific standards, enforceable through legal action by citizens and the Services.
