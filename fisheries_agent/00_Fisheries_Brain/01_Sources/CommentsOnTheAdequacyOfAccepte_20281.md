# Comments on the Adequacy of Accepted Stream Bottom Sampling Technique

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1939 paper by [[Justin W. Leonard]] of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] (Michigan) reviews the [[Stream Bottom Sampling]] methods used in fisheries surveys and tests the accuracy of a single 1-square-foot sample. Leonard endorses the collapsible brass-frame [[Surber Net]] as the most satisfactory tool available. Repeated sampling of three restricted, uniform areas in Michigan streams in 1937 showed that total food-organism volume was reasonably consistent per sample. The species composition of those samples varied widely, so one sample is a reliable index of food production per unit area but not a complete census of species. The paper also documents shrinkage of invertebrates in [[Alcohol Preservation]], lists practical limitations of the method, and identifies research gaps in the [[Aquatic Insect Food Cycle]] of [[Game Fish]].

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Fisheries workers sought ways to measure how different stream bottom types contribute to stream food supply. The bottom invertebrate fauna supplies most of the food eaten by stream fish. Its composition and abundance vary with bottom type, flow rate, depth, and streamside cover.
- **Context:** Bottom sampling was paired with fish censuses in stream survey programs. Results informed [[Stocking Policy]] and estimates of the [[Carrying Capacity]] of streams for game fish, especially [[Trout]].
- **Sampling devices:**
  - The [[Needham Sampler]], used in New York State drainage surveys (Needham, 1928), enclosed 1 square foot in a galvanized metal box. It was bulky.
  - A wooden, collapsible device enclosing 4 square feet was used in Michigan during stream improvement experiments. It was abandoned in 1934.
  - The [[Surber Net]], devised by A. S. Hazzard, P. R. Needham, and E. W. Surber for the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries, was adopted in 1934. It has two 2-foot-square brass frames hinged together, with a silk grit-cloth bag that catches dislodged material carried by the current (Surber, 1937). The Institute has used it since 1934.
- **Sampling sites:**
  - *North Branch of the Boardman River* (Grand Traverse County): a gravel bed of about 110 square feet. The stream was about 21 feet wide and 6 to 8 inches deep, with a surface velocity of about 2 feet per second. Banks of 2 to 5 feet were lined with an open growth of aspen (*Populus tremuloides*) that gave no appreciable shade.
  - *Pine River* (Lake County): a uniform gravel riffle with heavy marl coating. The stream was about 37 feet wide and 8 inches deep, with a surface velocity of about 2¼ feet per second. Shade came from a high bluff in late afternoon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dominant taxa:** Chironomid midge larvae were ubiquitous. In the Boardman gravel bed, the most influential organisms by number or volume were cranefly larvae (*Eriocera*), snipe fly larvae (*Atherix variegata*), and elmid beetle larvae. In the Pine River riffle, the greatest bulk came from larvae and pupae of a Glossosomatinae caddisfly, probably *Agapetus*. The cranefly role seen in the Boardman was taken by *Pedicia* in the Pine.
- **Life stages:** Most Glossosomatinae were very young larvae of 2.0 to 3.5 mm. Very young larvae and advanced pupae of the same species occurred together, suggesting possible overlapping generations with two adult emergence peaks.
- **Seasonal effects:** Mayflies and stoneflies were scarce in the Boardman samples, likely because most emergence peaks had passed by July.
- **Marl and beetles:** Elmid beetles were very numerous where marl was scanty (Boardman) and very scarce where marl was most abundant (Pine). Marl recesses shelter burrowing organisms such as *Atherix* larvae, elmid beetles, and midge larvae.
- **Plant beds:** Dense [[Potamogeton Filiformis]] beds (water about 24 inches deep, sand bottom with slight clay and organic debris) held almost no bottom fauna. Their fauna was mainly [[Simuliidae]] (blackfly) larvae and pupae and tanypodine midge larvae living on the plants. Leonard suggests these beds may be nearly free of animal life after the blackfly and midge emergence period.
- **Giant snails:** Large specimens of *Goniobasis livescens* were abundant in the Pine samples. Their distribution was very irregular, so they were excluded from the main invertebrate totals and listed separately.
- **Food-web gaps:** Complete life histories are known for few aquatic insects, and few immature stages are identifiable. Stomach analyses cannot yet show whether many species enter fish diets. The effect of predators (Odonata nymphs, *Atherix*, tabanid larvae) on herbivorous and detritus-feeding taxa is poorly understood.

- **Pine River, dense Potamogeton beds:** Five samples gave total volumes of 4.5, 4.3, 4.3, 4.3, and 4.1 cc. Taxonomic detail is deferred to a planned separate study.

**Shrinkage in preservation (Table 3)**

- Snails and insects were measured fresh in 1937 and again after 15 months in 73 per cent alcohol.
- Average snail volume fell from 7.1 cc (fresh) to 5.6 cc, a shrinkage of 1.5 cc.
- Average insect volume fell from 2.045 cc to 1.615 cc, a shrinkage of 0.430 cc.
- Leonard concludes that a correction factor is required when comparing preserved and fresh samples.

**Method conclusions**

- Repeated samples of a uniform bottom yield very similar total volumes, but species composition varies greatly.
- One carefully handled sample gives a reasonably accurate index of food organisms per unit area, but not a full picture of species abundance across larger areas.
- The sample set is small (five, ten, and fifteen samples across three areas), so positive conclusions are limited. Leonard states that the agreement among results is nonetheless strong enough to rely on single samples for volume estimates.

**Limitations of the [[Surber Net]] method**

- *Cold weather:* At about −25°F, anchor ice forms on the bottom and the net can freeze solid, letting dislodged material wash out.
- *Rough substrate:* It is hard to seat the frame accurately in rough gravel, rubble, or loose shale. Burlap padding partly helps on uneven bedrock.
- *Quiet water:* The net is nearly useless without enough current to carry material into the bag. A folding screen converting the sampler to a cube helped, but it fails in water a foot or more deep.
- *Winter sorting:* Organisms cannot be sorted alive in winter unless shelter is very close, so whole samples are preserved, and many organisms are likely overlooked.
- *Time:* Most samples required two people working through one to one and a half days. Sorting live organisms is slow, and burrowing forms in marl are hard to recover. The saturated salt-solution flotation method, developed in Britain and then tested in America, does not help with burrowing taxa.

**Priorities and recommendations**

- Insect life histories and identification of immature stages need much more study.
- Stomach analyses must determine whether many species enter the diet of game fish.
- The effects of predatory insects on the bulk of the bottom fauna must be studied.
- These needs must be met before stream fish food production can be managed efficiently through environmental control.
