# Most desert fish sinking toward endangered list

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Cyprinodontidae #desert-fishes #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This source is a newspaper opinion/review piece discussing the book *Battle Against Extinction*, co-edited by ichthyologists [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Deacon]], which compiles scientific, historical, and ethical perspectives on the decline of desert fish fauna in the American Southwest. The article uses the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically called "Colorado River salmon" or squawfish) as a case study for the broader collapse of native desert fish faunas due to damming, habitat alteration, and invasive species introduction. It highlights that nearly half of indigenous fish species west of the Continental Divide are threatened or endangered, with Arizona serving as a stark regional example. The piece also engages with conservation ethics, citing philosopher [[Holmes Rolston]] on the intrinsic value of endangered species independent of utilitarian human interest.

## Background & Description

- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (referred to historically as "squawfish" or "Colorado River salmon"):
  - Largest minnow species native to North America.
  - Historical size: up to 5 feet (~1.5 m) in length and 80 pounds (~36 kg).
  - Predatory/piscivorous body form consistent with large-bodied Cyprinidae.

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*):
  - Distinctive humped morphology (implied by common name); described in the source as perceived by the public as "ugly or useless," despite ecological/evolutionary significance.

- **Pupfishes** (Cyprinodontidae, e.g., Devils Hole/Desert Hole Pupfish, Monkey Spring Pupfish):
  - Small-bodied fish adapted to isolated, saline, thermally extreme desert spring habitats.
  - Example population cited: a self-sustaining school of ~20 individuals persisting for eons in a "bathtub-sized pool of saline water."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical range of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]**: Rivers of the western U.S. from Mexico to Wyoming, including the Colorado, Salt, Verde, and Gila rivers. Now extirpated from the Salt, Verde, and Gila rivers, and struggling to persist in the mainstem Colorado River.
- **Human use**: Historically consumed by Indigenous peoples; supported a commercial fishery in Arizona through the 1940s.
- **[[Colorado River Basin]]**: Cited as harboring more endemic fish species than any other river basin in North America, yet also hosting 87 non-native (introduced) fish species — an extreme case of faunal replacement.
- **Habitat degradation drivers**:
  - Damming of all major rivers in the region (e.g., [[Hoover Dam]], completed 1934), converting free-flowing rivers into reservoir/regulated systems.
  - Riparian habitat alteration: plowing and chemical spraying of river deltas.
  - Invasive species proliferation, notably:
    - **[[Red Shiner]]** — described as rapidly spreading with no known effective control method.
    - **[[Common Carp]]** — introduced to the western U.S. in the mid-1800s by an ichthyologist who later became the first U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service commissioner.
    - Non-native sport/food fishes now dominating Arizona lakes: bass, bluegill, carp, catfish, crappie — none native to the state.
- **Isolated spring/pupfish habitats**: Represent long-term, geographically isolated evolutionary lineages persisting in small saline desert pools, illustrating extreme habitat specialization within Cyprinodontidae.

## Key Findings

- Nearly **half of all indigenous fish species west of the Continental Divide** are now threatened or endangered (per Minckley).
- **Arizona status**: Of 35 native fish species, 20 are endangered or threatened, 5 are proposed for listing, and 1 (**Monkey Spring Pupfish**) is confirmed **extinct**.
- Minckley describes whole native fish faunas of major western river basins as "now collapsing."
- The greatest current threat to Arizona's indigenous fish fauna is identified as **invasive/exotic species**, rather than habitat loss alone — exemplified by the unchecked spread of the [[Red Shiner]].
- The [[Colorado River Basin]] exhibits a paradox: highest endemism of any North American river system, alongside the highest documented number of non-native species (87).
- Riparian habitat damage claims are addressed: a Chamber of Commerce study disputing environmentalist claims of "90 percent" riparian damage in Arizona is called partially correct by Minckley, who states the true figure is inaccurate but clarifies that "there is no pristine place left" in the state.
- **Conservation ethics framing** (via philosopher Holmes Rolston): argues against evaluating species worth solely through anthropocentric utility ("What's in it for us?"). Extinction is framed as an irreversible loss of a unique evolutionary lineage/gene pool — likened to "tearing pages out of an unread book."
- Minckley frames desert fish conservation as tied to long-term human survivability, not short-term economic costs, stating the issue is about "the survivability of our descendants."
