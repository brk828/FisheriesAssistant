# Razorback Sucker Recruitment in Lake Mead: Comments on Reasonable and Prudent Alternative 10

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This 1997 memorandum from [[W. L. Minckley]] of Arizona State University to [[Mike Walker]] at the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] comments on Provision (RPA) 10 of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Biological Opinion]] on Lower Colorado River operations and maintenance, and on the Lower Colorado River International Boundary. RPA 10 calls for Reclamation to identify conditions in [[Lake Mead]] or the [[Grand Canyon]] that allowed successful [[Recruitment]] of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*), develop a plan with state agencies to duplicate them, and begin implementation by May 15, 2001.

Minckley argues that the premise of RPA 10 rests on verbal reports, speculation and hearsay rather than citable published sources. He considers the presence of adult fish in Lake Mead plausible through several non-exclusive routes: reproduction within the reservoir, entry from [[Grand Canyon]] before [[Lake Powell]] cold-water releases, or survival of progeny from earlier stock. He concludes that the scenario is "not an unreasonable conclusion," but that the Lake Mead population is unremarkable and does not reveal any unique operational cause that could be reliably identified. He also concludes that reproducing the conditions is unlikely to be feasible, because it would require aging individual fish (which means sacrificing them) and very close-order operational data that are not available.

## Background & Description

The memo focuses on the razorback sucker, a long-lived, low-turnover catostomid, cited as having a lifespan of roughly 40 to 50+ years. The species was historically plentiful from the late 1930s until the early 1970s, according to the memo. Adults have been widely reported in the lower Colorado River system, including [[Lake Mohave]], Lake Mead and some canals. Minckley reviews reports of small fish (some confirmed as young) in several locations, including seepage ponds along canals in southern California and canals of the Colorado River, and cites [[Otolith Aging]] estimates for some individuals.

Minckley defines [[Recruitment]] as the addition of reproductive adults to a population through production, survival and maturation of progeny. He notes that a 1:1 replacement ratio is needed for a population to persist. In this species, "recruitment failure" refers to offspring survival and maturation being insufficient to maintain viable numbers, which could eventually lead to [[Species Extinction]]. Eggs number roughly 1,800 per centimetre of standard length. The memo illustrates the sensitivity of [[Population Viability]]: a rise in survival from about 0.1% or lower to around 1% could more than double a theoretical population of 1,000 in a single generation, though the population would remain on a final decline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memo emphasises that the razorback sucker's early life stages are exposed to [[Predation]] and other mortality. Minckley finds it "almost inconceivable" that all eggs and larvae are consumed, given fecundity and the reproduction reported in Lake Mohave and below, Lake Mead (larvae collected in spring 1997, per P. Holden, personal communication) and the upper basin. He notes that predation or other mortality factors may still be responsible, citing [[Nutritional Limitation]] of recruitment (Horn 1997).

Operational context includes the construction and filling of [[Davis Dam]] and its effects on water temperature. Minckley cites Jones and Sumner (1954) and later literature on how fish in Lake Mead could have arisen from [[Hypolimnic Water]] changes. Grand Canyon fish may have been affected after 1962–63, when cold hypolimnic water from Lake Powell began to chill the river. Reservoir water management may provide opportunities to maintain the augmented population, but its flexibility requires further study.

Genetic work by [[Mitochondrial DNA Diversity]] studies (Dowling et al. 1996a, b) is cited in the reference list as relevant to population structure and to [[Hatchery Propagation]] for conservation, though the memo does not draw specific conclusions from it.

## Key Findings

- **Premise of RPA 10 is weakly documented.** Minckley states that RPA 10 appears to rest on verbal reports and hearsay, not published sources, and that the basis for its claims could not be verified.
- **Multiple explanations are possible for adult fish in Lake Mead.** These include in-lake reproduction at one or more times over 60+ years, entry from Grand Canyon before cold water altered conditions, and progeny of the stock reported as plentiful from the late 1930s to the early 1970s. Minckley considers the combination of these routes plausible.
- **Fish age and size evidence is limited.** Ages of fish in Lake Mead are assumed from the time since entry, with fish older than 35+ years expected if no reproduction followed entry. Ageing individuals requires sacrifice unless new non-lethal techniques become available.
- **Anecdotal evidence.** A reported "[[Lunker]]" fish caught in Lake Mead was reportedly released unharmed; the memo says no photograph exists, though records may be on file with the Nevada Department of Wildlife.
- **Unlikely that Reclamation can replicate past conditions.** Minckley argues that the conditions that produced recruitment cannot be reproduced reliably, because they would depend on a historical sequence of events that occurred during dam construction and filling, and the required operational data are unavailable. Recruitment also depends on local conditions and cannot be assumed to apply across sites.
- **Recruitment is widespread but not demonstrated as a sustained cohort.** Small numbers of adults are reported across the basin, and some recruitment must occur in the upper basin, but a year class with sustained production has not been documented, according to the memo.
- **Timeline and implementation.** RPA 10 requires Reclamation to decide whether a plan is feasible and, if so, to start implementation by May 15, 2001.
