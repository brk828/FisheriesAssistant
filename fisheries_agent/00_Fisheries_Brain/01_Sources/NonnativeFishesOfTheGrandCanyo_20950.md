# Nonnative Fishes of the Grand Canyon: A Review of Their Effects on Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nonnative-fishes

## Executive Summary

This literature review by Allen Haden (Glen Canyon Environmental Studies, 1992) examines how introduced fishes have affected the native fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] through the [[Grand Canyon]]. The review was prepared to inform the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement (GCDEIS) and partly meet Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act review requirements. It documents at least 24 introduced species, of which seven are established in numbers sufficient to affect native fishes. It concludes that native fish in the Grand Canyon are under pressure from both nonnative species and dam-driven physical changes, and it recommends that any river temperature or flow change account for the risk of nonnative invasion.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The review supports Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) Phase II, which evaluates dam effects on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The objective was to assess, through literature review, the introduction of nonnative fishes and their effects on natives.

**Pristine conditions.** Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]], spring runoff peaks averaged 2400 cubic meters/second, and the river was turbid with a constantly changing substrate. Water temperatures ranged from just above freezing in winter to as high as 28°C in summer. The native assemblage consisted of only eight species, with a high degree of endemism (Minckley, 1982). Pre-dam distributions are incompletely known from early explorer records.

**Native fishes.** Four native species are federally listed as endangered: humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*), razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and Colorado River squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). The bonytail and squawfish are believed extirpated from the Grand Canyon reach. The last reported squawfish capture was in 1975 at the mouth of Havasu Creek. Three natives, [[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], persist throughout the river.

**Introduction routes.** Introductions occurred through sport fishery stocking, immigration from [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]], and "bait bucket" releases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Flows have been controlled by Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Powell since 1963. Flows generally stayed at or below the powerplant capacity of 900 cubic meters/second, except in 1965, 1980, and 1983–1986, when floods exceeded powerplant capacity and lasted longer than typical post-dam flows (Maddux et al., 1987). Sediment load at the Lees Ferry gauge fell from an average of 1500 ppm pre-dam to 7 ppm post-dam (Howard and Dolan, 1981).

**Temperature.** Releases are far less variable than pre-dam temperatures and have rarely ranged far from 8.8°C. Canyon temperatures have been recorded between 5 and 15°C at RM 87. Backwaters at the western end of the canyon exceeded 25°C under steady flows while the main channel stayed cold. The level of [[Lake Mead]] shifts the zone of warm-water influence from around Separation Canyon (RM 240) at high elevations to Pierces Ferry (RM 280) at low elevations.

**Productivity.** Clear releases let more light penetrate, so production is now almost entirely autochthonous. The base of the food web is the green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]], with attached diatoms, aquatic diptera, and the introduced amphipod *Gammarus lacustris*.

**Native status detail.** The razorback sucker is so reduced it is rarely seen, though one was captured near Shinumo Creek in 1984 and others or hybrids were reported near the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). Humpback chub are common in the LCR and around its confluence with the Colorado.

**Sampling caveat.** Eight ichthyological studies have been conducted in the region since 1968, plus McDonald and Dotson (1958) in Glen Canyon. Methods varied and each favored different habitats, so the author judged that trends could not be derived by comparing datasets. Species trends rely on researcher interpretation of abundance rather than standardized catch rates (see Table 1).

## Key Findings

**1. Scale of introductions.** At least 24 introduced species have been recorded. Seven are established in sufficient numbers to affect natives. The rest show how readily fish cross barriers such as dams and rapids.

**2. Major established nonnatives and their documented or suspected effects:**

- **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*): Introduced around 1890 and now one of the most abundant fishes in the system. Carp feed on eggs and larvae, and a stocked larval razorback population in Lake Mohave was lost to carp and green sunfish. Predation on early native life stages has not been documented in the Grand Canyon, though speculated in the LCR.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*): Spread mainly through bait use. Aggressive toward young natives in backwaters, a possible factor in squawfish decline in the Upper Basin. Holden and Stalnaker (1975) judged low densities in the Grand Canyon posed no serious threat.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella lutrensis*): Declined in the Glen Canyon tailwater after 1967–1968, possibly due to cold releases. Highly competitive, and it displaced spikedace in the Verde River.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): Piscivorous as adults and a documented predator on juvenile humpback chub. Rated the most serious potential threat in the LCR, where catfish co-occur with young natives.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** (*Ictalurus melas*): Rare, but a juvenile in the LCR indicates reproduction. Its diet resembles channel catfish, so a population increase could harm natives there.
- **[[Striped Bass]]** (*Morone saxatilis*): Piscivorous in reservoirs and a concern since its 1969 introduction to Lake Mead. Concentrated in the lower canyon April through July. Spawning in Lake Powell has been documented. McCall (1981) gave three arguments against a major effect on natives: turbidity limits sight-feeding, forage fish are scarce, and river temperatures are at the lower limit for egg survival.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): Repeatedly stocked and now reproducing. Documented disappearance of speckled dace from Tapeats Creek after introduction, and predation on native larvae and juveniles.
- **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*): Highly piscivorous at large size. Concentrations near Shinumo and Bright Angel Creeks may threaten juvenile humpback chub if chub populations are established there.

**3. Minor or limited species.** [[Brook Trout]] make up only a few percent of the fishery and depend on stocking. [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) are common in Lake Powell and occasionally captured in the canyon, but spawning in the river is undocumented. Incidental species (cutthroat trout, coho salmon, golden shiner, largemouth bass, green sunfish, Utah chub, yellow bullhead, threadfin shad, and others) are mostly isolated records likely tied to bait use or spillway releases.

**4. Mechanisms of displacement.** Moyle et al. (1985) identify six mechanisms: competition, predation, inhibition of reproduction, environmental modification, parasite and disease transfer, and hybridization. All except hybridization occur in the Grand Canyon. Environmental modification is attributed to human activity, not introduced fish. Complete elimination of natives is poorly documented, with the Tapeats Creek speckled dace loss the only recorded case.

**5. Introduced parasites.** *Lernaea cyprinacea*, a European copepod, was found on juvenile natives in the LCR and Kanab Creek. Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), not native to the Colorado, was recently found in LCR humpback chub.

**6. Critical habitats.** Low-velocity backwaters are important to all life stages of natives and also to nonnatives, so competition and predation are likely there. Warm, low-gradient tributaries (LCR, Kanab Creek) are key native spawning and nursery habitat and also hold catfish, carp and fathead minnows. Cool, high-gradient tributaries (Bright Angel, Shinumo, Havasu) mainly involve trout, and trout spawning runs may displace natives.

**7. Unresolved questions.** The author highlights two: why humpback chub do not spawn more in tributaries other than the LCR despite suitable temperatures, and why adults are not more abundant in the warmer, more turbid lower reach, which resembles pre-dam conditions.

**8. Community stability.** Moyle et al. (1985) reported that coevolved-like communities can form within 20 years of introductions. Since closure of the dam 29 years earlier, warmwater species have declined while salmonids have expanded. The author cautions that any apparent stabilization is tenuous, given fluctuating discharges and continued trout stocking.

**9. Dam management constraints.** Cold releases limit humpback chub spawning to one or a few tributaries. Warming the river could benefit chub but also allow warm-water exotics from Lake Mead to invade. Any warming scenario should therefore be flexible and intermittent. The lower canyon's low productivity may currently act as a "biological desert" that limits predator colonization.

**10. Proposed introduction of [[Rainbow Smelt]]** (*Osmerus mordax*) into Lake Powell as forage for striped bass. Smelt would likely spread as far downstream as Lake Havasu, and only barriers over 2 feet high would stop entry into tributaries. Effects on native fishes and on *Gammarus lacustris* are unknown.

**11. Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Native fish are suffering from both nonnative pressure and physical habitat modification; both must be reduced.
- Any temperature change must consider nonnative fish, with release timing coordinated with nonnative spawning seasons.
- Existing native populations in tributaries should be protected, and nonnative piscivores in those systems reduced.
- No further purposeful introductions of new species should occur.
- Managers should not stock piscivorous trout strains that could migrate downstream.
- Any reintroduction of natives into tributaries should include protection from nonnative competition and predation.
