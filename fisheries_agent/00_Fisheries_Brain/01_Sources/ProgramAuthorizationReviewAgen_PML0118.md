# Arizona Game and Fish Department Sportfish Subprogram: Program Authorization Review Agency Self-Assessment (1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sportfisheries

## Executive Summary

This April 1, 1996 self-assessment by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) Sportfish Subprogram responds to a state Program Authorization Review (PAR). It traces the subprogram from its 1881 territorial origins through statehood, the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] era, and modern ecosystem management; describes its funding through eight dedicated Funds; reviews its strategic plan (Wildlife 2000); evaluates performance measures against targets; compares performance with other states; and argues that the subprogram is effectively achieving its mission. The agency concludes that current funding is appropriate, recommends dropping several measures, and proposes adding new outcome and quality measures.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and mandate.** The subprogram's stated mission (Wildlife 2000) is "to protect, maintain or enhance the distribution, abundance, availability and diversity of cold and warm water sportfishes and their habitats; and to disseminate information about Arizona's sportfish and recreational opportunities for present and future generations." Its legal basis is [[Arizona Revised Statutes Title 17]], with powers and duties under section 17-231.

**Services.** Products and services fall into three legislatively mandated areas:
- Information: publications such as *Wild Times* and *Wildlife Views*, media releases, a [[Fax-On-Request]] line, an Internet home page, and 1-800 lines.
- Management: regulations, habitat work, research, monitoring, and enforcement.
- Recreation: hatchery-produced fish, facilities, and fishing clinics.

**Customers and stakeholders.** Anglers are the primary customers. Stakeholders include tourism, [[Natural Resource Industries]], government agencies, water users, educational institutions, and special-interest groups.

**Historical development.** Key milestones include:
- 1881: the Territorial "Arizona Fish Commission" began importing food fish.
- 1913: the Department of State Game Warden was created, and the first resident fishing license ($0.50) was required.
- 1922: the first state hatchery opened near Springerville.
- 1929: the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] was formed.
- Early 1940s: the [[Put-And-Take Trout Management]] policy was adopted.
- 1950: the Dingell-Johnson Act provided federal aid to sportfish restoration.
- 1984: the Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act was amended.

Trout were stocked extensively because they cannot reproduce in most Arizona waters, while warmwater species were found to reproduce well enough that stocking was rarely needed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's aquatic resources are managed across 165 lakes (359,000 surface acres) and 2,688 miles of streams, with native, non-native, and exotic species. Fisheries conditions change seasonally and annually with environmental conditions and angler use.

Key environmental constraints include:
- **Drought:** reduced water levels, closed trout waters during high fire danger, and fewer ephemeral waters to stock.
- **Endangered species:** many Arizona fishes are listed (7 threatened, 12 endangered) under the Endangered Species Act, which has reduced stocking of some species and may constrain harvest.
- **Water quality:** the subprogram monitors water quality, toxic spills, and fish tissue contaminants.
- **Federal water management:** possible privatization of power marketing by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation could reduce responsiveness to fisheries needs.

Over 80% of sportfishing opportunities occur on federal lands, so coordination with the [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] is central.

## Key Findings

**Funding and costs**
- The subprogram is funded through eight Funds defined in ARS Title 17, primarily license and stamp revenue and federal aid. The [[Sport Fish Restoration Act]] provides a 25% state match requirement.
- The 1992 subprogram cost was $9.2 million, or $1.24 per angler use day.
- Anglers spent an average of $50 per angler day, which the agency estimates generates about $370 million for the state economy. Applying a 1.8 expenditure multiplier gives an estimated $540 to $666 million economic benefit.
- Hatchery trout production cost about $2.50 per pound, compared with $2.85 (Utah), $5.60 (Wyoming), and $2.92 (New Mexico).
- The agency projects a 3 to 4% annual rise in operating costs and about 7.4% annual growth in license revenue.

**Performance results**
- Angler use days: 6.8 million (1986), 7.3 million (1989), 7.4 million (1992), and 7.5 million (1995). The Master List target was 8 million by 1995; the strategic plan now sets 8 million by 2000.
- Licenses sold: 405,041 (1986), a low around 350,000 in 1991 to 1992, and 390,804 (1994). The mean number of days fished rose from 17.2 (1986) to 21.9 (1992).
- Catch per unit effort: the five-year statewide average was 0.48 fish per hour against a 0.50 target. Individual waters ranged from 0.10 to 0.84.
- Angler satisfaction: 77% of 1994 respondents were satisfied (score 6 or higher), with a mean score of about 7 on a 10-point scale. The Master List figure was 70%.
- Management rating: 67% of residents rated fisheries management Good to Excellent (up from 62% in 1992 to 66% in 1994 for information products).
- Stocking: 10 species (3.5 million fish) were stocked in 1995, against a target of 14 species and 2.5 million fish. The agency states no biological reason existed to stock more species that year.
- Fishing clinics: 160 clinics in 1995, exceeding the target of 100.
- News clippings: a mean of 142 fishing-related clippings per month, though the agency notes this period is the slowest of the year.

**Reasons for dissatisfaction**
- The main reason was that anglers did not catch enough or any fish (71% of unsatisfied respondents). Other reasons included polluted lakes (6%), fish in poor condition (5%), and crowding (3%).
- The agency attributes much of this to skill variation, noting that a minority of anglers catch most fish.

**Performance measure decisions**
- Seven measures are recommended to be dropped, including stocked species, number of waters stocked, fishing clinics sponsored, average anglers per clinic, news clippings, species preference, and percent rating clinics good.
- Measures recommended to keep include [[Angler Use Days]] (the most comprehensive measure) and number of licenses sold.
- New measures to add include information products disseminated, public awareness of fisheries issues, public satisfaction with information products, catch per unit effort, revenue from licenses and stamps, satisfaction with recreational facilities, and satisfaction with fishing experience.

**Benchmarking**
- Among 12 state programs, Arizona's average days fished (21.9) ranked in the upper range, and its satisfaction rate (77%) in the middle. Iowa reported 23.0 average days, Illinois 4.5, and Missouri 95% satisfaction.

**Impacts if eliminated**
- Coldwater fishing, which depends on stocked trout, would decline rapidly.
- Warmwater populations could face overharvest and illegal methods such as gillnets.
- Rural economies would lose revenue. Tribal waters, which hosted 380,000 of 7.4 million angler days in 1992, would see increased demand for fee permits.
- The State would retain its public trust obligations under the Endangered Species Act and the Clean Water Act.

**Strategic issues and future outlook**
- Participation may decline if juveniles do not start fishing early, and drought or economic downturns reduce license sales (a two-year drought occurred in 1986).
- The agency plans to shift staff to regional offices and develop watershed management plans.
- A proposed National Diversity Fund (25% state, 75% federal match) could support more direct fisheries projects.
- The agency concludes that the mission is ongoing under state law and that the subprogram should continue, combining in-house and contracted services where appropriate.
