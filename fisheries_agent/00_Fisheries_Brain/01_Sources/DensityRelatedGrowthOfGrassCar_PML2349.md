# Density-Related Growth of Grass Carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) in Small Impoundments in Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how stocking density affects the growth, size and standing stock of [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) in small [[Pond Aquaculture]] impoundments in Alabama. Data came from annual reports of the [[Auburn University]] Department of Fisheries and Aquaculture and from pond trials spanning the first and second growing seasons. Density strongly controlled first-year growth, with individual size falling as density rose, while standing stock rose with density. Second-year standing stock also increased with density, but the relationship was more variable. The authors conclude that the availability of preferred food, especially aquatic vegetation, is the critical factor for individual growth under either fertilized or supplementally fed systems.

## Background & Description

The [[Grass Carp]] was introduced into the United States in 1963 by Auburn University and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to evaluate its value for [[Aquatic Weed Control]]. At Auburn it had been used in pond stocking experiments with a range of other species since 1962. The authors note that comprehensive growth information and pond management practices for the species were unavailable for North America, and that comparable data from other countries (e.g., Asia and Europe) were also limited.

The species is a herbivorous cyprinid whose growth is principally a function of food availability within its genetic growth potential. The study tests how this plays out in managed ponds, where grass carp were stocked alone or in combination with [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bighead Carp]], [[Silver Carp]] and [[Tilapia]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond types and management.** Ponds ranged from 0.02 to 5.0 ha. Smaller ponds were rectangular and managed for aquaculture studies. Larger ponds (0.04 to 5.0 ha) were managed mainly for [[Sport Fishing]] studies, often with bass-bluegill combinations and no supplemental feeding.

**Food and vegetation.** The principal food of young grass carp is aquatic plant material, with young fish being more omnivorous than adults. Grass carp grew rapidly at low stocking densities where vegetation was abundant. Fertilization is used to discourage macrophyte growth, but it can maintain a [[Phytoplankton]] bloom that shades the bottom and reduces vegetation. Daily supplemental feeding of organics was also used in some ponds.

**Feeding regimes.** Where feed was supplied, it was given at about 3% of body weight (or of catfish biomass in mixed ponds). Conversion of dry feed (food weight/fish weight) ranged from 0.9 to 2.2. The contribution of natural versus feed-induced production was not known.

**Survival.** Mortality is highest in the first few weeks after hatching. Protecting fry during this period increases survival. Fry stocked at one week of age into fertilized ponds typically survived at 20 to 60% during the first season.

## Key Findings

### First-Year Growth (Young-of-the-Year Grass Carp)

- **Density effect:** Growth was strongly affected by density despite supplementary feeding. Average size was inversely related to stocking density.
- **Length at draining:** Fish at the highest density (470,000/ha) were much smaller than those at about 14,000/ha, which reached 175–200 mm. The length-frequency distribution was fairly homogeneous at each density (Fig. 1, four ponds: 14,000, 27,000, 120,000 and 470,000/ha).
- **Maximum weight:** The largest individuals, up to 1 kg, were recorded in ponds at 170/ha, where fish fed alongside channel catfish at 3% of catfish biomass.
- **Standing stock:** Standing stock correlated closely with density at draining across 18 ponds (Table 1; r = 0.92, P < 0.01). The fitted polynomial was Y = 291.44 + 0.016X − 0.00000003X², where Y is standing stock (kg/ha) and X is density (no./ha).
- **Peak yield:** The highest observed standing stock was 2162 kg/ha, at a density of 470,000/ha.
- **Survival:** Survival of fry (10–15 mm) stocked into freshly filled, fertilized ponds was variable. Fingerlings restocked at 175 mm total length in July had high survival (70–100%).
- **Growth in crowded ponds:** Growth was consistently lower for grass carp cultured alone at densities above 900/ha, even when those fish were fed at higher rates.

### Second-Year Growth (Age-1+ Grass Carp)

- **Scope:** Growth was observed in 83 ponds (0.04–5.0 ha). Densities at draining ranged from 1 to 395/ha, except for one pond at 7730/ha.
- **Density and standing stock:** Standing stock increased with stocking density, but the correlation was weaker than in young-of-the-year fish (r = 0.81, P < 0.01).
- **Maximum standing stock:** 1288 kg/ha at 7730/ha. The next highest was 610 kg/ha at 370/ha.
- **Feeding effect:** Feeding did not improve growth. At 49/ha, mean standing stock was 70.1 kg/ha in 7 non-fed ponds versus 48.1 kg/ha in 21 fed ponds. At 99/ha, it was 67.2 kg/ha in 5 non-fed ponds versus 61.6 kg/ha in 3 fed ponds.
- **Individual size:** The maximum average weight was reported as 5.0 kg (printed as "50 kg" in the source, likely a scan error) at 74/ha with no supplemental feeding and continuous vegetation. The minimum was 0.1 kg at 395/ha.
- **Growth rate:** Average growth over the 271-day experiment was about 11 g/day in the best pond. Across the Discussion's data, the overall mean was 10.7 g/day, with a range of 3.0 to 16.7 g/day. Low-density ponds (49/ha) reached the highest gain, while the lowest gain was 0.8 g/day at 395/ha.
- **Survival:** Uniformly high, at 95–100%.

### Discussion and Comparative Points

- **Density and production:** High densities depressed individual growth, especially in the first season, but total standing stock was still higher because the depression was not proportional to the weight increase of the whole population.
- **Vegetation vs. prepared feed:** In two ponds with abundant macrophytes, average weight at draining was 1.3 and 1.9 kg. Comparably stocked ponds without vegetation produced much smaller fish. In a separate series (Lichtkoppler, 1977), ponds with scarce vegetation yielded fish averaging 1.0 kg, while ponds with abundant vegetation yielded 2.6–4.6 kg after 271 days.
- **Comparison with other studies:** Reported growth in age-1 fish at low densities includes 3.5 g/day in south China, 10.0 g/day in Malacca, and 8.3 g/day in Florida ([[Beach Et Al 1976]]). Tal and Ziv (1978) reported 1.8 and 1.? g/day at 10,000/ha with pellets or Lemna, respectively (one value is garbled in the source).
- **Asian stocking:** Chinese culture uses fry at about 1 million/ha to produce 40–100 kg/ha, a density far above US practice. The authors doubt that such high densities would be productive in North America.
- **Management implication:** Growth can reach about 17 g/day under favorable conditions, but approaches zero under crowded conditions where preferred food is scarce. Supplemental feeding in intensive polyculture does not appear to meet the optimal food requirements of grass carp. Information on growth potential in North America at each life stage therefore has important management implications.

### Conclusion

Density is the main driver of individual growth and size in grass carp, while standing stock rises with density. Preferred food, particularly aquatic vegetation, matters more than supplemental feed for individual growth. Managers aiming for both weed control and production should base stocking rates on vegetation availability rather than on fish numbers alone.
