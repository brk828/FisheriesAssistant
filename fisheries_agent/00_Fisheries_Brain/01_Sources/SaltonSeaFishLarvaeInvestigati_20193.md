# Salton Sea Fish Larvae Investigation: Techniques and Preliminary Osmotic Stress Experiments (Spring-Summer 1968)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae #Haemulidae #Nereididae

## Executive Summary
This 1968 progress report (Lasker and Tenaza, California Department of Fish and Game, Inland Fisheries Administrative Report No. 68-4) responds to the rising salinity of the [[Salton Sea]]. It describes methods for capturing ripe [[Bairdiella]], [[Sargo]] and [[Orangemouth Corvina]], artificially fertilizing their eggs, and rearing embryos and larvae at different salinities. Fertilization was nearly 100% at all salinities from 35 to 55 ‰ for both fish species, but hatching and larval survival fell sharply above 35 ‰. The authors tentatively conclude that any salinity over 35 ‰ harms bairdiella and sargo embryos and larvae. The polychaete *Neanthes succinea*, a food organism, was also tested.

## Background & Description
- Salinity of the Salton Sea has persistently increased, so the study examines whether fish can tolerate the added osmotic stress.
- Early larval stages are thought to be the weak link (a "critical period"). Pelagic larvae hatch before gills, mouth, intestine, scales and internal organs are fully developed.
- Dominant fishes: bairdiella (*Bairdiella icistius*), sargo (*Anisotremus davidsoni*) and orangemouth corvina (*Cynoscion xanthulus*, the top carnivore, scarce and apparently nomadic).
- Work was funded under Dingell-Johnson Project California F-24-R "Salton Sea Investigation".

**Capture methods**
- A [[Bottom Trawl]] was the easiest and most effective method for bairdiella and sargo. Tows should not exceed 15 min; one 20-min June tow caught an estimated 400 lb.
- Corvina are hard to sample. [[Gill Net]] sets overnight usually catch dead fish. Three trawls (30 min total) near San Diego Naval Base on July 31 took 12 corvina of 3-12 lb.
- Suggested corvina protocol: locate an aggregation by hook and line or by watching fishermen, mark it with a buoy, set a gill net for 1-2 hr, and trawl the area until retrieval.

**Artificial fertilization (shipboard)**
- Strip eggs into a finger bowl one-quarter to one-third full of fertilization water, add milt by pipette, then transfer the eggs to a pint jar of the same water for transport.
- Do not strip gametes directly into the water, because of fecal contamination and poor control of quantity.
- Test a few eggs in normal sea water first. Carry water in 1-liter bottles in a styrofoam chest, and add antibiotics only once ripe fish are in hand.
- Test water was Salton Sea water evaporated to 71.8 ‰, membrane filtered (0.5 µm) and diluted with demineralized water. The authors caution that organics or altered salt ratios may affect results.
- Sexing: ripe fish release milt or eggs under gentle abdominal pressure. Nonripe bairdiella have three anal-urogenital openings in females and two in males.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- All 1968 sampling was within 2 miles of shore between Desert Shores and San Diego Naval Base. The best zone was within half a mile, and the heaviest fishing was a quarter to half mile offshore between Salton Bay Marina and the Salton City Keys.
- **Bairdiella spawning:** eggs were extremely abundant in plankton on April 23, near peak spawning. Ripe females were taken only between 6:30 and 9:30 p.m. and on May 20 and 29 and June 10, 12, 19 and 24. None were found in July. On July 29 at least 200 fish were examined, with no visible ova in females and only one male with running milt.
- **Sargo:** only one ripe female was taken (gill net, morning of April 23 or May 2 depending on the passage; the text is inconsistent). Sargo of 15-25 mm were abundant near Salton Bay Marina by May 22. Spawning may be restricted to mornings.
- **Corvina:** ovaries were not enlarged on April 23. Twelve females examined on July 25 had enlarged, ova-filled ovaries. About one-fifth of carcasses on August 1 had spawned. Three tail-free-stage eggs were taken in a plankton tow on August 14 (about 10:00 a.m.), implying evening spawning at about 20 °C (the report gives a 12-15 hr age). Females on September 9 were spawned out, so most corvina probably spawned in August 1968.
- [[Neanthes Succinea]] is an important food for bairdiella and sargo, linking it to the [[Salton Sea Food Web]].

## Key Findings
**Bairdiella (*Bairdiella icistius*)**
- Fertilization was nearly 100% at 35-55 ‰. At 50 and 55 ‰ no eggs developed normally beyond polarization, and all died in under 22 hr.
- Hatching success with antibiotics: 84% at 35 ‰, 15% at 40 ‰, 7% at 45 ‰.
- Embryos at 40 and 45 ‰ developed more slowly, and all larvae died before 60 hr of age.
- [[Antibiotics]] improved hatching and larval survival. In 35 ‰ water, 84% hatched with antibiotics and 78% without. Four hours after hatching, 72% were alive in treated water versus 16% in untreated (24.5-27 °C).
- Table 1 (17 °C, 50 eggs per salinity): in 35 ‰ with antibiotics, 72% survived at 35 hr and 36% at 60 hr, against 16% and 7% without antibiotics at 35 and 60 hr.

**Sargo (*Anisotremus davidsoni*)**
- Fertilization was nearly 100% at all salinities. Hatching of 10-hr survivors was 90% at 35 ‰ and 10% at both 40 and 45 ‰. None hatched at 50 or 55 ‰.
- At 96 hr, 90% of 10-hr survivors were alive in 35 ‰ and 8% in 40 ‰. All were dead at 45-55 ‰. No antibiotics were used.
- Over 50% died in the gastrula stage within about 3 hr (Figure 3 notes 33 hr) at 45 ‰ and above. Controls at 35 ‰ survived.
- Table 1 (50 embryos, 17 °C): survival at 86 hr was 90% in 35 ‰, with declines at higher salinities and 0% in the highest.

**Polychaete (*Neanthes succinea*)**
- Adults survived 5 days at up to 55 ‰ with no apparent damage, though they were less active at 45-55 ‰ on the first day.
- Fertilization was about 60% at 35-45 ‰, 46% at 50 ‰ and 0% at 55 ‰ (23 °C). Sperm are immobilized immediately at 55 ‰.
- All 22-hr trochophores survived at 35-45 ‰ until the feeding stage, when they died, presumably from lack of food. Only about 20% survived 14 hr at 50 and 55 ‰.

**Conclusions and caveats**
- Any salinity above 35 ‰ appears detrimental to bairdiella and sargo embryos and larvae. The authors call this only a good beginning, with more meaningful experiments planned for the next breeding season.
- Results should be viewed with caution because of the evaporated and diluted test water and the limited, single-trial design.
