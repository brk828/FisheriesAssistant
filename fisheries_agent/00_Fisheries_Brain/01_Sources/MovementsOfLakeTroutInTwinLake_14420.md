# Movements of Lake Trout in Twin Lakes, Colorado, in Relation to the Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #telemetry #movement-ecology #pumped-storage #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary

This pre-operational study examined the movements, home ranges, and distribution patterns of [[Lake Trout]] (Salvelinus namaycush) in [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado, between July 1977 and November 1978, prior to the commencement of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. Using ultrasonic telemetry, gill netting, and electrofishing, researchers tracked 34 lake trout to assess their vulnerability to entrainment during future powerplant operations. The study documented seasonal and diel movement patterns, home range sizes (16.36 to 268.22 ha cumulative), temperature preferences (mean 10.5 °C), and spawning distributions. Key findings indicate that lake trout are least active at night (2230-0500 hours) and most active mid-morning (0830-1130 hours), with 40% of tracked fish occupying home ranges near the powerplant tailrace during summer. Spawning occurred in the eastern two-thirds of the lower lake at depths of 1.5-12 m during late October through early November, avoiding the powerplant vicinity. The research concluded that entrainment risk would be highest in spring and fall, lower in summer, and least in winter, assuming night pumping operations.

## Background & Description

[[Twin Lakes]] is a glacial lake system in central Colorado at 2802 m elevation, consisting of upper (263.4 ha) and lower (736.5 ha) basins with maximum depths to 28 m. The lakes are second-class dimictic with a connecting channel. The [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]], part of the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]], was scheduled to become operational in 1981, with pumping from the lower reservoir to an elevated forebay during low demand and generation during peak demand. Maximum daily water level fluctuations of 0.67 m were anticipated.

The lake trout population, first introduced in the late 1800s, reproduces naturally with supplemental stocking by the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]]. The fishery is supported primarily by introduced [[Mysis Shrimp]] (Mysis relicta). Other fish species include [[Rainbow Trout]] (Salmo gairdneri), [[Brown Trout]] (Salmo trutta), [[Brook Trout]] (Salvelinus fontinalis), [[White Sucker]] (Catostomus commersoni), and [[Longnose Sucker]] (Catostomus catostomus).

Researchers tagged 34 lake trout (405-1005 mm total length) with ultrasonic transmitters, including seven temperature-sensitive tags. Both surgical implantation and external attachment methods were employed, with external tagging adopted to minimize mortality. Tracking occurred at least four times weekly with continuous 2-hour samples representing each 6-hour period of the day during ice-free seasons, and daylight-only tracking during winter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake trout in [[Twin Lakes]] exhibited strong thermal preferences, occupying depths where water temperatures averaged 10.5 °C in the lower lake and 8.5 °C in the upper lake. Fish were typically found within 3 m of the bottom in water cooler than 12 °C, though excursions into water as warm as 15.4 °C were documented, likely related to feeding on [[White Suckers]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. The eastern third of the lower lake received minimal use except by spawning fish, probably due to temperature-depth interactions.

Vertical gill netting captured 71% of lake trout within 3 m of the bottom, with none taken in water warmer than 14.2 °C. Temperature-sensitive transmitters revealed that fish spent most time within 3 m of bottom where temperatures were less than 12 °C. No predictable diel vertical migration patterns were observed; vertical movements occurred throughout the day.

The benthic community included abundant [[Mysis Shrimp]], three genera of midges (Chironomus, Phaenopsectra, Dicrotendipes), and fingernail clams (Pisidium spp.). Mysid shrimp are critical forage for lake trout smaller than 550 mm, while larger trout feed primarily on [[White Suckers]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].

Water level fluctuations associated with powerplant operation posed potential risks to spawning success. Historical water levels were typically at minimum during fall spawning season. The proposed 0.67 m daily fluctuation was unlikely to cause serious egg losses if spawning occurred deeper than 2 m, though complete forebay drainage could cause 1.2 m increases potentially exposing shallow nests.

## Key Findings

**Movement Rates and Activity Patterns:**
- Mean movement rates ranged from 0 to 28 m/min, with seasonal averages of 1.08 to 1.61 m/min
- Lake trout were least active at night (2230-0500 hours) during spring, summer, and fall
- Highest activity occurred mid-morning (0830-1130 hours) across all seasons except winter
- Summer mean movement rate was significantly higher than fall (p < 0.05)
- Winter activity was constant throughout daylight hours with increased activity 1500-1800 hours
- Night movements during fall were significantly lower than spring or summer night movements
- Movement rates showed little correlation to fish size (R² = 0.008), surface temperature (R² = 0.006), or dissolved oxygen (R² = 0.004)

**Home Range Characteristics:**
- Cumulative home ranges: 16.36 to 268.22 ha
- Utilized home ranges: 11.38 to 173.00 ha
- Winter home ranges significantly smaller than other seasons (p < 0.05)
- Spring cumulative home ranges significantly larger than winter (p < 0.05)
- No relationship between fish size and home range size
- 40% of summer-tracked fish and 75% of spring-tracked fish had home ranges extending to powerplant tailrace vicinity
- Lake trout made more excursions outside utilized home ranges during spring and fall than summer or winter
- Tagged fish from upper lake never moved to lower lake; two lower lake fish moved to upper lake when surface temperatures < 11 °C

**Spatial Distribution:**
- Only 82 of 1247 sightings (6.6%) in lower lake occurred in eastern third
- Most lake trout avoided eastern third except spawners and one individual (fish No. 21)
- Upper lake fish (Nos. 31, 38, 39, 42, 46) utilized essentially entire upper lake as home range

**Shoreward Movements:**
- 38 shoreward movements (within 15 m of shore) observed for 13 of 34 tagged fish
- During ice-free seasons, 60% of 22 shoreward sightings occurred 0600-1300 hours
- No shoreward movements recorded at night except during spawning
- Large lake trout (> 550 mm) made shoreward movements more frequently than small fish
- Winter shoreward movements concentrated in early morning and late afternoon (1500-1800 hours)
- All lake trout exhibited shoreward movements in winter regardless of size

**Gill Netting Results:**
- Significant seasonal and station differences in catch rates (p < 0.05)
- Deepest station (station 6) had significantly higher summer catch rate than all other stations (p < 0.05)
- Shallow stations had higher catch rates during spring and fall than summer or winter
- Station 4 (tailrace channel head) had significantly higher catch rates in spring and fall than summer or winter (p < 0.05)

**Spawning Biology:**
- Spawning occurred last two weeks of October and first week of November in both 1977 and 1978
- Ripe spawners captured at depths 1.5-12 m, sizes 375-1008 mm total length
- All spawning activity confined to eastern two-thirds of lower lake
- No spawners captured in western third of lower lake or powerplant tailrace area
- Mean water temperature at spawning: 8.4 °C (range 7.2-11.5 °C available)
- Male lake trout covered larger areas than females during spawning
- Homing behavior documented: displaced ripe males (fish Nos. 12 and 50) returned to capture locations
- Five ripe tagged trout did not utilize western third of lower lake

**Juvenile Distribution:**
- Only 12 naturally reproduced lake trout 150-230 mm captured in over 400 hours small-mesh netting
- Nine small lake trout captured in summer in water > 15 m depth, within 1 m of bottom
- Three captured in winter at 3-10 m depths
- One 60 mm lake trout captured by otter trawl near rocky outcrop at ~13 m depth
- Small lake trout captured in areas occupied by adults
- Stocked fingerlings found in tailrace vicinity during October 1978 electrofishing

**Temperature and Depth Preferences:**
- Mean temperature from all temperature-tagged fish: 10.5 °C (lower lake), 8.5 °C (upper lake)
- Temperature selection range: 6.6 to 15.4 °C
- Depth selection range: 3.0 to 23.0 m
- Distance off bottom range: 0.3 to 16.3 m
- 71% of vertical gill net captures within 3 m of bottom
- No fish captured in water warmer than 14.2 °C by vertical nets
- Excursions into warm water (> 12 °C) likely related to feeding on [[White Suckers]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]

**Entrainment Vulnerability Assessment:**
- Highest entrainment risk: spring and fall (large home ranges, frequent excursions, 40-75% of fish near tailrace)
- Lower risk: summer (smaller home ranges, fewer excursions, but 40% still near tailrace)
- Lowest risk: winter (smallest home ranges, fewest excursions, constant daytime activity)
- Night pumping operations (2230-0500 hours) would minimize entrainment during ice-free seasons
- Spawning lake trout unlikely to be entrained due to spatial separation from tailrace
- Lake trout eggs safe from entrainment due to spawning location
- Larval lake trout potentially vulnerable if present near tailrace after emergence
- Stocked fingerlings near tailrace potentially vulnerable during pumping operations
- Water level fluctuations of 0.67 m unlikely to cause serious spawning losses if fish spawn > 2 m depth

**Statistical Analyses:**
- Two-way ANOVA showed significant seasonal (F = 3.95, p = 0.020) and daily (F = 7.12, p = 0.001) differences in movement rates (excluding winter)
- Kruskal-Wallis tests confirmed seasonal differences when winter excluded (k = 3.44, p < 0.05)
- One-way ANOVA of home ranges showed significant seasonal differences for cumulative (F = 5.09, p = 0.01) and utilized (F = 4.24, p = 0.02) estimates
- Gill net catch rates showed significant seasonal (F = 18.737, p = 0.001) and station (F = 4.371, p = 0.001) effects

**Management Implications:**
- Powerplant operation timing could be adjusted to minimize entrainment (night pumping preferred)
- Artificial spawning rubble placement in eastern lower lake could attract spawners away from tailrace
- Upper lake spawners should experience minimal impact unless extensive movements to lower lake occur
- Post-operational monitoring essential to compare with pre-operational baseline
- Individual movement variability suggests population-level assessments more reliable than individual predictions
