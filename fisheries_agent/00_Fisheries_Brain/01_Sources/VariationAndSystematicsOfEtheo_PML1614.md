# Variation and Systematics of *Etheostoma radiosum* (Orangebelly Darter, Pisces: Percidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This systematic study by Matthews and Gelwick (Copeia, 1988, No. 3) re-examines the taxonomy of the [[Orangebelly Darter]] ([[Etheostoma Radiosum]]). The species was previously split into three subspecies by [[Moore and Rigney]] (1952). The authors' discovery of [[Washita River]] populations extended the known range westward and prompted a re-evaluation using meristic, morphometric, and color data. The authors conclude that the Moore and Rigney pattern is not supported by multivariate phenetic analysis, but they recommend retaining three subspecies, with the Washita populations assigned to [[Etheostoma Radiosum Paludosum]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Etheostoma Radiosum]], a small percid darter of upland streams draining into the [[Red River]] in southern Oklahoma and southwestern Arkansas.
- **Prior taxonomy:** Before 1952 the complex was treated as one species. Moore and Rigney (1952) recognized three subspecies:
  - [[Etheostoma Radiosum Radiosum]] (Ouachita and Little River systems)
  - [[Etheostoma Radiosum Cyanorum]] (Blue River system)
  - [[Etheostoma Radiosum Paludosum]] (Kiamichi and Boggy systems)
- **Earlier work:** Hubbs and Black (1941) described the nominal subspecies' nuptial coloration. Echelle et al. (1975) analyzed genic continuity in this headwater fish.
- **Washita discovery:** Matthews et al. (1986) reported the species from the Washita River system, the westernmost known populations.
- **Study aims:** (1) Test whether the Moore and Rigney divisions are supported by phenetic multivariate analysis of meristic and morphometric characters and by reevaluation of single diagnostic characters. (2) If subspecies are recognized, assign the correct trinomial to the Washita form.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat isolation:** The species occurs in upland streams and does not typically penetrate the turbid, low-gradient lower mainstreams of rivers such as the [[Little River]]. Those mainstreams act as barriers, so populations are isolated in no fewer than 13 distinct upland streams, with little extant gene flow.
- **Systems named:** Washita, Blue, Boggy (Clear and Muddy), Kiamichi, Little, Glover, Mountain Fork, Rolling Fork, Cossatot, Saline, Little Missouri, Caddo, and upper Ouachita.
- **Contact zones:** Because the species is absent from lowlands, no contact zones exist between putative subspecies where a "biological" species concept could be tested.
- **Dispersal:** Dispersal is likely through a preglacial [[Ouachita River]] drainage. The authors note that the nuptial males are the main dispersal avenue for stream captures.
- **Not found in Red River proper:** To the authors' knowledge, the species has never been collected from the Red River mainstem (pers. comm., A. A. Echelle).

## Key Findings

**1. Meristic variation (phenetic PCA)**
- Specimens from different systems overlapped substantially. No major system could be separated at more than 59% on the meristic PCA.
- Little and Ouachita specimens showed greater separation from each other than Little River specimens did from Kiamichi/Boggy specimens. This partly supports Moore and Rigney's view that these forms could be distinct.
- Within recognized subspecies, separation among minor drainages was often as large as separation among subspecies.

**2. Morphometric variation (sheared PCA)**
- Sheared PCA axes 2 and 3 showed no overall distinctiveness for any recognized subspecies or major system.
- Overlap of individuals between major regions:
  - Ouachita to Little River: 78%
  - Little River to Kiamichi/Boggy: 74%
  - Kiamichi/Boggy to Blue River: 61%
  - Blue River to Washita: 30%
- The Washita form overlapped 67% with Kiamichi/Boggy material, 33% with Little River material, and 30% with Ouachita material. Its overall shape is most like *E. r. paludosum*.
- Among minor drainages, only about 6% of Saline River individuals overlapped those from the Rolling Fork, and about 6% overlapped those from the Cossatot.
- The authors found no support for Moore and Rigney's suggestions that Little River populations deserve subspecific recognition or that the Blue River form should be elevated to species rank.

**3. Coloration (diagnostic character)**
- In *E. r. paludosum*, the distal blue band of the spinous dorsal fin is broken into spots by red-orange extending to the fin spine tips. The blue is largely confined to the interradial membranes.
- Washita, Boggy, and Kiamichi males at peak nuptial color share this pattern. Butcher Pen Creek males (collected 18 April 1987, WJM 2273) showed the spotted pattern, though the spines were clear, which the authors attribute to being just past peak color.
- On this single shared character, peak nuptial males of the Washita, Boggy, and Kiamichi systems can be separated from all others at "virtually 100%".
- The authors suspect the Washita males lacked full red-orange extension only because they were slightly past peak. Eight males collected on 19 April 1988 (WJM 2340, Butcher Pen Creek) confirmed full coloration matching *E. r. paludosum*.
- Little and Ouachita males and Blue River (*E. r. cyanorum*) males have an unbroken blue distal band.

**4. Blue River distinctiveness**
Blue River *E. r. cyanorum* differs from all other populations in three characters:
- Over 90% separable by number of unpored lateral line scales.
- Deeper head, so most Blue River adults can be separated from most Washita or Boggy-Kiamichi adults on relative head depth.
- Greater snout length than adults of other systems.

**5. Scales (squamation)**
Washita specimens were typically scaleless or nearly so on cheeks, opercle, and breast. Squamation is highly variable within subspecies, and the authors consider these differences tenuous.

**6. Taxonomic decisions**
- The authors reject the five-subspecies option (one per major system), because separation among systems is only partial and no univariate or color character supports it.
- They reject recognizing no subspecies, because the Blue River form and the shared Washita-Boggy-Kiamichi color character are recognizable.
- They reject elevating *E. r. cyanorum* to species rank, because the differences are quantitative and there is no contact zone for testing.
- **Recommendation:** Continue recognizing three subspecies:
  - *E. r. radiosum*: Ouachita and Little River systems
  - *E. r. cyanorum*: Blue River system
  - *E. r. paludosum*: Kiamichi, Boggy, and Washita systems
- No distinct Little River subspecies is supported.
- The Washita population makes *E. r. paludosum* a polytopic subspecies, present in three non-adjacent tributaries of the Red River, with *E. r. cyanorum* lying between the ranges of geographically separated *paludosum* populations. The authors retain the name until a more detailed (possibly electrophoretic) analysis refutes a close evolutionary history.

**7. Zoogeographic implications**
The authors offer two alternative scenarios for the Washita population's origin. In one, the species reached the Washita more recently than other southern Oklahoma rivers. In the other, a preglacial Ouachita mainstream extended west to the Washita, and *E. r. cyanorum* later became the most derived form. The data do not allow a choice between them.

**8. Criteria for subspecies**
The authors follow Matthews et al. (1982) and Mayr (1969). Subspecies should be geographically separate, show no inexplicable mosaic distribution, not be simply points on a cline, and be recognizably different. Following Chernoff et al. (1982), Retzer et al. (1986), and Miller and Fuiman (1987), they note that phenetic subspecies should show little or no overlap on PCA scatterplots. They do not draw taxonomic lines rigidly at any fixed percent separation.
