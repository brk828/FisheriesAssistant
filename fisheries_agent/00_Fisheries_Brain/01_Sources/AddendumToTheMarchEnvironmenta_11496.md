# Mogollon Creek Restoration for Gila Trout Recovery

#ichthyology #salmonidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration #wilderness-management

## Executive Summary

This addendum to the March 24, 1980 Environmental Analysis addresses the restoration of [[Mogollon Creek]] in the [[Gila National Forest]] to secure populations of the endangered [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*). In 1993, [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) were discovered upstream of a barrier at the junction of [[Trail Canyon]], threatening the [[Gila Trout]] population through competition and potential hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*). The preferred alternative involves removing [[Gila Trout]] temporarily, treating approximately 4 miles of [[Mogollon Creek]] with [[Antimycin]] to eliminate non-native trout, and restocking [[Gila Trout]] to extend their secure range downstream to a 10-foot natural waterfall barrier. This action is essential for downlisting [[Gila Trout]] from endangered to threatened status.

## Background & Description

### Species and Historical Distribution

[[Gila Trout]] is one of three trout species native to Arizona and New Mexico, and the only one native to both states. Prior to the 1900s, [[Gila Trout]] occurred throughout reaches of the [[Gila River]] system in New Mexico from approximately the confluence of [[Mogollon Creek]] upstream, and in tributaries of the [[San Francisco River]]. In Arizona, populations existed in the [[Verde River]] drainage and possibly the [[Agua Fria]] system. By the 1960s, [[Gila Trout]] persisted only in five small headwater streams: [[Main Diamond Creek]], [[South Diamond Creek]], [[Iron Creek]], [[McKenna Creek]] (all in the [[Gila River]] system), and [[Spruce Creek]] (in the [[San Francisco River]] drainage).

[[Gila Trout]] was first listed as endangered in 1967, and subsequently under the Endangered Species Act of 1973 and New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act of 1974. A recovery plan was cooperatively developed in 1978 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, U.S. Forest Service, and New Mexico State University, with updates in 1984 and 1993.

### Recovery Efforts and Setbacks

Recovery efforts began in 1970 with the conversion of [[McKnight Creek]] to [[Gila Trout]]. Subsequent stockings occurred in [[Sheep Corral Creek]] and [[Gap Creek]] (Arizona) in 1972. In 1981, the [[Iron Creek]] population was secured by removing [[Brown Trout]] and constructing a waterfall barrier. The [[McKenna Creek]] population was replicated in [[Little Creek]] (1982), and the [[Spruce Creek]] population in [[Big Dry Creek]] (1985). [[Trail Canyon]] was reclaimed in 1986–1987, and [[Woodrow Canyon]] in 1988.

By late 1988, sufficient progress had been made to recommend downlisting. However, natural catastrophes reversed this trend:
- Summer 1988: Major flood in [[McKnight Creek]] eliminated >90% of trout
- July 1989: Wildfire and floods in [[Main Diamond Creek]] and [[South Diamond Creek]] eliminated the [[Main Diamond Creek]] population and reduced [[South Diamond Creek]] by >95%
- 1989: Genetic investigations revealed potential [[Rainbow Trout]] contamination in [[McKenna Creek]]
- 1987–1990: [[Gap Creek]] population in Arizona lost to drought

These events demonstrated that small, isolated headwater populations did not guarantee security. Recovery strategy shifted to establishing [[Gila Trout]] in larger, more resilient drainages with permanently flowing tributaries.

### Current Situation

In 1993, monitoring surveys of [[Mogollon Creek]] found [[Brown Trout]] upstream of a 3-foot waterfall at the [[Trail Canyon]] junction, believed to have moved above the barrier during high flow. The vertical drop was increased by blasting and boulder removal, but this provided only temporary protection. An impenetrable barrier could not be constructed at this site. If [[Brown Trout]] persist, they will eventually reduce [[Gila Trout]] numbers through aggressive competition, and [[Rainbow Trout]] may also negotiate the barrier and hybridize with [[Gila Trout]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Characteristics

[[Mogollon Creek]] restoration area extends from 5 miles east of [[Woodrow Canyon]] westward to a 10-foot waterfall in T13S, R17W, S4, entirely within the [[Gila Wilderness]]. Soils are basaltic with rhyolite bedrock outcrops. Alluvial areas include gravel, cobble, and boulder substrates with large bedrock outcrops.

**Vegetation:** High elevation riparian species intermixed with mixed conifer forest, including Douglas Fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*), Southwestern White Pine (*Pinus strobiformis*), Arizona Alder (*Alnus oblongifolia*), Narrowleaf Cottonwood (*Populus angustifolia*), Red-osier Dogwood (*Cornus stolonifera*), Willow (*Salix* spp.), and Ponderosa Pine (*Pinus ponderosa*). Understory includes Mountain Muhley, Pine Dropseed, New Mexican Locust (*Robinia neomexicana*), and Chokecherry (*Prunus*).

**Stream Characteristics:**
- Flow: 2–3 cfs (continuous)
- Alkalinity: ~46 mg/L
- pH: 7–8
- Pool:riffle ratio: 40:60
- Conductivity: 74 µmol/cm
- Average summer temperature: 53°F
- Dissolved oxygen: 9–10 ppm
- Gradient: 2–4%

**Aquatic Fauna:** [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*, Candidate species), [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and aquatic invertebrates including Mayfly, Stonefly, Caddisfly, and Truefly.

**Wildlife:** Species from Upper Sonoran, Transition, and Canadian Life zones. [[Spotted Owl]] (Threatened) present. [[Narrow-headed Garter Snake]] (*Thamnophis rufipunctatus*, Candidate) and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*, Candidate) not recorded but within probable range.

### Land Use

**Recreation:** Very light use, averaging <90 Recreation Visitor Days annually. Three traditional campsites present. Majority of use by backpackers; three outfitter-guide site visits in 1994 for elk hunting.

**Livestock Grazing:** [[Rain Creek Allotment]] includes ~0.5 mile of stream zone in renovation area. Grazed with 151 head of cattle March 1–31, plus additional 151 head March 1–April 30. Grazing excluded May 1–August 15. Grazing resumes August 15–February 28 (151 head) and September 15–February 28 (additional 151 head). Current management considered not inconsistent with [[Gila Trout]] objectives.

## Key Findings

### Proposed Action (Alternative B - Preferred)

1. **Temporary Removal:** [[Gila Trout]] in [[Mogollon Creek]] above [[Trail Canyon]] will be electroshocked and temporarily placed in [[Trail Canyon]], [[Woodrow Canyon]], and [[South Diamond Creek]]. Transport to [[South Diamond Creek]] via helicopter.

2. **Antimycin Treatment:** Section from headwaters downstream 4 miles below [[Trail Canyon]] to 10-foot barrier (1 mile above Bud's Hole) treated with [[Antimycin]] to remove [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. At least two, possibly three treatments required during 10-day period.

3. **Restocking:** [[Gila Trout]] restocked following year or sooner if conditions warrant.

4. **Authorization:** Regional Forester will authorize [[Antimycin]] use and helicopter transport in [[Gila Wilderness]] and [[Aldo Leopold Wilderness]].

### Mitigation and Monitoring

**Pre-Implementation:**
- Intensive survey to determine [[Brown Trout]] distribution
- [[Gila Trout]] removal and placement in holding areas
- [[Speckled Dace]] removal and placement in [[Trail Canyon]]
- Macroinvertebrate sampling for species composition and density
- [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] electroshocked and moved downstream
- Live cars with trout placed downstream to monitor [[Antimycin]] effects
- [[Antimycin]] applied per label directions
- [[Antimycin]] neutralized at barrier with 1.0 ppm potassium permanganate
- Public notification prior to treatment

**Post-Implementation:**
- Macroinvertebrate sampling prior to [[Gila Trout]] introduction
- [[Gila Trout]] restocked from [[Trail Canyon]] and [[South Diamond Creek]]
- [[Speckled Dace]] restocked
- Population surveys within two years to determine recovery

### Environmental Effects

**Antimycin Toxicity and Degradation:**
- [[Antimycin]] (trade name Fintrol) registered by EPA in 1969 for fisheries applications
- Degradation rapid in natural waters (1–14 days, typically 4–7 days), accelerated by high pH, temperature, and light
- Half-life: 310 hours at pH 6.0 to 1.5 hours at pH 10 at 12°C
- Potassium permanganate deactivates [[Antimycin]] within minutes
- Not hazardous to humans: maximum daily intake from water and fish would be 1/17,000 times oral LD50 for rats
- No toxic effects to mammals, birds, plankton, or aquatic plants at fish-killing concentrations
- Minimal, short-term effect on macroinvertebrate community; no long-term effects documented

**Aquatic Life:**
- Taxa temporarily reduced: Cladocera, Copepoda, Amphipoda, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera
- Taxa generally unaffected: Protozoa, Rotatoria, Nematoda, Nematomorpha, Annelida, Ostracoda, Decapoda, Plecoptera, Odonata, Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Diptera, Gastropoda, Pelecypoda
- Recovery within one year based on [[Big Dry Creek]] treatment (1985)

**Threatened/Endangered/Sensitive Species:**
- [[Gila Trout]] and [[Speckled Dace]] removed prior to treatment, restocked later; temporary reduction, full recovery expected within few years
- [[Antimycin]] unlikely to affect [[Narrow-headed Garter Snake]] (would exit water if irritated; no effects on water snakes at treatment levels)
- No effects expected on [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] tadpoles; none found during inventories; habitat restrictive (high gradient, rare backwater areas)

**Water Quality:**
- Temporary, localized discoloration from potassium permanganate; dissipates to undetectable levels within short distance
- No effect on aquatic organisms at levels used
- Live fish found within few hundred feet of neutralization station post-treatment

**Recreation:**
- Temporary fishing closure in renovated portion; majority of anglers fish downstream (Bud's Hole, West Fork) which remain open
- ~330 miles of streams available for fishing on [[Gila National Forest]]; fishing prohibited in ~40 miles of 12 streams with [[Gila Trout]]
- Potential long-term benefit: unique [[Gila Trout]] fishery attracting anglers and local income (if downlisted and fishing allowed)

**Livestock Grazing:**
- No effects; majority of [[Rain Creek Allotment]] outside renovation zone
- No changes in permitted cattle numbers or management

**Wilderness Character:**
- Alternative A (No Action) adversely affects wilderness by perpetuating loss of native species
- Alternative B enhances wilderness by stabilizing and restoring native species; temporary effects from removal/restocking

**Costs:**
- Alternative A: $0
- Alternative B: ~$10,000

**Gila Trout Security:**
- Alternative A: [[Brown Trout]] increase; [[Gila Trout]] eventually eliminated
- Alternative B: [[Gila Trout]] numbers increase; range extended ~4 miles downstream to 10-foot waterfall; [[Brown Trout]] eliminated; population security improved; essential for downlisting to threatened status

### Historic Range

Conflicting reports on [[Gila Trout]] native status in [[Mogollon Creek]]. Miller (1950) cited 1898 interviews indicating [[Gila Trout]] in all [[Gila River]] headwaters, including "overpopulation" on [[Mogollon Creek]] (particularly West Fork). Recovery Plan cites report of absence until 1915, but recognizes [[Gila Trout]] probably inhabited [[Gila River]] and most tributaries upstream from [[Mogollon Creek]] confluence. [[Trail Canyon]] stocked 1987; upper [[Mogollon Creek]] stocked beginning 1988. Alternative B extends range ~4 miles downstream from [[Trail Canyon]] confluence.
