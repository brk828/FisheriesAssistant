## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 2) to the Arizona Game & Fish Department addresses the critical conservation status of the [[Bonytail Chub]], described as the most endangered fish species in the entire [[Colorado River Drainage]]. The correspondence outlines the near-total collapse of the wild population in [[Lake Mohave]] and details the Service's strategy to use captive-bred stock from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to reintroduce the species into historic Arizona habitats. The primary policy focus is a request for support regarding the designation of these reintroduced populations as "experimental nonessential" under the Endangered Species Act—a controversial classification necessary to gain interagency cooperation despite reducing legal protections for the fish.

## Background & Description
The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is a large cyprinid native to the mainstream rivers of the Colorado River Basin. By the time of this letter (1988), the species was functionally extinct in the wild in terms of natural reproduction, surviving only as a relict population of aging adults.

- **Wild Population Status:** For the preceding 15 years, only old adult fish had been captured, with all specimens originating exclusively from [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Population Collapse:** A dedicated survey effort conducted approximately one month prior to this letter (March 1988) failed to locate any of the remaining old fish in Lake Mohave, suggesting the wild source population may have been extirpated or reduced beyond detection.
- **Captive Breeding Program:** In response to the wild decline, the Service established a brood stock at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] using a limited number of fish salvaged from Lake Mohave. This program has proven highly successful, generating a robust, "almost unlimited" supply of juvenile bonytail chubs available for reintroduction efforts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The management strategy discussed in this letter centers on the reintroduction of a hatchery-reared, genetically limited population into historic riverine habitats from which the species has been extirpated.

- **Target Habitats:** The proposed reintroduction sites include the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] in Arizona.
- **Interagency Conflict:** Land and water management agencies expressed significant reluctance to permit reintroduction under standard "full protection" Endangered Species Act status. This reluctance stemmed from concerns over:
    1. Increased regulatory and management responsibility for agencies in areas where the species had already been extirpated.
    2. The potential for reintroduced populations to "preempt" future use or development of high-quality aquatic habitats.
- **Regulatory Mechanism:** To circumvent this interagency resistance, the Service proposed designating the new populations as "Experimental Nonessential" (a classification under Section 10(j) of the ESA). This designation removes most standard endangered species protections and responsibilities, making agencies more willing to permit the stocking, though it leaves the fish more vulnerable.
- **Stakeholder Feedback:** Comments on the proposal from various agencies ranged widely:
    - Support ("good idea").
    - Opposition due to the "extreme threat of extinction in the wild," arguing the species needed *more* protection, not less.
    - Opposition based on fears of "possibility of added protection in the future" complicating land-use.

## Key Findings
- **Critical Status:** The Bonytail Chub is confirmed as the most endangered fish in the Colorado River drainage as of 1988, with the wild [[Lake Mohave]] population likely extirpated based on a failed 1988 survey.
- **Successful Aquaculture:** The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] program successfully established a viable brood stock, solving the supply problem for reintroduction stock (juvenile bonytail chubs are now readily available).
- **Policy Bottleneck:** The primary barrier to recovery is not biological (lack of fish) but political/administrative (agency reluctance to accept liability for a fully protected endangered species).
- **Trade-off Decision:** The Service concludes that "Experimental Nonessential" designation, despite providing weaker legal protections, represents the only feasible pathway to initiate reintroduction into Arizona rivers, as agencies will not support "full protection" stocking in mainstream rivers.
- **AGFD Position:** The Arizona Game & Fish Department (per a Jan. 8, 1986 letter) previously supported the proposal and suggested additional stocking sites, but also recommended stocking under full protection status—a position seemingly in tension with the Service's current push for the nonessential designation.
- **Contact for Follow-up:** Jim Johnson was designated as the point of contact for further consultation.
