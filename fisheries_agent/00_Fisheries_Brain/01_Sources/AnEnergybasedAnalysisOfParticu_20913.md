# Energetics of Particulate-Feeding and Filter-Feeding in Blue Tilapia (*Tilapia aurea*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines why [[Blue Tilapia]] (*Tilapia aurea*) switch from visual particulate-feeding on zooplankton as juveniles to pump filter-feeding as adults. The authors test whether energy profitability, rather than only proximal morphological changes, drives this shift. Filter-feeding costs were measured as oxygen consumption in closed chambers, and particulate-feeding costs were estimated from videotaped attacks on [[Daphnia Magna]] using fast-start swimming equations. Combining these costs with published plankton size data, the authors built a predictive model. It predicts that blue tilapia should switch from particulate-feeding to filter-feeding at approximately 5.7 cm standard length (SL), within the 4 to 8 cm range reported in field studies.

## Background & Description

- **Feeding ontogeny:** Juvenile blue tilapia are visual zooplanktivores. Upon reaching about 6 to 7 cm SL they switch to pump filter-feeding on phytoplankton and zooplankton ([[Spataru And Zorn 1978]], [[Gophen 1980]]).
- **Distribution:** Native to rift lakes and river systems of northern Africa and the Middle East. Reproducing populations occur in several southeastern U.S. states.
- **Study animals:** 63 fish ranging from 2.0 to 16.1 cm SL, progeny of individuals from a central Texas reservoir cooling pond. Fish were held at about 20°C, fed live zooplankton and pelletized trout chow, and fasted for 24 h before trials.
- **Context:** Filter-feeding fishes are typically visual particulate-feeding planktivores as juveniles. Earlier explanations of the switch focused on digestive tract and [[Gill Raker]] morphology, while energy acquisition was largely ignored. The authors state that the energetics of each feeding mode had not previously been examined in any species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diet shift:** Juveniles feed mainly on large zooplankton, while adults rely more on phytoplankton and smaller, non-evasive zooplankton ([[Kutkuhn 1957]], [[Cramer And Marzolf 1970]], [[Drenner Et Al 1982]]).
- **Plankton size structure:** Using [[Sprules Et Al 1983]] data from 26 north temperate lakes, the authors defined two size classes. Particles of 25 to 200 µm equivalent spherical diameter (ESD) were treated as phytoplankton, with biomass 1.89 mg L⁻¹. Particles of 200 to 2600 µm ESD were treated as zooplankton, with biomass 84.72 mg L⁻¹.
- **Minimum retention:** The minimum particle size retained by filtering blue tilapia is about 25 µm ([[Drenner Et Al 1984b]]).
- **Prey evasion:** Zooplankton evasion reduces filter-feeding capture efficiency. Pump-filtering fish captured an average of 51 percent of a cladoceran and copepod assemblage ([[Drenner Et Al 1978]], [[Drenner And Mccomas 1980]]).
- **Facultative planktivores:** Fishes that use both modes usually filter-feed on small, abundant zooplankton and particulate-feed on larger, less abundant forms ([[Helfman 1990]]).
- **Broader filter-feeding success:** The authors cite large body sizes in the [[Whale Shark]] and [[Basking Shark]], and two filter-feeding clupeids that make up 45 percent of fish standing crop in central U.S. reservoirs.

## Key Findings

**1. Filter-feeding costs decline with size.**
- Routine metabolic rate per fish (RMRF) rose with size: RMRF = 0.105 × SL^2.3 (r = 0.94, n = 31). Weight-specific routine rate (RMRG) changed little with size (r = 0.04).
- Filter-feeding metabolic rate per fish (FMRF) rose with size: FMRF = 0.348 × SL^2.? (r = 0.92, n = 31). Weight-specific filter-feeding rate (FMRG) declined with size: FMRG = 12.032 × SL^-? (r = 0.53, n = 31).
- The filter-feeding increment over routine metabolism was inversely related to size (Fig. 2; y = 0.285 × SL^-?; r = 0.75, n = 31). Small fish pay a much higher relative cost for filter-feeding.

**2. Particulate-feeding costs rise with size and attack distance.**
- Attacks on *D. magna* were analyzed from videotape at 1/60 s intervals. Multiple regression of work against fish weight and distance travelled gave R² = 0.636 (p < 0.0001, n = 167). Both weight and distance were significant (p < 0.0001).
- Reactive distance increased with prey length (RD = 46.91 × DL^?; r = 0.44, n = 169) but was uncorrelated with fish SL (p = 0.55).
- Prey size classes used were 1.08, 1.51, 2.48 and 3.58 mm (mean lengths).

**3. Model prediction of the feeding switch.**
- The model compares the energy cost of particulate-feeding and filter-feeding that yield equal net energy gain. A cost ratio below 1 favors particulate-feeding, and above 1 favors filter-feeding.
- Under baseline conditions, the predicted switch is at about **5.7 cm SL**.
- Doubling zooplankton biomass shifts the switch to about 4.1 cm SL. Halving it shifts the switch to about 7.2 cm SL.
- Increased phytoplankton biomass shifts the switch to smaller sizes. The model is less sensitive to phytoplankton than to zooplankton biomass.

**4. Energetic conclusions and implications.**
- The authors propose that energy maximization at a proximal level drives short-term feeding-mode shifts in blue tilapia.
- Small filter-feeders may be limited to zooplanktivory because of high weight-specific routine metabolism. As fish grow, filtering capacity increases and weight-specific costs fall, so filter-feeding becomes more efficient.
- The authors state that they were testing model predictions across a range of fish sizes and plankton distributions at the time of writing.
