# Bajada 1995:3(2): Native Desert Fishes, Park Management, and Warm Desert Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This issue of *Bajada*, a free triannual newsletter from the [[National Biological Service]] Cooperative Park Studies Unit at The University of Arizona, is a multi-article communication piece rather than a single study. Its dominant theme is the conservation of endangered native desert fishes in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. Key items include the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon]] refuges for Yaqui fishes, a [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery effort at [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]], Gila topminnow habitat work on [[Cienega Creek]], and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] management. The issue also covers management philosophy, park news, a coyote movement study, a staff directory, and meeting listings.

## Background & Description

The issue is organized as a set of short contributions, including feature articles, managers' forum pieces, field notes, and editorial material:

- **Yaqui fishes (Rio Yaqui drainage):** Eight native fish species once occurred in the U.S. portion of the Rio Yaqui. The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) established San Bernardino refuge in 1982 to protect them. Leslie Canyon was acquired in 1988. Current populations at San Bernardino include the [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonorensis*), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*), and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*). Four other species are planned for reintroduction from Mexico, including the [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*).
- **Management essay:** Editor [[Bill Halvorson]] describes a shift from simple protection toward people-centered resource management, linked to [[Ecopsychology]].
- **Tumacacori National Historical Park:** The park was expanded in 1990 to include the Guevavi and Calabazas mission sites. Its management is discussed in the Managers' Forum.
- **Coyote study:** Researchers Dixie Bounds and Bill Shaw tracked [[Coyote]] movements at [[Saguaro National Park]].
- **Native fish status:** Gene Maughan reviews the historical and current decline of desert fishes.
- **Field notes and directories:** These cover [[Heritage Data Management System]] (HDMS) data services, staff contacts, news from park resource staffs, and upcoming meetings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical drivers of decline.** Native desert fishes lived in cienegas and springs linked by ephemeral streams. Their populations historically expanded in wet years and contracted in droughts, with recolonization from persistent refugia. Water diversion, pumping, and damming reduced stream connectivity and dried refugial habitats, which eliminated recolonization. Maughan notes a possible climatic shift toward xeric conditions and the introduction of [[Nonnative Fishes]] and their diseases. Bass, sunfish, and catfish were introduced into nearly all permanent southwestern waters earlier in the twentieth century. Eliminating nonnative fishes from native habitat is described as infeasible at large scale, because it requires poisoning and reintroduction.

**Yaqui fish habitat.** San Bernardino relies on ten artesian wells, several small springs, and perennial water along Black Draw. Leslie Canyon has a small perennial stream. Water supply depends on regulated groundwater withdrawal from the San Bernardino valley aquifer in Mexico. The status of these fishes in Mexico may not be known, which underscores the need to maintain them in the United States.

**Razorback sucker.** The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991. It persists only in isolated populations in the Green River (Utah), the Upper Colorado River Basin, and Lakes Mead and Mojave. Causes of decline include habitat loss from damming and irrigation, and predation on eggs and young by introduced game fishes.

**Gila topminnow.** Cienega Creek is part of the 45,000-acre [[Empire-Cienega Resource Conservation Area]] near Sonoita, Arizona. The main threat is [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other nonnative fishes in stock ponds. Grazing effects are also a concern, especially where spring and summer grazing occurs.

**Quitobaquito pupfish.** This species is endemic to the pond and springs at Quitobaquito, in the southwest corner of [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. It tolerates temperatures up to 45°C, low dissolved oxygen, and salinity up to twice that of seawater. The site lies on the international boundary and near a major Mexican highway.

**Coyote movements.** Coyotes in suburban and rural areas near Saguaro were studied in the Rincon Mountain District. Their habitat use is discussed under Key Findings.

## Key Findings

**Yaqui fishes and refuges**
- San Bernardino was described as likely the first large-scale FWS refuge project with a mission to protect and restore native fishes.
- The [[Yaqui Chub]] was federally listed in 1984 and by Arizona in 1988. Its U.S. range is restricted to the two refuges and a private ranch where it was reintroduced.
- The [[Yaqui Topminnow]]'s total range lies solely in the Rio Yaqui basin.
- Longfin dace are found mainly in the Gila and Bill Williams drainages and are the only member of their genus in Arizona.
- The [[Beautiful Shiner]] was extirpated in the United States in 1970 and reintroduced at San Bernardino in 1990 from Mexican stock.
- The recovery plan outlined by FWS includes cooperation with Mexico, securing water and habitat, research on biology and habitat needs, population management, establishing self-sustaining populations within historic range, and monitoring. The refuges were purchased from [[The Nature Conservancy]].

**Razorback sucker recovery**
- The species was harvested commercially in Arizona as recently as 1949.
- The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), formed in 1990, is led by the Bureau of Reclamation with several agency partners.
- Lake Mojave holds the largest and most genetically diverse remaining population. It declined from an estimated 60,000 fish in 1988 to 25,000 in 1993. Most fish are senescent, 40 to 50 years old, from early 1950s year-classes.
- Projections indicate that without recruitment the population could become extinct within a decade.
- The outgrowth program captures larvae in late winter and spring using lights and dipnets, holds them in aquariums for 3 weeks, then releases about 20 mm juveniles into predator-free coves isolated with berms.
- In 1994, roughly 9,000 larvae were captured and about 2,300 were returned to Lake Mojave, a reported survival rate of 25%. Projections for 1995 are similar.

**Gila topminnow and Cienega Creek**
- Of nine remaining natural topminnow populations, only three are free of competitive or predatory nonnative fishes. Cienega Creek supports the largest Gila topminnow population in the United States.
- BLM has pursued instream-flow water rights and erosion control protecting more than 2 miles of cienega marsh.
- Stock-pond inventories in 1991 found few suitable perennial waters on public land, so BLM manages stock ponds seasonally.
- Riparian exclosures protecting 4 miles of Cienega Creek began in 1990, with favorable vegetation response. Further fencing and off-channel watering are proposed.

**Quitobaquito pupfish**
- The population fluctuates from 5,000 to 10,000 fish. It is Arizona's only representative of the genus *Cyprinodon* and is listed as endangered.
- Annual census uses minnow traps emptied at 2-hour intervals, 3 times per day for 2 days. Size distribution and checks for nonnative species are considered the most useful results.
- Management must balance channel maintenance (clearing roots and debris) against habitat needs, and manage wilderness, cultural, and visitor-use constraints.

**Management philosophy**
- Halvorson argues that endangered species management is shaped by conflict under the [[Endangered Species Act]], multiple-use pressures, strained interagency relations, local community pressures, and funding priorities. He notes that federal spending on trout stocking exceeds spending on endangered fishes.
- He concludes that decisions should rest on the best scientific information and open consensus-building among affected groups.

**Coyote movements at Saguaro National Park**
- Eleven adult coyotes were fitted with radio collars, yielding 1,412 locations. Housing density was 0.4 houses/ha in the suburban area and 0.02 houses/ha in the rural area.
- Suburban coyotes spent 69% of their time outside park boundaries, likely because of supplemental food from residents.
- Average annual home range was about 16 km², with a den-defending home range under 3 km² during gestation and about 9 km² during pup rearing.
- Feeding reduces home range size and habituates coyotes to humans, raising the risk of aggression. The authors report that 46% of national parks have coyotes and are surrounded by development, and recommend cooperation with homeowner associations and local businesses.

**Tumacacori National Historical Park**
- The 1990 legislation expanded responsibilities without added funds or staff. Visitation in 1994 exceeded 66,000, a 12-year record.
- The park is revising its General Management Plan and developing a Natural and Cultural Resources Management Plan.
- Volunteers from the Southwest Archeology Team applied protective capping to at-risk adobe surfaces at Calabazas, Guevavi, and Tumacacori in late 1994, funded by the National Park Foundation.

**Other park and program notes**
- Lesser long-nosed bats at Coronado National Memorial made more than 1,000 visits per night per plant at blooming agaves. Bat-pollinated agaves produced twice as many fruits as those pollinated only by insects and birds.
- Organ Pipe received a 30-m climate tower from USGS, and a night-sky brightness monitoring protocol is being developed.
- The [[National Biological Information Infrastructure]] (NBII) is under development to link biological data across organizations.
