# Distribution and Prevalence of the Asian Fish Tapeworm (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi) in the Colorado River and Tributaries, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bothriocephalidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department study, by [[Mark J. Brouder]] and [[Timothy L. Hoffnagle]], documents the distribution and prevalence of the [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and tributaries of [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The parasite is confined mainly to the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), where water temperatures exceed the roughly 20°C needed for its life cycle. Infection was found in [[Humpback Chub]], an endangered species, and in [[Plains Killifish]], both new host records. The authors warn that planned seasonal warming of the river through a [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] could allow the parasite to spread and threaten native fishes.

The accompanying cover correspondence (page 1) is a short letter from Hoffnagle to the Bureau of Reclamation about the manuscript and the need to obtain a U.S. National Parasite Collection accession number before publication.

## Background & Description

The [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] is a pseudophyllidean cestode first described from *Acheilognathus rhombea* in Japan (Yamaguti 1934). It spread to Europe, Russia, and North America, probably with [[Grass Carp]] introductions in the early 1970s. It is now established in several cyprinids and in [[Mosquitofish]] in the southern and southeastern United States, and it has been found in Virgin River fishes in Utah, Nevada, and Arizona.

The closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 changed the Colorado River in Grand Canyon from a warm, muddy river to a clear, cold one, fed by hypolimnetic discharge from [[Lake Powell]]. Of the original eight endemic fishes, reproducing populations of only four remain. Those four are [[Humpback Chub]] (federally endangered), flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. Reproduction is now largely limited to a few perennial tributaries.

The parasite's life cycle uses [[Cyclopoid Copepods]] as intermediate hosts, and cyprinids are the main definitive hosts. Maturation requires temperatures above 20°C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal limitation:** Mainstem Colorado River temperatures reached only 18.4°C (1991–1994), while the LCR exceeded 20°C from May through September 1993 and peaked at 26.1°C. Backwaters reached 28.0°C in shallow areas, but mean backwater temperatures never exceeded 20°C over four years of study.
- **Flow dynamics:** Daily hydropower fluctuations inundate and desiccate backwaters and flush zooplankton, destabilizing the habitat. The LCR base flow comes from springs (about 5.6 m³/s), but floods can exceed 850 m³/s.
- **Warming scenario:** The proposed MLIS could raise mainstem temperatures by 3–10°C, possibly enabling establishment in tributaries such as Kanab Creek and in the mainstem.
- **Kanab Creek:** Mean temperatures exceed 20°C from May to August and reach 34°C, making reproduction possible.
- **Invasion components:** Three of four invasion requirements are present in the mainstem: definitive hosts, intermediate copepod hosts, and parasite source populations in the LCR. Temperature is the limiting fourth factor.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- 1994: fish were collected from eight mainstem reaches over three river trips, using seines, hoop nets, dip nets, minnow traps, and electrofishing. Fish were preserved in 70% ethanol or 10% formalin and examined in the laboratory.
- 1995: five speckled dace and five fathead minnows were targeted from each of eight reaches and seven tributaries over three trips. Humpback chub were not collected in 1995 because of their endangered status.

**Prevalence (1994)** — 1,669 fish examined (nine species/groups)
- Humpback chub: 27 of 120 infected (22.5%); new host record
- Plains killifish: 3 of 29 infected (10.3%); new host record
- Speckled dace: 7 of 185 infected (3.8%)
- Fathead minnow: 5 of 234 infected (2.2%)
- No infection in 329 flannelmouth suckers, 562 bluehead suckers, or 210 rainbow trout

**Prevalence (1995)**
- Fathead minnow: 2 of 85 infected (2.4%)
- Speckled dace: 2 of 148 infected (1.4%)

**Spatial distribution**
- About 66.7–100% of infected fish per species came from Reaches 2–4, near the LCR confluence, probably by emigration from the LCR.
- Infected fish outside this zone: one fathead minnow at RK 265.2 and another at RK 301.1; one plains killifish at RK 191.8; one speckled dace at RK 266.6 (backwater); and one speckled dace in the lower 500 m of Kanab Creek (RK 230.9).
- No infected fish were found in Nankoweap, Shinumo, Royal Arch, Stone, or Havasu creeks.

**Conclusions**
- The parasite currently completes its life cycle only in the LCR. Its presence in Kanab Creek suggests possible colonization, which the authors say warrants further study.
- Humpback chub infection is a conservation concern. Prior LCR surveys found rates as high as 78.9% (1990) and 77.8% (1992), and a 1995 study found 3.6% of 168 adult chub infected. The Grand Canyon humpback chub population was estimated at 4,508–10,444 fish (>150 mm TL).
- Warming the river to speed native fish recovery may increase parasite prevalence and harm YOY native fishes over the long term.
