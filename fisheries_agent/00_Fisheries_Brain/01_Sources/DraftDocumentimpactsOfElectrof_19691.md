# Impacts of Electrofishing on Fish - Proposal and Preliminary Investigation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #sampling-methodology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a 1990 proposal and preliminary investigation by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at [[Colorado State University]] addressing concerns about electrofishing impacts on fish in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The [[National Park Service]] mandated evaluation of electrofishing effects following a study by Sharber and Carothers (1988) documenting 44-67% injury rates in [[Rainbow Trout]] collected in [[Glen Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]]. The proposal outlines a three-phase approach: Phase I involves comprehensive literature review and synthesis; Phase II would conduct controlled laboratory/field experiments; Phase III would verify results through uncontrolled field experiments. The preliminary investigation identified over 250 references on electrofishing, but found only 11 papers specifically addressing cyprinids and none on catostomids, highlighting critical knowledge gaps for endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].

## Background & Description

Electrofishing emerged as one of the most effective fish collection methods during Phase I of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES). However, the technique has caused documented injury to fish since its development in the 1940s-1950s. The Sharber and Carothers (1988a) study revealed extensive spinal injuries in large trout, detectable only through X-ray photography or autopsy analysis—methods not previously used for injury evaluation.

The current controversy involves not only injury extent from various pulse waveforms, but whether discrete thresholds of electrical energy exist for various injurious and non-injurious responses (the power density hypothesis). The [[National Park Service]] expressed particular concern about electrofishing spawning aggregations of [[Humpback Chub]] in the [[Little Colorado River]] and its confluence, potentially affecting reproduction of a significant but unknown portion of the population.

The proposal team consisted of Darrel E. Snyder (lead), Robert T. Muth, and Kevin Bestgen from the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]]. The preliminary investigation assessed available literature through electronic databases (ASFA 1987-1990, Wildlife and Fish Worldwide 1971-1990, CARL, UNCOVER), existing bibliographies (Friedman 1974, Wydoski 1980), and various other sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River System]] supports multiple endangered fish species requiring monitoring and study, creating tension between research needs and species protection mandates. Electrofishing effectiveness varies with environmental conditions including water conductivity, depth, substrate, and physical characteristics of target species.

The [[Grand Canyon]] ecosystem presents unique challenges: the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence serves as critical spawning habitat for [[Humpback Chub]], and extensive electrofishing during spawning periods could alter reproductive behavior. The percentage of the total [[Humpback Chub]] population represented by spawning concentrations remained unknown in 1990.

Water quality parameters significantly affect electrofishing efficiency and injury potential. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] memo identified specific research questions regarding water quality effects, exposure time, electrical frequencies, and threshold levels for injury across different species, ages, and environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

**Literature Assessment:**
- Over 250 references compiled on electrofishing and related matters
- Eight electrofishing bibliographies identified (1954-1980)
- Approximately 30% of references discuss effects on fishes
- Only 11 papers (4%) specifically address cyprinids; none address catostomids
- Most electrofishing research focused on salmonids, particularly [[Rainbow Trout]]
- Substantial knowledge gaps exist for endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes

**Species-Specific Literature:**
- Cyprinid studies included: [[Goldfish]] (power density thresholds, anesthesia), [[Common Carp]] (narcotizing ability, electrode effectiveness), [[Grass Carp]] (control methods), [[Common Shiner]] (recovery rates), [[Northern Squawfish]] (galvanotaxis), and Indian carps (voltage gradient responses)
- No catostomid-specific studies identified despite [[Razorback Sucker]] being endangered
- [[Rainbow Trout]] may serve as better model for [[Gila]] species than studied carps due to similar shape, size, and behavior

**Injury Documentation:**
- Sharber and Carothers (1988a) documented 44-67% injury rates in large [[Rainbow Trout]] depending on pulse waveform
- Injuries include spinal fractures, internal bleeding, and tissue damage
- Injury incidence increases with fish size
- X-ray analysis and autopsy necessary to detect internal injuries
- Visual examination alone inadequate for assessing electrofishing impacts

**Power Density Hypothesis:**
- Recent papers by Kolz (1989) and Kolz and Reynolds (1989) present power density concept for understanding specific physiological response thresholds
- Controversy exists whether discrete thresholds exist for various response levels
- Thresholds identified for twitch, anodic attraction, narcosis, and anesthesia in [[Goldfish]]
- Relationship between narcosis and compression fractures unclear

**Research Questions Identified:**
- Does electrofishing impact native species as severely as [[Rainbow Trout]]?
- What fish species are most representative anatomically/physiologically of [[Humpback Chub]]?
- What are physiological and anatomical effects on musculature, bone structure, blood, and reproductive organs?
- Are impacts related to fish age, water quality, exposure time, and electrical frequencies?
- What influences injury rate besides pulse shape, power density, and frequency?
- Can threshold levels for injury be identified for each species?
- Is power density the main parameter for electrotaxis, narcosis, and injury?
- What are impacts to eggs and developing alevins?
- Are there means to reduce or eliminate injury?

**Proposed Budget and Timeline:**
- Total Phase I cost: $21,407
- Timeline: 5-8 months (November 1990 - March/June 1991)
- Personnel: 2.5 man-months principal investigators, 1 man-month workstudy students
- Deliverables: draft position paper, final report, Phase II proposal, journal review paper

**Alternative Approaches:**
- Mark-recapture databases may provide indirect assessment of effects on native [[Colorado River]] fishes
- Some tagged specimens repeatedly captured over years
- Database size and environmental/electrofishing parameter documentation may be sufficient to answer some questions
- [[Roundtail Chub]] suggested as potential surrogate for [[Humpback Chub]] in experiments

**Management Implications:**
- [[National Park Service]] mandated to preserve and protect species within jurisdiction
- Electrofishing should be kept to minimum and used to minimize stress/injury
- Guidelines needed for minimizing detrimental effects
- Section 7 Consultation requires full evaluation of impacts
- Alternatives to electrofishing may need consideration
- Technology changes or application limits may be necessary

The proposal recommended immediate commencement of the three-phase program, with concurrent experimental work comparing [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Humpback Chub]] responses (or [[Roundtail Chub]] as surrogate) before finalizing Phase I report.
