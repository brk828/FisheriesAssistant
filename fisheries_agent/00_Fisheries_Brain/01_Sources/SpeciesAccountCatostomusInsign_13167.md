# Catostomus insignis (Sonoran Sucker)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-fish #Sonoran-Desert-Fishes

## Executive Summary

The [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) is a large-bodied catostomid fish endemic to the Gila River drainage and adjacent basins in Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Sonora, Mexico. It is a benthic, omnivorous species most closely associated with pool habitats and is frequently found syntopically with the [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), with which it occasionally hybridizes. The species is in decline across the southern portion of its historical range due to habitat dewatering, impoundment, channelization, and flow regulation. Substantial gaps remain in knowledge of its life history, including fecundity, age at first spawning, and larval ecology.

## Background & Description

- **Body form:** Fusiform, chubby body; adults reach up to 80 cm total length (TL, snout tip to caudal fin end).
- **Fin ray counts:** Typically 11 dorsal rays, 16–18 pectoral rays, 9–11 pelvic rays, 7–8 anal rays.
- **Scales:** Large, fewer than 60 along the lateral line.
- **Mouth:** Ventrally located; lower lips moderately enlarged and heavily papillose (contrast with the laterally flared, notched mouth of *Pantosteus clarki*, which also bears a cartilaginous jaw sheath).
- **Pharyngeal arches:** 31–41 teeth.
- **Coloration:** Sharply bi-colored — brownish dorsally, yellow ventrally. Dorso-lateral scales outlined with melanophores, forming faint dashed lines along the flanks. Pectoral, pelvic, and anal fins often washed yellowish/whitish, with darkened interradial membranes on all fins.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males darken during breeding season and develop large nuptial tubercles on the anal fin, caudal fin, and ventral body surfaces.
- **Hybridization:** Known to hybridize with [[Desert Sucker]] where syntopic; hybrid morphology tends to resemble *C. insignis* more than *P. clarki* (Clarkson & Minckley 1988).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat & Range**
- Common in the [[Gila River Basin]] and [[Bill Williams River]] Basin (Arizona), Gila and San Francisco River basins (New Mexico), and headwaters of the Santa Cruz and San Pedro Rivers (Sonora, Mexico).
- Historical but sparse occurrence in Sonoita Creek (single 1904 collection) and rare persistence in the Babacomari River and upper Santa Cruz River, southern Arizona.
- Elevation range: 300 m to over 2000 m.
- Also occupies artificial canal systems in the Phoenix Metropolitan area.
- Larvae concentrate along stream margins; adults favor pools, pools behind irrigation diversions, and avoid shallow turbulent water.

**Community Associations**
- Co-occurs with native fishes: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*).
- Historically associated with now-extirpated Gila River basin species: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

**Diet**
- Larvae: micro-crustaceans and protozoans; mouth position migrates from dorsal to ventral, presumably tracking a dietary shift.
- Adults (Aravaipa Creek): primarily carnivorous on aquatic insect larvae; also consumes plant debris, mud, algae, and seasonally cottonwood seeds.
- Diet study (Schreiber & Minckley 1981): 38 diet items recorded; dietary overlap with other Aravaipa Creek fishes averaged only 44.3%. Unique prey items included microcrustaceans and Acarina. Baetid mayfly nymphs and chironomid larvae were consistently important. Swift-water individuals favored *Ephemerella* mayfly nymphs, simuliid, and glossosomatid trichopteran larvae; pool-dwelling individuals had more diverse diets including dipteran larvae, microcrustaceans, oligochaetes, and nematodes.

**Movement & Behavior**
- In Aravaipa Creek, seasonal movement into cooler canyon reaches during summer and out to warmer areas in winter (Siebert 1980).
- Contrasting data from the Gila and San Francisco Rivers found minimal movement (only 2 of 39 recaptures of 1009 tagged fish moved from tagging site), attributed to differing habitat stability between smaller, more thermally variable Aravaipa Creek and larger river systems (Bestgen et al. 1987).
- Larval drift in the Gila River peaked April–May; catostomid larvae were primarily diurnal drifters (79% collected at noon/dusk), with strong nearshore concentration at noon (6.5:1 nearshore:midstream) shifting to even distribution at dawn (1:1).

**Relative Abundance (Aravaipa Creek, Arizona)**
- 8% (1966–1988 avg.), Woods Ranch to San Pedro confluence.
- 12.9% (1965–1988 avg.), headwaters to above Turkey Creek confluence.
- 15.6% (1950–1988 avg.), BLM wilderness area.

**Parasites**
- Protozoans: *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*, *Myxobolus catostomi*, *Myxobolus discrepans*, *Myxobolus nodularis*.
- Trematodes: *Ornithodiplotomum ptychocheilus*, *Clinostomum marginatum*.
- Cestodes: *Ligula intestinalis*, *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus*.
- Acanthocephalan: *Neoechinorhynchus* sp.
- Nematode: *Rhabdochona decaturensis*.
- Documented from Aravaipa Creek, Oak Creek, and Salt River, Arizona populations.

## Key Findings

- **Spawning behavior:** Typically two males flank a female; gametes released while the group vibrates over gravel substrate, stirring the bed (Minckley 1973).
- **Spawning timing:** Tuberculate/gonad-mature fish found October–July; observed spawning activity February–early July; recently spawned young collected December and February–September, suggesting spawning may occur under favorable conditions nearly year-round. Whether spawning is annual for individual fish is unknown (contrast with non-annual spawning documented in [[White Sucker]], *Catostomus commersoni*).
- **Hybridization:** Naturally hybridizes with syntopic [[Desert Sucker]]; hybrids morphologically intermediate but skew toward *C. insignis* traits.
- **Flow regime importance:** Natural flooding may serve as a spawning cue and helps suppress non-native fish populations (Meffe & Minckley 1987; Minckley & Meffe 1987).
- **Population decline:** Extirpations documented from the southern portion of the historical range (Minckley 1980), driven by dewatering of mid-elevation streams, impoundment, channelization, and flow diversion/regulation.
- **Reservoir intolerance:** Sonoran sucker is intolerant of reservoir conditions and competition/predation pressure from concentrated non-native fishes, making new impoundments a direct habitat threat.
- **Conservation status:** Receives no formal protection outside stream segments managed by conservation organizations (e.g. [[The Nature Conservancy]]); large-scale (kilometer-scale) protection of pools, backwaters, undercut banks, and spawning-suitable riffle substrate (gravel/cobble/rubble) is recommended.
- **Research gaps identified:** Population systematics, spawning trigger factors, fecundity, spawning frequency, age at first spawning, dispersal quantification, larval habitat/diet/dispersal, age and growth studies, interactions with introduced species, and effects of habitat modification on life-cycle completion.
