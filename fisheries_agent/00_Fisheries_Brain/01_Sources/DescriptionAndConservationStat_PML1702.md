# Description and Conservation Status of *Cyprinodon macularius eremus*, a New Subspecies of Pupfish from Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

Miller and Fuiman (1987, *Copeia*) describe *Cyprinodon macularius eremus* as a new subspecies of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*C. macularius*) endemic to the spring-fed pond at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. Morphometric and meristic analyses separate the Quitobaquito population from all other sampled populations, though overlap with the adjacent [[Rio Sonoyta]] population led the authors to assign subspecific rather than specific rank. The paper also reviews the biology, habitat history, threats and conservation status of the subspecies, which survives in the wild only at Quitobaquito and is extinct in most of its former range.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic action:** *C. macularius eremus* n. subsp. is named from the Greek *eremos* ("solitary or lonely"), reflecting the isolation of the population, which may be the only surviving natural stock of the species in Arizona.
- **Type series:** Holotype UMMZ 162661 (male, 40.0 mm SL), with paratypes from collections made by R. R. Miller and F. H. Miller on 14 April 1950, and additional paratypes collected by R. R. Miller, T. E. McMahon, and M. R. Kunzman on 20 May 1982.
- **Morphology:** Compared with the Rio Sonoyta fish, Quitobaquito males have a longer, deeper head, a broader and deeper body, a shorter anal fin, and smaller pelvic and fin lengths. Females are broader, have smaller fins, and a shorter peduncle. Five characters summarize the difference: body, head and mouth widths, depressed anal-fin length, and pelvic-fin length.
- **Meristics (15 males, 15 females):** Dorsal-fin rays mean 10.07 (males) and anal-fin rays mean 10.14; lateral scale series mostly 25–26; vertebrae mostly 26–27 total; mandibular pores mostly 2 (mean 1.97).
- **Coloration:** Nuptial males are bright dark metallic blue to turquoise with chalky blue dorsal and anal fins and a black terminal caudal bar. Females are golden yellow to brownish olive with silvery lower sides and a discontinuous series of dark lateral blotches. In 20 females, vertical bars were mostly continuous (12 of 20), unlike the disrupted bars typical of most *C. macularius* populations.
- **Etymology:** Latinized from Greek *eremos*, meaning solitary or lonely.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The pond at Quitobaquito, observed on 14 April 1950, was a shallow, man-made marshy pond of roughly 70 × 70 m, fed by two springs. Depth was no greater than about 26 cm over most of the pond, and water temperature was 26.2 °C in mid-morning. Substrate was thick mud with silt, and vegetation included *Chara*, *Eleocharis* and *Scirpus*. Summer shallow-water temperatures reached 39.6–41.0 °C. Springs were 26.3–25 °C in April 1950; in 1983 pond water was 24 °C with salinity 0.7 ppt.

**Biology.**
- Omnivorous and unselective, with guts dominated by detritus; prey include insect larvae, water mites, ostracods and *Daphnia* (Cox, 1972).
- Tolerates dissolved oxygen as low as 0.13 mg O₂/L (Lowe et al., 1967) and laboratory temperatures up to 44.6 ± 0.05 °C (Lowe and Heath, 1969).
- Life span about 3 years; age-1 fish averaged 29.6 mm TL, age-2 40.2 mm, age-3 48 mm (Kynard and Garrett, 1979).
- Breeding began in April–May and ceased between mid-July and late August (1975) and by 9 September (1976). Males breed in water under 15 cm deep over solid substrate.
- Population estimates: 7,986 (22 Oct 1975), 3,592 (10 June 1976), 4,558 (10 Nov 1976), and 3,143 (fall 1980).
- A nematode parasite was found in 13 of 58 fish (22.4%) (Cox, 1966).

**Origin and history.** The authors find no evidence of a recent water connection between Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta, though fossil spring deposits suggest a Pleistocene or Holocene link. Rio Sonoyta pupfish likely derive from the lower Colorado River region. The Quitobaquito area was used by Sand Papago people and modified through channelling, diking, grazing and planting before the monument was established in 1937.

**Threats.**
1. Introduction of the golden shiner (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), eradicated on 18 December 1969.
2. Drastic habitat modification by people.
3. Potential loss of habitat through groundwater pumping in the Mexican part of the Rio Sonoyta basin.
4. Pesticide contamination from agriculture in Mexico. A 1976 fish kill reduced the population to fewer than 3,000 by fall and under 1,800 the next spring; fish tissue showed lethal levels of endosulfan.

**Range.** Historically widespread across the Colorado River, Salton Sink, Gila River basin and Sonora. The species is now scarce or gone from the Salton Sea, and in Arizona survives only at Quitobaquito and in cultured stock at Arizona State University. Populations also persist in Baja California (Laguna Salada region) and in Mexican refugia, though exotic species such as tilapia are a major threat.

## Key Findings

- *C. macularius eremus* is a distinct form separable from all other sampled *C. macularius* populations (principal components analysis of 26 measurements), but the authors regard it as a subspecies because of overlap with other populations.
- The Quitobaquito population is distinct in five summary characters and shows no significant allometric explanation for its shape differences.
- Long-term survival of the species in the United States may depend on the Quitobaquito population.
- Management recommendations include reducing pond depth to no more than 15 cm in places, maintaining deeper refuges, and not managing the pond solely as a bird-watching site.
- Artificial refugia (such as Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Borrego Desert State Park) are described as only a temporary expedient, with wild populations essential for maintaining local genomes.
- Allozyme comparisons showed levels of polymorphism and heterozygosity nearly identical between natural and cultured stocks (Turner, 1984).
