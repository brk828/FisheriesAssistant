# Methods of Measuring Drift in Production Biology Studies of Running Waters

#freshwater-ecology #limnology #amphipoda #gammarus-fossarum #stream-drift

## Executive Summary

This methodological paper, a reprint from the *Verhandlungsbericht der Deutschen Zoologischen Gesellschaft* (65th Annual Meeting, 1972), by [[Meyering]] of the [[Limnologische Flussstation]] in [[Schlitz]], tests a way to measure the full population turnover of a benthic invertebrate in a defined stream reach. Over a 28-day experiment (19 June to 16 July 1971) on a 10 m section of the [[Rohrwiesenbach]], a woodland tributary, the study counted all [[Gammarus Fossarum]] leaving the section by downstream drift and by upstream migration. Daily losses by drift stayed between 1 and 2% of the standing crop. Upstream migration was the stronger movement in the first two weeks and then nearly stopped. Losses from both ends halved the population within 20 days. The author concludes that short, enclosed reaches can support production studies, but that reaches with unstable population densities should be avoided.

## Background & Description

**Motivation.** Earlier work had tried to link invertebrate drift to stream productivity ([[Waters 1965]], [[Waters 1966]], [[Elliott 1970]]). Those attempts failed for three reasons, as the paper describes:

- The upstream catchment feeding the drift nets was not delimited, so the source reach was unknown.
- The fraction of the population that entered the nets was unknown.
- Drift distances differ among species (see [[Mclay 1970]]).

**Study organism.** *Gammarus fossarum* is the only gammarid colonising the upper Rohrwiesenbach (cited as [[Meyering 1971]]). The paper treats it as a purely aquatic amphipod. *Gammarus pulex* is mentioned only as a comparison for winter densities near its upper distributional limit.

**Design rationale.** The author argues that control reaches must be large enough to span the microhabitats present, because substrates change over short distances under complex flow patterns. A 10 m reach was judged sufficient in a favourable location. The method was built on the emergence-measurement approach of [[Illies 1971]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reach construction.**

- The 10 m reach was enclosed by barrier nets of 0.7 mm mesh, which excluded all gammarids longer than 3 mm from entering or leaving.
- At the downstream end, the whole flow passed through a concrete pipe and a fine sieve to catch total drift.
- At the upstream end, a 1 m [[Glass Channel Trap]] (an upstream migration trap) lay in the main flow. Animals crossing its smooth surface were swept into a net. Animals in the rough-bottomed side flow walked upstream to the barrier net and were then swept into the trap.
- A sieve above the upstream barrier caught any drift entering from above.
- A bypass ditch carried water during exceptional floods to protect the traps.

**Physical conditions (19 June to 16 July 1971).**

- Mean width about 70 cm, so the usable bottom area was about 7 m².
- Discharge ranged from 8.3 to 2.9 l/s, highest mid-period and lowest near the end.
- Mean water temperature 11.7 °C (maximum 18.0 °C, minimum 7.0 °C).
- No fish, particularly trout, were present, so predation losses were assumed negligible.

**Population estimation.** At the end of the 4-week study, the remaining population was sampled by [[Electrofishing]] on repeated days (six sampling dates, 20 to 29 July 1971, 11,725 specimens in total). The distribution of catches indicated that almost all animals were captured. Daily population sizes were then calculated by adding each day's drifting and migrating animals to the residual population.

- Initial population: about 25,000 individuals, dry weight about 40 g, energy content about 175 kcal.
- Density: about 2,500 individuals per metre of stream, or about 3,600 per m² of stream bed.
- Dead, weakened animals were captured by drift and so were included in the counts.

## Key Findings

**1. Population dynamics**

- Table 1 gives daily population estimates, falling from 25,427 (19 to 21 June) to 11,824 (16 July).
- The total was halved within 20 days, mainly because of upstream migration in the first weeks.

**2. Upstream migration**

- Migration intensity fluctuated strongly. Peaks reached as high as about 7% of the population in June (the OCR reading "7/9" in the Abb. 3 text has been interpreted as 7%).
- Migration almost ceased during July. Table 1 shows upstream counts dropping from several hundred per day in June to 8 by 16 July.
- The author links the timing to the known spring and early-summer upstream migration of *G. fossarum* ([[Meyering 1966]], cited as [[Mayer 1966]] in the reference list). The 4-week study probably fell late in this migration period, so results from other seasons would likely differ.

**3. Downstream drift**

- Daily drift rates stayed fairly constant at 1 to 2% of the standing population.
- Rates dropped somewhat only near the end of the experiment.

**4. Interpretation and recommendations**

- In a stable region, drift outflow and inflow by upstream migration roughly balance, which makes the reach suitable for production studies.
- At range margins (for example, near the upper limit of *G. pulex* distribution), densities can fluctuate widely. Species can disappear temporarily from a reach, or accumulate behind barriers ([[Leumann 1967]]).
- Reaches with unstable densities, especially where abundant species are involved, should be avoided in productivity studies.

**5. Methodological value**

- The paper demonstrates a closed-reach approach that measures drift and migration quantitatively, which earlier open-ended drift traps could not do.
- It shows that drift alone can underestimate or misrepresent total movement, since upstream migration can exceed downstream drift in some periods.
