# Changes in Fish Populations in the Green River Due to Inlet Modifications of Flaming Gorge Dam

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Catostomidae #dam-effects #thermal-regime

## Executive Summary

This study documents fish population changes in the [[Green River]] following inlet modifications to [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1978. The dam closure in 1962 created an excellent tailwater trout fishery but eliminated [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius) for 105 km downstream. Both the trout fishery and squawfish reproduction declined after 1967-1968, attributed to altered flow and temperature regimes. Analysis revealed that increased flows (more than doubled after 1967) rather than release temperatures were primarily responsible for thermal changes downstream. The [[Water and Power Resources Service]] installed inlet modifications in June 1978 to warm tailwater temperatures to approximately 13°C. The three-year study (1978-1980) evaluated effects on warm water fish distribution and reproduction, with special emphasis on rare native species. Results showed successful conversion of the upper river section from cold to warm water habitat, allowing warm water species invasion and reproduction, and documented the first successful [[Colorado Squawfish]] reproduction in 13-14 years.

## Background & Description

[[Flaming Gorge Dam]] was closed in November 1962 following extensive fish eradication programs on the [[Green River]] and tributaries in Wyoming. The initial tailwater period (1963-1966) established one of the country's premier "blue ribbon" trout fisheries. Studies by Vanicek et al. (1970) concluded that native species, especially [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha), [[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus), were nearly eliminated for 105 km below the dam due to cold temperatures. The [[Yampa River]] confluence at this point sufficiently ameliorated cold flows to allow successful reproduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and most other native species downstream.

Around 1967-1968, both the trout fishery below the dam and [[Colorado Squawfish]] reproductive success below the [[Yampa River]] mouth declined drastically. No young squawfish were found for approximately 72 km below the Yampa confluence until 1978.

Temperature data from Greendale, Utah (0.5 miles below dam) showed only slight cooling between periods: 1963-1966 versus 1967-1977 release temperatures decreased minimally (e.g., July from 6.9°C to 5.6°C). However, flows more than doubled after 1967 for practically all months. At Jensen, Utah (94 miles below dam), mean daily flows increased substantially (e.g., July from 2,671 cfs in 1963-1966 to 4,789 cfs in 1967-1977), while temperatures decreased slightly (July from 20.1°C to 19.2°C). Higher flows moved downstream faster and warmed more slowly than lower flows of 1963-1966, reducing the [[Yampa River]]'s capacity to ameliorate temperatures, especially after June when spring high flows receded. [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawn in July-August at approximately 20-22°C, higher than other native species, making them particularly vulnerable to unnatural temperature declines during spawning season.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area encompassed five stations on the [[Green River]]: two above the [[Yampa River]] mouth ([[Lodore]] and [[Alcove Brook]]), two below ([[Island Park]] and [[Jensen]]), and one at the confluence ([[Echo Park]]). Two control stations were established on the [[Yampa River]]. Sampling occurred in March, June, August, and October each year using boat-mounted electrofishing gear and seines. Fish over 200 mm total length received numbered Floy tags; rare species received small numbered dangler tags.

The inlet modifications installed in June 1978 allowed selection of preferred water temperatures, targeting approximately 13°C tailwater releases. This represented a management intervention to address both the declining trout fishery and the loss of native fish reproduction. The study was funded by the [[Water and Power Resources Service]] with additional support from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[National Park Service]].

The [[Green River]] system supports both cold water and warm water fish assemblages, with the [[Yampa River]] serving as a critical thermal refuge and source of warm water influence. The river flows through [[Dinosaur National Monument]], providing habitat for federally protected native species. Daily flow fluctuations from dam operations create additional environmental stressors beyond mean temperature changes.

## Key Findings

**Habitat Conversion Above Yampa River Confluence:**
The [[Green River]] at [[Lodore]] and [[Alcove Brook]] stations transformed from cold water to cool/warm water habitat following inlet modifications. Several warm water species moved upstream to [[Alcove Brook]] by August 1978. Successful reproduction occurred at both stations in 1978 for [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (Catostomus discobolus), and [[Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus). By 1979, additional warm water species reproduced at [[Alcove Brook]], including roundtail chubs, redside shiners, and red shiners. Cold water species, primarily [[Rainbow Trout]] (Oncorhynchus mykiss), declined in abundance except during March sampling periods.

**Colorado Squawfish Reproductive Success:**
Below the [[Yampa River]] mouth, the primary change was return of successful [[Colorado Squawfish]] reproduction after 13-14 years of reproductive failure. The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] found young squawfish 15 km up the [[Yampa River]] in August 1980. Young-of-the-year were collected at [[Lower Yampa]], [[Echo Park]], and [[Island Park]] stations in 1980, but not in 1978 or 1979. Total numbers found were relatively small (less than 100 total by several researchers) compared to numbers documented by Vanicek et al. (1969). Holden and Crist (1981) hypothesized that inlet modifications enabled return of reproductive success, while cold ambient temperatures may have limited overall success rates.

**Growth and Movement Patterns:**
Growth of young fish differed above versus below the [[Yampa River]] mouth, probably due to daily flow fluctuations above the confluence. Considerable recapture data documented movement patterns of both native and introduced fishes. A reduction in number of species reproducing at [[Alcove Brook]] and lowered numbers of young fish at [[Echo Park]] and [[Island Park]] occurred in 1980, while other stations (especially [[Lower Yampa]]) were unaffected. This pattern suggests flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] may have influenced reproductive success of several species at these three stations.

**Species Composition Changes:**
At [[Lodore]] station before modifications: mountain whitefish, [[Rainbow Trout]], cutthroat trout, brown trout, carp, [[Speckled Dace]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], roundtail chub, Utah chub, redside shiner, fathead minnow, [[Razorback Sucker]], mountain sucker, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], white sucker, channel catfish, black bullhead, and mottled sculpin were present. After modifications, successful reproduction (R) was documented for [[Speckled Dace]], fathead minnow, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and mottled sculpin.

At [[Alcove Brook]] station, species diversity increased after modifications, with successful reproduction documented for [[Speckled Dace]], roundtail chub, redside shiner, fathead minnow, red shiner, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and white sucker.

**Temperature-Flow Interactions:**
The analysis demonstrated that flow regime changes were more influential than absolute release temperatures in determining downstream thermal conditions. Pre-1967 lower flows (e.g., 1,178 cfs in July 1963-1966) allowed greater warming during downstream transit compared to post-1967 higher flows (2,625 cfs in July 1967-1977), despite only minor differences in release temperatures (6.9°C versus 5.6°C). This finding has important implications for dam operations and native fish management in regulated river systems.
