# Lee's Ferry Rainbow Trout Fishery: Fish Management Report 1989–1993 and Agency Objectives

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department report (Federal Aid Project F-7-M-36, Statewide Fisheries Investigations) reviews the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater trout fishery on the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] for 1989–1993. The fishery, once a celebrated trophy rainbow trout water, declined sharply after 1990 because of interacting stressors: fluctuating and "test" flows that scoured and desiccated food-producing areas, a heavy infestation of a parasitic nematode, and high angling pressure. Management responses included a four-fish bag limit (1978), immediate-kill and artificial-only rules (1980s), and a 16–22 inch slot limit with a two-fish bag (1990). The report closes with research, stocking, creel, survey and regulation recommendations. A companion letter (July 8, 1994) from the Department's Wildlife Management Division sets out native-fish and aquatic objectives for Glen and Grand Canyons.

## Background & Description

The document is a draft management report with a cover letter and an appendix letter. It is organized around four data sources: electrofishing (GCES data), creel surveys (boat launch at Lee's Ferry), stocking records, and angler opinion surveys. Authors are [[Scott Reger]] (Fisheries Program Manager, Region II), [[Charles Benedict]] (Fisheries Specialist) and Deanne Wayne (Creel Clerk).

The fishery lies in a one-mile-long tailwater reach. The 1963 completion of Glen Canyon Dam altered runoff, flow, temperature, dissolved oxygen, sediment transport and the aquatic food chain. Anglers were the main harvesters, and access was limited to the boat launch. The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program funded much of the later research.

Stocked species during the period included rainbow trout (Belknap/BelAir strain), brook trout, Kamloops rainbow (1985–1988) and brown trout. Brook and Kamloops stocking was discontinued in 1989. Cutthroat trout were not stocked.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Hydropeaking caused rapid fluctuation and low minimum flows. Minimum flows below 5,100 cfs and high fluctuations during 1990–1991 test flows strongly reduced food supply. Reger et al. (1988) and Persons et al. (1995) recommended a minimum of 8,000 cfs. Stranding loss of adults was estimated at 1,500–2,500 adults per year, mostly during spawning. Interim flows from late 1991 greatly reduced fluctuation and ramping rates.
- **Food base:** Scouring and desiccation reduced [[Gammarus]] and other invertebrates, which was linked to the decline in condition.
- **Parasitism:** A [[Nematode Parasite]], *Bulbodacnitis ampullastorma* (as spelled in the text), infected the pyloric caeca of all trout sampled in a December 1990 health exam (Landye, 1991). The parasite was present in preserved specimens as early as 1980. It was synergistic with food shortage and stress.
- **Native fish:** Sampling recorded flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, carp and striped bass. Flannelmouth sucker abundance fluctuated strongly, high in 1985–1986 and low thereafter.
- **Reservoir ageing:** Lake Powell aging (decreasing plankton and nutrients) was considered a possible cause of slower growth.

## Key Findings

**Population and growth**
- Stocked fish made up about 75% of harvest (Janisch, 1985). Under steady high flows, natural reproduction was estimated at 27.5% and stocking at 72.5% (Maddux et al., 1987).
- Morgenson (1991) estimated 98,000 ± 29,000 fish for 15.4 miles, based on 15 small reaches.
- Wire-tagged fish survival through 9/93: 60% for fingerlings and 48% for catchables. Projected survival: 22% of fingerlings to exceed 16 inches after 3 years, and 23% of catchables after two years.
- Growth in 1984–1988 was about 0.5 inches per month (range 0.45–0.54). Post-1990 natural growth was about 0.333 inches per month, against "virtual no growth" in 1990–1991.
- Recovery of the adult segment would take three to four years under stable flows.

**Condition**
- Relative weight declined from 1984 to 1990 and then began to rise. It had not returned to 1984 levels by 1993.
- Anglers appear to select plumper fish; over 90% of fish caught are released.

**Angling and harvest**
- Catch rates were about 0.60 fish per hour (since 1989). The San Juan River recorded 0.85 fish per hour.
- Released fish per angler hour: 0.750 (1990), 0.509 (1991), 0.380 (1992), 0.449 (1993).
- About 20% of anglers caught nothing; boat anglers succeeded at about 80%.
- Non-resident share rose to 34% in 1977–1979, fell to about 20% by the late 1980s, and reached 34% again in 1991–1993.
- Harvest fell after 1989, with a sharp drop in 1992–1993 (3,216 and 2,051 fish).

**Regulations and opinion**
- Regulations in force by year: four-fish bag (1978), immediate kill (1980), artificial-only (1986), two-fish bag with 16–22 inch slot (1990).
- Overall satisfaction: 70.7% very good. Dissatisfaction was highest for fish numbers (about 25% fair or poor) and fish size.
- Guided anglers differed from the overall group (e.g., 39% vs 64% for catch-and-release only).
- A 1994 telephone survey (Behavior Research Center) found 79% support for current management and 53% for no-kill rules. The survey also found 83% of users were Arizona residents.
- Economic benefit was estimated at $5 million (1982 dollars) for recreation, and nearly $[unreadable] million in total economic output for 1991.

**Management implications and recommendations**
- Objectives: at least 50% of Age III rainbow trout naturally reproduced; more than 100,000 fish of age III and older; Age III trout reaching 18 inches with relative weight at least 0.80.
- Research: study flow effects on food production and trout growth, evaluate habitat-maintenance flows, and develop a study of the nematode.
- Stocking: coded-wire tag all stockings and adjust rates using annual survey data.
- Creel: seek more funding, better release data and opinion questions.
- Regulations: maintain artificial-only and immediate-kill rules, allow a one-day license (the only change proposed now), and investigate a stratified regulation system like the San Juan River's.

**Agency objectives letter (July 8, 1994)**
- Guiding principle: conserve and enhance native fishes in Grand Canyon while providing coldwater sportfish opportunities where compatible with conservation.
- Humpback chub: maintain population at or above 1987 levels (about 11,750 fish over 200 mm) and establish a second self-sustaining population by 2005, if feasible.
- Native species such as flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker and speckled dace should be self-sustaining in the mainstem and tributaries.
- Aquatic food base: maintain continuously inundated areas in Glen Canyon at or above 5,000 cfs discharge.
- Riparian and wildlife habitat: maintain bald eagle forage populations, waterfowl at 1993 levels, and the Kanab ambersnail population.
