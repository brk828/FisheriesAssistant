# The Coho Salmon of Cultus Lake and Sweltzer Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 paper by [[R. E. Foerster]] and [[W. E. Ricker]] (Pacific Biological Station, Nanaimo) describes coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) in [[Cultus Lake]] and its outflow [[Sweltzer Creek]], a Fraser River tributary system. The data were collected incidentally during [[Sockeye Salmon]] investigations and are based on fence counts, screen-fence counts of downstream migrants, gill-net catches, and marking experiments. The central finding is that lake-produced smolts are too few to sustain the lake's coho run, which depends heavily on creek-bred adults, while a large share of lake-born juveniles remain as non-migrating "residual" coho that rarely reproduce.

## Background & Description

- **Life history context:** Most coastal commercial coho are three-year-old fish (3₃ class, 97.9% in [[Pritchard]]'s 1940 sample). Young coho usually spend about a year in fresh water before going to sea.
- **Age terminology:** Males maturing at age II are called "jacks" when entering rivers. Jacks were numerous at Cultus Lake.
- **Adult size:** Regular adults entering Sweltzer Creek measured 21–30 inches (53–76 cm, average about 24 inches / 61 cm). Jacks averaged about 12 inches (30 cm).
- **Fence infrastructure:** Several fences (nos. 1–7) were installed on Sweltzer Creek for sockeye propagation. Fence 6 was the main count site, and fence 7 (the "screen fence") trapped downstream migrants.
- **Sex identification:** The sex of many large coho was hard to determine by external features, so some recorded sex ratios are estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake versus stream habitat:** Coho in the Fraser region are normally stream dwellers. At Cultus Lake they live in a lake, where they compete and interact with sockeye, their main prey as young fish.
- **Smolt production:** Natural smolt yield from spawning in and above the lake averaged 0.13% of eggs (about 3 smolts per female) in 1925–30. After predator control (1935 onward), the geometric mean rose to 0.33% (about 8 smolts per female).
- **Comparison with creeks:** Nile Creek on Vancouver Island yielded 18–44 smolts per female, and another creek yielded 15–16.
- **Salmincola parasitism:** The freshwater copepod *Salmincola* occurs on lake-resident coho but not on sea-run coho, making it a diagnostic marker of residual origin.
- **Temperature and growth:** Winter air temperature at Agassiz correlated with coho size at r = 0.69, and a multiple correlation with sockeye abundance reached 0.74. Both were statistically non-significant.
- **Predation:** Sockeye smolts are the main food of lake coho. Predator control in the lake affected smolt yield.

## Key Findings

- **Run size:** Only a few hundred to one or two thousand coho entered Cultus Lake each year, while Sweltzer Creek held several times as many.
- **Lake recruitment failure:** Lake spawning and tributary spawning produced fry and yearlings, but the smolt yield was insufficient to maintain the run. The lake run therefore depends on creek-bred fish.
- **Marking experiment (1927):** Of 1,512 yearling and 72 two-year-old smolts marked, 122 adults were recovered in 1928. This gave an estimated survival of 8.1% for yearlings. The authors considered this an underestimate because of fin-clipping mortality and undetected jack maturity.
- **Smolt-to-adult return:** The 8.1% return is above average creek return rates, but it was insufficient to offset low smolt production.
- **Residual coho:** Many lake-born yearlings did not migrate. They were caught mainly in autumn of their second year and in spring of their third. Estimated non-migrant yearlings were roughly 4 times the number of migrants (range 12,000–58,000 non-migrants versus 12,840 known smolts over 1935–39).
- **Residual correlation:** Residual numbers and smolt numbers were correlated at r = 0.87 (95% confidence level) over five year-classes, consistent with a common stock.
- **Maturity:** Residuals mature at the same ages as anadromous coho (males at II, both sexes at III), with little evidence of early maturity. Reproduction by residuals appears negligible or absent.
- **Sex ratio:** Among January–June third-year fish, 44.8% were male (747 males, 917 females). The 1934 year-class showed a male excess (33 males, 17 females).
- **Size:** The largest residual coho recorded was 595 mm (2,400 g). Anadromous coho average about 610 mm. Residuals are smaller and duller in colour than sea-run fish.
- **Two-year-old migrants:** Two-year-old migrants were very scarce, with a maximum of 157 (0.029% of eggs). The largest coho known to leave the lake was 455 mm and 1,000 g.
- **Hypothesis:** The authors propose that lake-bred anadromous salmon need specialised behaviour to leave a lake, which Sweltzer Creek-derived fish largely lack. Testing this would involve experiments with smolts in lake-simulating tanks.
- **Lake versus stream contrast:** Stream coho populations in British Columbia have shown no residual spawners, and coho do not form known permanent freshwater populations in North America.
