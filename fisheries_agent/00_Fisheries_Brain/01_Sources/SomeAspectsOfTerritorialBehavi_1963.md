# Some Aspects of Territorial Behavior of the Fantail Darter

## Executive Summary
This study by Melvin F. Seibert examines territorial and aggressive behavior in the striped fantail darter (*Etheostoma flabellare lineolatum*) under controlled aquarium conditions. Specimens collected from Bluff Creek, Boone County, Iowa, were observed to establish and defend discrete territories—typically small covers or "nooks" under rocks—when provided with natural substrate. Territory establishment, cover selection, and dominance hierarchies were strongly correlated with body size, though prior occupancy and established residency also conferred competitive advantages. The study additionally documents a striking, rapid (≤7 minute) diel pigmentation change, suggesting a link between background matching, photoperiod, and habitat selection.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: [[Fantail Darter]] (*Etheostoma flabellare lineolatum*), a subspecies/striped form within the [[Percidae|Darter Family]].
- **Size classes observed**:
  - Small: ~1 inch (non-territorial)
  - Medium: 1–1.5 inches (aggressive, territory-bound to cover)
  - Large: 2–2.5 inches (dominant, defend only extent of cover; select more open covers)
- **Pigmentation**:
  - Exhibits pronounced, rapid pigmentation change correlated with light conditions and substrate background.
  - Daytime/illuminated coloration: light, matching pale sand substrate.
  - Nighttime/dark coloration: pronounced dark blotches along the dorsal fin base, each producing two dark bands extending past the lateral line toward (but not reaching) the ventral region.
  - Ventral region remains unpigmented/unchanged regardless of light condition.
  - Transition from dark (night) to light (day) coloration occurs within a maximum of 7 minutes after light exposure.
  - Faint outlines of blotch/band pattern visible during the day beneath normal parallel striping.
- **Fin behavior as behavioral signal**: Erect fins during aggressive confrontation/display; relaxed fins during flight response.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Natural habitat**: Shallow riffle areas with gravel substrate in small streams; also found on gravel beach areas of some lakes across much of the eastern United States ([[Hubbs and Lagler 1947]]).
- **Collection locality**: Riffle areas of Bluff Creek, Boone County, Iowa.
- **Feeding ecology**: Visual predator; feeds on insect larvae in the wild. In captivity, readily accepted live tubificid worms as a substitute prey item, indicating reliance on movement cues for prey detection.
- **Substrate preference hierarchy** (aquarium bottom material): 
  1. Stone (most preferred)
  2. Pebble
  3. Sand (least preferred)
- **Habitat/cover preference hierarchy**:
  1. Small, single-occupant cover (fully enclosed, concealing)
  2. Large cover (open on multiple sides) — claimed only by largest darters
  3. Large rocks (partial shelter along edges)
  4. Bare aquarium corners (no physical cover, but defensible space)
- **Substrate dependency for territoriality**: Darters housed in a substrate-free "reserve tank" did not establish territories and instead ranged freely, indicating that natural substrate/cover cues are necessary to trigger territorial establishment and associated aggressive/defensive behavior.
- **Air hose orientation behavior**: Coverless "extra" darters in each aquarium consistently oriented around and defended the air hose bubbler, sometimes wrapping around the nozzle; this preference persisted even when the air hose was relocated, independent of substrate change — suggesting current/aeration or associated stimuli serve as an alternate territorial anchor point.

## Key Findings
- **Size-dependent dominance**: Larger darters consistently displace smaller ones from preferred covers; when a dominant fish was removed, the next-largest individual assumed its position.
- **Territory size vs. cover size**: The *size of the defended territory* was not correlated with darter body size, but the *size/type of cover selected* was — large darters preferred more open, larger covers (up to 4 sq. in.), while medium darters preferred small, secluded, single-occupant covers.
- **Open-territory anomaly**: One medium-sized darter defended a large, open pebbled area (~60 sq. in., ~1/3 of aquarium floor) oriented around an air hose, demonstrating that non-cover-based territories can be substantially larger than cover-based ones.
- **Step-releaser aggression mechanism**: Intruders could approach within 2–3 inches of a resident undetected; upon discovery, a static fin-erect standoff occurred until the intruder made any movement, which then triggered a charge/attack by the resident (head-butting and biting). Stationary intruders did not provoke attack, indicating a movement-based behavioral releaser.
- **Prior occupancy effect**: A dominant darter removed for 5 days and later reintroduced immediately reclaimed its cover from a substitute occupant (the former second-ranked darter), which yielded without counter-attack — suggesting individual recognition of prior dominance status independent of a fresh size-based contest.
- **Established residency can override size**: Long-established darters successfully defended their covers even against newly introduced larger individuals, indicating that residency/familiarity can offset a pure size-based dominance hierarchy.
- **Context-dependent dominance**: In a non-territorial context (competition over food outside defended areas), a small darter successfully drove off a dominant darter roughly three times its size, suggesting non-territorial aggression is less strongly size-correlated than territorial aggression.
- **Pigmentation and habitat selection linkage**: Darters appeared to select background substrate matching their own coloration during daylight; a darker-pigmented individual abandoned a "won" contest for territory in favor of relocating to the only patch of exposed dark aquarium cement, implying pigmentation-driven microhabitat selection independent of social/size dominance outcomes.
- **Broader implication**: The authors suggest aquarium-based behavioral observation of darters is a viable method for comparative ethological study and may help clarify the ecological distribution patterns of various darter species ([[Etheostoma]] spp.) in the wild.
