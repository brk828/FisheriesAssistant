# Rainbow Smelt Introduction Proposal for Lake Powell: Review and Opposition Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of correspondence and a draft resolution from 1989 to 1990 concerning a proposal by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] to stock non-native [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] as a forage fish to enhance the [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) fishery. Reviewer Paul C. Marsh, Associate Professor at Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies, repeatedly opposes the proposal. He argues that the risks to native [[Colorado River Fishes]] are unquantified and potentially severe, and that the expected fishery benefit is speculative. The letters also contain a resolution by the Desert Fishes Council, which initially endorsed stocking, and a notice that the state of Utah had been encouraged to pursue management strategies.

## Background & Description

- **Rainbow smelt (*Osmerus mordax*):** Introduced species with a documented history of establishing large populations in lakes. Its diet and predators are wide-ranging, including fishes and invertebrates. The proposal describes it as a deep-water species occupying cold habitats below the [[Thermocline]].
- **Proposal:** Utah's "Assessment of a Forage Fish Introduction into Lake Powell" (Gustaveson et al., 1990) and an earlier version were reviewed. The proposal was to stock smelt to enhance the striped bass fishery.
- **Imperiled native fishes:** The letters focus on [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], all listed as endangered, threatened or of special concern and subject to [[Recovery Implementation Program]] efforts.
- **Lake Powell fishery:** The current forage base is [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*). The reservoir has a mean depth of 166 feet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation and competition:** Cited literature (Evans and Loftus, 1987) reports that smelt affect native fishes both directly (predation) and indirectly (competition). Documented recruitment declines occurred in some systems, while no impacts were observed in others.
- **Literature base:** Evans and Loftus reviewed 24 case histories of smelt introductions in eastern US and Canadian lakes, drawn from 34 published documents. Sixteen of these studies reported an association between smelt introduction and recruitment decline of one or more indigenous species. Marsh argues that the Gustaveson et al. (1990) assessment did not adequately consult this literature; only one of the 16 studies was cited.
- **Habitat and foodweb concerns:** Marsh argues that if striped bass shift to deep water to feed on smelt, anglers would have less access to them, since the species would no longer be concentrated near the surface where shad are caught.
- **Fishery data:** Catch-per-unit-effort in Lake Powell has remained near 0.4 fish/hr. Approximately 130,000 fishing boat days were estimated, with an estimated 1,000,000 angler-days cited for the proposal's calculation (as reported in the source).
- **Management history:** Lake Powell's fishery has been described as "hit or miss," with stocking of largemouth bass, threadfin shad and rainbow trout producing mixed or failed results.

## Key Findings

1. **Risk to native fishes is unquantified and potentially severe.** Marsh concludes that the risk of smelt invasion is "sufficiently high" to warrant exclusion, as recommended by Evans and Loftus (p. 262), and that the potential for extirpation or recruitment failure of native species is not adequately addressed.

2. **Benefit to striped bass is speculative.** Marsh questions whether smelt would enhance striped bass numbers or size, noting that the proposal provides no quantitative prediction of its effects on the fishery.

3. **Burden of proof.** Marsh criticizes the state for placing the burden of proof on others. He argues that Utah must demonstrate beyond speculation that native fishes will not be harmed before any introduction occurs.

4. **Data gaps.** Major questions remain about the Lake Powell food web, primary productivity, nutrient inputs and the status of shad and striped bass populations. Marsh notes that available data cover only about a decade.

5. **Resolution outcome.** A draft resolution from the Desert Fishes Council (16 November 1989, Albuquerque, New Mexico) initially supported the stocking of rainbow smelt into Colorado River system waters. It also encouraged Utah to develop and implement striped bass management strategies consistent with native fish conservation. Copies were to be sent to federal and state agencies and recovery teams.

6. **Marsh's conclusion.** The introduction of rainbow smelt to Lake Powell is "ill-advised" and "premature," and he urges Utah to abandon the proposal. He recommends that the authors seek advisement from scientific agencies, private organizations and the public.
