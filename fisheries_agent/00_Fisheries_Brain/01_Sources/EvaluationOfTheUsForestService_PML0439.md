# Evaluation of the U.S. Forest Service Fish Habitat Relationship System in East-Central Arizona Trout Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Research Branch technical report (Technical Report No. 8, September 1991, Federal Aid Project F-14-R) by [[Robert W. Clarkson]] and [[Jeffrey R. Wilson]] tests the U.S. Forest Service [[Fish Habitat Relationship System]] (FHRS) for high-elevation trout streams in east-central Arizona. Habitat and fish population data were collected from 243 sampling stations across 39 stream reaches in 1986–1990. The authors found that most FHRS models performed poorly locally: the [[Habitat Condition Index]] showed weak relationships with trout standing crops (R² 0.017–0.155), and the [[COWFISH Model]] explained little variation. Regression models developed locally explained about 60% of variation in biomass, with bank damage from ungulates (cattle) the strongest predictor. The authors conclude that the [[GAWS Survey Design]] requires multiple stations per reach, that the [[Rosgen Channel Classification]] needs refinement, and that long-term monitoring is essential.

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The study set five objectives: (1) determine whether the GAWS sampling design and data adequately describe stream conditions; (2) determine whether the land-aquatic classification system is applicable to Arizona lotic ecosystems; (3) test FHRS models locally; (4) modify existing or develop new habitat models; and (5) implicitly, evaluate the utility of the approach for management.

**Study streams.** Surveyed streams were selected from the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and the [[White Mountain Apache Nation]]. Priority was given to streams with resident [[Apache Trout]] populations. Only unstocked, lightly fished, first- and second-order streams were used in model analyses. Three "pristine" (ungrazed or lightly grazed) White Mountain streams were included for range and comparison. [[Coyote Creek]] was deleted after fish mortality linked to drought-related high water temperatures was observed. Coconino National Forest GAWS data were incompatible and excluded.

**Native and introduced fauna.** Historically, these streams held the endemic Apache trout, sometimes with [[Speckled Dace]]; [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] occupied lower reaches. Exotic [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Brook Trout]] now occupy many streams and have displaced, replaced, or genetically swamped Apache trout.

**Governance.** A 10-member steering committee from AGFD, USFS, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the University of Arizona was formed in 1989 and met March 30, 1989 to shape the study plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat-independent fluctuation.** The authors stress that stream fish standing crops fluctuate with drought, flood, winter ice, and affect mortality, spawning success, and recruitment independent of habitat. This confounds habitat–fish relationships and model assumptions. Populations were sampled over four years, which partly mitigates the problem because nearby streams share climate.

**Carrying capacity and measurement error.** Carrying capacity is treated as a concept rather than a measurable response, so standing-crop point estimates may not coincide with it. Measurement precision was high (confidence interval half-width generally <5% of the mean) for measured variables but lower for visually judged ones.

**Habitat types.** Habitat units followed [[Bisson Habitat Classification]] and [[McCain Habitat Classification]] categories, including low-gradient riffle, cascade, run, glide, and pool.

**Land use.** Livestock grazing, especially by cattle, damages stream banks and affects temperature, sedimentation, channel width, and cover. The authors link ungulate bank damage to trout declines through these pathways.

**Standing-crop benchmark.** Standing crops in ungrazed streams equal or exceed the highest values reported for nonanadromous salmonid streams in North America (Platts and McHenry 1988).

## Key Findings

- **Sampling scale.** Data came from 243 sampling stations within 39 reaches (1986–1990). Clustered transect data (n = 1,201 transects) were pooled to 243 station means for regression.
- **Within-reach variability.** Coefficients of variation among clustered transects were usually in double digits and often exceeded 100%. Single-station reach summaries misrepresented conditions in most cases (K-W tests significant for 10–80% of variables per reach). Multiple stations per reach are needed for unbiased fish estimates.
- **Survey designs.** Clustered-transect and systematic-transect data gave similar results (only 57 of 874 variable-by-reach combinations significantly different), suggesting systematic transects could reduce effort without serious loss of data quality. Recommendation: retain clustered transects for modeling, and treat the two designs as complementary.
- **Rosgen classification.** Reach-level channel types were homogeneous in only about 41 of 69 reaches with fish data; within-stream comparisons showed significant habitat differences between channel types. The authors conclude that the Rosgen system "does not always define reaches that display significant differences" in habitat.
- **Habitat use.** Trout abundance correlated negatively with absolute pool and high-gradient riffle area and positively with percent glide, low-gradient riffle, and run area. This contradicts much of the literature and may reflect cover limits, sampling bias, or juvenile habitat behavior.
- **Habitat Condition Index (HCI).** Only a few components (pool measure, bank stability, bank vegetation) were significant, with R² of 0.020–0.155. The authors found little utility for Arizona.
- **Habitat Vulnerability Index (HVI).** A Delphi evaluation found that the index may suit hydrologists but not trout biology. The channel stability component was judged poorly suited to fish habitat. The authors warn that HVI could justify further land use in already degraded riparian systems.
- **Potential Spawning and Rearing Areas.** Gravel size limits should be restricted to 32 mm for Apache trout; rearing area (<0.3 m/s) seldom limits Arizona streams; overwintering area was considered potentially severely limiting but undefined.
- **COWFISH.** Tested on 45 meadow transect sections. Existing-trout predictions were poor (R² = 0.09 for the overall regression, best R² = 0.306 for actual trout ≥2 numbers). The model predicted optimum trout numbers better (R² = 0.326 for top-25% stations). The authors recommend against using COWFISH in Arizona without modification.
- **Regression models.** Best trout model (square root of trout biomass, n = 170 subset) explained about 60% of variation (adjusted R² = 0.5999); best total fish biomass model (log transformation) explained about 60% (adjusted R² = 0.5866). Ungulate bank damage was the most important variable in both. Validation: 94.4% of 43 new trout observations fell within predicted 95% confidence intervals.
- **Model reliability.** At least 10–20% of predicted standing crops are likely outside acceptable limits, with average differences exceeding 20%.
- **Statistical problems.** Data were non-normal, heteroscedastic, and had many zeros; only nonzero observations were used in regressions. Logistic regression was used for presence/absence.
- **Monitoring recommendation.** Sample streams on a five-year rotation, or monitor representative streams annually for at least five years, then switch to the rotation. Long-term funding is required.
- **Management conclusion.** Better cattle management in most White Mountain riparian zones is needed to realize these streams' productive potential.
