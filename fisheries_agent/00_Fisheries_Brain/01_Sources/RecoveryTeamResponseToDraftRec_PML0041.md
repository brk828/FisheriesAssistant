# Recovery Team Response to Draft Recovery Plans for Upper Colorado River Endangered Fishes (1983)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This July 26, 1983 letter from the Recovery Team Leader to Regional Director [[Galen Buterbaugh]] of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Denver, responds to comments on the draft recovery plans for the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. The team disputes several points in the Regional Director's comments. It argues that the squawfish's remaining range is fragmented and declining, that downlisting is premature, and that legal protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] is needed before any downlisting or translocation. It also argues that additional populations are needed to spread risk, and that habitat study should continue even where no viable bonytail population exists.

## Background & Description

The letter is a formal reply on the Recovery Team's behalf. Its subject is three endangered big-river fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]:

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the main focus of the letter.
- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*), addressed in a short section.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*), named in the letter's subject line, with comments on draft plans and on other issues.

The letter responds to the Regional Director's letter of June 16, 1983, which commented on the draft conservation and recovery plans. It also raises several general points about the recovery program, including stocking numbers, self-sustaining populations, and the need for an upper basin hatchery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range loss:** The squawfish has been eliminated from an estimated 75 percent of its historic range, according to the draft conservation plan.
- **Habitat fragmentation:** The remaining range is being broken up by water development projects. One project is under construction and others are planned on the mainstem Yampa and Colorado rivers. The letter describes this as a likely effective loss of a significant number of Colorado squawfish from the upper Colorado River.
- **Migration between basins:** Recent investigations by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the National Park Service, the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, and the Colorado Division of Wildlife documented squawfish migrating from the White River into the Green River. Recruitment from these fish is not yet quantified, but the letter expects a significant contribution to the Green River ecosystem.
- **Tributary development:** The letter notes that development on tributaries of the Yampa, Green, and upper Colorado rivers (including the White, Duchesne, and Blue rivers and others) fragments populations further. The team considers this fragmentation, combined with habitat degradation, to be insufficient to sustain the species on its own terms.
- **Population trend:** Catch-per-unit-effort data show squawfish numbers in the upper Colorado River are considerably lower than in the Green River. The team reads this as possible evidence of a faster population decline in the upper Colorado.

## Key Findings

**Colorado Squawfish**

1. **Stability is not established.** Squawfish undertake long spawning migrations and seasonal movements, which makes accurate population estimates extremely difficult. The team says no assertion of stability can be made on current data.
2. **Downlisting is premature.** The team recommends against any downlisting until more data are available.
3. **Self-sustaining populations are needed.** Establishing another stable, self-sustaining population outside the Green/Yampa system is necessary to diversify the species' distribution. This would reduce the risk of major losses from a catastrophic event on the Colorado River.
4. **Artificial propagation is questioned.** The team suggests that artificial propagation of populations where reproduction is the only limiting factor be reconsidered in light of the Endangered Species Act.
5. **Legal protection first.** Squawfish habitat should receive legal protection under the Endangered Species Act before any downlisting or translocation. Without such protection, the habitat remains highly vulnerable to ecosystem perturbations.

**Bonytail Chub**

6. **Habitat study remains necessary.** The absence of a viable bonytail population does not remove the need to study its habitat. The team suggests that study of reintroduced populations is another way to gather information.

**General Recovery Program**

7. **Recovery objectives.** The team questions whether the stated recovery objectives for additional populations (beyond the Green/Yampa system) are valid, and states that these populations are necessary for diversity and to occupy as much of the remaining historic range as possible.
8. **Other comments.** Issues raised by the Regional Director, including stocking numbers, self-sustaining populations, and the need for an upper basin hatchery for endangered fishes, are considered by the team to be adequately addressed in the most recent draft plans.
9. **Remaining gaps.** The Regional Director asked the team to identify issues overlooked or not adequately addressed in the most recent drafts. The team's reply implies that the main gaps are the legal protection of habitat and the assessment of population trends.

**Follow-up**

10. The team asks the Regional Director to contact it after reviewing the letter, and copies the letter to all Recovery Team members.
