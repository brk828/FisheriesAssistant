# Biological Opinion on Livestock Grazing, Baseline and Horse Springs Allotments (Eagle Creek, Arizona)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #accipitridae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Arizona Ecological Services State Office) biological opinion, dated July 20, 1995 (reference 2-21-95-F-020), evaluates a proposed [[Allotment Management Plan]] for the combined Baseline and Horse Springs livestock grazing allotments in Greenlee County, Arizona. The opinion was prepared under section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] for the requesting [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] (Clifton Ranger District). Species of concern are the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bald Eagle]], and the threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. The Service concluded that implementation of livestock grazing under either the proposed [[Biologically Controlled Grazing]] system or the [[Backup Rotational Grazing]] system is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of any of these species. No critical habitat for these species occurs in the action area. An incidental take statement was issued for the three listed fishes, with take indexed to fish community and habitat condition rather than numerical quantities.

## Background & Description

**Proposed action.** Cattle grazing on 9,494 acres of Clifton Ranger District National Forest land plus 640 acres of private land, under a permit issued in 1992 to the Anchor Ranch (permit expires December 31, 2002). The two allotments would be combined. Stocking would range from a minimum of 188 cows and calves (3,019 animal unit months) to a maximum of 300 cows and calves (4,818 animal unit months), moved as one herd through 33 paddocks. The 1995 Annual Operating Plan permitted 284 head.

**Setting.** Allotments range from 4,800 to 6,400 feet in elevation, receiving 12 to 16 inches of annual precipitation. Pinyon-juniper, juniper-oak, or juniper-grass woodlands cover 82 percent of 9,356 acres of uplands; grasslands cover 17 percent. Riparian vegetation along [[Eagle Creek]], Bear Canyon, and Bee Canyon includes Arizona sycamore, narrowleaf cottonwood, Arizona walnut, boxelder, and alder, with low woody regeneration.

**Vertisol soils.** Deteriorated clay-loam [[Vertisol Soils]] support little vegetation and are vulnerable to compaction, gullying, and goldeneye and broom snakeweed invasion.

**Grazing history.** Grazing since the mid to late 1800s; first permit in the early 1920s; first management system in 1972. In 1990 numbers were reduced 20 percent, followed by non-use in the second half of 1990 and all of 1991.

**Listed species status.**
- *Razorback sucker*: listed endangered October 23, 1991; critical habitat designated March 21, 1994, excluding Eagle Creek. Grows over two feet; lives up to about 50 years. Eagle Creek is presumed to have supported the species historically and was restocked between 1981 and 1988 with [[Lake Mohave]] hatchery stock.
- *Spikedace*: listed threatened July 1, 1986; critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, excluding Eagle Creek. A small silvery fish with a dorsal spine; lives about two years.
- *Loach minnow*: listed threatened October 28, 1986; critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, excluding Eagle Creek. A small, slender, bottom-dwelling fish; lifespan about 2 years.
- *Bald eagle*: listed endangered March 11, 1967; proposed reclassification to threatened (July 12, 1994) in most of the lower 48 states. The southwestern population is treated as a distinct population; no bald eagle nests are known on Eagle Creek, though wintering eagles frequent the area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Eagle Creek is a spatially and temporally intermittent tributary of the [[Gila River]] in Greenlee County. Between Mud Springs Canyon and Big Dry Canyon it carries surface water only about 2 to 3 months per year, though it flowed perennially in the early to mid 1900s according to local residents.

**Human impacts.** Principal disturbances are livestock grazing (the dominant land use for about 150 years in the watershed), water diversion and transfer (a 1945 [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]] diversion from the Black River augments flow below Willow Creek by about 27 percent, with a further 9 percent removed about 15 miles downstream at a diversion dam and pumping station), groundwater pumping, mining, and residential, ranch, and cropping activities along the floodplain.

**Nonnative fishes.** Reported species include black and yellow bullheads, common carp, [[Red Shiner]], mosquitofish, channel and flathead catfish, smallmouth and largemouth bass, rainbow trout, fathead minnow, and crayfish. Native species still form the majority of the fish community above the Phelps Dodge diversion dam; nonnatives predominate below it.

**Fragmentation.** The Eagle Creek spikedace population is roughly 100 river miles from its nearest neighbor. Genetic and morphological work indicates distinct remnant units within both spikedace and loach minnow.

**Mechanisms of effect identified in the opinion:** (1) watershed alteration, (2) physical damage to streambanks, channels, and water column, (3) alteration of riparian vegetation, (4) alteration of the faunal community, and (5) effects from non-grazing and structural elements.

**Environmental baseline.** The Service characterized the continued existence of spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker in Eagle Creek as "seriously imperiled."

## Key Findings

- **Jeopardy determination:** Grazing under either the biologically controlled or backup rotational system is "not likely to jeopardize the continued existence" of spikedace, loach minnow, razorback sucker, or bald eagle.
- **Watershed condition:** Overall ground cover is "often only 40-60 percent"; soil compaction, erosion, and gullying are present. Vertisol soils occupy 20-30 percent of the grassland association.
- **Vegetation trend:** Upland vegetation trend is upward under similar management; soil condition was generally stable from 1955 to 1988.
- **Stocking and rest:** Minimum rest period of 120 days; growing-season use limited to 4 to 11 consecutive days per paddock; dormant-season use limited to 30 consecutive days.
- **Backup system:** Seven-pasture deferred rotation with a minimum of 60 days rest and maximum of 60 days use during growing season and 90 days during dormancy. Expected to produce less improvement and possibly a stable or decreasing trend with greater adverse effects to Eagle Creek.
- **Direct effects:** Considered few and of very low probability (injury from cross-river fence work, crushing of fish or eggs by hooves, cattle waste toxicity). Indirect effects are "certain to occur."
- **Habitat impacts:** Wider, shallower channels reduce pools needed by adult razorback sucker, reduce shear zones favored by spikedace, and favor red shiner. Loach minnow is more sensitive than spikedace or razorback sucker to fine sediment.
- **Temperature:** Riparian shade loss increases temperature fluctuation, which may disrupt spawning initiation (spawning is tied to water temperature in all three fishes).
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Spikedace and loach minnow require relatively high dissolved oxygen; excessive algal growth may cause temporary depletion.
- **Bald eagle:** No direct take anticipated; effects arise through reduced perch-tree regeneration and food base changes.
- **Incidental take threshold:** Exceeded if more than 20 dead fish of any species are found in the project area or within 500 yards downstream, or if channel morphology shows a long-term trend (five years of data) of decreasing stability or increasing erosion. Take is not quantified because of limited population data and rapid fluctuations in short-lived species.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures:** Minimize direct mortality and habitat loss; monitor fish community and habitat; keep complete records.
- **Terms and conditions:** No heavy equipment within the wetted area of Eagle Creek; minimize channel and floodplain alteration; monitor for dead or dying fish; establish a minimum of two photopoints and one permanent cross-channel transect read annually.
- **Conservation recommendations:** (1) evaluate monitoring data at least every other year; (2) conduct a comprehensive drainage-wide section 7 consultation on Forest Service grazing in the Eagle Creek basin; (3) allow riparian grazing only during the dormant season of woody vegetation.
- **Cumulative effects:** Private inholding activities (residential use, roads, grazing, irrigated cropping) and Phelps Dodge water manipulation are cumulative to the proposed action; the grazing contribution "must be ameliorated" for ecosystem recovery.
- **Prior consultations:** The 1986 Apache-Sitgreaves Forest Plan consultation found no jeopardy for spikedace, loach minnow, or bald eagle; a January 1994 Fillman Ranch channel stabilization consultation found no jeopardy for spikedace.
