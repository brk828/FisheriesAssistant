# *Ambloplites constellatus*: A New Species of Rock Bass from the Ozark Upland

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper describes *[[Ambloplites Constellatus]]*, the Ozark rock bass, a new centrarchid from the upland section of the [[White River]] in Arkansas and Missouri. The authors separate it from its congeners, mainly [[Ambloplites Rupestris]] (northern rock bass) and [[Ambloplites Ariommus]] (southern rock bass), using a freckled lateral color pattern, a slender body form, and higher meristic counts for fin rays and scales. The species is restricted to the Ozark Upland portion of the White River drainage, with two questionable records from the [[Osage River]] drainage.

## Background & Description

**Context.** The new form was first noticed by [[Pflieger 1971]], who observed that rock bass from the upper White River differed strikingly in color from other Missouri populations. The authors examined material from across the [[Ozark Upland]] and other western populations before proposing the new species.

**Diagnosis.** The species differs from all congeners in its freckled lateral pigment. Compared with *A. rupestris*, it has a slimmer body, higher average dorsal and anal fin element counts, and finer squamation.

**Holotype.** TU 90909, an adult male, 186 mm standard length (SL), collected from the [[Buffalo River]] at the mouth of Rush Creek, Marion County, Arkansas, on 12 May 1972 by R. C. Cashner and J. M. Humphries. Five paratypes (TU 78124, 119–190 mm SL) were collected with it, and many other paratype lots were listed from the Buffalo and White rivers.

**Morphology.**
- Relatively large rock bass with a slender body; body depth about 40% of SL.
- Large eye with red iris in life; large mouth with a protruding lower jaw.
- Cheek fully scaled, with scales not deeply embedded.
- Lachrymal bone subrectangular, with fine serrations on its posteroventral edge.
- Seven infraorbital bones; branchiostegal rays 6–6; vertebrae 13 + 18 = 31.

**Meristic ranges (from the text).**
- Total dorsal rays: (22) 23 (24)
- Total anal rays: (15) 17 (18)
- Pectoral rays: 14 or 15
- Lateral line scales: (38) 40–46 (48)
- Scale rows below lateral line: (11) 12 or 13 (14)
- Diagonal scale rows: (21) 22–24
- Caudal peduncle scale rows: (17) 19–21
- Breast scale rows: (20) 21–25 (26)

**Coloration.**
- Ground color dark olive to tan, with more uniform color above and below the lateral line than in congeners.
- Discernible pigment on each body scale, forming an irregular freckled pattern. Usually three, but no more than six, successive scales in a row carry spots.
- Above the lateral line, four or five saddle-like blotches may be visible.
- Spots are scattered on the cheek, opercle and preopercle, which the authors describe as unique in the genus. The pattern is clearest in fish over 50 mm.
- Fins are olive green, with prominent dark spots on the bases of the dorsal and caudal fins.
- Males do not develop the black marginal band on the anal fin seen in *rupestris*.

**Methods.** Meristics follow [[Hubbs and Lagler 1947]] with modifications, and the range notation follows [[Snelson 1972]]. Measurements were made to 0.05 mm. Comparisons were restricted to specimens of equivalent size and analyzed by [[Analysis of Variance]] and [[Analysis of Covariance]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The Ozark rock bass prefers small to moderate streams with permanent flow, high dissolved oxygen, abundant aquatic vegetation, low turbidity and silt-free substrates. It is very abundant in the Buffalo River, where most specimens were taken near the bank, around boulders, logs or tree stumps, in 2–5 ft of water where the current was weaker than in the main channel. Its habitat closely resembles that of upland *rupestris* populations.

**Distribution.**
- Restricted to the Ozark Upland section of the White River drainage in Missouri and Arkansas, ranging throughout the upper White River and nearly disappearing at the border with the [[Gulf Coastal Plain]].
- Absent from the upper [[Black River]] and its upland tributaries.
- Two records from the [[Osage River]] drainage (UMMZ 151720 and TU 38038). Three specimens from 1944 and 1965 may represent a natural range extension, but stocking is considered more likely.

**Biogeographic parallels.** The [[Yolk Darter]] (*Etheostoma juliae*) has a similar distribution, avoiding the coastal plain of the lower White and the upper Black. Several other darters, including the [[Arkansas Saddled Darter]], fantail darter, banded darter and orangethroat darter, show comparable differentiation between the White and Black systems. The authors attribute this to the [[Mississippi Embayment]] lowlands, which may act as a barrier to dispersal of upland species.

**Stocking history.** The Arkansas Game and Fish Commission hatchery at Centerton has used upper White River brood stock and stocked fingerlings in other drainages. Many early Missouri stocking efforts also used upper White River brood stock. Arkansas records from 1946–1970 indicate no rock bass were stocked in the Black River drainage.

## Key Findings

1. **New species recognized.** *Ambloplites constellatus* is distinguished by its freckled lateral pattern and slender body. Differences from *A. rupestris* are in color, squamation and fin ray counts. No intergradation was found with *A. rupestris* or *A. ariommus*, and *A. ariommus* is treated as a full species.

2. **Body shape.** Most Ozark rock bass have a body depth to SL ratio between .38 and .42. *A. rupestris* from the [[Meramec River]] and [[Missouri River]] ranged from .42 to .44. Body depth separated 93% of Ozark rock bass from the other populations and about 83% from Meramec and Missouri *rupestris*. *A. ariommus* is the deepest-bodied member of the genus.

3. **Fin counts.** Mean total dorsal fin elements were 22.80 and mean anal fin elements 16.77 (upper White sample, n = 91). These are higher than in *rupestris*, which has 22 and 16. Ozark rock bass differed significantly (p < .01) in dorsal counts from all western populations except the Little River *ariommus*.

4. **Scale counts.** Mean lateral line scales were 43.41 (n = 91), and mean breast scale rows were 23.37 (n = 82). The species has significantly higher means (p < .01) than other populations for cheek scale rows, scale rows above the lateral line, lateral line scales and breast scale rows. It has finer squamation than any rock bass in adjacent drainages.

5. **Holotype and paratype data.** The holotype was 186.0 mm SL with a body depth of 72.4 mm (389 per thousand SL), 22 dorsal and 17 anal elements, 42 lateral line scales, and 24 diagonal scale rows. Five paratypes (119–190 mm SL) are reported in Table 1.

6. **Relationship to other species.** *[[Ambloplites Cavifrons]]*, the Roanoke bass, has higher breast scale counts (30–34) and more scale rows above the lateral line (9–11). *A. cavifrons* also has lighter spots on the nape, cheek and opercle, which the authors use to separate it from sympatric *rupestris*.

7. **Distribution limits.** The species is confined to the Ozark Upland of the White River, with possible native occurrence in the Osage drainage. The authors consider the Osage records equivocal because stocking may explain them.

8. **Validity.** The authors argue the upper White population is a valid species. The presence of constellatus-type fish in a drainage that also contains *rupestris* supports this conclusion.

9. **Etymology.** The name *constellatus* refers to the clustered dark spots on the side of the body. The name was suggested by [[Carl L. Hubbs]].
