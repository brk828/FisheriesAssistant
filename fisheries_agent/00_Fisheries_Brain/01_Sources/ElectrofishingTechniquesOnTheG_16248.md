## Executive Summary

This report documents the history, methodology, and limitations of electrofishing as a fisheries assessment tool on the [[Green River]] below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], Utah. Electrofishing was used sporadically from 1964 onward to study [[Rainbow Trout]] populations, but efforts intensified in 1977–1978 in response to a dam modification project that allowed warmer water releases into the river. The core objective was to develop a reliable [[Population Estimate]] technique for trout via electrofishing, but the paper concludes that equipment limitations, safety concerns, and methodological shortcomings (insufficient marked/recaptured fish, short survey sections) prevented statistically valid population estimates. The study is primarily a methods/equipment evaluation rather than a species biology paper, focusing on gear configuration, day vs. night efficiency, and river access constraints.

## Background & Description

This paper does not provide detailed morphological data on [[Rainbow Trout]] (e.g., length-weight relationships, meristics). The only size-related observation is behavioral/size-linked: larger trout were noted to be more effective at avoiding the electrofishing boat during daytime runs, suggesting size-dependent evasion behavior potentially linked to sensory acuity or experience in clear water conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The study river is a regulated tailwater fishery created in 1964 following construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery exists in a section of the [[Green River]] characterized by clear water conditions, boulder-strewn substrate, and small rapids.
- **Thermal regime alteration**: In 1977–1978, penstock modifications allowed release of warmer water from the reservoir during summer months, prompting research into effects on trout populations — indicating sensitivity of the trout fishery to thermal regime changes typical of dam-regulated systems.
- **Diel behavior**: Trout distribution shifted with light conditions — fish occupied deeper water and avoided the boat during the day (in clear water), but moved into **shallower water at night**, and may have been drawn toward the boat's running lights, greatly increasing capture efficiency after dark (5–10x more effective than daytime electrofishing).
- **Movement/migration**: Fish migration within study sections was recognized as a confounding factor for population estimates, indicating notable movement/mobility of trout within the river over the study timeframe.
- **Site accessibility**: The river reach between the dam and the Colorado state line has limited road access — only the tailrace ramp, Little Hole ramp (7 miles downstream), and several launch points in the [[Browns Park]] area — constraining sampling effort spatially to areas near access points.

## Key Findings

- **Daytime electrofishing was largely ineffective**, especially for larger trout, likely due to clear water allowing fish to visually detect and avoid the boat.
- **Nighttime electrofishing was 5–10 times more effective** than daytime efforts, attributed to trout occupying shallower water and possible attraction to boat lighting.
- Original gear setup (1977): 16 ft "john" boat, 70 hp jet outboard, [[Coffelt Electronics]] system with a model VVP-15 power control unit driving two positive booms with single-cable droppers.
- **1978 gear modification**: A dual electrode array (two circular positive electrodes with 8–10 droppers each, plus 5 negative droppers per side), based on a design by Novotny and Priegel ([[Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources]]), modestly improved daytime efficiency but remained insufficient for population estimation.
- Using the **Peterson mark-recapture method**, nighttime electrofishing in 1978 produced population estimates as high as **8,000 rainbow trout per mile**, but sample sizes (marked/recaptured fish) were too low for statistical validity.
- Survey sections (~600 yards) were determined to be **too short**, allowing fish migration to bias estimates; longer sections were recommended for future work.
- **Safety concerns**: Nighttime work on the boulder-laden river was hazardous using the existing low-freeboard, riveted aluminum hull boat; a **larger welded aluminum hull** was recommended for future safety and effectiveness.
- **Current management use** (at time of writing): Electrofishing continued to be used for age/growth studies, species composition, and survival/movement studies, but **SCUBA surveys** were adopted as the preferred method for population indices in lieu of reliable electrofishing-based population estimates.
- **Conclusion**: Reliable electrofishing-based population estimates on the Green River await improved equipment and greater survey effort, particularly for safe and effective nighttime operations.
