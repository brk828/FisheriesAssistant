# The Fish Population of the Main Stream of the Big Muddy River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study by William M. Lewis of [[Southern Illinois University]] describes the fish community of the main stream of the [[Big Muddy River]], a [[Mississippi River]] tributary in southern Illinois. The river is dominated by commercially harvested species such as [[Common Carp]] and buffaloes, while sport fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are scarce. The author attributes the weak game fishery mainly to extreme water-level fluctuations, lack of spawning habitat, and [[Creosote Pollution]] and [[Coal Mine Waste]] pollution. The report also documents a [[Fish Kill]] and the loss of fish downstream of a creosote source. It concludes that flow control and pollution removal are needed before fisheries management can improve recreational fishing.

## Background & Description

**Physical setting.** The Big Muddy River basin covers 2,360 square miles, extending 105 miles north to south and 70 miles east to west. Water levels fluctuate by 20 to 30 feet, and high water on the Mississippi can reverse the Big Muddy's current up to 40 miles upstream. The main-stream channel is 20 to 50 feet wide and 50 to 70 feet deep. Banks are almost entirely mud, with a few rock outcrops and at least one gravel area. Sandstone is abundant in at least five of the river's eight principal tributaries. The shoreline is heavily forested, but much of the watershed is cultivated or pastured.

**Prior studies.** Two earlier theses on the river are cited:
- [[Walker (1952)]] examined physico-chemical conditions and pollution. He found chemical conditions usually above minimum requirements for fish, with spasmodic, localized toxic pollution concentrated in the tributaries. Major pollutants were sewage, creosote, silt, garbage, iron, and coal-mine wastes.
- [[Schuster (1953)]] studied water quality. He reported bacterial counts indicating some sewage pollution, normal BOD values, dissolved oxygen always above a critical minimum, and high chlorides from Pond Creek that raised values in the Big Muddy. Water was slightly alkaline at low water and slightly acid at high water. Turbidity was moderately low and mainly from erosion silt, and water temperature closely tracked air temperature.

**Sampling methods.** Three methods were used, each with a distinct bias:
- **[[Hoopnet]] and wing-net sampling** (April and May 1951, stations 1 to 3, high water). Nets were lifted every three days.
- **[[Electrofishing]]** (August 22 to October 16, 1951, stations 4 to 7, normal water; May 25 to July 13, 1953, mostly station 8; one extra sample on October 9, 1953). A boat-mounted 230-volt generator was used.
- **[[Seining]]** (October 1953, stations 4, 7, 8, 10, 11, and 12, low water) with a one-quarter-inch mesh minnow seine.

The author stresses that gear selectivity matters. Water level strongly affects catch: high water raised hoopnet catches but lowered electrofishing catches. Largemouth bass in clear water were not vulnerable to hoopnets, but electrofishing was very effective for bass, carp, and gizzard shad. It was less effective for crappie, sunfish, buffaloes, gars, and catfishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollution.** Walker's work and the author's observations point to pollution as a major limit on fish. Pollution probably harms fish more through sublethal effects, such as lower food-organism abundance, destroyed spawn, and fish tainted for consumption, than through direct kills. It likely makes the river less suitable for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

**Fish kill.** [[Schuster]] reported hundreds of dead fish of all species, plus some turtles and mussels, in [[Crab Orchard Creek]] and just below its mouth on the Big Muddy, observed on June 16, 1953, two or three days after the event. He suggested a rain may have washed toxic material into the river. A tie-treating plant lies in the Crab Orchard Creek watershed.

**Creosote effects.** On February 11, 1954, Southern Illinois University personnel found abundant fish one-quarter mile above the mouth of Piles Fork, a tributary draining the tie plant area. There were no fish at or below the mouth for about three miles. The author notes that creosote accumulates in tie storage and washes into the river during rain, so peak loads may go unobserved.

**Coal-washing waste.** At least one coal-washing plant stores waste in settling ponds that are periodically drained into the river. Heavy fish kills have been linked to this practice.

**Trophic structure.** Both [[Longnose Gar]] and shortnose gar are abundant and highly efficient predators. The author suggests the river may support a gar-shad economy separate from a carp and catostomid-invertebrate economy. [[Gizzard Shad]] form great schools and serve as forage, but game fishes are not numerous enough to use them.

## Key Findings

**Commercial fishes.** [[Common Carp]], largemouth buffalo, smallmouth buffalo, and [[Freshwater Drum]] are abundant and dominate the fish population, so most fishing is commercial. The [[Blue Sucker]] was not captured by electrofishing, but a commercial fisherman took several hundred large ripe fish at station 9, suggesting spawning migrations up the Big Muddy. [[Channel Catfish]], [[Blue Catfish]], and [[Shovelhead Catfish]] are rather scarce. Electrofishing is poor for catfish, but the river is not considered productive for them, and seining found no young catfish.

**Commercial catch (1951).** Starrett and Parr (1951) reported ten part-time and one full-time fisherman. Carp catch was 19,167 pounds and catfish catch was 396 pounds. Buffalo and drum totals are illegible in the scanned text. Commercial catch, made mainly with hoopnets, is best during high water.

**Bait fishes.** Three species are listed: [[Steel-colored Minnow]], [[Emerald Shiner]], and bluntnose minnow. Only the steel-colored minnow is abundant enough to matter, and it is available only at low water, usually in hot weather.

**Game fishes.** Nine species are listed: [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], yellow bass, white bass, bluegill, warmouth, [[Largemouth Bass]], eastern sauger, and walleye. The white crappie is the only species abundant enough to support significant recreational fishing, and large individuals are often caught at certain points when water is not high or turbid.

**Miscellaneous fishes.** Fourteen species fall in this group, including gizzard shad, longnose gar, highfin sucker, carpsucker, blackstripe topminnow, mosquito fish, [[Bowfin]], orangespot sunfish, goldeneye, [[American Eel]], northern redhorse, river darter, logperch, and eastern burbot. Gizzard shad are the most prominent and have no sport or pan value.

**Controlling factors.** The author identifies three main limits on game fish: extreme water-level fluctuation, lack of spawning facilities, and creosote and mine-waste pollution.

**Management recommendations.**
1. Build more lakes and use other means to hold water on the watersheds, eventually gaining control of major flow fluctuations.
2. Eliminate chemical pollution.
3. Then apply developing management techniques to increase recreational fishing.

**Reference note.** The text cites Schuster as 1953, while the literature list gives 1952 for his thesis. Both are cited under the author's name in the text.
