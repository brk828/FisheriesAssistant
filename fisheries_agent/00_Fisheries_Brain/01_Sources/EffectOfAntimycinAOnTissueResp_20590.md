# Effects of Antimycin A on Tissue Respiration of Rainbow Trout and Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 report from the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (Fish Control Laboratory, La Crosse, Wisconsin) by Richard A. Schoettger and Gerald E. Svendsen measures how the piscicide [[Antimycin A]] affects oxygen consumption (respiration) in liver, kidney, brain and gill tissue of a sensitive species, [[Rainbow Trout]], and a resistant species, [[Channel Catfish]]. Respiration was measured both in vitro (tissue homogenates) and in vivo (fish exposed for 4 hours at 12°C). The authors conclude that tissue-level sensitivity differences in vitro are too small to explain the large species difference in toxicity, so the resistance of catfish is attributed mainly to poor absorption, faster deactivation or metabolism, and gill structure.

## Background & Description

- **Toxicant:** Antimycin A (98% active ingredient, obtained from Ayerst Laboratories, New York). It is a known potent inhibitor of electron transport in oxidative phosphorylation, specifically at the cytochrome b–c1 segment (Complex III) of the respiratory chain.
- **Prior toxicity ranking:** Walker, Lennon, and Berger (1964) found that trout families (trouts, perches, herrings) are most susceptible; pikes, sunfishes, suckers and cyprinids are intermediate; freshwater catfishes, gars and bowfins are least sensitive. Bioassays showed channel catfish were roughly 20 times as resistant as rainbow trout.
- **Research question:** Whether tissue sensitivities of trout and catfish differ enough, in vitro and in vivo, to account for species selectivity.
- **Test fish:**
  - Rainbow trout: 10.6–12.2 in (about 27–31 cm), 200–300 g, from the National Fish Hatchery at Manchester, Iowa, and Peterson Trout Farm, Minnesota.
  - Channel catfish: 11–13 in (about 28–33 cm), 138–312 g, trapped from the Mississippi River by National Fish Hatcheries at Guttenberg and Fairport, Iowa, and Genoa, Wisconsin.
  - All fish were acclimated to reconstituted water at 12°C following [[Lennon and Walker Methods]].
- **Methods overview:**
  - [[Warburg Respirometry]] was used to measure oxygen uptake (µl O₂ per mg dry weight per hour; gill tissue per mg wet weight).
  - In vitro homogenates (1 part tissue to 7 parts reaction medium) were tested at 25°C, with replicates of 4 or 9 per experiment. Significance was set at the 0.05 level using analysis of variance and least significant differences.
  - In vivo exposures used 45 L polyethylene tanks with one fish each, at concentrations of 3–80 ppb for 4 hours, with acetone solvent controls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mode of action:** Antimycin A inhibits succinate oxidation and, in fish liver mitochondria, alpha-ketoglutarate oxidation. Hepatic aldehyde oxidase is the only non-respiratory-chain enzyme known to be inhibited by it.
- **Tissue-level context:** Liver showed the greatest oxygen consumption of all tissues, followed by brain and kidney. Trout tissues generally respired faster than catfish tissues at 25°C, consistent with Beamish (1964), who reported brook trout respiration at about twice that of brown bullheads at 20°C.
- **Gill structure:** Steen and Berg (1966) reported that inactive fish (such as brown bullhead) have fewer, thicker gill lamellae and a diffusion distance about three times that of trout. Steen and Kruysse (1964) described a circulatory shunt in gill lamellae that was most developed in *Silurus glanis*.
- **Blood chemistry:** Schoettger and Svendsen (1968) reported that antimycin A disrupts acid-base balance in trout and catfish.
- **Reversibility and bypass:** Rieske (1967) reported that succinate oxidase in rat liver, lung and spleen recovers within 2–4 hours after sublethal doses, possibly via serum albumin binding. Liver was the only tissue shown to chemically inactivate the toxicant in vitro.
- **Other taxa:** Ritter and Strong (1966) found that carp, which are more resistant, survived longer and took up more tritium-labelled antimycin than rainbow trout. Cheah (1967) described a branched electron transport system in the cestode *Moniezia expansa* that could act as a bypass.

## Key Findings

1. **In vitro sensitivity:** Concentrations of 5 ppb or more significantly inhibited kidney respiration in both species. Liver was inhibited at 10 and 20 ppb (Table 1). Brain was approximately 4 to 8 times as resistant as liver. At 80 ppb, trout brain respiration fell 54% and catfish brain fell 33%.
2. **In vivo effects:**
   - No observable effects occurred at 3 ppb in trout or 10 ppb in catfish.
   - At 5 ppb, trout began surfacing within 1–2 hours and then settled in a sedated state.
   - Catfish showed similar sedation within 1 hour at 20 and 40 ppb.
   - Trout exposed to 10 ppb and catfish to 80 ppb showed poisoning signs within 30 minutes and were moribund at 4 hours.
3. **Tissue comparison:**
   - Trout brain was about eight times as sensitive in vivo as in vitro (80 ppb in vitro versus 10 ppb in vivo to cause significant inhibition).
   - Catfish liver and kidney were about one-quarter as sensitive in vivo (in vitro concentrations were four times the in vivo ones).
   - Liver and kidney of trout and brain of catfish responded similarly in both settings.
4. **Gill tissue:** Catfish gill was more than four times as resistant as trout gill. Kidney and gill had similar in vivo sensitivity in trout.
5. **Blood chemistry:** At 2 ppb for 6 hours, trout blood total CO₂ dropped from 26 to 9 volumes percent, pH fell from 7.4 to 6.9, and lactate rose from about 43 to 184 mg percent. Catfish exposed to 80 ppb for 3 hours showed similar changes, but these were less acute until just before death.
6. **Conclusions:** In vitro tissue sensitivities are not different enough to explain the magnitude of species differences. The authors conclude that antimycin A is either poorly absorbed or more readily deactivated or metabolized by catfish. Gill structure, a well-developed shunt system and thicker gill epithelium may contribute, as may exclusion or detoxification. A bypass of the inhibited site in catfish has not been demonstrated and requires further study.
7. **Research need:** The authors call for comparative studies of biochemical and physiological mechanisms of uptake and detoxication to understand resistance and to guide development of [[Fish Control Agents]].
