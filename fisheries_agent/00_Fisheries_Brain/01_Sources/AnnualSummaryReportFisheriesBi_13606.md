# Cataract Canyon Fisheries Biology and Rafting: 1987 Annual Summary

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Annual Summary Report, prepared by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) under Contract No. 6-CS-40-03980 (dated January 12, 1988), describes the 1987 fish survey of the Cataract Canyon region of the upper Colorado River basin. The study sought to locate spawning sites of endangered fishes, determine whether [[Humpback Chub]] populations exist in Cataract Canyon, and assess how BOR's operation of [[Lake Powell]] affects nursery habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Eight sample trips were made between April and October 1987. Twenty-eight fish species were recorded, most of them non-native. Endangered species made up 4.27% of the total catch. No spawning sites were identified, but the presence of larvae, young-of-the-year (YOY), juveniles and adults supports a reproducing humpback chub population in the canyon.

## Background & Description

The study was part of BOR's ongoing program for endangered fishes of the upper Colorado River basin, carried out under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. Since 1978, BOR and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) have funded studies of the endangered Colorado squawfish, humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) and bonytail (*Gila elegans*), and of the federal candidate species [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

[[Cataract Canyon]] is a 16-mile whitewater reach of the Colorado River within [[Canyonlands National Park]]. It lies between the confluence of the [[Colorado River]] and [[Green River]] and the upper end of Lake Powell. Earlier work was limited because the reach is difficult to access and sample. Attention to the area's endangered fishes began in April 1980, when 45 adult Colorado squawfish were captured near Gypsum Canyon during a striped bass study.

This is the second of three scheduled Annual Summary Reports. Its eight progress reports correspond to eight sample trips.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling design.** The Cataract Canyon Region covers the Colorado River from Goose Neck (RM 37) to Imperial Canyon (RM 200), and the Green River from Mineral Bottom (RM 52) to the confluence. Six 10-day "canyon trips" ran from June 23 to October 12, 1987. Two 7-day "flatwater confluence trips" in mid-April and early September sampled chubs above the confluence. Trips launched from Potash (RM 47) on the Colorado River or Mineral Bottom on the Green River.

**Sampling gear.** Six gear types were used:
- [[Electrofishing]] (220 V DC from an 18-foot raft), with catch per unit effort (CPE) expressed per 10 hours of electrofishing.
- [[Gill And Trammel Nets]], with CPE per 100 linear feet of net per 100 hours.
- Small-mesh seines (10 × 4 ft), with CPE per 10 m².
- Kick screens, for which no CPE was computed.
- [[Drift Net Sampling]] (560-micron Nitex nets), with CPE per 1000 cubic feet of water.

Fish were identified in the field and preserved larval specimens were verified by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] in Fort Collins, Colorado.

**Habitat.** Per-sample water depth, velocity and substrate were recorded where possible. Habitat types for seining included backwaters, shorelines, pools, isolated pools, runs, eddies, embayments and concavities. Sampling was largely confined to calmer water between rapids.

**Lake Powell influence.** Some riverine species were captured in quiet backwaters within the canyon, including black crappie, largemouth bass, green sunfish and walleye. Non-native striped bass, threadfin shad, bluegill and kokanee salmon were taken below the canyon, where riverine habitat is inundated by Lake Powell.

## Key Findings

### Community Composition
- **Species total:** 28 species in 10 families were captured. This is similar to 1985 and 1986, except for two suspected adult bonytail and one adult razorback sucker in 1987.
- **Origin:** 20 species are non-native (exotic) and 8 are native or endemic. Ratio of non-native to native catch was 87.22% to 12.41%, with 0.37% unclassified (a glass vial was broken in shipment).
- **Dominant species:** [[Red Shiner]] (52.70%), [[Channel Catfish]] (11.70%), [[Common Carp]] (9.40%), sand shiner (6.23%) and fathead minnow (5.05%). These five non-native species made up 85.08% of the catch.
- **Endangered species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] 4.17%, humpback chub 0.09%, bonytail 0.01%; combined 4.27% of the total catch.
- The full data set is in Appendix A and was supplied to BOR on diskette in dBASE III+ format.

### Colorado Squawfish
- **Total handled:** 1016 fish: 2 adults, 69 juveniles, 834 YOY and 111 larvae. All adults, juveniles and field-identified YOY were released alive. Of 2235 specimens sent to the Larval Fish Laboratory, 279 were larval or YOY squawfish.
- **Adults:** Two adults were captured, one in April at RM 16.2 near Indian Creek and one in July at the confluence (RM 216.3). Four adults were captured in 1985 and five in 1986.
- **Seine CPE:** 1.77 fish per 10 m² overall. In backwaters the rate was 1.73. In isolated pools it was 3.79, which the authors describe as a concern because stranded fish face desiccation, high temperature, low oxygen and predation, although some may survive isolation or benefit from abundant food.
- **Electrofishing CPE:** 2.8 fish per 10 hours. No squawfish were caught in gill or trammel nets.
- **Drift nets:** One squawfish was captured in drift samples in 1987, compared with three in 1986.
- **Spawning sites:** None were identified. Variation in larval sizes and back-calculated hatching dates suggests fish are drifting from multiple upstream sites. Most larvae appear to come from the Green River system.

### Hatching Dates and Year-Class Survival
- Hatching dates were back-calculated for 490 YOY and larvae using length/age regressions from Haynes et al. (1985) (see [[Back-Calculated Hatching Dates]]).
- In 1987, hatching occurred mainly from mid-June through late July, with some in May. Peak hatching was in early July in 1985, early August in 1986, and late July in 1987.
- Fall 1985 YOY (n=295) averaged 15.7 mm longer than fall 1986 YOY (n=297). By the following spring the difference was 7.2 mm (n=32 for 1985; n=60 for 1986).
- Fall CPE was 2.0 for the 1985 class and 6.0 for the 1986 class. The following July CPE was nearly equal (0.23 and 0.29), so fall YOY monitoring may not reliably index year-class strength (see [[Year-Class Survival]]).
- YOY distribution was stratified into four regions. Region 1 (Green River, Mineral Bottom to confluence) had the highest seine CPE in all three years, above the 0.9 fish per 10 m² threshold used to designate high-density nursery areas.

### Humpback Chub
- **Total:** 21 fish: 6 adults, 11 juveniles, 4 YOY and no larvae. Catches in 1985 and 1986 were 11 and 17 fish.
- **Taxonomy:** Canyon chubs are generally smaller than 200 mm TL, with blunt heads, shallow nuchal humps and deep bodies. Dorsal and anal fin ray counts were not consistently 9 and 10. The authors hypothesize a unique regional form, possibly a geomorph, of *G. cypha*. A "Chub Biography" supplement with photographs and meristics was submitted for expert review.
- The authors consider the presence of larval and YOY humpback chub strong evidence of a reproducing population in the canyon.

### Bonytail and Razorback Sucker
- **Bonytail:** Two tentatively identified fish were captured: a juvenile (264 mm TL, 120 g) and an adult (287 mm TL, 129 g). Both were tagged with red Carlin tags.
- **Razorback sucker:** One adult (493 mm TL, 1618 g) was captured, tagged and released on September 11, 1987, at a riffle at the mouth of Salt Creek (RM 3.5). It is the first razorback captured in Cataract Canyon during the 1985–1987 studies. Earlier records include catches at The Slide and at Gypsum Canyon in upper Lake Powell.

### Gear Performance
- **Electrofishing:** Highest catch rates were for carp (360.7 fish per 10 h) and channel catfish (206.8). Red shiner (220.8) and sand shiner (51.3) rates likely underestimate density because small fish escaped the dip net mesh.
- **Gill and trammel nets:** Gill nets were set for 1164.2 hours and trammel nets for 357.2 hours. Carp had the highest rates with both gears. Gill nets captured four humpback chub and one suspected bonytail.
- **Drift nets:** 116 sets yielded 74 fish. Channel catfish were most common (0.89 per 1000 cubic feet). Roundtail chub (0.80) and red shiner (0.57) were low.

### Findings by Objective
1. **Spawning locations:** Spawning sites of Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were not located in 1985–1987. High larval and YOY densities in the lower 50 miles of the Green River indicate significant reproduction above the canyon, with only low numbers drifting into Cataract Canyon and Lake Powell. Peak spawning shifted by as much as one month between years. The authors hypothesize that late-hatched fish enter winter smaller and have lower overwinter survival.
2. **Humpback chub population:** Evidence of reproduction supports a population in the canyon, although its taxonomic identity remains uncertain.
3. **Lake Powell influence:** Endangered fish were relatively rare in the lake inflow. They used backwaters that form at medium lake levels in midsummer, which are diminished at high spring levels and low late-summer levels. Predators and competitors may cause mortality, but this was not assessed.

### Recommendations for 1988
- Conduct at least one more year of study, with six canyon trips from late June to early October.
- Hold an intensive 5–7 day seining survey in late March 1988 to assess overwinter survival of YOY squawfish, coordinated with the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and FWS.
- Concentrate electrofishing and gill/trammel netting for 3–4 days at Cataract Lake (RM 206.8–205.5) and Upper Ten Cent (RM 201.1–201.4).
- Continue larval drift netting for the first three or four canyon trips, to understand drift patterns.
- Analyze chub morphotypes biochemically, meristically and morphologically, with minimal sacrifice of specimens.
- Record lake levels with each sample to relate habitat availability to lake operations.
