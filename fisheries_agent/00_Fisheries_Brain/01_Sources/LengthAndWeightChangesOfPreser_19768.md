# Length and Weight Changes of Preserved Black Crappie and Yellow Perch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 study by [[James E. Johnson]] and [[Bruce L. Swanson]] (Department of Forestry and Wildlife Management, University of Massachusetts, Amherst) tests whether the weight and length changes that occur when fish are preserved are statistically significant, and how long those changes take to stabilize. The work used [[Black Crappie]] and [[Yellow Perch]] collected by [[Electrofishing]] and preserved in [[Formalin Preservation]] (10% and 20%) and then in 50% [[Isopropyl Alcohol]]. Preserved fish gained weight in formalin and lost weight in alcohol, while total length shrank by about 1% to 3%. The authors conclude that most weight change occurs early but continues for months at lower rates, so specimens should be measured at standardized times.

## Background & Description

Investigators who need weights and lengths of fish must choose between measuring fresh specimens in the field and preserving them for later laboratory measurement. Earlier studies showed that preserved fish change in both length and weight. [[Shetter]], [[Amosov]], [[Parker Study]], and [[Stobo]] reported weight changes of about 5% to 15% and length changes of about 1% to 4%. Most of this work involved [[Salmonids]], and the correction factors it produced were rarely applied. The authors suggest this may reflect doubts about validity or a belief that the changes are too small to matter.

The study had two objectives:
- Determine whether length and weight changes in preserved fish are statistically significant.
- If they are, determine when the changes reach stability.

Black crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) and yellow perch (*Perca flavescens*) were chosen for their wide range, local abundance, and the limited preservation research on perciform fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species were collected from two Massachusetts sites: the Oxbow Lake of the [[Connecticut River]] at Northampton and [[Lake Warner]] at Hadley. Fish were held in insulated coolers with cracked ice and taken straight to the laboratory. The authors kept fish from settling in the coolers or resting in meltwater, because [[Parker Study]] had found that this affects initial weights. No fish showed opercular movement when measured 1 to 2 hours after being placed on ice.

Methods included:
- Total length measured to the nearest millimeter.
- Blotting with a damp towel (the "heavy blotting" method of Parker).
- Weighing to 0.01 g for fish under 160 g and to 0.1 g for heavier fish, on Mettler H5 and K4T scales.
- Slitting the right side of most fish to admit preservative into the body cavity. Four smaller crappie were not slit.
- Fixation in 10% or 20% formalin buffered with borax, with some fish rinsed in water after 6 days, followed by storage in 50% isopropyl alcohol.

Analysis used [[Analysis Of Variance]] (BMDO8V, run on University of Massachusetts CDC 3600/3800 computers) and [[Duncan's Multiple Range Test]] at p = 0.05.

## Key Findings

**Weight changes in 10% formalin**
- Weight rose rapidly and significantly (p = 0.01) and was nearly complete by day 6.
- At day 6, perch were at 108.48% and crappie at 106.08% of initial mean weight. Final means were 108.08% (perch) and 106.51% (crappie), and the differences were not significant.
- Combined, the day-6 mean was 106.66% ± 0.345 (N = 109, including 44 crappie transferred to alcohol after 6 days). The final combined mean was 107.31% ± 0.588 (N = 65).

**Weight changes in 20% formalin**
- Both species kept gaining weight after day 6. Significant change continued until day 38 in perch and day 82 in crappie.
- After 272 days, final means were 108.02% (crappie), 108.68% (perch), and 108.31% ± 0.727 combined (N = 35).
- The authors conclude that stronger formalin slows the rate of change but does not change the final result.

**Weight changes in alcohol**
- Fish had been in 10% formalin for 6 days, with combined change of 105.72%, before transfer to alcohol.
- By day 15 in alcohol, crappie had dropped to 94.78% of initial weight and perch to 99.52%. The authors attribute the difference to small perch sample size (N = 15) or to crappie being more flattened, which may make them more prone to water loss. Body size did not explain it.
- Both species then began to regain weight. Perch returned to their initial weight and stayed statistically unchanged for up to 70 days. Crappie showed no significant change after day 15 but had not reached initial weight by day 259.

**Length changes**
- Significant differences occurred in both species, but mean changes were only 1% to 3% of initial length.
- Roughly half the shrinkage happened in the first 6 days, and the rate slowed over time. The lack of significant differences late in the study does not mean shrinkage had stopped.
- The authors recommend waiting about one week before measuring preserved specimens, and then taking all measurements at about the same time.
- Crappie in 20% formalin shrank less than the other groups.

**Fish not slit**
- The four uncut crappie gained the most weight of any fish in 10% formalin, with a mean increase of 10.78% after 6 days.
- Three of them were transferred to water and stayed proportionally heavier than other fish in water. In alcohol they lost slightly less weight than average.

**Practical recommendations**
- If preservative concentration is unknown, wait several months for weights to stabilize before measuring.
- If measurement must be quick, weigh after day 6, when most of the change has already occurred.

*Editorial note: the paper's discussion sometimes refers to "weight loss" in formalin, but the reported data show weight gain in formalin and loss in alcohol. This note follows the reported data.*
