# Salmon and Steelhead Production, Escapement, and Harvest Studies: Lemhi River (1965–1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Job Completion Report, prepared by [[Terry Holubetz]] for the [[Idaho Fish and Game Department]] (Federal Aid to Fish Restoration, Projects F 49-R-4 and F 49-R-5, Job 4), covers March 1, 1965 to December 31, 1966. The study examined juvenile production, adult enumeration, harvest, and spawning escapement of [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]] in the upper [[Lemhi River]]. Its main tools were the [[Lemhi Weir]] with its [[Louver Trap]] and the [[L-5 Screen Trap]], complete weir counts of adult salmon, and [[Spawning Ground Surveys]] using [[Redd Counts]].

The louver trap gave a reliable index of juvenile emigration, while the L-5 screen trap did not. Complete adult chinook counts were 765 in 1965 and 1,473 in 1966. Steelhead returns were not counted at the weir in either year. The report also finds a positive correlation between juvenile chinook production and spawning escapement.

## Background & Description

The Lemhi River is a tributary system of the Salmon River basin in Idaho. Study reaches ran from the mouth of [[Hayden Creek]] upstream to Leadore. Two stream-side sites were central: the Lemhi weir, which served as a migration barrier and trap for both upstream adults and downstream juveniles, and the L-5 screen trap at a irrigation diversion.

Steelhead were being reintroduced to the system. Eyed eggs were planted in [[Big Springs Creek]], a tributary of the upper Lemhi, in 1962, and the eggs came from 3-year-old ocean fish. The Big Springs Creek experimental facility was part of the monitoring rationale.

The adult trap operated as a migration barrier. It was installed on March 8 and removed on December 14 in 1965, and installed on March 1 and removed on December 9 in 1966. It was run daily except during the coldest period, when floating ice stopped upstream migration.

Methods included:
- **Juvenile enumeration:** louver-captured proportions expanded to total emigrants ([[Proportion-Expansion Estimate]]), validated by [[Mark-and-Recapture]] studies with thermal marks on smolts.
- **Adult enumeration:** total weir counts and random samples for sex and fork length.
- **Harvest:** creel-style check stations at the Lemhi weir and Leadore, sampled on randomly chosen days.
- **Escapement:** weir count minus upper-river harvest and fish taken for artificial spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Flow and water temperature shaped migration and sampling in this system. In spring 1966, low, clear water in the Columbia, Snake, and Salmon rivers brought the chinook run to the Lemhi mouth early in May. Heavy irrigation demand dewatered the lower Lemhi for the entire month of May and part of June, blocking passage. Periodic June rainstorms restored enough flow for salmon to pass dewatered sections. In contrast, 1965 had high flows and turbid water, which delayed the first movement peak.

Irrigation practices also affected the L-5 screen trap. Ranchers repeatedly changed flow patterns and physical conditions at the diversion, which altered trap efficiency and made its catches unreliable as an abundance index.

Adult chinook movement was keyed to spawning timing. The second upstream peak occurred in the third week of August in 1964, 1965, and 1966, regardless of year-to-year differences in physical conditions. Males also drifted downstream after spawning. About 90 percent of the salmon found dead or dying on the rack at the weir were males, which helps explain the sex-ratio mismatch between the weir and the spawning grounds.

## Key Findings

**Juvenile production and emigration**
- The louver trap provided a reliable index of juvenile salmon and steelhead emigrating from the upper Lemhi River. Mark-recapture work in 1965 showed the louver-captured proportion of total emigrants remained at a uniform level.
- Estimated total chinook smolt emigrants passing the Lemhi weir were **325,020 in 1965** and **289,580 in 1966** (Tables 1 and 2). Peak monthly emigration in 1965 was October (about 86,380) and in 1966 it was March (about 67,640).
- Chinook fry emigration began at emergence in early spring. A total fry estimate could not be computed because fry marking failed: bath staining was not retained, and fluorescent granules appeared too small to penetrate the mucous layer. Catch-per-unit-effort data were used to show migration timing and trends.
- Juvenile steelhead production was reported with Job 3 (F 49-R-5) and in a separate louver-efficiency M.S. thesis (Holubetz, 1967).

**Adult chinook salmon**
- Complete weir counts were **765 (1965)** and **1,473 (1966)**.
- Each run showed two movement peaks. The first peak varied with flow and turbidity: June 9, 1966 (175 fish) and July 16, 1965 (69 fish). The second peak fell in the third week of August.
- Sex composition in random weir samples was 54% female in 1965 (n = 188) and 46% female in 1966 (n = 309).
- Length-frequency data from the weir and from spawning-ground surveys showed no significant difference.

**Harvest and escapement**
- The chinook sport fishery in the Lemhi River was closed in 1965, so no harvest was estimated for that year. Indian fishers checked in 1965 were unsuccessful in spearing salmon.
- The 1966 season ran from June 8 to July 15 (38 days). The estimated 1966 harvest was **136 fish**.
- Escapement was **765 in 1965**, equal to the weir count, and **1,262 in 1966**. In 1966, 75 salmon were artificially spawned at the Lemhi holding pond.

**Spawning ground surveys**
- Aerial and ground redd counts were below the 1960–1964 five-year average in both study years (Table 7).

- Carcass sex ratios were 62% female in 1965 and 65% female in 1966 (Table 8).

**Correlations and comparisons**
- A **positive correlation** exists between juvenile chinook production and adult spawning escapement, as measured by redd counts.
- Redd counts followed the same trend as estimated escapement in 1965–1966.
- The female-to-male ratio on the spawning grounds differed from the weir ratio, with fewer males on the grounds, probably because males drift downstream after spawning.

**Steelhead**
- No adult steelhead were counted through the Lemhi weir upstream trap in 1965 or 1966.
- A spent male steelhead of about 28 inches, bearing a left ventral-adipose fin clip applied at the Big Springs Creek weir, was caught in the L-5 screen trap in early June 1966.
- Because the 1962 eggs came from 3-year ocean fish, adult returns to the Lemhi were not expected in numbers until spring 1967.

**Recommendations**
- Continue operating the downstream emigrant facility and spawning-escapement enumeration to evaluate steelhead reintroduction, chinook run status in the Lemhi, and the Big Springs Creek experimental facility.
