# Quatrefoil Trap: A New Larval Fish Light Trap

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fishes

## Executive Summary

This methods paper describes the **Quatrefoil Trap**, a battery-powered [[Light Trap]] designed to collect [[Larval Fish]]. The design was built to seven criteria set out by [[Kindschi et al. 1979]], including easy entry and difficult escape, a large capture area, and economical construction. The trap uses a spirally scribed [[Plexiglas]] light-distribution rod, four entrance slots, and a removable collection pan. In a habitat-partitioning study of a small Kentucky stream (Floyd 1983), the trap captured most of the 6,057 larval fish collected, outperforming [[Push Seine]] and [[Ichthyoplankton Drift Net]] sampling in numbers of individuals and species.

## Background & Description

Light trapping is a widely used method for sampling larval fish. Many species of larval fish, along with other organisms, are attracted to light, although the mechanisms are poorly understood (see [[Faber 1981]] for a literature review and [[Verheijen 1958]] for extended treatment). [[Kindschi et al. 1979]] showed that light trapping suits field conditions in the authors' region and proposed design criteria for a larval fish trap:

- a) easy entry and difficult escape for larvae
- b) large capture area relative to total trap size
- c) broadly distributed light source to lead larvae into the trap
- d) sufficiently rugged, with minimal maintenance
- e) reliable, serviceable electronics
- f) easy to transport and use
- g) economically feasible

The trap body is made from [[Plexiglas]] tube (7.62 cm O.D., 0.32 cm wall) and sheet (0.64 cm thick). It must be fabricated in a machine shop. Key specifications:

- **Weight:** 1.8 kg (4 lb), including the battery pack
- **Height:** 40.4 cm (15.9 in) without the lamp assembly; 44.8 cm (17.6 in) with it
- **Top plate:** 19 cm (7.5 in) diameter
- **Bottom plate:** 24.3 cm (9.5 in) diameter
- **Collection pan:** stainless steel kitchenware bowl, 24 cm diameter × 8.6 cm deep, with a 7 cm central opening covered by 120 µm polyester mesh (#12 bolting cloth)
- **Pan attachment:** four stainless steel snap clips that allow quick specimen removal

Most dimensions are not critical, but the bottom plate must match the margin of the collection pan.

**Light distribution:** A plexiglas rod (1.27 cm diameter, 30.5 cm long) carries a 0.16 cm wide spiral groove, scribed around its lower 27.3 cm. It hangs from the center of the top plate and spreads light evenly around the trap.

**Lamp assembly:** The trap uses a No. 1490 miniature bayonet-base bulb (3.2 V, 0.16 A, 0.2 candlepower, rated life 3000 hours). The bulb sits in a machined 2024-T4 aluminum housing sealed with [[Silicone Rubber Sealant]], making the lamp waterproof and submersible. Under normal water quality, this bulb lit an average radius of 1.5 m (4.9 ft). Bulbs up to 3.5 cp can be substituted, but these would require more frequent battery changes or a heavier pack.

**Power:** A plastic battery holder with two "D" cells is sealed in a freezer bag. A hanging loop keeps the pack above the waterline, so the trap can rest on the bottom or be suspended in the water column.

**Construction:** Tube ends are squared and relieved with a 1 cm gap to control strain, since commercial tubing springs out of round when cut. Tubes are milled to three-quarters of a true circle, and the entry width W is set at W = D/2. The four tubular segments are arranged at the corners of a square, which gives the trap its name. Joints are cemented with a solution of plexiglas scrap in 1,1,2-trichloroethane. The entry slot is 0.15 cm wide, chosen from larval fish measurements to admit most larvae while excluding larger predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trap was evaluated in a habitat-partitioning study of larval fishes in a small stream in Kentucky (Floyd 1983). The light trap was used alongside [[Push Seine]] and [[Ichthyoplankton Drift Net]] sampling. The push seine sampled microhabitat zones in low-flow to no-flow areas just before the traps were set, while drift nets sampled current areas. Because the trap relies on attraction to light, it is best suited to sampling the littoral and limnetic zones of lakes and the quiet margins of streams. Its operation depends on water quality, which affects how far light carries.

## Key Findings

- **Design validation:** The trap was reported as reliable and effective in the Kentucky stream study, meeting the design criteria of [[Kindschi et al. 1979]].
- **Total catch:** 6,057 larval fish representing 29 species were captured across all methods. The species count was read from a scanned page and should be checked against the original.
- **Light trap:** 25 species totaling 4,980 individuals were taken in the light traps, making it the most productive method in the study.
- **Push seine:** 20 species totaling 994 individuals were collected, sampled immediately before the light traps were set.
- **Drift nets:** Only a small number of species (count unclear in the scan) represented by 83 specimens were taken.
- **Cost:** Equipment for eight units totaled approximately $205 (1982 costs; the scanned figure reads "g20b", and 8 × $25.60 = $204.80). The average cost was $25.60 per unit.
- **Labor:** 70 hours were spent on planning and tooling, and 39 hours on construction and installation. Labor costs were not assigned a dollar value because shop rates differ between institutions.
- **Adaptability:** The design can be modified with dimensionally similar bulbs or battery configurations to suit different field conditions.
