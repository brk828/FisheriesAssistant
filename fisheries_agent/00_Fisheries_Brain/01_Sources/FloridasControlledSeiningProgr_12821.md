# Florida's Controlled Seining Program: Fish Management Bulletin No. 1 (1952)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 bulletin, written by [[John F. Dequine]], Chief Fisheries Biologist, and published by the [[Florida Game And Fresh Water Fish Commission]], presents the case for a three-year experimental [[Controlled Seining Program]] in six large Florida lakes. Sport-fishing pressure had grown sharply, yet restocking, bag and size limits, closed seasons and the 1946 ban on commercial sale of bream and crappie had not restored good fishing. The author argues that fish populations should be managed like a crop: cleared, fertilized where practical, cultivated, thinned and harvested. Surveys showed that rough fish and surplus panfish were competing with [[Largemouth Bass]] and that most panfish were never caught by anglers. Using [[Haul Seine]] operations under permit, with supervision, tagging and inspection fees, the program aimed to remove surplus fish and improve bass angling. A panel of four fishery scientists unanimously endorsed the findings.

## Background & Description

The bulletin addresses long-running controversy over managing Florida's fresh waters, especially the northern St. Johns River, including the 73.5 square-mile [[Lake George]], and [[Lake Okeechobee]], a shallow lake of more than 700 square miles. Both waters gained world-wide fame for black bass during 1920–1930. The Commission was created by a 1942 constitutional amendment giving a five-man body authority over freshwater fish.

Fishing license sales rose from 30,843 in 1940 to 275,751 in 1950, an increase of almost 900 percent (Figure 1). Earlier measures, including hatchery fingerling releases, closed seasons, smaller bag limits, size limits and commercial bans, failed to keep pace with demand. Effective October 1, 1946, commercial taking and sale of bream and black crappie in Lakes George and Okeechobee was banned, and all commercial gear except catfish trotlines was eliminated.

Surveys begun in 1947–1948 inventoried populations with commercial catfish seines, catalogued diets, growth and reproduction, and recorded harvest by sport and commercial methods. The bulletin's core rationale is an analogy with agriculture. Fish are described as a "living, growing crop" governed by the same principles as corn, cattle or timber: [[Carrying Capacity]], clearing, planting (stocking), fertilization, cultivation, thinning and harvesting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Carrying capacity and productivity.** Florida waters supported anywhere from 22 to over 1,000 pounds of fish per acre. Productivity depends largely on natural fertility. Newly impounded waters are highly productive for the first few years. Large lakes such as George, Apopka, Harris and Okeechobee are already rich, partly from annual inflows of nutrients from flooded vegetation.

**Fertilization.** For a one-acre pond, fertilizer at $12 to $15 per year could raise catch from roughly 15–20 to 100 or more pounds per acre per year. Costs on large lakes were judged prohibitive.

**Composition of a typical population.** The bulletin's example of a 100 lb/acre Florida population was 5 lb bass, 10 lb crappie, 25 lb bluegill and shellcracker, 12 lb garfish, 3 lb mudfish, 15 lb catfish, and 30 lb gizzard shad and suckers. Rough fish and other "weed" species were argued to compete with game fish for food and space, limiting bass production.

**Bluegill overcrowding and stunting.** [[Bluegill]] are highly prolific and spawn most of the year in Florida. In balanced populations, predators such as bass and crappie keep them in check. Where predation is insufficient, bluegill stunt, stop spawning, and compete with other species. The bulletin describes overcrowding as one of the most common causes of poor fishing in southeastern lakes, and notes that bass, crappie, [[Channel Catfish]], gizzard shad and golden shiners can also cause it.

**Balanced populations and the "expanding population" effect.** Fishing success is highest in newly flooded waters and declines as populations stabilize at carrying capacity. Heavy mortality, whether from seining, partial poisoning or storms, has been followed by better fishing. The 300,000-pound storm loss in [[Lake Apopka]] in 1950 was followed by excellent fishing in 1951 and 1952. The author calls this the [[Theory Of Expanding Fish Populations]].

**Harvest by anglers.** Tagging studies showed low angler harvest. Crappie and bream were especially underharvested, which the bulletin links to stunting and to waste of edible fish.

## Key Findings

**Survey conclusions (1947–1950 studies, Lakes George and Okeechobee):**
- Fluctuations in fish abundance could not be attributed to commercial fishing.
- Bass, bream and crappie lived only a few years.
- Most bass, bream, crappie and catfish were not caught by anglers. Surplus bream, crappie and catfish represented a potential of at least six million pounds of edible fish annually.
- Competition from less-desirable species limited bass production.
- Commercial catch records for the ten years before the 1946 ban showed no decline.
- Controlled seining of surplus bream, crappie and catfish would not harm sport fishing and would likely improve it, especially for bass.
- Rising sport-fishing activity makes major management practices essential.

**Hollingsworth case study ([[Lake Hollingsworth]], 366 acres, Lakeland):**
- Late 1949: adults were 0.3% game and panfish and 99.7% rough fish (98.0% shad). Sport fishing was nonexistent, and 46.3 lb/acre of rough fish were removed.
- June 1950: game fish had risen to 3.4%, and 119 lb/acre of rough fish were removed.
- June 1951: game fish reached 12.2%, and another 188 lb/acre of rough fish were removed. Bass were too few to re-establish, so about 20 hatchery fingerlings per acre were stocked.

**Tagging and angler returns:**
- Bass: 370 of 1,616 tagged in Lake Apopka and five other Lake County lakes were reported (22.9%). Lake Okeechobee returned 107 of 1,444 (7.4%). Norris Lake, Tennessee, returned 18.4% over ten years.
- Crappie: none of more than 100 tagged in Florida was returned. Wheeler Reservoir, Alabama, reported 88 of 4,748 (given as 17%). Guntersville Reservoir returned 42 of 5,440 (0.8%). Illinois studies estimated about 2% of crappie were taken by anglers.
- Bream: 13 of 296 bluegill, shellcracker and redbreast tagged in the Dead Lakes (Gulf County) were reported (4.4%).

**Expert panel (memorandum dated May 7, 1951):** [[R. W. Eschmeyer]], [[O. Lloyd Meehean]], [[F. G. Walton Smith]] and [[H. S. Swingle]] unanimously endorsed the conclusions. They stated that controlled commercial fishing is "the principal tool, and in many areas the only tool," for improving sport fishing in mixed waters. They also stressed that public education is needed to gain support for regulated commercial fishing.

**Program authorization (February 4, 1952; amended June 2, 1952):** The three-year experiment covers six lakes:
- [[Lake Okeechobee]], [[Lake George]] and Lake Crescent (Lakes George and Crescent: July 1 to March 1 season).
- Lake Harris (about 18,000 acres) and Lake Eustis (about 7,400 acres), chosen at the request of resort owners.
- Lake Reedy (about 2,400 acres, Polk County), chosen at the request of a sportsmen's association.

Requests for Orange and Lochloosa lakes and others were denied for lack of trained staff.

**Operating rules:**
- Seines of 1,000 to 1,600 yards take all species except black bass, pickerel and pike. Bass and pickerel are counted, some are tagged and released, and spawning areas and popular fishing spots are avoided.
- Permits go only to Florida residents of two or more years who have the necessary skill and equipment. Seining is allowed Monday through Friday only.
- A Commission technician or wildlife officer accompanies each seine crew.
- Inspection fees per pound: one-half cent for rough fish, two cents for dressed catfish (one cent for rough catfish), and four cents for bream, crappie or related species.
- Bream and crappie sold must bear a destructible metal seal. Shipping containers carry official permit tags void 72 hours after issue.
- Anglers may keep 25 bream and crappie per day (50 for two or more days) statewide. Excess catch above bag limits must be checked at a station.

**Financial and policy outcomes:**
- Supervision and administration were estimated at nearly $250,000 in the first year, with fee revenue expected to be about the same.
- Part of the research budget was to come from the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]].
- The author expected legal supply to reduce illegal ("bootlegging") sales. Reported South Georgia prices of about 38 cents per pound in March 1952 were expected to fall toward 25 cents or less.

**Research plan:** Studies of age and growth, feeding, spawning, migration, parasites and population interactions, catch-per-effort analysis of sport catches, and harvest-rate tests on mature fish of all species.
