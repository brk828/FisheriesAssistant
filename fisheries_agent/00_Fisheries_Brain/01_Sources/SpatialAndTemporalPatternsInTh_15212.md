# Spatial and Temporal Patterns in the Fish Assemblages of Individual Pools in a Midwestern Stream (U.S.A.)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 study in [[Brier Creek]], a low-gradient prairie-margin stream in Oklahoma, quantified fish assemblages in 14 adjacent pools (6–120 m long) over eight [[Snorkeling Survey]] censuses spanning 19 months. The authors tested whether individual pools are biologically discrete units suitable for stream fish research. The whole 1 km reach was highly consistent despite two major floods, individual pools were generally consistent but more variable than the reach as a whole, and the authors conclude that pools separated by shallow riffles are an appropriate scale for biologically meaningful study.

## Background & Description

The study sits within a long-running research program on [[Stream Fish Assemblage Stability]]. Earlier work had examined stability at the entire-stream scale, at 100–300 m reaches, and at mesohabitat or single-pool scales. The authors argue that spatial scale should be chosen according to the spacing of biologically meaningful units rather than fixed linear distances.

Brier Creek has a total mainstream length of 24 km and a known fauna of 30 species. Headwaters drain pasture and often cease to flow, while the midreach is bordered by gallery forest and maintained by subsurface springs (base flow 0.1–0.2 m³/s). The study reach contained 14 pools of 6–120 m length and 45–160 cm depth, separated by shallow riffles 1–20 m long and less than 10 cm deep at base flow. Pools were 2–10 m wide, with silt, sand, sandstone bedrock and fractured limestone substrates. Canopy cover ranged from nearly complete to about 75% open. Water was clear except after floods, and seasonal algae (e.g., *Rhizoclonium* and *Spirogyra*) provided cover for small fishes. Grazing by [[Central Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*) regulates algal distribution in these pools.

Fish were enumerated by [[Snorkeling Survey]] on eight dates between November 1982 and May 1984. Two major floods occurred in April and June 1983; the June flood crested 3.45 m above base flow. Bullhead catfishes and orangethroat darters were excluded from analyses because their cryptic habitat use made snorkel census unreliable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame pools as potentially important natural units for biotic interactions, especially predator–prey relationships. Shallow riffles act as semi-permeable barriers: small cyprinids cross them readily, whereas adult [[Largemouth Bass]] cross infrequently and with difficulty. Crossing ability therefore varies with body size and stage level.

Predator–prey dynamics were central. Predators were defined as *Micropterus* >100 mm and *Lepomis* >75 mm; prey included small bass and sunfish, and minnows and topminnows except bigeye shiners. Experimental largemouth bass introductions in earlier work (Power et al. 1985; Harvey 1991) displaced small fishes, and the authors cite a [[Bigger-Deeper Hypothesis]] and the [[Ideal Free Distribution]] model as possible explanations for distribution patterns.

## Key Findings

- **Study scale and dominant fauna:** 19,437 fish observations were recorded across 23 "ecotaxa" (including size classes). The most numerous were central stonerollers (33.8%), bigeye shiners (14.5%), green sunfish <75 mm (12.1%), green sunfish >75 mm (10.9%), blackspotted topminnows (8.2%), and longear sunfish >75 mm (8.1%).
- **Reach-level consistency:** Summed across all pools, the reach assemblage changed little between surveys (Morisita's indices 0.83–0.98) despite both floods.
- **Pool-level variability:** Morisita's index for successive pool surveys ranged from 0.07 to 1.00. It exceeded 0.80 in 47.4% of cases, with a median of 0.78 (95 comparisons). Pool-level variation was greater than reach-level variation.
- **Flood effects on pools:** Mean pool similarity did not differ significantly between flood and non-flood intervals (one-way ANOVA, p = 0.24). No significant difference among pools in mean similarity was found (p = 0.27).
- **Taxon consistency:** Bass <100 mm (mean 0.892), longear sunfish >75 mm (0.876) and green sunfish >75 mm (0.778) were most consistent among pools. Blackstriped topminnows (0.652), central stonerollers (0.608) and bigeye shiners (0.579) were least consistent, and smaller fishes varied more across flood intervals.
- **Species associations:** 25 of 91 pairwise correlations among the 14 most abundant taxa were significant at p < 0.01 (18 positive, 7 negative). UPGMA clustering split large bass and bluegills from small green sunfish, stonerollers, bullhead minnows and shiners.
- **Predator–prey structure:** Predators and prey were not strictly segregated. Pools ranged from predator-rich to prey-rich, with many intermediate pools. Large bass constrained prey numbers, whereas sunfish >75 mm did not preclude prey use. Blackstriped topminnows occupied bass-containing pools.
- **Flood disruption:** Before floods, pools with many predators had few prey; after floods, some predator-rich pools held many prey, suggesting floods temporarily disrupt segregation.
- **Pool stability and size:** Mean pool similarity was not significantly related to maximum depth (p = 0.34) or pool volume (p = 0.08), contrary to a prediction by Schlosser (1987).
- **Conclusion:** Individual pools remained within discrete subsets of multivariate space across time, supporting their use as discrete habitat units and as a baseline for experimental or monitoring studies.
