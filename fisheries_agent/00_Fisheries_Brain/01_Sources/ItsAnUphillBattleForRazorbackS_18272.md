# Razorback Sucker Recovery and the Endangered Species Listing Dilemma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This Associated Press article from Kingman, Arizona, reports that the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*) is in serious decline across the American Southwest. Conservationists working to restore the species are reluctant to have it federally listed as endangered, because they fear listing would hinder efforts to reintroduce it to rivers. The article uses the [[Snail Darter]] and the [[Tellico Dam]] controversy to show how endangered status can create political conflict. It also describes a 1980s-era rescue effort in which biologists collected brood stock from [[Lake Mohave]] to prop up a dwindling population.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is reddish with a yellow underside, an elongated snout, and a cylindrical body. It grows up to 3 feet long and 14 pounds. The fish was common in Arizona and Southern California irrigation canals fed by the [[Colorado River]] until the 1940s. Wagonloads were collected from Phoenix canals, and a commercial fishery operated at [[Saguaro Lake]] until 1949.

Officials have sought to return the species to waters where it once thrived, including the [[Salt River]], the [[Gila River]], and the [[Verde River]] in Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Predation is considered the chief cause of the species' decline. Non-native [[Largemouth Bass]] and catfish were introduced into the Colorado River and are believed to have preyed heavily on razorbacks.

Razorbacks were not found when one lake was drained in 1966. According to [[W.L. Minckley]], an Arizona State University zoologist, the species has been reduced to a scattered few in southwestern waters.

The only known concentration appears to be in Lake Mohave, a man-made reservoir created between 1950 and 1952. However, [[W.L. Minckley]] reported that no razorback offspring had been found there recently, indicating a population that is aging without replacement.

Health problems were also documented. Per statistics compiled by Minckley, more than 30 percent of razorbacks in Lake Mohave are blind in at least one eye, and 4 percent have external tumors that signal old age. [[Jerry Burton]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] compared the population to "a bunch of 80-year-old people."

## Key Findings

- **Population decline:** The razorback sucker is dying out, with its range reduced to a few scattered individuals and one known concentration in Lake Mohave.
- **Likely cause:** Predation by introduced largemouth bass and catfish is believed to be the chief reason for the decline.
- **Lack of recruitment:** No recent razorback offspring have been found in Lake Mohave.
- **Population health:** More than 30 percent of Lake Mohave razorbacks are blind in at least one eye, and 4 percent show external tumors, per Minckley's statistics.
- **Listing dilemma:** Conservationists fear that federal endangered-species listing would create political obstacles to reintroduction. Burton stated that the razorback was "the best candidate for listing that we've ever had," but that listing would cause "a fight on our hands every time we tried to introduce them to new areas."
- **Snail darter precedent:** Environmentalists used the snail darter's endangered status to block the $136 million [[Tellico Dam]] for six years, arguing that the [[Little Tennessee River]] was the fish's last habitat. Congress and President [[Jimmy Carter]] passed legislation in 1979 exempting the snail darter from the act, and the dam began operating in November 1979. The snail darter was subsequently found in other eastern Tennessee streams.
- **Rescue action:** Faced with the possibility that razorbacks could disappear within five years, federal and state biologists, working with Arizona State University researchers, netted 136 razorbacks from Lake Mohave in January. They sent the fish as [[Brood Stock]] to a national fish hatchery in Dexter, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The article is truncated at this point, so the outcome of the hatchery effort is not reported.
