# Can the Ruffe Be Contained Before It Becomes Your Problem?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 *Fisheries* feature by [[Thomas R. Busiahn]], chair of the [[Ruffe Control Committee]], argues that the invasive Eurasian [[Ruffe]] (*Gymnocephalus cernuus*) can still be contained if managers act quickly. The ruffe was introduced to [[Duluth Harbor]] on [[Lake Superior]] via ballast water and has spread to estuaries and Thunder Bay. The article outlines a five-part containment program authorized under the [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] of 1990, centered on near-term [[Piscicide]] use, while acknowledging significant disagreement and a shortage of control technology.

## Background & Description

- **Origin and spread:** The ruffe is a Eurasian percid, abundant within a decade of introduction in Loch Lomond, Scotland, where endemic species declined sharply (Maitland and East 1989).
- **Discovery in North America:** Introduced to Duluth Harbor via ballast water of an ocean-going freighter; first collected in fish surveys in 1986 (Pratt et al. 1992).
- **Status by 1991:** The most abundant fish in the 5,200-ha harbor; had spread 88 km east to several south-shore estuaries and was collected in Thunder Bay, Ontario, 290 km northeast, likely carried in cargo ferry ballast tanks.
- **Reproduction:** In favorable conditions, ruffe mature in one year and produce up to three batches of ova each spring.
- **Morphology and behavior:** A bottom dweller that avoids light and detects predators and prey with a highly sensitive [[Lateral Line System]]. Total length is 100–150 mm, giving it no commercial or recreational value.
- **Temperature tolerance:** Similar to [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), suggesting a potential range from the Great Plains to the eastern seaboard and north into Canada.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Susceptible areas:** Lake Erie, Lake St. Clair, Saginaw Bay, and Green Bay, all supporting major fisheries for [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) and yellow perch. Colonizing southern Lake Michigan could provide access to the Mississippi River basin via the Chicago River.
- **Limiting habitat:** The oligotrophic water of Lake Superior is unfavorable for ruffe and has slowed its range expansion.
- **Management conflict:** Chemical treatments could affect Lake Superior and its estuaries, while ruffe control would mainly benefit coolwater fisheries of the lower Great Lakes, the Mississippi basin, and the Canadian Shield (the N.I.M.B.Y. problem).
- **Limits of IPM:** [[Integrated Pest Management]] is poorly developed for open aquatic systems. Economic injury analysis is ill-suited to common-property resources and non-economic values such as biodiversity.
- **Broader context:** The author notes that the invasion is ultimately a symptom of ecosystem health problems, and that ecosystems with integrity and biodiversity may resist invasion.

## Key Findings

1. **Five-part program:**
   - Educational and regulatory actions to prevent [[Bait Bucket]] transport.
   - A [[Ballast Water Management]] plan, developed with the maritime industry, to keep Great Lakes ships from carrying ruffe out of western Lake Superior.
   - Elimination of reproducing ruffe populations on the periphery of the range using piscicides.
   - Research on established populations and surveillance for new colonies.
   - Evaluation of past [[Biological Control]] attempts by Minnesota and Wisconsin, which enhanced predator fish populations.
2. **Goal:** Prevent or delay spread by containing ruffe to its current range in western Lake Superior.
3. **Registered piscicides:** [[Rotenone]], [[Antimycin]], [[TFM]], and [[Bayluscide]]. Ruffe are more susceptible to all of these than most endemic fishes. Choice depends on selectivity, cost, registration, availability, and technical factors.
4. **Field evidence:** A [[Sea Lamprey]] lampricide (TFM) treatment in the Brule River, Wisconsin, killed more than 97% of the ruffe population with no significant mortality among endemic fishes (Bills et al. 1992). Under certain water chemistry conditions TFM selectively kills ruffe.
5. **Long-term option:** A bottom-release antimycin formulation may best eradicate ruffe colonies in lentic habitat with minimal nontarget impact.
6. **Conditions for termination:** The program continues until ruffe spread beyond western Lake Superior or new evidence shows they pose no threat. If ruffe escape, the program should be reexamined and ended if control is infeasible.
7. **Central message:** The committee sees a "window of opportunity" that closes if action is delayed.
