# Sacramento Perch (*Archoplites interruptus*) Life History in Clear Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 *California Fish and Game* paper by Garth I. Murphy of the California Division of Fish and Game reports field observations on the [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*) in [[Clear Lake]], Lake County, California. The study covers spawning behavior, growth, food habits, and the species' decline. The author describes the species as the only native member of the sunfish family ([[Centrarchidae]]) west of the Rockies. Its numbers were greatly reduced by introduced predators, which the author links mainly to predation on its unguarded eggs. The paper closes with recommendations for further study, protection in limited areas, and continued pond culture experiments.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Archoplites interruptus* is the only native member of the family [[Centrarchidae]] west of the Rockies. The author describes it as a survivor of an ancient fauna that probably predates the Rocky Mountain barrier (Miller 1946).
- **Size:** Jordan and Evermann (1896-1900) reported lengths of 12 to 24 inches.
- **Distribution:** Restricted to the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage]] and the smaller neighboring Pajaro River system. Before the introduction of alien species it was "exceedingly abundant" in lowland areas. By 1948 it was scarce except in isolated localities, including [[Clear Lake]].
- **Study site:** [[Clear Lake]] is a large lowland lake of 40,000 surface acres in the Upper Sonoran Life Zone of central Northern California. It drains through Cache Creek into the Sacramento River via the [[Yolo By-Pass]].
- **Fishery role:** In 1947 the Sacramento Perch made up 1 percent of the total sport catch at Clear Lake. Formerly, trout and this species were the only game fish in the lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding (inferred, not directly studied):**
- Young of the year associate with [[Bluegill]] in the littoral zone, probably feeding on larger zooplankton and insects.
- Adults are taken on spinners and live minnows but almost never on worms, which suggests they become at least partly piscivorous.
- [[Lindquist Et Al 1943]] found perch had fed on all stages of the [[Phantom Midge]] (*Chaoborus astictopus*), which is distributed across most of the offshore area of Clear Lake.
- Young of native cyprinids such as the [[Sacramento Hitch]] (*Lavinia exilicauda*) move offshore in midsummer and supply forage fish to the perch.

**Seasonal presence:** In 1947 the catch was made only in April, May, and June, roughly matching the prespawning and spawning period. The author proposes that the fish range the whole lake after spawning. Because numbers are small, offshore anglers rarely capture them.

**Competing and predatory introductions:** Black bass, crappie, bluegill, carp, and catfish were introduced into California. Neale (1931) found that bluegill, carp, and catfish (*Ameiurus nebulosus* or *Ictalurus catus*) eat the eggs of spawning fish. See [[Alien Species Introduction]].

**Thurston Lake comparison:** In [[Thurston Lake]], near Clear Lake, Sacramento Perch coexisted with [[Brown Bullhead]] and [[Largemouth Black Bass]]. After [[White Catfish]] were introduced in 1933, the perch disappeared completely.

## Key Findings

### Spawning Habits (Clear Lake, June 1947)

- A spawning school was observed on June 15, 1947, off Austins Pier near Lower Lake. No spawning fish were present on June 14 or June 16. Spawning began about 9 a.m. on June 15.
- About 50 adult fish were spawning in an area 12 feet long and 4 feet wide, next to a rock breakwater.
- Water depth was 1 to 2 feet and temperature was 75.5°F.
- The substrate was boulders 3 inches to 1 foot in diameter, covered with heavy algae, with scattered *Potamogeton* (pondweed).
- Fish were paired, with some exchange of mates. Each pair spawned in a definite area about 1.5 feet in diameter. No nest-building was observed.
- Eggs were adhesive and attached directly to algae, with fewer on *Potamogeton* and bare rock.
- Females were the first to leave after spawning. Males guarded the deposition area for up to 30 minutes.
- Eggs were abundant on June 15 and nearly absent on June 16, when the few found were well developed with beating hearts. The author attributes the loss to [[Bluegill]] one to two inches long, which were abundant at the site.
- A 10.5-inch female snagged off the spawning bed held an estimated 84,000 unspawned mature eggs.
- Spawning schools persisted during the spawning act itself, which the author describes as unusual among centrarchids.

### Reproductive Timing and Environment

- The first perch of the year were taken on June 9, 1947, at an average length of 0.8 inch, which indicates spawning began in May.
- By June 24 most adults had spawned, based on gonad condition in net samples.
- From May 19 to June 26, water temperature at 2 feet depth ranged from a maximum of 84°F to a minimum of 62°F (maximum-minimum thermometer).
- Adults in Clear Lake spawn after their first year, based on the samples collected.

### Growth

- Young of the year leave the immediate shoreline when they reach about 2 inches.
- Seine hauls on July 7 and August 7 took specimens 1.9 and 2.0 inches long near shore.
- The largest young-of-the-year taken in 1947 was 3.5 inches, caught October 11 in a fyke net set 120 yards offshore.
- The growth-rate graph is based on 59 scale samples from 1946 and 1947. Scale reading was difficult because the species forms "false checks" readily, but the first annulus was consistently identifiable.
- A female 20.0 cm (8 inches) long, collected April 27, 1947, was assigned to Age Class 2.
- A length-weight relationship curve was fitted by eye for Clear Lake, 1946-1947.

### Comparison with Centrarchid Reproductive Traits (Breder 1936)

Breder's review covered 25 species, with data for 20 showing largely uniform breeding habits. Of eight generalized family attributes, only three were observed in *Archoplites*:
- Differential sex behavior (sex recognition)
- Pair-based spawning position with ventral surfaces in contact
- Moderately adhesive eggs

Territoriality is present during spawning only. The author concludes that the reproductive pattern is primitive:
- Nest building is absent despite available sites.
- Parental care is absent or nearly absent.
- Schooling is retained through spawning.

### Curtis Pond Experiment (Curtis 1946)

- On March 19, 1946, 16 adult Sacramento Perch and 30 adult [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) were placed in a one-third acre pond at Elk Grove, California.
- When the pond was drained on August 9, 23,000 young Sacramento Perch and 1,500 young Green Sunfish were recovered.
- The author attributes the perch success to greater fecundity and earlier completion of spawning, before green sunfish young were large enough to eat eggs.
- Similar experiments with largemouth black bass, bluegill, and brown bullhead resulted in a predominance of Sacramento Perch.

### Summary and Recommendations

- **Cause of decline:** The author concludes that the reduction in numbers most likely resulted from the introduction of species that prey on the unguarded eggs of this fish.
- **Management:** The species is "gravely threatened" by alien introductions and is both a zoological rarity and a worthwhile game fish.
- **Recommended actions:**
  - Further studies to explain its decline more clearly.
  - Steps to preserve the species in limited areas.
  - Continued California Division of Fish and Game experiments on its value for pond fish culture, which had already begun.
