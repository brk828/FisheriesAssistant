# Control of Radioactive Pollution of the Animas River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study, published in the *Journal of the Water Pollution Control Federation*, examines interstate pollution of the [[Animas River]] (Colorado–New Mexico) by a [[Uranium Ore Refinery]] at Durango, Colorado. The refinery discharged chemical toxicants and [[Radium]]-bearing solids that raised dissolved radium downstream, interfered with domestic, irrigation, stock, and recreational uses, and reduced bottom fauna. The authors combine a 1958 field survey, bioassays, hydrology, and population exposure estimates to show that the main hazard lay in the total daily intake of bone-seeking radionuclides, [[Radium]] and [[Strontium-90]], from all pathways. Enforcement under the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act]] and [[Public Law 660]] led to a voluntary agreement requiring the company to remove settleable solids, cut soluble radium by at least 70 per cent, and eliminate toxic discharges by October 24, 1959.

## Background & Description

**Uranium milling.** The study outlines the uranium refining process. Uranium is extracted from local ore, concentrated, and sent for fuel fabrication. Its natural daughters, including thorium, polonium, radium, and bismuth, are rejected as process wastes. Wastes go to [[Tailings Pond]]s, where solids settle and liquids evaporate or seep, with excess liquid often discharged directly to a nearby stream. At the time, 23 operating uranium mills in the western states processed about 21,000 tons of ore per day containing about 0.28 per cent U₃O₈. The Durango mill processed slightly more than 500 tons per day (514 tons/day in the August 1958 in-plant survey, with plans for 700–750 tons/day).

**Process chemicals.** The mill used [[Sulfuric Acid]], salt, and [[Soda Ash]] (about 65,000, 48,000, and 64,000 lb/day respectively), plus solvent extraction reagents including tributylphosphate and kerosene. The main effluent to the Animas averaged 270 gpm, with 35,400 mg/l dissolved and 5,600 mg/l suspended solids. The tailings pond effluent to Lightner Creek averaged about 70 gpm.

**Study area and water uses.** The Animas rises near Silverton, Colorado, flows about 50 miles south to Durango, and joins the [[San Juan River]] at Farmington, New Mexico. The primary survey covered about 60 river miles. Water uses include:
- Domestic supply: Aztec (about 6,000 people) and Farmington (about 22,000) draw treated Animas water. Durango (11,500) uses the upper Florida River. About 100 people in Colorado and an estimated 1,845 in New Mexico use raw Animas water, and 2,310 use the San Juan.
- Irrigation: about 26,000 acres above Farmington and 36,000 acres between Farmington and Shiprock.
- Recreation (swimming and fishing), stock watering, and industrial process water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Minimum 30-day average flow at Durango is 155 cfs once every five years, and the minimum daily flow of record is 110 cfs. Minimum flows at Durango occur mainly in winter, while at Farmington they occur in late summer because of irrigation diversion (a recent minimum daily average of only 2.4 cfs). Rainfall in the summer survey was 3.42 in. at Durango and 2.70 in. at Farmington.

**Radium dynamics.** Radium-bearing solids from the mill settled in the river bed. Laboratory tests showed that 0.1 to 1.0 per cent of mud radium dissolves on brief mixing, so bed muds act as a reservoir. Dissolved radium is expected to rise with flow velocity and scour, which is a counterintuitive effect on dilution.

**Bottom fauna and fish.** Aquatic insects, algae, and other bottom organisms are important fish food. Below the mill at Station 2, bottom fauna were nearly eliminated. Recovery began about 36 miles downstream, and the fauna approached normal only about 45 miles below Durango. Fish samples were collected once in summer 1958, and minnows were analyzed for gross radioactivity, paralleling algae and insects.

**Toxicity to fish.** Rainbow trout were the test organisms in bioassays performed at the [[Colorado Game and Fish Department]] hatchery in Durango. Median tolerance limits (TLm) at 72 hours were:
- Main effluent: 0.44 and 1.5 per cent
- Organic raffinate (most toxic): 0.09 to 0.21 per cent
- Vanadium filter tray wash: 1.0 to 4.2 per cent
- Tailings pond effluent: 1.3 to 3.7 per cent
- Primary tails (least toxic): 13 to 18 per cent

Applying an [[Application Factor]] of one-third to one-tenth gives the dilution needed. Two direct effluents require at least 441 cfs, and the authors estimate that 600 cfs at Durango is needed at times to protect aquatic life. Summer 1958 flows fell to about half that figure.

## Key Findings

**1. Source of pollution.** The uranium refinery was the principal source of interstate pollution. Dissolved radium rose from 0.2 pCi/l above the mill to 4.5 pCi/l below it in 1950 (at 220 cfs). In 1955, it rose from 0.2 to 3.3 pCi/l at 250 cfs, a gain of about 20-fold.

**2. Radium in water and sediment.** The 1958 survey recorded a gain of about 11.0 mg/day of dissolved radium between Stations 1 and 2. Measured dissolved radium in the effluents was only 0.3 to 0.8 mg/day, but an estimated 30 mg/day of undissolved radium settled above Station 2 and became the main source of dissolved radium. Radium in bed muds at Station 2 averaged 171 pCi/g, compared with 1.7 pCi/g at the upstream control.

**3. Biota.** Algae at Station 2 had gross alpha activity of 3,910 pCi/g, about 100 times background, and radium of 390 pCi/g (versus 4.8 pCi/g at Station 1). Algae at the State Line (Station 5) remained about 30 times background alpha activity. Aquatic insects at Station 2 contained 71 pCi/g of radium. Algae near Station 4 reached 50 times background gross alpha activity.

**4. Water supply quality.** Treatment at Aztec and Farmington did not remove dissolved radium. Dissolved radium in raw water at Aztec and Farmington was 5 to 6 times natural background. At Station 5 the mean was 7.6 pCi/l, about 1.9 times the 4.0 pCi/l [[Maximum Permissible Concentration]]. Raw water at Aztec and Farmington approached the MPC during the 1958 survey. Strontium-90 in raw water averaged 1.6 pCi/l, well below the 80 pCi/l MPC.

**5. Human exposure.** The authors estimate total daily intake of bone-seeking radionuclides as a fraction of the allowable intake (8.8 pCi radium and 176 pCi Sr-90 per person per day, based on a 2.2 l/day water intake). Results from the 1958 survey:

| Group | Radium in water | Total (1958) |
|---|---|---|
| Raw river water near the State Line | 1.90 | 2.63 |
| Mill expansion to 700 tons/day | +0.69 | 3.32 |
| Raw river users, 1955–56-like water year | — | 4.57 |

Downstream irrigated crops averaged 3.6 pCi/kg of radium, compared with 2.0 pCi/kg for upstream farms. The authors caution that averages mask individual variation and that the exposure figures may be underestimates.

**6. Chemical quality.** Sulfate, chloride, and sodium increased below the mill (sulfate 113 mg/l at Station 1 vs. 151 at Station 2). Uranium loss was about 200–300 lb/day, which was not hazardous under the uranium MPC but represented a substantial financial loss to the company. Dissolved oxygen remained above 8.0 mg/l until Farmington, and sewage effects were minor.

**7. Remedial measures.** At the June 24, 1959, conference, parties agreed that the discharges had harmed public water supplies, ranch water supplies, irrigation, fishing, swimming, and stock watering in New Mexico, and had made conditions inimical to fish and aquatic life. The company agreed to:
- Install treatment to remove substantially all settleable solids
- Reduce soluble radium in the effluent by at least 70 per cent
- Eliminate toxic discharges harmful to fish and aquatic life

By the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare's account, the first federal enforcement action on radioactive waste was concluded voluntarily before residents ingested enough material to cause detectable health damage.

**8. Principle for radioactive waste control.** The authors argue that MPCs should be treated as maximums rather than targets, and that effluent discharges should be minimized. They also emphasize assessing total intake from all pathways rather than individual water concentrations.
