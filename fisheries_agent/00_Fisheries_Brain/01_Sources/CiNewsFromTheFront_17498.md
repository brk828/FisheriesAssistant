# Conservation International News: Amphibian Toxicity, Refugee Resettlement, Maya Forest, Madidi, and Andean Roads

#herpetology #conservation-biology #dendrobatidae

## Executive Summary

This is the November–December 1995 issue of *Cl News from the Front*, the bimonthly newsletter of [[Conservation International]] (CI). It is a news digest rather than a research report, and it contains no fish data. Its main items cover:

- The toxicity of Colombian [[Phyllobates Terribilis]] and its dependence on intact rain forest.
- The conflict between resettling more than 40,000 Guatemalan refugees and protecting land in [[Guatemala Protected Areas]].
- Transboundary cooperation on the [[Maya Forest]] across Mexico, Belize, and Guatemala.
- The declaration of [[Madidi National Park]] in Bolivia, supported by CI's [[Rapid Assessment Program]] (RAP) results.
- RAP training for Latin American field biologists.
- Community rejection of logging contracts in the [[Makira Conservation Area]], Solomon Islands.
- CI's work with the [[Andean Development Corporation]] (CAF) on road-building planning.
- An award for CI's documentary *Fanamby* on Madagascar.

## Background & Description

**Organisation and tools.** CI is a U.S.-based, nonprofit, international organisation whose stated mission is to conserve the earth's living natural heritage and to show that human societies can live harmoniously with nature. The newsletter introduces several program tools:

- [[Cisig]] (CI's Geographic Information System): a computer tool that integrates and displays biological, social, and economic data for conservation analysis.
- [[Conservation Priority-Setting Workshops]]: meetings of scientists who set regional and national priorities using CISIG mapping.
- [[Rapid Assessment Program]]: fast, first-cut assessments of little-known ecosystems, with recommendations for protection.
- [[Ecotourism]]: travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and sustains local well-being.
- [[Local Capacity-Building]]: cultivating in-country partners' skills in science, economics, communications, and other fields.

**The poison dart frog article.** The lead item describes the three poison dart frog species of Colombia's Choco region. *Phyllobates terribilis* is a bright yellow frog 2–3 inches long. The article states it is 20 times more toxic than any other amphibian. Indigenous Embera hunters use its skin toxins by rubbing blowgun darts on the frog's back. The frogs appear to obtain the insects that supply their toxins from the rain forest. In captivity or outside the forest they quickly lose their toxicity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Choco frogs and rain forest dependence.** The article presents the frogs' toxicity as "a prime example of the dependence of species on intact ecosystems."

**Guatemalan refugees and protected areas.** More than 40,000 Guatemalan refugees want to return. Some have waited more than 15 years, and others were born in Mexico. About 93 percent are farmers whose former plots have been taken over. The government and some international actors proposed settling them in the remaining large tracts, which are declared or proposed protected areas. CI raised these concerns:
- Existing and proposed sites are too small for communities that may double in size over 15 years.
- Corn and bean farming quickly depletes soils, pushing farmers deeper into protected areas.
- Sites lie in remote northern Guatemala, where few if any toads exist. Most lack sewage, health clinics, and schools, and some lack drinking water.

Many refugees and some NGOs favour settlement near protected areas where land is plentiful. Outgoing President Ramiro de León Carpio also supports this approach. CI argues that identifying land outside protected areas would give families a better chance at a peaceful, prosperous future.

**Maya Forest.** The Maya Tropical Forest is described as the largest remaining block of tropical forest in Central America. It spans northern Guatemala, western Belize, and southern Mexico. It faces assault from loggers, oil producers, ranchers, and farmers. Because three nations have seldom cooperated, CI, the US [[Man and the Biosphere Program]], and the MAYAFOR Initiative of USAID launched an information-exchange network. An August workshop pooled environmental, cultural, and socioeconomic data, displayed on CISIG maps. A final report and conservation-priority maps are due in early 1996 in Spanish and English.

**Madidi National Park, Bolivia.** Madidi covers 4.6 million acres of lowland forest, savanna, and other ecosystems on Andean slopes of the Amazon Basin. It is among the largest protected areas in South America and forms a link with three other Bolivian protected areas and two just inside Peru. Its RAP mission in 1990 recorded:
- Large populations of tapirs and spider monkeys.
- Two new mammal species for Bolivia: a little big-eared bat and a spiny tree rat.
- An estimate by Ted Parker that the region may hold more than 1,000 bird species, about 11 percent of all bird species on Earth.

Threats remain from colonisation, logging, oil extraction, and mining. In the adjoining multiple-use zone, CI works with the government, NGOs, and Tacana-Quechua families in San José de Uchupiamonas on research, community development, capacity-building, ecotourism, and nontimber forest products.

**Field science capacity.** CI notes that many Latin American forests are being destroyed faster than scientists can survey them. RAP has relied on a handful of biologists covering ecosystems from Belize to Bolivia. Bolivia has hosted six RAP surveys in five years.

**Makira, Solomon Islands.** The 35 villages of the Makira Conservation Area (63,000 hectares) rejected all logging contract offers. Land is held by traditional clans, so loggers must negotiate with each group. The program, called Conservation in Development, is a partnership of local communities, CI, the Maruia Society of New Zealand, and the Solomon Islands Development Trust. Landowners rejected the offers before the program's enterprises had produced long-term economic benefits.

**Andean road-building.** New roads into intact ecosystems often open them to settlement, agriculture, and industry. CAF is working with CI to evaluate 45 road projects in Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. These countries cover about 1.8 million square miles and 98.6 million people. CI invited 35 regional experts to a meeting in October to assess biodiversity impacts. CI also analysed economic, environmental, and social consequences, including colonisation, indigenous cultures, and land tenure. The Andean region is described as the most diverse of the world's hotspots, and Mittermeier argued that infrastructure must maintain biological resources rather than further their destruction.

## Key Findings

- **Toxicity and dependence:** *Phyllobates terribilis* is described as 20 times more toxic than any other amphibian. Its toxicity depends on the rain forest environment and is lost outside it.
- **Refugee resettlement conflict:** More than 40,000 refugees (93 percent farmers) seek return. Proposed protected-area sites are too small, lack services, and face soil depletion, so CI urges settlement outside protected areas.
- **Maya Forest cooperation:** A three-nation information network and CISIG-based priority maps are due in early 1996.
- **Madidi National Park:** A 4.6-million-acre park, declared with RAP support, with two new Bolivian mammal records and an estimated 1,000+ bird species. Threats remain.
- **RAP training:** A month-long course trained 24 Bolivian field biologists in four groups (plants, mammals, birds, reptiles), with further RAP training planned for Peru and Indonesia in 1996.
- **Community conservation in Makira:** 35 villages rejected all logging offers covering 63,000 hectares.
- **Andean infrastructure:** CAF is evaluating 45 road projects with CI input. Results and a GIS database are due in early 1996.
- **Global conservation finance:** New World Bank president Wolfensohn identified poverty fuelled by population growth and threats to biodiversity as the Bank's chief challenge.
- **Award-winning outreach:** *Fanamby*, on Madagascar's biodiversity and culture, won a Jackson Hole Wildlife Film Festival award for a film produced for under $50,000. It is available in Malagasy, French, and English.
- **Bird diversity indicator:** CI notes birds are among the best biodiversity indicators. The newsletter's chart names Brazil, Ecuador, and Indonesia as the top countries for bird diversity, though no reliable values are given in the text.
