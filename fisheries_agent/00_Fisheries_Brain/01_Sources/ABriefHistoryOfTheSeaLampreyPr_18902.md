# A Brief History of the Sea Lamprey Problem in Michigan Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 paper by Davin S. Shetter of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 76), reviews the history of the [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) invasion of Michigan's Great Lakes waters. It outlines the species' [[Life Cycle]], documents its spread from Lake Ontario through Lake Erie into Lakes Huron, Michigan and Superior, and reports results of a [[Weir Trapping Experiment]] on the Ocqueoc River in 1944 and 1945. It also summarizes a June 1946 survey of commercial fishermen on lamprey scarring and economic losses, and it concludes that trapping on major spawning streams might control the pest, while complete eradication would be costly and possibly impossible.

## Background & Description

- **Life history:** Adults are anadromous and spawn mainly in May and June, dying afterward. Larvae (ammocoetes) spend 4 to 5 years in sand, gravel and silt banks before metamorphosing. Free-living parasitic adults then feed on fish for about 1½ to 3¾ years before maturing (Gage, 1928). Shetter estimates total life-cycle duration at no less than 5 years and possibly up to 8 years.
- **Parasitic feeding:** Adults attach with an oral sucking disc, use horny circumoral teeth and a rasping tongue, and secrete anticoagulant from buccal glands. Gage (1928) estimated about one meal every 36 days and roughly 1 ounce of blood per feeding.
- **Size:** Michigan specimens range from 12.5 to 30.0 inches, with reports of up to 40 inches. Inland "lake" forms seldom exceed 15 inches, which Hubbs and Pope (1937) considered a dwarf form.
- **Identification:** Taxonomic keys from Hubbs and Lagler (1941) separate the species from four other lamprey species in Michigan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spread:** Before 1921 the species was known only from Lake Ontario. The invasion of the upper Great Lakes occurred between 1921 and 1936, and by 1946 the species was established in all the Great Lakes.
- **Damage to fisheries:** Lake trout were the most heavily affected species, followed by whitefish, suckers, yellow pikeperch and carp. Fish with healed scars were typically about the size of a 25-cent coin, while some fish bore up to nine fresh wounds.
- **Environmental cues:** The spawning peak occurred when water temperature reached about 50°F, and most of the run passed when daily averages were 51° to 60°F.
- **Native lampreys:** Four other lamprey species occur in Michigan, including the silver lamprey, which is parasitic on Lake Huron fish, and the non-parasitic Michigan and American brook lampreys.

## Key Findings

1. **Establishment:** Sea lampreys were recorded in 68 Michigan spawning streams or areas in 1946, with the most in northern Lake Michigan (30) and northern Lake Huron (17). Spawning runs were reported in 26 of the 41 Michigan counties bordering the Great Lakes.
2. **Ocqueoc weir, 1944:** 3,366 lampreys were destroyed (May 2,000; June 1,225; July 141). The weir operated from May 22 to July 24 and did not fully block the run. Daily catch records were not kept.
3. **Ocqueoc weir, 1945:** 4,608 lampreys were trapped and destroyed out of 9,911 total catch, which included 15 fish species, 2 turtles and 5 water snakes. Observers estimated that sea lampreys made up 90 percent or more of the lamprey run.
4. **Migration timing:** 95 percent or more of upstream movement occurred between midnight and noon, and slightly over half between midnight and 6 a.m. The hours of greatest activity were in full darkness.
5. **Size and sex:** Ocqueoc River sea lampreys ranged from 14.1 to 30 inches. Females (n = 18, early July) averaged 17.9 inches and males (n = 12) averaged 17.6 inches. The sex ratio before the peak was 70 percent male, and after the peak it was 80 percent female, suggesting males run earlier.
6. **Temperature response:** 2,452 of 4,579 upstream migrants (about 53 percent) were trapped May 19–28, when average daily water temperatures ranged from 51.0° to 60.5°F.
7. **Weir performance:** Blocking arms were undercut and overtopped by floods during April 25–28 and May 28–June 6, 1945. Escapement was observed on spawning grounds below Ocqueoc Falls in both years.
8. **Commercial impact (June 1946 survey):** Of 59 reporting interviews, about 52 percent (31 of 59) stated no loss in sales value from scars. Roughly 26 percent of reports described losses of 5 to 75 percent on scarred fish. About one-third of commercial species were reported scarred. Losses were concentrated in ports north of Saginaw Bay (Lake Huron) and north of Muskegon (Lake Michigan).
9. **Management conclusions:** Weirs require sheet-piling foundations and screens of no more than ¼-inch mesh. A weir on the Ocqueoc River was estimated at $5,000 to build, and eradication was judged difficult if not impossible. Operating weirs on the most important spawning streams might keep the pest under control.
10. **Policy:** House Joint Resolution 366 directed the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to investigate and eradicate the sea lamprey, authorizing up to $20,000 per year for 10 years. Shetter also recommended research on the life cycle and the possible commercial use of the species.
