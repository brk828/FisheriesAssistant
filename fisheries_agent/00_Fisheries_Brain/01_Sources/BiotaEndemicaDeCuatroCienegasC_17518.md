# Endemic Biota of the Cuatro Ciénegas Valley, Coahuila, Mexico

#ichthyology #aquatic-biology #endemism #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This short review by Salvador Contreras Balderas, of the Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León, presents the endemic biota of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]] in north-central Mexico and an annotated checklist of its endemic species. About 70 local species have been described, and a similar number are under study, mostly animals. The valley's aquatic and semi-aquatic fauna, including fishes, is highly endemic, and the author argues that its level of endemism is comparable to a handful of famous lake systems worldwide. The paper also documents ongoing degradation from water extraction and land use, the failure of previous conservation and cooperative efforts, and a call for protection, study, and non-destructive tourism and education.

## Background & Description

The valley is a desert basin (bolsón) whose endemism is concentrated in its springs, pools, and their outflows, and in the gypsum dunes. Endemism appears among aquatic and semi-aquatic species and, less often, in the mesophytic community and the dunes. The author notes that the region has attracted much attention from foreign researchers but little within Mexico. The reasons given are a lack of dissemination of knowledge and the general subordination of ecological considerations to economic ones.

The paper is a general overview and species list, not a full monograph. A broader ecological panorama is cited from [[Minckley]] (1969), and a graphic account with biological notes was announced as forthcoming. The checklist is partial for botany and is not an exhaustive bibliography. The list was drawn from a field guide to the valley's biota prepared by the Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, and the document is marked as Contribution No. 25 of the Laboratorio de Ictiología y Pesquerías.

Comparable concentrations of endemic species are known from only a few places worldwide, including [[Lake Lanao]] (Philippines, fishes), [[Lake Baikal]] (Siberia, fishes), [[Lake Ohrid]] (Yugoslavia and Albania, molluscs), the [[East African Rift Lakes]] (fishes), the [[Galapagos Islands]] (several groups), and the islands of the Gulf of California (reptiles). The presence of endemic subfamilies led a Russian author, cited as a personal communication, to propose recognizing the valley as a new high-level biogeographic realm. Under that proposal, northern Mexico would be Nearctic, the valley a separate central "island" realm, and the rest of Mexico Neotropical.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate and history:** Desert conditions have persisted in the valley since at least the middle of the Wisconsin Glaciation and possibly through the whole Quaternary (Meyer, 1973).
- **Phylogenetic relationships:** Endemic molluscs have approximate phylogenetic ties going back to the Miocene and no close living relatives. Crustaceans appear to form a Tethyan unit, with related forms in the Gulf of Mexico coastal plain, the Antilles, and the Mediterranean.
- **Human history:** The basin has been inhabited for more than 10,000 years, one of the longest continuous human occupation sequences in North America. The town of Cuatro Ciénegas is described as the cradle of the Mexican Revolution and Modernity.
- **Habitat heterogeneity:** Salt concentration, depth, flow, and temperature vary extremely, sometimes over distances of only a few meters, yet the fauna reflects this divergence. The gypsum dunes are described as having 99.4% purity.
- **Water and land use:** Gypsum-laden spring waters are exported and used for irrigation as far as San Buenaventura. The mayor of that town reported in 1976 that orchards there had disappeared after irrigation with Cuatro Ciénegas water, and the area is no longer fruit-producing.

## Key Findings

**Endemic species checklist (summary):**

- **Vascular plants:** Gypsophilous endemics include *Dyssodia gypsophyla* and *Gaillardia gypsophyla*. Cactaceae are also represented, with *Grusonia bradtiana* listed.
- **Molluscs:** 34 species, 23 endemic, mostly Hydrobiidae. Genera include *Mexipyrgus* (several species), *Cochliopina*, *Coahuilix*, *Durangonella*, *Nymphophilus*, *Mexithauma quadripaludium*, and *Paludiscala caramba*.
- **Crustaceans:** 8 species, 7 endemic, excluding microcrustaceans. Examples are the cirolanid isopods *Speocirolana thermydronis* and *Sphaerolana* spp., the amphipods *Mexiweckelia colei* and *M. particeps*, the stenasellid isopod *Mexistenaselus coahuilae*, and the shrimp *Palaemonetes suttkusi*.
- **Lepidoptera:** About 40 species listed, with *Brephidium* sp. noted.
- **Fishes:** 20 species, possibly 18 endemic. The list includes:
  - *Cyprinodon atrorus* and *C. bifasciatus*
  - *Lucania interioris*
  - *Notropis xanthicara* and a *Notropis* sp. that includes Río Salado populations
  - *Gambusia marshi*, which extends into the Río Salado, and *G. longispinis*
  - *Xiphophorus gordoni*
  - *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatus pavonaceus*, which also extends into the Río Salado, plus several undescribed *Cichlasoma* spp.
  - *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Dionda episcopa*, *Micropterus salmoides*, and *Etheostoma* sp., listed at subspecies or genus level
- **Amphibians:** 7 species, 1 endemic, *Eleutherodactylus augusti fuscofemora*.
- **Reptiles:** 37 species, possibly 8 endemic. Endemics include *Terrapene coahuilae*, *Trionyx ater*, *Pseudemys scripta taylori*, *Sceloporus merriami australis*, *Gerrhonotus lugoi*, and *Cnemidophorus scalaris pallidus*.
- **Other groups:** Undescribed probable endemics are reported among gypsophilous plants, aquatic plants (including a *Potamogeton*), four reptiles, one bird, one mammal, helminths, and insects. Scorpions of the genus *Vejovis* are reported, with five species described and the valley said to hold the richest scorpion fauna in America (19 species).

**Key scientific problems noted:**

- Sympatric speciation in cichlids, for which no plausible explanatory model yet exists.
- Two divergent *Cyprinodon* forms, considered the most distinct in the genus, come into secondary contact through human disturbance, hybridize, and fix hybrid traits in a single generation.

**Threats and conservation status:**

- Inappropriate use has probably caused the extinction of at least one species and threatens many more whose value is unknown.
- Three ejidos (communal land holdings) were founded in the valley and failed. Management norms that work elsewhere do not work here.
- Conservation efforts have had little traction, because decision-makers tend to regard the desert as an enemy to be destroyed.
- Only a very small part of the aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats has been explored.

**Recommendations:**

- Urgently protect and study the area to understand and use it rationally.
- Promote non-destructive intensive tourism and environmental education, highlighting the valley's cultural and aesthetic value and its use as a natural evolutionary laboratory with no parallel in the world.
- The author argues that solutions require a radical change in mentalities and objectives.
