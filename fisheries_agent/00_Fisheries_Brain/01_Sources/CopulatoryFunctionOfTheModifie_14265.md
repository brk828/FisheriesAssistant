## Executive Summary

This 1957 letter (Hubbs & Reynolds, *American Naturalist*) addresses the functional significance of sexually dimorphic pectoral fin rays in the fish tribe Gambusiini (family [[Poeciliidae]]), a group that includes live-bearing mosquitofishes. Building on earlier morphological observations (Turner 1942; Hubbs & Hubbs 1945; Hubbs 1950; Hubbs 1957), the authors experimentally confirm via behavioral observation that the thickened/modified upper pectoral fin rays in males of *[[Gambusia affinis]]*, *[[Gambusia geiseri]]*, and *[[Gambusia hurtadoi]]* function to support and stabilize the [[Gonopodium]] (modified anal fin used for sperm transfer) during copulation — a role previously demonstrated only for the more extremely modified pectoral fin of *[[Gambusia heterochir]]*. The authors argue that this behavioral use of the pectoral fin is ancestral to the tribe, and that the extreme structural specialization (a notch on the fin's upper margin) seen uniquely in *G. heterochir* evolved secondarily to reinforce a pre-existing behavior, rather than the behavior evolving in response to the structure.

## Background & Description

- **Sexual dimorphism of pectoral fin rays**: Males across the tribe Gambusiini show thickened upper pectoral fin rays relative to females; this is documented in all examined species of *Gambusia*, as well as in *Flexipenis*, *Heterophallus*, and (to a lesser extent) *Belonesox* (Turner 1942; via Carl L. Hubbs).
- **Bilateral asymmetry**: Hubbs & Hubbs (1945) demonstrated that the right and left modified pectoral fin rays of adult male *Gambusia* are not symmetrical.
- **Extreme modification in *Gambusia heterochir***: This species possesses the most highly derived pectoral fin structure in the genus, featuring a distinct **notch on the upper margin** of the fin — a feature absent in congeners.
- **Related extreme modification**: *[[Xenodexia ctenolepis]]* possesses a highly modified right pectoral fin, hypothesized by Carl L. Hubbs (1950) to hold the gonopodium during copulation, though unconfirmed at the time due to lack of live specimens.
- **General pattern**: Aside from *G. heterochir*, other examined *Gambusia* species show only slight-to-moderate pectoral modification, providing what the authors term "ventral stability" to the gonopodium, versus the notched fin of *G. heterochir*, which provides both ventral **and** lateral stability.
- **Other sexually dimorphic traits in Poeciliidae** (contextual, per Rosen & Gordon 1953): elongated dorsal and caudal fin rays with highly colored membranes in males; pelvic fin dimorphism linked to courtship (Clark, Aronson, & Gordon 1954).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Freshwater systems (springs, streams) of the southern United States, Mexico, and Central America.
- **Taxonomic scope**: Tribe **Gambusiini**, comprising four genera (per Carl L. Hubbs 1926) within the family [[Poeciliidae]] — live-bearing (viviparous) fishes with internal fertilization via the male gonopodium.
- **Species examined**:
  - *[[Gambusia affinis]]* (common mosquitofish)
  - *[[Gambusia geiseri]]*
  - *[[Gambusia hurtadoi]]*
  - *[[Gambusia heterochir]]* (reference species with extreme pectoral modification)
  - Hybrids between *G. affinis* × *G. heterochir*
- **Species referenced but not directly tested** (due to lack of live laboratory stock): *[[Gambusia panuco]]*, *[[Gambusia nicaraguensis]]*, *[[Gambusia lemaitrei]]* — distantly related forms presumed to share the same functional mechanism based on structural similarity.
- **Reproductive ecology**: Reproduction involves male courtship behavior and copulation via the gonopodium; the pectoral fin serves as a structural and behavioral aid during mating, a novel functional link between paired-fin morphology and reproductive behavior in [[Livebearing Fishes]].

## Key Findings

- **Experimental confirmation of function**: Males of *G. affinis*, *G. geiseri*, and *G. hurtadoi* (and *G. affinis* × *G. heterochir* hybrids) were isolated for ≥24 hours, then presented with females; courtship was observed for up to 30 minutes.
- **Isolation increases activity**: Consistent with Clark, Aronson, & Gordon (1954), pre-isolating males increased sexual activity and facilitated behavioral observation.
- **Universal copulatory behavior**: In all species and hybrids tested, males repeatedly placed the gonopodium over the pectoral fin during courtship and copulation — the pectoral fin was held rigidly at ~90° to the body axis, with the body often curved toward that side.
- **Ambidexterity**: Males of all four species and the hybrids used either the left or right pectoral fin interchangeably (ambidextrous behavior), both during full copulation and during copulatory "thrusts."
- **Behavior matches *G. heterochir***: The observed copulatory support behavior in these species is essentially identical to that previously described for *G. heterochir* (Warburton et al., in press), where the fin's notch provides additional lateral stability.
- **Evolutionary sequence inferred**: Because pectoral-supported copulation is common to all examined *Gambusia* species (not just the structurally specialized *G. heterochir*), the authors conclude this behavior is ancestral to the genus/tribe, and that the extreme notched pectoral modification in *G. heterochir* is a **derived structural adaptation that followed (reinforced) a pre-existing behavior**, rather than the behavior arising independently or the notch being lost repeatedly in other lineages.
- **Selective advantage hypothesis**: The pectoral notch in *G. heterochir* is proposed to confer added lateral (in addition to ventral) stability to the gonopodium during copulation, representing a refinement of a generalized ventral-support function present across the genus.
- **Broader taxonomic implication**: This supports classifying pectoral-fin sexual dimorphism as a synapomorphy of the tribe [[Gambusiini]], linked functionally to [[Copulatory Behavior]] in poeciliid fishes, and provides a model for how morphological specialization can evolve to reinforce pre-existing adaptive behaviors.
