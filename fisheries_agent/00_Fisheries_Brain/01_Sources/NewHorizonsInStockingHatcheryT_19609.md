# New Horizons in Stocking Hatchery Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by [[Paul R. Needham]] (University of California, Berkeley) at the Twenty-Fourth [[North American Wildlife Conference]] (1959), synthesizes marked-trout planting experiments to test the assumption that hatchery stocking is the main answer to angling demand. Drawing on 244 plants reported in about 36 papers, the author finds that recovery rates from hatchery trout are generally low, that over-winter survival is negligible, and that most creeled trout in many waters come from natural reproduction. He argues that the [[Catchable Trout]] "put and take" program is costly, concentrates catch among a small group of skilled anglers, and degrades sport quality. He recommends designating "catchable" and "natural fish" waters, shifting funds toward [[Habitat Improvement]] and research, and emphasizing sport values through [[Fly-Only Waters]] and [[Catch-and-Release]] areas. Discussion comments from [[Aubert Hazard]] and [[Wallace]] (National Park Service) support limited-kill and no-kill management.

## Background & Description

The paper compiles recovery data for [[Eastern Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Because no marked differences in survival were seen among the three species, their data are pooled. The author states that after thirty years of investigation, fisheries workers should reach general conclusions about planting hatchery trout. He frames hatchery trout as "creel insurance" that serves few anglers.

Survival data were grouped into six categories by fish size, season of planting, and habitat (lake or stream). About twenty-five days of survival data were excluded because of missing information, artificial experimental conditions, or mismatched subject matter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Survival and recovery**

- Hatchery-reared trout are described as poorly adapted to wild waters. They are said to be physically and genetically unprepared after one to two years in hatcheries, and an apparent natural mortality of 10 to 30 per cent occurs immediately after each plant.
- Survival is lower where wild trout or other fishes already occupy the water. Lake plantings of fingerlings in such waters "are usually less than five per cent."
- Spring-fed streams such as [[Spring Creek]] (Pennsylvania) and [[Rush Creek]] (California) are free of drought, floods, temperature extremes, and heavy winter ice, so high recoveries there are not surprising. In more average streams, recoveries are much lower.
- Over-winter carry-over is described as negligible. [[Mullan]] (1958) reported carry-over from 0 to 9.4 per cent in twenty lots of eastern brook trout stocked in Cape Cod streams.

**Delayed mortality and behavior**

- Losses of planted trout in streams with resident populations are often immediate and heavy, a phenomenon termed "delayed mortality." [[Richard B. Miller]] (1958) suggested that competition for living space or niches, rather than food, forces introduced fish into excessive exercise, leading to death by acidosis or starvation. He found significant differences in blood lactic acid levels between hatchery trout with and without competition from resident trout.
- Underwater tank observations (Needham and Jones, in press) show that trout are territorial. One large brown trout occupied the same hole in a beaver pond in [[Sagehen Creek]] for five years despite floods and other changes.

**Natural propagation**

- The [[Sagehen Creek]] project near Truckee, California, has operated a [[Creel Census]] on the upper five miles since stocking stopped in 1951. The stream yields roughly 1,600 to 3,200 trout weighing 162 to 287 pounds annually, all from natural propagation.
- Annual exploitation of fish four inches and longer averaged below 45 per cent of available fish. Catch rates ranged from 1.08 to 1.88 fish per angling hour.
- Fish and Game officials are cited as estimating that 75 to 85 per cent of creeled trout originate from natural spawning.

## Key Findings

**Recovery rates by planting category (Table 1, 244 plants)**

| Category | Experiments | Average Recovery | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lake plants of fingerlings, all seasons | 32 | 7.4% | 36.4% to 0.06% |
| Lake plants of legal-sized trout, all seasons | 38 | 34.5% | 88.4% to 1.1% |
| Stream plants of fingerlings, all seasons | 21 | 2.5% | 14.0% to 0.00% |
| Stream plants of legal-sized trout, before season | 54 | 28.6% | 82.0% to 2.60% |
| Stream plants of legal-sized trout, during season | 68 | 41.3% | 92.2% to 1.0% |
| Stream plants of legal-sized trout, after season | 31 | 16.8% | 88.6% to 0.02%* |

*Table 1 gives 0.02 per cent as the minimum; the text gives 0.20 per cent.

- Overall average across all 244 plants: 27.2 per cent (maximum 92.20, minimum 0.00).
- Highest recoveries came from [[Castle Lake]] (Wales and German, 1956) for fingerlings, from [[Rush Creek]] (Vestal, 1954) for in-season stream plants, and from [[Spring Creek]] (Trembley, 1945) for post-season plants. Excluding Spring Creek's five plants lowers that category's mean to 8.9 per cent.
- Ten of 21 stream fingerling experiments yielded under 1 per cent, and four yielded zero.
- Among legal-sized stream plants made before the season, 24 of 54 experiments returned less than 20 per cent.

**Specific cases**

- A 1946 [[Clackamas River]] (Oregon) plant of 30,363 marked 3 to 4 inch rainbow fingerlings returned nine fish (0.03 per cent). Rearing cost, charged against those nine fish, was $28.53 per fish.
- The highest stream return came from a plant of 200 seven- to 13-inch eastern brook trout in the [[Deerskin River]], Wisconsin, a few days before the season opened.

**Economics and angler distribution**

- Between 65 and 85 per cent of most state game-fish budgets go to trout propagation, yet about 30 per cent of California anglers fish for trout.
- Creel checks in California found that 65 to 75 per cent of anglers seeking catchables went home empty-handed. Another 25 per cent took one to five fish. About 10 per cent of anglers took over 50 per cent of the catchables.
- At [[Crystal Lake]] (Evans, 1957), 3 per cent of anglers took 30 per cent of the trout.
- Since 1948, over $4.3 million has been spent on California hatcheries, raising catchable production by over 325 per cent.
- Assuming a 20-cent planting cost and 50 per cent mortality, each creeled catchable costs about 40 cents. A $3.00 license is "paid for" after about eight fish.
- A family of four on two licenses reportedly took 60 catchables in one day (bag limit 15). Over a 12-week season at two bag limits per week, the author calculates up to 1,440 fish, worth $576, for a $6 fee. In practice, creel data show catch rates fall to zero within 8 to 16 days after planting ([[Jensen]], 1958).

**Sport quality and angling ethics**

- Crowding around planting trucks degrades sport and etiquette. The author contrasts this with unstocked streams, where anglers respect one another's rights.
- The author argues that "fish-out" ponds at hatcheries, where anglers pay per fish, would produce the same result for a fee and spare non-users from funding catchable programs.

**Anglers' comments and alternative management**

- [[Aubert Hazard]] (Pennsylvania Fish Commission) argued that natural reproduction alone cannot meet demand in the densely populated East. He proposed stocking marginal streams and limiting kill. A Pennsylvania trial on Youngman's Creek allowed unlimited catch-and-release fishing, which was to be continued with possible trophy-size regulations.
- [[Wallace]] (National Park Service) reported that in four [[Great Smokies National Park]] streams open year-round, anglers caught up to 11 fish per angler-hour, compared with about half a fish per hour in nearby streams where fish could be kept.

**Recommendations**

- Designate "catchable" waters and set a ceiling on hatchery production that leaves funds for habitat and research.
- Designate other waters as "natural fish" waters, reserving stocking for lakes without suitable spawning habitat, winter-kill, pollution, or flash-flood losses.
- Invest in creating new spawning beds rather than only removing barriers to migration.
- Establish fly-only and barbless-hook waters, and catch-and-put-back areas, to restore sport quality.
- Reassess stocking for reproduction. The author notes that introductions such as [[Brown Trout]] from Europe succeeded, but no barren waters remain for such introductions.

**Conclusion**

The author concludes that "blind faith" in hatcheries must give way to research on natural behavior, survival, physiology, and genetics. He notes that funding for hatcheries is easy to obtain while research money is scarce.
