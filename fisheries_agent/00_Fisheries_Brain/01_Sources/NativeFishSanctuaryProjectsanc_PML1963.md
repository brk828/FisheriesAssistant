# Native Fish Sanctuary Project: Sanctuary Development Phase, 2007 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (Mueller, 2007 annual report, 59 p.) documents the development phase of the [[Native Fish Sanctuary Project]], funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and run in partnership with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[National Park Service]] and other agencies. The project tested the [[Minckley Conservation Plan]], which proposes small off-channel sanctuaries as predator-free habitats where [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] can complete their life cycle. Over 2005–2007 the project assisted in developing or supporting 10 sites (15 ponds), with management plans written for four of them. The report concludes that predator control, not habitat alone, is the decisive factor: fish breed readily where non-native predators are absent, and communities collapse quickly once predators are introduced.

## Background & Description

- **Native species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are endemic to the Colorado River and two of four endangered large-river fishes. The recovery program (started 1987) also covers humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) and Colorado pikeminnow (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).
- **Status:** Wild bonytail are extirpated from the main stem. Razorback sucker was listed in 1991; fewer than 1,000 wild individuals remain, and juveniles are largely absent from the main stem and reservoirs.
- **Stocking history:** A 1981 FWS–AGFD agreement to stock 100,000 razorback suckers per year for 10 years largely failed, mainly due to predation. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (from 1989) augmented the [[Lake Mohave]] population using wild larvae and repatriated adults. The [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program]] reached its goal of stocking 30,000 bonytail and razorback suckers and ended in 2005.
- **Sanctuary concept:** Sanctuaries are seminatural manmade habitats where native fish complete their life cycle, including sustainable recruitment. The report defines three functions: sanctuary (supports natural recruitment), grow-out facility (fish are grown larger), and repository (large fish are temporarily held for later stocking, brood stock, or research).
- **Study design:** Sites were evaluated on predator control, habitat suitability, conversion cost, logistics, location and landowner willingness, then prioritized. Study goals were to use small ponds (under 5 acres) and build multiagency partnerships.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predators as the limiting factor:** Natural recruitment has occurred in several sites (Cibola High Levee Pond, Rock Tank, Yuma and Davis Coves, Farmington grow-out ponds). The common factor was the absence of non-native predators, not physical habitat.
- **Cibola High Levee Pond collapse:** Natural recruitment of both species ran from 1994 to 2003. Illegally introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] in 2003 produced thousands of young, and native recruitment ceased in 2004. The pond had supported more than 1,000 adult razorback suckers and more than 10,000 bonytail in 1998.
- **Water quality:** Isolation creates its own problems, including salinity increase, low dissolved oxygen, high temperature and anoxia. At Davis Cove, salinity rose to over 5,000 µmhos/cm, about 5 times the reservoir value. An experimental pump test in November 2006 reduced conductivity by nearly 1,000 points.
- **Flood-plain hydrology:** Oxbow productivity depends on ground-water exchange, which is poorly understood. Hydraulically isolated oxbows can become "death traps" through warming, evaporation and oxygen depletion.
- **Hatchery-reared fish:** Hatchery-reared bonytail and razorback suckers are predator-naive and attracted to large predators. Predator exposure and flow conditioning significantly increased survival in trials (Mueller et al., 2007). Sanctuary-reared fish are the second-best option after wild translocation.

## Key Findings

**Sites developed or supported (Table 2, 10 sites, 15 ponds):**

- **[[Davis Cove]]** (Lake Mohave, NPS): Self-reproducing bonytail and razorback sucker populations. Stocked September 2005 with 150 adult razorback suckers and 1,500 bonytail. Recruitment was documented in 2006. Estimated carrying capacity: minimum 300 adult razorbacks and about 5,000 adult bonytail. Crayfish caused high mortality after early renovation.
- **[[Office Cove]]** (Lake Havasu, Bill Williams NWR): About 1.1 ha; solar aeration and supplemental feeding. Free of non-native predators. Several year-classes of razorback sucker present in 2008. Estimated natural capacity: minimum 500 adult razorbacks and 2,500 bonytail.
- **[[Cibola High Levee Pond]]** (Cibola NWR): Compromised by largemouth bass. Six mature razorbacks moved to Office Cove. Renovation scheduled for fall 2008. Expected capacity after restoration: 1,000 adult razorbacks and 7,500 bonytail.
- **[[Parker Dam Pond]]** (FWS, Bureau of Reclamation): Chemically renovated January 2007 (rotenone followed by antimycin) and stocked April 2007 with about 100 bonytail. Recruitment documented in July 2007, with thousands of young seen. Described as the first successful bonytail-only sanctuary. Estimated capacity: minimum 250 adult and 1,250 juvenile bonytail.
- **[[Three Fingers Lake]]** (Cibola NWR): Received 135 large razorback suckers in December 2006. Two solar aerators installed January 2007; herbicide treatment against coontail. Expenditures: 45 staff days and $22,000. A 2007–2010 management plan was drafted, and a compromise with recreational anglers was reached.
- **[[River's Edge Golf Course]]** (Needles, CA): Two ponds (about 1 ha total) drained in July 2007 and refilled. Stocked with bonytail for mosquito abatement. Estimated capacity: about 500 adult razorbacks or 1,500 juveniles.
- **[[Emerald Canyon Golf Course]]** (La Paz County, AZ): Four ponds redesigned in 2007 with screening to exclude non-native fish from river water. Stocked in fall 2007.
- **[[Bulkhead Cove]]** (Lake Havasu, CA): Grow-out site in the 1990s, later invaded by sunfish and bullhead. Renovation is pending, with the main constraint being state restrictions on rotenone and antimycin.
- **[[Mohave Community College Pond]]** (Kingman, AZ): Concrete-lined, about 0.1 ha. Stocked with longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*), which reproduce naturally, plus six razorback suckers in 2006. Monitoring found pH above 10, and a new aeration pump was installed.
- **[[Cerbat Cliffs Golf Course]]** (Kingman, AZ): Two ponds of about 1 ha each, currently with non-native fish. Conversion led by AGFD; renovation possibly in 2008.

**Management plans:** Written for Davis Cove (Appendix A), Cibola High Levee Pond (B), Parker Dam Pond (C) and Three Fingers Lake (D). Plans for other sites are expected.

**Sites dropped (9):** [[Rock Tank]] (Chiricahua leopard frog reintroduction), [[Pittsburgh Point]] (heavy recreational use, poor water quality), [[Palm Lake]] (chronic dissolved-oxygen problems, spring-fed and impossible to drain), [[Boulder City Veteran's Park]] (West Nile Virus concerns), Cocopah Ponds (tribe declined), No Entry Cove, Twin Cove South and Twin Cove North (barrier nets failed; about 80,000 bonytail stocked in 1994), and [[Dead Horse State Park]] (inflow screening costs).

**Sites for further consideration:** [[Yuma Cove]] (site of first documented razorback recruitment, 1992; possible sanctuary if deepened), [[Palo Verde Dam Abandoned Intake]], [[Long Lake]] (Fort Mohave reservation) and [[Beal Lake]] (80 ha; renovated 2001 and 2006 but nonnative fish reappeared quickly).

**Other facilities:** [[Achii Hanyo]] (FWS facility with seven ponds returned to production, producing more than 15,000 bonytail annually), [[DU2 Ponds Imperial]] (about 1,200 razorbacks produced from the first pond), [[Stillman Lake]] (8 ha, eradication planned), [[Pipe Springs National Monument]] (proposed bonytail stocking) and [[Iceberg Canyon]] (Lake Powell, NPS grant pending).

**Capacity and production estimates:** Flood-plain facilities could support about 6,000 adult razorback suckers and nearly 20,000 bonytail. Note that these are rough predictions based on Cibola High Levee Pond measurements, not management goals.

**Costs:** Reported as staff days and direct costs, not total costs. Davis Cove: 42 staff days and $4,500 in materials. Three Fingers Lake: 45 staff days and $22,000. River's Edge: 30 staff days and $2,000. Parker Dam Pond: 20 staff days.

**Key conclusions and recommendations:**
- Draining is the ideal renovation method (used once, at River's Edge), but pumping is costly and hard to permit.
- Partial pumping can cause incomplete fish kills through seepage, possibly what happened at Beal Lake.
- Operation and maintenance costs are rarely estimated before construction and need to be considered.
- Long-term commitment by agencies is needed; "hobby projects" are no longer sufficient.
- Interagency differences, environmental compliance and state-level sport-fishery interests slowed several projects.
- Sanctuaries provide a test of whether conservation and recovery are feasible, and their value depends on sustained monitoring and management.
