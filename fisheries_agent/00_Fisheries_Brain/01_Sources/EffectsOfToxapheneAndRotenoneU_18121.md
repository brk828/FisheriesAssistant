# Effects of Toxaphene and Rotenone upon the Macroscopic Bottom Fauna of Two Northern Colorado Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by [[Colbert E. Cushing Jr.]] and [[John R. Olive]], published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, compared the effects of two [[Piscicides]], [[Toxaphene]] and [[Rotenone]] (derris), on the macroscopic bottom fauna of two shallow northern Colorado lakes. Both treatments were applied to remove rough fish, primarily [[Common Carp]]. [[Reservoir No. 4]] received toxaphene at about 0.1 p.p.m., and [[Smith Lake]] received derris at about 1.0 p.p.m. [[Reservoir No. 2]] served as an untreated control. Bottom samples were collected with an [[Ekman Dredge]] at roughly two-week intervals from October 21, 1954, to September 30, 1955.

Toxaphene had a far greater immediate and residual effect on [[Chironomidae]] (Tendipedidae) than rotenone did. Oligochaetes were not adversely affected by either poison.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the effects of fish toxicants on fish populations are well documented, but effects on other lake organisms had mostly been reported incidentally in fish studies. This study addresses that gap.

**Study sites**

- **Reservoir No. 4:** single-basin lake, about 60 acres, maximum depth 14 ft during most of the study, part of an irrigation storage system. Treated October 28, 1954; surface temperature 42.0°F at poisoning.
- **Smith Lake:** single-basin natural lake, about 24 acres, maximum depth 5 ft. Treated November 4, 1954 with derris powder containing 5% rotenone; surface temperature 38.0°F at poisoning.
- **Reservoir No. 2:** untreated control, maximum depth 7 ft.

All three lakes had silt and finely decomposed organic bottoms that gave the mud a black color.

**Sampling methods**

Each sample consisted of one square foot of bottom mud taken as four ¼ sq. ft. Ekman dredge hauls along a transect from the deepest point to shore. Samples were washed through graduated screens, the finest with 20 meshes per inch. Dissolved oxygen, free CO₂, phenolphthalein and methyl orange alkalinity, and pH were measured by standard limnological methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry**

- Dissolved oxygen was supersaturated for most of the study. Maximum saturation occurred in February or March.
- Methyl orange alkalinity ranged from 30 to 284 p.p.m. across the three lakes, with highest values in colder months and lowest in August and September.
- Hardness came from carbonates and bicarbonates. pH ranged from 7.6 to 8.9, consistent with earlier regional work by [[Pennak]] (1945).
- Shallowness allowed wind mixing, so thermal stratification did not develop.

**Water clarity**

Both poisoned lakes cleared soon after treatment. By early winter, about two months after poisoning, the bottom was visible. The authors attributed this to the loss of suspended silt caused by the feeding activity of carp, and to a reduction in that activity after poisoning.

**Aquatic vegetation**

Dense mats of [[Potamogeton foliosus]] (pondweed) developed in Reservoir No. 4 after treatment and grew at all depths. The authors attributed this to increased light penetration after clearing and to nutrients from decomposing fish. In Smith Lake the vegetation grew in scattered patches throughout the lake.

## Key Findings

**Chironomidae (Tendipedidae)**
- In Reservoir No. 4, toxaphene reduced the midge population sharply. Living larvae numbered 2 on October 29, 1954, compared with 22 and 35 in the two prior samples. Living larvae were not collected again until July 1955, about nine months after poisoning.
- Dead bloodworm masses washed ashore four days after poisoning.
- In Smith Lake, the November 7, 1954 sample (three days post-treatment) contained 40 larvae, most of them dead. Later samples had mostly living larvae, so the rotenone effect was brief.
- The authors suggest the rotenone concentration may have been slightly above 1.0 p.p.m. initially, but that it was probably close to the calculated 1.0 p.p.m. given the short-lived effects.

**Chaoborus**
- No immediate effect in Reservoir No. 4. Numbers rose in November and December and remained high into January.
- Larvae declined in February and were absent by April, six months after treatment, and did not return.
- The authors considered direct toxicity an unlikely cause of this late decline and suggested starvation as a possibility, since Chaoborus feed on Entomostraca, which had also disappeared from the surface samples. The contrast with [[Hooper]] (personal communication), who reported near-total Chaoborus kill within a day or two of toxaphene treatment in a Michigan lake, was noted as unresolved.
- In the control, Chaoborus remained relatively stable over winter and began pupating in spring.

**Oligochaeta**
- Oligochaetes showed no adverse effects from toxaphene. Their numbers in Reservoir No. 4 rose steadily from the start to the end of the study.
- In Smith Lake, oligochaetes rose initially after treatment and then stabilized. They never reached the level seen in Reservoir No. 4.
- The authors suggested that decomposing poisoned fish provided an organic food source.

**Comparative conclusions**
- Toxaphene caused much greater immediate and residual damage to the bottom fauna than rotenone.
- Neither poison harmed oligochaetes.
- The authors noted that results for derris at about 1.0 p.p.m. agreed with several earlier laboratory and field studies, including those of [[Brown and Ball]] (1943), [[Leonard]] (1939), and [[Smith]] (1940, 1941, 1950), although some of those studies reported toxicity at higher concentrations.
