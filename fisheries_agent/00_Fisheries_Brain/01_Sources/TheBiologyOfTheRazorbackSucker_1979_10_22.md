# The Biology of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This 1979 research proposal, submitted by [[T.G. Miller]], [[James E. Deacon]], and [[Larry J. Paulson]] of the University of Nevada, Las Vegas to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, outlines a study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population in [[Lake Mohave]], which harbors the largest known population of this species in the Lower Colorado River Basin. The species was, at the time, under consideration for federal listing as **Threatened**, driven primarily by an apparent absence of successful recruitment — the population appeared to consist entirely of adult fish, with no documented juveniles in recent decades. The proposal seeks to determine population distribution, movement patterns, spawning success, and critical habitat requirements, in the context of hydroelectric operations at [[Hoover Dam]] and proposed Powerplant modifications affecting flow and water level regimes.

## Background & Description

The source document is a funding proposal rather than a morphological study; direct anatomical descriptions are minimal. However, relevant biological details include:

- **Species**: *Xyrauchen texanus*, family Catostomidae (suckers), notable for a distinctive keeled/razor-backed nuchal hump (not explicitly described in text but implied by common name).
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Referenced via [[Gustafson 1975]], who documented "adult sexual dimorphism" and fecundity, though specifics are not detailed in this excerpt.
- **Aging methods proposed**: Scales, otoliths, fin rays, and vertebrae were planned for use in age determination of sampled individuals.
- **Reproductive condition**: In specimens sampled during March–April (per [[Gustafson 1975]]), most males were found in active spawning condition, while most females had either already spawned or had immature (unmatured) eggs — suggesting a temporal mismatch between sexes during the spawning window.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Distribution
- [[Lake Mohave]] supports **two distinct razorback sucker sub-populations** inhabiting ecologically different zones:
  1. **Black Canyon** (river mile 1–12 below [[Hoover Dam]]): High primary productivity driven by extensive periphyton and macrophyte beds on stable substrates; dense macroinvertebrate populations serve as a food base.
  2. **Lower Lake Mohave** (below the thermal/limnological "interface"): Productivity driven by phytoplankton, supporting dense zooplankton populations.
- A middle zone (river mile 3 to the interface) has a silty/sandy bottom with high suspended solids load and is largely avoided by razorbacks except in fall when Lake Mohave levels are lowest and periphyton production peaks.
- Population segregation between the two zones appears to be driven by **food availability** rather than random distribution.

### Diet
- Razorback suckers are **omnivorous**, feeding on:
  - Algae
  - Chironomid larvae
  - Terrestrial insects
  - Decaying organic detritus
- (Citing [[Jonez and Sumner 1954]] and [[Minckley 1973]])

### Movement and Current Velocity
- Fish abundance at underwater observation stations shifted downstream (from near mile 1 to near mile 3) as discharge from Hoover Dam increased toward 40,000 CFS in May, suggesting **current velocity strongly influences spatial distribution**.
- Daily hydropower peaking cycles produce highly variable conditions in Black Canyon: near-calm at night, with current velocities exceeding 2 m/sec by mid-afternoon, and water level fluctuations of nearly 3 m within the canyon.

### Threats to Reproduction and Recruitment
Several interacting factors were hypothesized to suppress recruitment:
1. **Thermal regime disruption** — constant cold hypolimnetic discharge from [[Lake Mead]] may eliminate temperature as a spawning cue and impair gonadal development timing.
2. **Flow fluctuation / redd instability** — daily power-cycling causes substrate instability and water level swings that can strand eggs (documented by [[Gustafson 1975]] in 1975) or wash eggs from unstable gravel-cobble substrates.
3. **Predation by exotic species** — introduced [[Centrarchidae|centrarchid]], serranid, and salmonid fishes (established in the 1930s–40s) are considered by [[Minckley 1979]] to be the most significant factor in population decline, primarily through predation on eggs, larvae, and juveniles.
4. **Historical flow instability** — [[Dill 1944]] noted that greatly fluctuating flows in the early 1930s reduced native fish populations concurrent with a shift to clear-water conditions favoring exotic predator establishment.
- Notably, centrarchid abundance is reported as **very low in Black Canyon**, suggesting flow/thermal factors may dominate there, while in lower Lake Mohave, predation pressure may outweigh hydrological factors.

## Key Findings

- **Population structure**: The Lake Mohave razorback sucker population appears to be composed almost entirely of adults, with no confirmed juveniles found in recent decades — a central concern driving the proposed Threatened listing.
- **Spawning observed but unconfirmed as successful**: Spawning attempts have been repeatedly observed in [[Black Canyon]] and [[Cottonwood Basin]], but egg fertilization, viability, and larval survival remain undocumented.
- **Two ecologically distinct sub-populations**: Razorbacks appear to partition into a Black Canyon (periphyton/macroinvertebrate-based) group and a lower Lake Mohave (phytoplankton/zooplankton-based) group, tracking food resource availability.
- **Hydropower operations as a likely limiting factor**: Daily and seasonal fluctuations in discharge from Hoover Dam directly affect current velocity, water depth, and substrate stability — all potentially critical to spawning success and egg/larval survival.
- **Study objectives proposed**:
  1. Literature synthesis on life history, food habits, and historic/current distribution.
  2. Determine spatial, size, and age distribution of Lake Mohave population(s).
  3. Evaluate spawning success, egg viability, and recruitment potential; locate juveniles.
  4. Determine movement patterns relative to temperature, current velocity, and food availability.
  5. Establish minimum/maximum flow requirements and recommend critical habitat and mitigation measures.
- **Proposed methods**: Gill nets, trammel nets, hoop nets, seines, minnow traps, electrofishing, scuba survey transects, radio-telemetry (intraperitoneal transmitter implantation per [[Crossman 1977]] methodology), and controlled egg incubation/artificial stream rearing experiments to assess food habits and habitat preference of early life stages.
- **Study timeline**: Proposed 20 months of data collection followed by 4 months of analysis, with draft report due January 15, 1982 and final report due March 15, 1982; recommended start in February/March 1980 to align with spawning season.
- **Total estimated budget**: $156,716, covering personnel, radiotelemetry equipment, nets, boat rental, and expendables.
