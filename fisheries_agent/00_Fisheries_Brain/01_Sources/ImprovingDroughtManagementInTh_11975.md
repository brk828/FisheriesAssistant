# Improving Drought Management in the West: The Role of Mitigation and Preparedness

#drought-management #water-policy #hydroclimatology

## Executive Summary

This June 1997 report by [[Donald Wilhite]] of the [[National Drought Mitigation Center]] (NDMC), University of Nebraska, was prepared for the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]]. It reviews drought and drought management in the Western United States and recommends how the Federal Government, States, and other partners can improve the region's capacity to cope with drought. The report argues that drought is a normal feature of western climate, that its impacts are escalating, and that U.S. drought management remains reactive ("[[Crisis Management]]") rather than anticipatory ("[[Risk Management]]"). Its central recommendations are a national drought policy and plan, an integrated national climate monitoring system, redirection of drought relief funds toward preparedness and mitigation, and state drought plans focused on mitigation.

No fish, invertebrates, or aquatic species are studied. Aquatic content is limited to water supply, streamflow, reservoirs, and secondary effects on fish and wildlife.

## Background & Description

The report addresses the Commission's charge to review Federal activities affecting water allocation and use in the 19 Western States. It is organized into sections on the concept of drought, the climatology of drought from 1895 to 1996, state-level drought planning, state mitigation tools, and integration of drought management with water policy.

**Concept of drought.** Drought is described as a "creeping phenomenon" with diffuse impacts and no structural damage. It is classified as meteorological, agricultural, hydrological, and socioeconomic. The report holds that impacts depend both on the natural event and on society's vulnerability. Rising demand can produce greater impacts even without a change in drought frequency or intensity.

**Climatology.** Using the [[Palmer Drought Severity Index]] (PDSI) and the [[Standardized Precipitation Index]] (SPI), the report reviews national and basin-level drought from 1895 to 1995. Severe and extreme drought is defined as PDSI < -3.0. Key benchmarks are the 1930s drought, the 1950s drought, and the 1986 to 1996 sequence of drought years.

**Policy context.** Drought relief has been the main Federal tool. The report calls for a shift toward risk management, citing an [[Office of Technology Assessment]] recommendation for "no-regrets" options.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes drought as a hazard that acts through water supply and ecosystems:

- **Hydrological drought** affects streamflow, reservoir and lake levels, and groundwater. It often lags meteorological and agricultural drought. Competition for stored water rises during drought, and upstream changes in land use, such as deforestation or cropping changes, can alter runoff and infiltration and affect downstream droughts.
- **Environmental impacts** of the 1996 drought included "tremendous increase in forest and range fires, soil erosion, and effects on fish and wildlife populations." The report does not quantify these effects.
- **Climate change** could alter regional hydrology and increase the frequency and intensity of severe drought in the West, which the report describes as already highly sensitive to climate variability.
- **Basin-level exposure:** Figures 2 to 5 show drought time series for western basins, including the Pacific Northwest, Missouri, Upper Colorado, Great Basin, California, Lower Colorado, Arkansas-White-Red, Rio Grande, Texas Gulf, and Souris-Red-Rainy basins. The report notes that "no portion of the West is immune to the ravages of drought."

## Key Findings

**Drought climatology**

- Drought occurs somewhere in the West almost every year. Most of the West experiences severe to extreme drought more than 10 percent of the time, and a significant portion more than 15 percent.
- Figure 1 (1895 to 1995): drought frequently affects more than 10 percent of the Nation and often more than 30 percent. Severe to extreme drought covered about 65 percent of the country at the 1934 peak, with more than 95 percent of the Great Plains affected. In 1954 and 1956, nearly 50 percent of the Nation was affected.
- The 1996 drought's geographic footprint was similar to that of the 1950s drought.
- Drought from 1986 to 1996 was persistent. About 17 percent of the Nation was in severe to extreme drought by the end of 1987. More than 35 percent was affected by August 1988. Coverage leveled at about 25 percent in 1990 and 1991. The report notes that six or seven consecutive drought years occurred in parts of the West from 1987 to 1992.
- The SPI can flag emerging drought earlier than the PDSI. In the spring of 1996, the 6-month SPI showed severe to extreme dryness across a large area where the PDSI did not yet show it. The report cites the SPI threshold of -2.0 as occurring about 2.3 percent of the time, or about 1 year in 50.

**State drought planning**

- States with drought plans grew from 3 in 1982 to 23 in 1991 and 28 in 1996 (Figure 9).
- Five states (Alabama, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Texas, New Mexico) were developing plans in 1996. Florida and California delegated planning to regional or local authorities.
- Most state plans remain "largely reactive," treating drought as an emergency response problem rather than a mitigation problem.
- The [[Colorado Drought Response Plan]] (1981, updated 1986 and 1990) is presented as a model. It has three components: monitoring by the Water Availability Task Force, impact assessment by eight impact task forces, and a response system led by designated agencies. The plan is currently being revised to give greater emphasis to mitigation.

**State mitigation tools (1986 to 1992 survey)**

- State actions are grouped into nine categories in Table 1. These include assessment programs, legislation and public policy, water supply augmentation, public awareness and education, technical assistance, demand reduction, emergency response, water use conflict resolution, and drought contingency plans.
- The [[California Drought Water Bank]] (created 1991) is cited as a successful example. It acquired water by purchasing water from farmers who chose not to irrigate, buying surplus water from local districts, and paying farmers or districts to use groundwater instead of surface water.
- The report concludes that most individual state actions were narrow and that a contingency plan facilitated the timely adoption of many mitigation actions.

**Federal drought relief and coordination**

- The Federal Government became the principal provider of drought relief during the 1930s. More than $7 billion was provided from 1974 to 1977, and nearly $5 billion in 1988.
- In 1976 to 1977, 40 separate emergency assistance programs were administered by 16 Federal agencies. The GAO judged this response inefficient and poorly coordinated.
- The report characterizes these interventions as postimpact measures that "did little, if anything, to reduce the Nation's underlying vulnerability to drought."
- Postdrought evaluations are not routinely completed. Several reviews (GAO 1979, Wilhite et al. 1986, Riebsame et al. 1991, Wilhite 1993a, OTA 1993, Wilhite and Wood 1994, FEMA 1996) reached similar conclusions. Recurring recommendations include a national drought policy, a national drought watch or monitoring system, postdrought audits, and regional drought forums.
- The report cites [[Federal Emergency Management Agency]] (FEMA) testimony that every dollar spent on mitigation saves about two dollars in future disaster costs, and calls this estimate conservative.

**Recommendations (Conclusions and New Directions)**

1. **Create a national drought policy and plan.** An interagency task force should lead it, with a lead Federal agency appointed. Its approach should be preventive and self-reliant. The report recommends using the [[Australian National Drought Policy]] as a model.
2. **Develop a National Climate Monitoring System (NCMS)** integrating precipitation, temperature, streamflow, reservoir and lake levels, groundwater, snowpack, and soil moisture. Satellite data (e.g., AVHRR) would track vegetation stress.
3. **Incorporate drought in the National Mitigation Strategy.** The 1995 strategy includes all major natural hazards except drought.
4. **Conduct postdrought audits** of Federal and state response efforts.
5. **Establish regional drought forums** to keep policies current and avoid a return to reactive management.
6. **Encourage state drought mitigation plans** through Federal financial incentives and technical assistance.
7. **Redirect most drought relief funds** toward planning, mitigation, and timely information.

**Australian comparison.** Australia's policy treats drought as a normal part of a variable climate rather than a natural disaster. Its objectives are self-reliance, protection of the resource base, and early recovery. Relief is discouraged except in exceptional circumstances, defined as droughts lasting more than 12 months with a recurrence interval of 1 in 20 years.

**Core conclusion.** Drought management in the West remains largely reactive, although states have built substantial mitigation capacity. The report concludes that "the shift from crisis management to risk management continues to be a difficult transition," and that a national policy must be developed through extensive public involvement.
