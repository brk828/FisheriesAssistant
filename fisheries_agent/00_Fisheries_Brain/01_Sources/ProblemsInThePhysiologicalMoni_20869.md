# Problems in the Physiological Monitoring of Wild Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osteichthyes

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Richard S. Wydoski]] and [[Gary A. Wedemeyer]], examines the difficulties of using physiological, mainly [[Blood Chemistry]], data to monitor wild fish populations. The authors argue that fish constantly adjust [[Osmoregulation]] and metabolism to environmental change, that sublethal stress can alter these processes, and that blood measurements could provide early warnings of environmental degradation. They identify four main obstacles: non-standardized methodology, the small [[Blood Volume]] of fish, confounding effects of nutrition, water quality, disease and biology, and the stress imposed by capture and handling. The paper closes with practical suggestions for field sampling and a call for standardized methods and baseline "normal" values for each population.

## Background & Description

Fish differ from terrestrial animals in their osmoregulatory mechanisms. Marine fish lose water to their environment and must actively retain ions, while freshwater fish gain water and must excrete it. Both situations consume energy continuously. The authors note that [[Stress]] has never been satisfactorily defined. They cite the [[General Adaptation Syndrome]] of [[Selye]] (1952) and the working definition proposed by [[Brett]] (1958): a state produced by any environmental factor that extends normal adaptive responses or significantly reduces survival chances.

Clinical blood chemistry is well established in human and veterinary medicine, but fish physiology lags behind. The paper argues that blood chemistry could identify the point at which abnormal conditions begin to impose stress, and that the decline of a species may indicate that a water body is no longer suitable for human use.

Methodology is a major barrier. Values in the literature come from different species and conditions, using varied analytical methods. The authors call for standardization, citing clinical stress assessment protocols by [[Wedemeyer]] and [[Yasutake]] and acute toxicity methods from the [[Committee on Methods for Toxicity Tests]] (1975). They also cite the six-volume [[Fish Physiology]] series edited by [[Hoar]] and [[Randall]] as evidence that knowledge exists but remains scattered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Blood volume constraints.** Mean blood volume ranges from 2.8 to 3.6% of body weight, and plasma volume from 1.8 to 2.5% (Table 1, adapted from [[Holmes and Donaldson]] 1969). Small fish may yield enough blood for only one test, or require pooling, which obscures individual variation. Table 1 lists fluid compartments for freshwater Chondrostei, Holostei and Teleostei and marine Teleostei.

**Non-destructive and microtechniques.** [[Osmometry]] measures freezing-point depression and expresses [[Osmolality]] in mOsm per kg of water. Because plasma is not consumed, it remains available for other tests. A [[Blood Lactate]] micromethod needs only 25 µl of blood (Harrower and Brown 1972). A colorimetric [[Plasma Glucose]] test (ortho-toluidine method) needs 50 µl of plasma. [[Flame Spectrophotometry]] can measure sodium and potassium in small plasma volumes. The main drawbacks are cost and the impracticality of field use for equipment such as blood gas analyzers.

**Environmental history and categories.** [[Fry]] (1971) stated that the physiological state of a fish reflects its environmental history and described five factor categories: lethal, controlling, limiting, masking and directive. Adaptation may be behavioral (moving to better habitat) or biochemical, including the formation of specific isozymes (Hochachka and Somero 1971).

**Nutrition.** In *Notopterus notopterus*, starvation raised blood glucose from day 1 onward, beginning after 24 h and rising until day 5. In [[Atlantic Cod]], starvation and refeeding (Kamra 1966) changed liver glycogen and plasma glucose markedly. Mean liver glycogen was 37 mg/100 g when satiated, fell during 5 weeks of starvation, and rose to 702 mg/100 g when feeding resumed. Plasma glucose was 108 mg/100 ml when satiated, 72–75 mg/100 ml during starvation, and 130 mg/100 ml after refeeding. Growth also shifts values: in [[Cutthroat Trout]] (Lientz and Smith 1974), hemoglobin rose from 6.4 to 7.7 g% and red blood cell count from 94 to 122 (×10⁴) as mean weight increased from 6 to 20 g over 5 months.

**Disease.** In [[Brook Trout]] experimentally infected with [[Aeromonas Salmonicida]], the agent of [[Furunculosis]], Shieh and MacLean (1976) reported significant decreases in protein (2.2 to 1.5 g/100 ml), total lipids (1104 to 768 mg/100 ml) and hemoglobin (9.11 to 6.02). Blood glucose rose significantly (55.8 to 83.9 mg/100 ml), while lactic acid rose slightly and not significantly (5.37 to 6.61 mg/100 ml). In [[Common Carp]], Field et al. (1944) observed blood sugar falling from 100 mg/100 ml to 5.8–12.3 mg/100 ml within 3 days of inoculation. The authors conclude that blood chemistry can detect pathogen-induced physiological change but cannot yet identify specific pathogens.

## Key Findings

**1. Capture method strongly affects blood chemistry and survival.**
- Common capture methods, including [[Otter Trawling]], [[Seining]], [[Hook and Line]] and [[Electrofishing]], are stressful and linked to fatigue, altered blood chemistry and mortality.
- In [[Rainbow Trout]], Bouck and Ball (1966) found 10-day delayed mortality of 5% for seining, 10% for electroshocking and 87% for hook and line. Hook-and-line fish had highly variable responses; the authors attributed angling mortality to "progressive shock" from impaired circulation and internal blood clotting.
- Horak and Klein (1967) used a [[Stamina Tunnel]] performance index (Table 3). Control trout averaged 60.0 min (95% CI 54.0–66.0), fly-caught fish 54.7 min (48.0–61.4), and electrofished fish 35.2 min (27.8–42.6), a significant difference (p < .05). In electrofished fish, 39% showed burn marks from internal bleeding and possible vertebral fracture.

**2. Lactic acidosis is a key mechanism of exhaustion death.**
- Severe exercise shifts energy supply to anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactic acid that can cause death if it cannot be oxidized.
- Parker, Black and Larkin (1959) proposed a critical blood lactic acid level of 125 mg/100 ml for troll-caught [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Coho Salmon]]. Acid-base disruption also impairs oxygen transport and carbon dioxide removal.

**3. Response speed and sensitivity vary.**
- Wydoski, Wedemeyer and Nelson (1976) found that hatchery rainbow trout plasma glucose rose significantly after 3 min of hooking, and that hatchery fish showed significant osmolality disruption after 2 min. Wild trout maintained homeostasis even after 5 min on a hook (water at 10–12 °C).
- In [[Largemouth Bass]], Gustaveson (M.S. thesis, in preparation) found blood lactate rose significantly after 2 min of playing, was higher at higher temperature, and returned to control levels within 24 h. Small bass (about 8 in) showed significantly elevated lactate after only 1 min.

**4. Goldfish handling responses are transient.**
- Chavin and Young (1970) reported that [[Goldfish]] serum glucose was elevated on days 1 and 2 (53.6 and 42.6 mg/100 ml), then stabilized at 25.8–30.7 mg/100 ml after 4 days.
- Capture method did not significantly alter serum glucose within the 4–5 min between capture and sampling. Mean values were 28.5 mg/100 ml (net), 20.9 (ice), 25.6 (electrofishing) and 35.6 (MS-222), with no significant differences from net capture.

**5. Handling effects persist and vary by species.**
- Wedemeyer (1970) gently netted juvenile steelhead and coho salmon under optimal conditions. Both species showed disturbed plasma glucose, chloride, calcium and cholesterol for about 24 h. Cholesterol rose in coho but not steelhead, which the authors linked to coho's tendency to refuse food after handling.
- Adding NaCl (about 100 mOsm, 0.3%) and calcium (75 or 120 ppm) partly or fully alleviated hyperglycemia and hypochloremia, suggesting that salt can reduce handling stress.
- [[Muskellunge]] adapt poorly to handling or captivity (Miles et al. 1974). Seine capture gave the best approximation of normal values, and 0.3% NaCl reduced stress.
- In [[European Plaice]], Wardle (1972) found blood glucose elevated for about 8 h after capture, returning to normal within 2–3 days, after which fish no longer responded to stimuli that raise glucose in newly captured wild fish.

**6. Stocking method influences survival and physiology.**
- Brook trout planted by air drop had significantly lower recovery than land-planted groups (Fraser 1968). Fraser and Beamish (1969) found blood lactic acid rose after air drop and rose further with crowding, although levels were below initial fatigue thresholds in other species. Sublethal disturbance may still contribute to delayed mortality.

**7. Passive gear produces unreliable baselines.**
- Gill nets, trammel nets and trap nets hold fish for variable periods, making them unsuitable for physiological sampling. Wide ranges in [[White Sucker]] blood sugar (Mackay and Beatty 1968) likely reflected confinement time, and gill-net fish had higher blood sugar than trap-net fish.
- [[Toxicants]] are also unsuitable: they cause vasoconstriction in the gill capillaries or inhibit respiration, creating hypoxia that invalidates "normal" values.

**8. Recommended field practices.**
- Rapid active capture, such as small seines, dip nets, electrofishing and angling, is preferred. Angled fish landed within about 15 s may give near-normal baseline values (Wydoski et al. 1976).
- Repeated seining in the same area can harass fish and distort values. Electroshocking produces stress lasting several hours (Schreck et al. 1976). Glucose may approximate normal values immediately after capture, whereas lactate and corticoids may not.
- Fish held for experiments should be acclimated, and their physiology monitored from capture until normality is reached.
- Each population should have its own "normal" values, since temperature, photoperiod, season, sex, size, age, disease, nutrition, ionic composition, dissolved gases and pollutants all influence results.

**9. Overall conclusion.**
- All capture and handling methods are stressful, but better baselines are emerging. Blood glucose has been proposed as a sensitive indicator of environmental stress (Silbergeld 1974), with the caveat that normal values must be known for each species. The authors conclude that standardization and filling knowledge gaps are vital before blood data from different field methods can be interpreted reliably.
