# Development of Methods of Population Estimation and Habitat Evaluation for Management of the Arizona and Gila Trouts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by John N. Finne (Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Arizona State University) develops and tests methods for estimating populations and evaluating habitat of the threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and the endangered [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*). Three electrofishing passes over 100 m blocked stream sections captured most fish, and population estimates from three passes were accurate to within ±10% error in the study streams. Physical-hydromorphological habitat factors explained roughly half of the variation in Gila Trout numbers in McKnight Creek, and fish size was closely tied to water depth. The author concludes that Gila Trout use limited stream space efficiently, that small headwater streams could support introduced populations, and that a quality sport fishery would require larger downstream waters.

## Background & Description

- **Apache Trout (*Salmo apache*)**: First recorded in the White Mountains of Arizona (Cope and Yarrow 1875). Its range is greatly restricted to headwater streams of the Black, White and Little Colorado rivers on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest. It has been introduced into streams on several other Arizona national forests in the past decade.
- **Gila Trout (*Salmo gilae*)**: Apparently once widespread in upper-elevation streams of the Gila River basin. Its type locality is Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico, and most prior information came from a stunted population there ([[Regan]] 1966; [[Hanson]] 1971).
- **Causes of decline**: Hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), predation and competition from introduced [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*).
- **Status**: The U.S. Department of the Interior (1977) listed the Apache Trout as threatened and the Gila Trout as endangered.
- **Study areas**:
  - **Ord Creek** (Arizona): headwaters in the White Mountains, about 3,000 to 3,170 m elevation. Flows range from 0.03 m³/s in the upper reaches to about 0.28 m³/s at the lower end of Smith Cienega. A U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service chemical renovation was carried out in summer 1977 to remove non-native trout.
  - **Mamie Creek** (Arizona): 2.5 km reach at 2,500 to 2,640 m on Escudilla Mountain. Apache Trout were introduced in 1968. Flow is less than 0.03 m³/s.
  - **McKnight Creek** (New Mexico): about 3 km of stream at 2,200 to 2,300 m, draining the Black Range toward the Mimbres River, in the Gila National Forest. Flow is 0.03 m³/s or less. The stream was chemically renovated in 1970, stocked with 300 Gila Trout from Main Diamond Creek, and the population flourished after a second introduction in 1972.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mark-recapture limits**: Finclips, dyes and external tags can cause mortality or stress in rare fishes, so methods must be feasible, reliable and repeatable.
- **Prior estimate**: [[Mello and Turner]] (1978) estimated about 7,600 Gila Trout in the Gila National Forest using a single electrofishing pass over 200-foot sections. The author judged this too low to be credible without a defined error term.
- **Habitat-data gap**: Habitat knowledge for both species was limited, and extrapolation from other salmonid studies was common. Most physical factors were better predictors of suitable habitat than chemical or biological factors, which varied widely and were not well correlated with trout numbers. The small size of these streams limited comparisons with larger study waters.
- **Comparative literature**: The study relied on [[Allen]] (1969) for salmonid standing crop densities, [[Lewis]] (1969) and [[Kraft]] (1968) for habitat-abundance relationships in larger streams, and [[Larimore]] (1961) and [[Sullivan]] (1956) for electrofishing capture efficiency and size-grouping effects.

## Key Findings

**Population estimation**
- In McKnight Creek, the first electrofishing pass captured 25% to 100% of Gila Trout in riffles and 33% to 100% in pools. By the second pass, 93% to 95% of fish had been captured. The author found that three passes gave reliable estimates within ±10% error.
- Ord Creek was renovated with rotenone-type treatment (antimycin-A) in August 1977, which provided a known total population to test estimates against.
- Population estimates of Apache Trout, Gila Trout and brown and brook trout averaged about 37% error, with a range of roughly 8% to 132%. Estimates of fry were less accurate than those for adults. Fry were underestimated 7 of 9 times in the Ord Creek trials.
- Estimates were more accurate when multiple estimator sections were used within a reach. Single 100 m sections often gave large errors, and habitat complexity strongly influenced accuracy.
- Rarity of a species affected accuracy. Apache Trout made up less than 3% of the fish in Ord Creek, yet about 65% of roughly 200 individuals were in reaches III and IV, where none were captured in the estimator sections.

**Habitat evaluation**
- Six physical-hydromorphological factors explained nearly half the variation in the Gila Trout resource in McKnight Creek. Explained variation was 27% to 41% for the fish resource overall.
- In riffles, volume of water explained 15% to 30% of the variation in numbers, biomass and maximum size. Percent cover was the most important factor for mean fish size in riffles.
- In pools, volume was the strongest predictor of numbers and biomass (about 41% and 39% explained). Maximum depth explained more variation in fish size than other factors.
- Fish size was significantly related to maximum depth and, to a lesser extent, cover. Numbers were spatially controlled by available space.
- Mean fish biomass was significantly greater in pools than in riffles because fish were larger in pools, although mean numbers were similar.
- Pool:riffle ratio was not a significant predictor of Gila Trout numbers. Meander factor (r = 0.71) correlated better with fish populations than pool:riffle ratio (r = 0.12). Gradient was also closely correlated (r = 0.71) with Gila Trout numbers.

**Density and space use**
- Gila Trout in McKnight Creek averaged about 3.24 m² of space per fish, compared with 13.9 m² for salmonids in [[Allen]]'s (1969) survey. Gila Trout were therefore considered efficient users of space.
- Recalculated salmonid densities from Allen's data averaged 0.09 fish/m² of streambed, roughly one-fifth of the density calculated for Gila Trout in McKnight Creek pools and riffles.
- Of 510 fish measured in McKnight Creek, 12 (about 2%) exceeded 200 mm total length. The maximum recorded length was 312 mm. Only 20% of pools had maximum depths greater than 0.5 m.

**Management implications**
- Expansion of the restricted range is needed for delisting. Interstream comparisons using gradient, meander factor and water temperature could identify suitable introduction streams.
- The Gila Trout is capable of surviving harsh conditions and could be introduced into small headwater streams of the Gila River to maintain populations.
- A viable sport fishery would require larger downstream waters, most of which already support introduced salmonid sport fisheries. Such habitat would have to be made available before a quality wild trout fishery could be achieved after delisting.
