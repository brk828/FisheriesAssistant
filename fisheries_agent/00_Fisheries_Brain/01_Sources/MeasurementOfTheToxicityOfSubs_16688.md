# Measurement of the Toxicity of Substances to Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 paper by D. W. M. Herbert (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, Watford) was presented to the Midland Branch of the Institute of Sewage Purification. It reviews how toxicity of industrial wastes to fish is measured and interpreted, and argues that existing methods (the "lethal limit", Powers' reciprocal method, and the American 24- and 48-hour median tolerance limits) can mislead when used to judge safe concentrations in rivers. Using a continuous-flow apparatus and [[Rainbow Trout]] exposed to [[Cyanide]], the author shows that survival time is inversely proportional to a power of concentration, that individual survival times are log-normally distributed, and that no threshold concentration is implied by the data.

## Background & Description

- Published toxicity data are hard to compare because test conditions differ in duration, species, age, water hardness, temperature, and the presence of other waste components ([[Doudoroff And Katz]], 1950).
- Typical tests place 2–10 fish in a fixed volume of poisoned water and record time to death, overturning, or another symptom.
- Fixed-volume tests have drawbacks: fish bind or remove toxicants (e.g. [[Mucus Binding Of Toxicants]] with lead nitrate and colloidal silver), consume oxygen, and release carbon dioxide and wastes.
- The [[Continuous-Flow Apparatus]] used at the laboratory tests 50 yearling trout together. Water is aerated, pumped to a constant-level tank, and flows at about 2 litres per minute through an orifice plate to a mixing device where poison is dosed at a constant rate. It then passes a delay tank and a temperature-controlled test tank. An earlier version by Erichsen-Jones (1938) held two minnows.
- The apparatus is considered too large, costly and maintenance-heavy for routine testing; routine tests will probably remain fixed-volume, with fish transferred to fresh solution at intervals and gentle oxygenation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Stream pollution control requires knowing the effect of toxic industrial effluents on aquatic animals. Fish in a continuously polluted stream have little effect on water chemistry, unlike in fixed-volume tests.
- Variables that modify toxicity include dissolved oxygen, temperature, CO2, pH, and water chemistry:
  - Low but non-lethal oxygen shortens survival time in metallic salts and acids that damage gills (Ellis 1937).
  - Copper sulphate toxicity rises with sulphuric acid and falls with calcium chloride.
  - [[Ammonia Toxicity]]: undissociated ammonia is more toxic than ammonium ions, and its proportion rises with pH; Ellis concluded toxicity of ammonium salts increases by 200 per cent or more between pH 7.4 and 8.0.
- Prior environment affects resistance: the temperature difference between holding and test water matters (Sumner and Wells 1935); several weeks of [[Thermal Acclimatization]] to test temperature may be needed. Effects of diet, stocking density, aquarium water chemistry and season are poorly known.
- True thresholds probably exist for some substances (e.g. trace heavy metals needed by organisms), but would require far longer experiments to determine.

## Key Findings

- **Misleading "lethal limit" terms:** the lowest concentration killing fish within a fixed test period is only the highest concentration without deaths by that time, not a harmless level.
- **[[Powers Reciprocal Method]]:** plotting 1/survival time against concentration gives a sigmoid curve; the straight central portion extrapolated to the axis gave a "theoretical threshold" of about 0.14 ppm CN for rainbow trout, yet fish die at lower concentrations. Such threshold claims should be used with great caution.
- **[[Median Tolerance Limit]] (US method):** 24- and 48-hour concentrations at which 50 per cent survive, interpolated from percentage survivors against log concentration (ignoring 0 and 100 per cent points). It gives no way to assess lower concentrations.
- **Power law:** with 40–50 fish per concentration at 17.5°C, survival time is inversely proportional to a power of concentration: n log C + log T = log k (C^n T = k). Between 0.25 and 0.07 ppm cyanide the power is 4.64, with no departure suspected below 0.25 ppm; only the three higher concentrations deviate significantly from the fitted line. The relation was also found in most of Powers' goldfish data, and has been used for disinfectants (Chick 1908) and drugs in mice (Perry 1950).
- **No threshold implied:** if the law holds, fish eventually die at any concentration, though at very low levels cyanide deaths would be a small fraction of deaths from all causes. Extrapolation gives 0.023 ppm CN for a median survival time of 5 years, of uncertain validity.
- **Individual variation:** in one test, 42 yearling rainbow trout in 0.14 ppm CN at 17.5°C first overturned at 29 minutes and last at 207 minutes. Differences are mainly inherent (Herbert and Merkens, in preparation): fish resisting longer in one test tend to do so again on retest.
- **[[Log-Normal Distribution]]:** survival times are skewed, but log survival times are approximately normal, with departures from the best-fitting normal curve not significant. Median survival time is best estimated by the geometric mean (antilog of the mean of log T).
- **Sampling precision:** with three trout in 0.14 ppm CN at 17.5°C, there are 19 chances in 20 the true median lies within about -56 to +130 per cent of the estimate; with 50 fish, within -12 to +14 per cent.
- **Average toxicity** (mean of 100/CT) is a less suitable measure because log-normal fits cyanide data better than reciprocal-normal; log-normality is the safer default when data are limited.
- **Spread of survival times:** the spread of log times does not vary systematically with concentration in the A-A' range, so with Equation 1 the survival times of 25 and 75 per cent of fish could be predicted reasonably well (Fig. 5), though with considerable error and several assumptions.
- **Recommendation:** standardized tests need controlled temperature, pH, CO2 and oxygen, but not enough is known to set limits for these; further research is part of the laboratory programme, funded through the Water Pollution Research Board of the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research.
