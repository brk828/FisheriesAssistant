# Recovery Plan for the Humpback Chub (Gila cypha)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and approved by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], supersedes the original 1978 recovery plan for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The plan synthesizes research conducted since 1978 and establishes comprehensive recovery objectives. The primary goal is to restore and maintain five self-sustaining populations plus two refugia before considering downlisting from endangered status. The humpback chub is a medium-sized (less than 500 mm TL) freshwater minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], characterized by a distinctive dorsal hump, flattened head, and ventral mouth—adaptations believed to aid survival in turbulent whitewater environments. Historic distribution included the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and four major tributaries ([[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], [[Little Colorado River]]). Current populations are severely reduced and fragmented, with major concentrations in [[Black Rocks Canyon]], [[Westwater Canyon]], and the [[Little Colorado River]]. Primary threats include habitat loss from dam construction (particularly [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]]), altered flow regimes, cold tailwaters, competition and predation from introduced nonnative species, parasitism (particularly *Lernaea* anchorworm), and potential hybridization with related *Gila* species.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] was first described by Miller in 1946 based on specimens collected in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The earliest written record dates to 1914, though the fish was originally misidentified as "bonytail" by Kolb and Kolb (1914). Archaeological evidence from Indian ruins near [[Hoover Dam]] site suggests the species existed in the lower basin as early as 4000 B.C. (Euler 1978). The species remained largely unknown until post-World War II, when surplus military rafts made whitewater canyon exploration feasible for scientists.

**Morphological Characteristics:**
- Medium-sized cyprinid, less than 500 mm total length
- Silvery sides with brown or olivaceous back
- Pronounced dorsal hump arising over opercula, rounding to dorsal fin origin
- Narrow, flattened head, concave on top
- Long, fleshy snout with inferior-subterminal mouth
- Small eyes
- Body tapers abruptly to deeply forked caudal fin
- Dorsal rays: 8-10 (typically 9); anal rays: 9-11 (typically 10)
- Loss of squamation on nuchal hump
- Caudal peduncle depth intermediate between [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*, slender) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*, deep)

The compressed body and prominent hump are believed to be adaptations for turbulent water: the hump pushes the fish toward the bottom where currents are quieter, the ventral mouth facilitates feeding without water distension, and grooves associated with the hump may irrigate compressed gills (Minckley 1973). However, the species also occupies deep pool and boulder habitats, suggesting a more versatile lifestyle than initially assumed.

**Taxonomic Challenges:**
Identification of larval *Gila* spp. and even larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is difficult. Specimens collected since the 1950s have shown intermediate morphology between humpback, bonytail, and roundtail chubs. Suttkus and Clemmer (1977) interpreted high variation in nuchal hump development and fin-ray counts as within-species variation rather than evidence of hybridization. However, Kaeding and Zimmerman (1983) found eight combinations of dorsal/anal fin-ray counts with different frequencies between [[Colorado River]] mainstem and [[Little Colorado River]] populations, speculating some interbreeding with bonytail chubs had occurred. Smith et al. (1979) found most specimens classifiable into distinct species with few intermediates, suggesting humpback and bonytail evolved from roundtail chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic Distribution:**
The humpback chub was endemic to large whitewater canyons of the [[Colorado River Basin]], including:
- Mainstem [[Colorado River]]
- [[Green River]]
- [[Yampa River]]
- [[White River]]
- [[Little Colorado River]]

The species is part of a native fish fauna traced to the mid-Pliocene in the fossil record (Miller 1959).

**Present Distribution (as of early 1980s):**
Recent collections indicate highly fragmented, sporadic distribution:
- [[Green River]] in [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Gray Canyon]], [[Labyrinth Canyon]], and [[Dinosaur National Monument]]
- [[Yampa River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]
- [[Colorado River]] in [[Black Rocks Canyon]] and [[Debeque Canyon]], Colorado
- [[Colorado River]] in [[Westwater Canyon]] near Moab, Utah
- [[Colorado River]] in [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]] (km 25.8 to 312.8)
- [[Little Colorado River]] from mouth to 13 km upstream

**Major Population Changes:**
- Eliminated from [[Green River]] above [[Yampa River]] mouth when [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] became operational in 1962
- Reported from [[Lake Powell]] after dam closure in 1960s but not collected recently
- Major reductions in [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]] due to cold tailwaters from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]
- Population may persist in [[Grand Canyon]] through spawning in [[Little Colorado River]]

**Habitat Preferences:**
Generally associated with fast current and/or deep channels, though shallow, slower areas within canyons also used.

Upper [[Colorado River]] (Valdez 1981; Valdez and Clemmer 1982; Valdez et al. 1982):
- Deep, swift riverine areas with large boulders and steep cliffs
- Average depth: 15.2 m (maximum 28 m)
- Adults (>260 mm): water <9.1 m deep, over silt/sand/boulder/bedrock, velocity usually <30 cm/sec
- Age 2-3 years: 6.1-9 m depth
- Age 1 year: <3 m depth
- Young-of-year: <3 m depth, <30 cm/sec velocity, silt bottom

Lower [[Colorado River]] Basin (Minckley 1973; Minckley et al. 1981):
- Eddies adjacent to fast currents and backwaters
- Sand substrate, 2-5 m depth
- [[Little Colorado River]]: pools adjacent to eddies, large pools with little current, areas below travertine (CaCO₃) dams

**Reproduction:**
- Spawning likely occurs June-July in [[Grand Canyon]] (Suttkus and Clemmer 1977)
- [[Little Colorado River]] identified as major spawning area for [[Grand Canyon]] population
- Reproductive adults collected March-April 1977 at 16-20°C water temperature
- Ripe fish collected in [[Black Rocks]] area, June 1980; three females stripped of ~18,000 eggs total
- Sexual maturity reached at 250-300 mm length
- Gonadosomic index suggests humpback chub does not successfully reproduce in mainstem [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]; [[Grand Canyon]] population maintained by recruitment from [[Little Colorado River]] (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983)

**Food Habits:**
- Bottom feeders, as indicated by subterminal mouth (Miller 1946)
- Young-of-year in [[Little Colorado River]]: bottom feeding confirmed by stomach analysis and visual observation
- Adults forage by inspecting and taking material from *Cladophora glomerata* algae, bottom, mid-water, and surface
- Young-of-year (50 mm) likely forage on epipelagic and epilithic diatoms plus small invertebrates
- Below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]: fed primarily on planktonic crustaceans originating from [[Lake Powell]] (Minckley 1973)
- Kaeding and Zimmerman (1983): stomachs dominated by immature Chironomidae and Simuliidae; *Gammarus* sp. abundant in river but uncommon in stomachs

**Threats to Survival:**

*Habitat Alteration:*
- [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and [[Hoover Dam]] eliminated significant habitat through impoundments and cold tailwaters
- Reduced flows below dams altered river hydraulics, reducing or significantly altering habitat
- Man-dominated flows in [[Grand Canyon]] are colder and fluctuate more than historic conditions; future "questionable" (Suttkus and Clemmer 1977)

*Competition and Predation:*
- Number of fish species in [[Colorado River Basin]] has tripled due to introductions
- Introduced species are "good invaders and competitors" with high productivity, generalized life cycles, and wide physiological tolerance (Molles 1980)
- Channel catfish predation on humpback chub documented in [[Grand Canyon]] (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983)
- Molles (1980) compared native fauna to insular island fauna: high endemism suggests long isolation period during which natives lost ability to compete with introduced species
- Dam fragmentation isolates subpopulations, restricts gene flow, reduces adaptive capacity

*Parasitism:*
- Introduced parasitic crustacean *Lernaea* (anchorworm) infests 50% of humpback chubs in [[Little Colorado River]] (Carothers et al. 1981; Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983)
- *Lernaea* infestation noted throughout upper [[Colorado Basin]]
- Deacon and Bradley (1972) reported increased parasitism in [[Roundtail Chub]] after fish introductions in [[Moapa River]], Nevada, suggesting parasitism as decline factor

*Pollution:*
- Pesticides and other pollutants may depress populations (Haynes and Muth 1982)
- Over 16% of *Gila robusta* young-of-year from [[Yampa River]] and [[Colorado River]] in 1981 showed spinal deformities (lordosis)

*Hybridization:*
- Suspected hybrids reported from some locations, though extent unknown
- High morphologic variation makes identification difficult
- Smith et al. (1979) concluded *G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, and *G. elegans* coexist as reproductively isolated species in main channels but admitted isolating mechanisms may break down under disturbed (reservoir) conditions
- Valdez and Clemmer (1982) hypothesized that reduced flows and increased temperatures caused overlapping spawning periods between humpback and roundtail chubs, altering genetic isolation mechanisms
- Hybridization typically traced to disturbed, stressful, or unnatural conditions forcing ecological coexistence

## Key Findings

**Recovery Objective:**
Restore and maintain the humpback chub in non-endangered status. **Downlisting criteria:** minimum of five self-sustaining populations located and two refugia established, including but not limited to:
1. [[Black Rocks Canyon]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] of [[Colorado River]]
2. [[Little Colorado River]]
3. [[Green River]] in [[Gray Canyon]]
4. [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]

**Delisting criteria:** Five self-sustaining populations and two refugia maintained with habitats (flow characteristics, water quality parameters) legally protected.

**Recovery Strategy (Stepdown Outline):**

*1. Gather field data on life history and status:*
- Expand life history knowledge: spawning requirements, feeding behavior, age/growth rates
- Study population dynamics: productivity, recruitment, longevity, mortality rates, competition
- Compile historical population data
- Identify and monitor populations using improved identification techniques (electrophoresis, cytogenetics)
- Develop and implement standardized monitoring procedures
- Conduct field investigations to search for remnant populations

*2. Develop and implement habitat management plan:*
- Determine essential habitat through intensive field investigations
- Determine biological, chemical, and physical requirements for all life stages
- Develop criteria to identify suitable habitat
- Monitor and assess impacts of development projects
- Enforce laws and regulations (Endangered Species Act, Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act, Lacey Act)
- Assure Section 7 ESA compliance by Federal agencies
- Assess effectiveness of current regulations/management
- Identify and assess impacts of introduced nonnative species
- Study nature and extent of parasitism
- Determine significance of *Gila* spp. hybridization
- Analyze and modify management practices as needed

*3. Maintain or establish populations in five stable habitats plus two refugia:*
- Establish hatchery facilities (minimum 5 years production)
- Develop propagation and holding techniques
- Select/construct facilities ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] plus [[Page Springs Hatchery]] as refugia; feasibility study for new hatchery)
- Obtain broodstock
- Explore experimental population designation or downlisting
- Conduct reintroduction program: inventory sites, restore/prepare sites, develop stocking/monitoring plan

*4. Maintain intensive information and education program:*
- Conduct local and national I&E programs
- Provide workshops for public agencies
- Develop informational leaflets
- Publish in Endangered Species Bulletin
- Contact environmental groups and media

*5. Determine when non-endangered status achieved:*
- Define viable, self-sustaining population relative to humpback chub
- Establish quantifiable goals for downlisting and delisting
- Review recovery effort annually

**Current Population Status:**
- Upper mainstem [[Colorado River]] may contain highest concentration, particularly [[Black Rocks]]-[[Westwater]] areas
- [[Little Colorado River]] contains humpback chub and may be major reproduction area for [[Grand Canyon]]
- Rare in [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] of [[Dinosaur National Monument]]
- Possible decline in [[Desolation Canyon]]-[[Gray Canyon]] areas of [[Green River]]: extensive 1979-81 sampling collected few adults despite previous reports of "relatively common"
- 7,600 Age Class I marked humpback chub stocked in [[Cataract Canyon]], December 1981 (all marked for future identification)

**Research Priorities:**
- **Priority 1:** Actions absolutely essential to prevent extinction
- **Priority 2:** Actions necessary to maintain current population status
- **Priority 3:** All other actions necessary for full recovery

**Implementation Schedule:**
Detailed fiscal year costs and responsible agencies identified for each task. Key agencies include: [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Fishery Resources, Endangered Species, Law Enforcement divisions), [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[National Park Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

**Critical Knowledge Gaps:**
- Specific spawning habitat requirements and locations
- Population dynamics parameters (productivity, recruitment, mortality)
- Extent and significance of hybridization
- Role of parasitism in population decline
- Impact of introduced nonnative species
- Definition of viable, self-sustaining population
- Quantifiable recovery goals

**Management Recommendations:**
- Protect existing populations in [[Black Rocks]], [[Westwater Canyon]], and [[Little Colorado River]]
- Establish hatchery propagation program with two refugia
- Conduct intensive monitoring using standardized procedures
- Restore habitat or modify management practices to address cold tailwaters and altered flows
- Control or assess impacts of nonnative species
- Investigate and address parasitism
- Ensure Section 7 ESA consultation for all Federal projects
- Consider experimental population designation to facilitate reintroductions

The recovery plan emphasizes that additional information is needed before specific quantifiable recovery goals can be established. Interim objectives focus on obtaining and analyzing population data to define viable populations and establish measurable criteria for downlisting and delisting. Annual meetings will review recovery progress, with each State agency preparing reports of their efforts.
