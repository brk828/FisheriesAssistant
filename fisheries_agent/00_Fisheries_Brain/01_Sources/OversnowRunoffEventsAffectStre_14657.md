# Oversnow Flow: Snowmelt Runoff and Sediment Transport at Sane Creek, Wyoming

#hydrology #snow-hydrology #sediment-transport

## Executive Summary

This paper describes "[[Oversnow Flow]]," a previously undescribed hydrologic process in which snowmelt water runs across the surface of a wind-compacted snowpack and, in extreme cases, erodes a well-defined channel into it. The observations come from [[Sane Creek]], a 587-acre (238-ha) watershed at the [[Stratton Sagebrush Hydrology Study Site]] in southcentral Wyoming. Over 7 years of streamflow measurement, oversnow flow occurred in 2 years (1970 and 1973). In those years, peak flows were 3 to 5 times higher, snowmelt runoff was a much larger share of winter precipitation, and suspended and bedload sediment transport rose sharply. Much of the eroded sediment was retained in the snowpack rather than reaching the watershed outlet.

The document contains no fish, invertebrate or other biological survey data. Its subject is watershed hydrology and sediment dynamics in an intermittent drainage.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The study area lies between 7,500 and 7,800 ft (2,286 to 2,438 m) on a moderately dissected plateau on the northeastern flank of the [[Sierra Madre Mountains]], about 20 miles (32 km) west of Saratoga, Wyoming. Annual precipitation is about 21 inches (530 mm), and about one-third falls from November through March. Winds average about 15 mph (24 km/h) in November, February and March, and 20 mph (32 km/h) in December and January. Wind redistributes snow into small drifts behind sagebrush on windward slopes and into large drifts in depressions. Pre-melt snow depth ranges from a few inches on windward slopes to 20 ft (6.1 m) or more in drainages.

**Watershed.** Sane Creek drains a southeast-to-southwest exposure. A spring 150 ft (45.7 m) above the stream gage sustains perennial flow, while an upslope channel carries only surface flow during snowmelt. Soils formed in place from Brown's Park Formation sandstone, with loam or sandy loam A and B horizons. Infiltration and soil stability are described as good. Vegetation is [[Mountain Big Sagebrush]] on moist sites and [[Wyoming Big Sagebrush]] on upland areas, where wind removal of snow reduces effective precipitation.

**Methods.** A pumping sampler (PS-67) was installed at the gage in spring 1973. Two-pint (0.4 L) samples were collected daily during snowmelt. Sediment was isolated by membrane filtration (45 µm pores). Daily melt was estimated with a [[Snowmelt Meter]], a 2-ft (61 cm) square lysimeter containing a sagebrush plant, located on a north-facing slope near Sane Creek. Bedload was measured as accumulation in stilling basins at Sane Creek and at the adjacent [[Loco Creek]] watershed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conditions that favor oversnow flow.** The process appears limited to regions with substantial snowfall, wind relocation and compaction, and intermittent channels. The author suggests it could occur on plains, shrublands and alpine areas.
- **Snowpack structure.** Perennial drainages have a cavity under the snow that carries water. Intermittent channels lack this cavity, and lateral water movement through dense, wind-packed snow is slow. [[Depth Hoar]] at the base of the pack can form a low-resistance conduit, but its thickness varies from year to year.
- **Energy and melt.** Treeless terrain and a southerly exposure increase energy input to the snowpack. Sagebrush protruding through the snow lowers surface albedo and re-radiates short-wave energy as long-wave radiation, which accelerates melt.
- **Soil conditions.** A frozen, impervious soil surface in 1970 increased runoff because little meltwater infiltrated.
- **Sediment fate.** Sediment is trapped in the snow along the [[Incised Channel]] and later deposited on the ground as snow melts, where it may be stabilized by vegetation or moved by a subsequent runoff event.

## Key Findings

### Streamflow and Peak Flow
- Oversnow runoff occurred at Sane Creek in 1970 and 1973. The 1971 hydrograph, with no oversnow flow, served as the comparison.
- Maximum daily flow was 8.9 csm in 1970 and 4.7 csm in 1973. Years without oversnow flow stayed below 2 csm.
- Maximum instantaneous flow was 11.5 csm in 1970, the highest rate ever measured at the Stratton site, and 5.3 csm in 1973.
- [[Peak Flow]] rates were roughly 3 to 5 times higher in oversnow years (Conclusions).

### Water Yield
- Snowmelt runoff was about 5% of winter precipitation in years without oversnow flow and about 20% in years with it (Results).
- The Conclusions state that snowmelt runoff as a percent of winter precipitation was four times as large. The Results section describes the proportion of annual flow from snowmelt as double. These two figures are not reconciled in the source.
- [[Water Yield Efficiency]] was higher in oversnow years, as shown in Table 1 (1968–1974).

### Suspended Sediment
- Maximum suspended sediment reached 862 ppm in 1973 and exceeded 50 ppm for 5 consecutive days.
- By May 24, 1973, concentrations were below 20 ppm, even though flow stayed above 1 csm until June 2.
- In 1974, a year without oversnow flow, concentrations stayed below 20 ppm even at a maximum flow of about 2 csm.
- Bank erosion along the snow channel was a major source of sediment, with some upland contribution.

### Sediment Retention in Snow
- On May 16, 1973, sediment at the incised channel was 1,979 and 3,652 ppm, while the stream gage read 38 and 29 ppm.
- Sediment deposited on the channel side (May 17, 1973) was 13.5 tons/acre in the top 0.75 inch at Location A and 5.2 tons/acre in the top 2 inches at Location B.
- The mechanism of deposition is attributed mainly to physical straining as water percolates into the snowpack. Electrostatic attraction may also contribute.

### Bedload Deposition
- Annual bedload accumulation at Sane Creek ranged from 8 to 179 ft³ per square mile (Table 3, 1970–1974).
- Sane Creek deposition in 1970 and 1973 was 12 and 6 times the average rate of other years.
- Loco Creek, which has a spring about 1 mile (1.6 km) above its gage, recorded higher bedload every year, ranging from 121 to 355 ft³ per square mile.
- The 1970 accumulation at both weirs was the highest ever recorded. The 1973 accumulation at Loco Creek was the second highest.

### Snowmelt Rates (1973)
- Daily melt exceeded 1 inch (2.5 cm) on 9 days between May 8 and 18.
- Melt exceeded 2.3 inches (5.8 cm) per day on May 17 and 18, when sagebrush protruded above the snow.
- On May 14, 1973, total melt was 1.13 inches. The peak 2-hour intensity was 0.20 inch/hr (0.5 cm/hr) from 2 to 4 p.m.

### Conclusions and Management Implications
- Oversnow flow develops when meltwater supply at the start of melt exceeds the transport capacity of the drainage. A high early melt rate is associated with rapid conduit formation under the snow, and a low early melt rate favors a surface channel.
- The absence of an adequate subnivean cavity is identified as the main trigger. Thick depth hoar and frozen soil increase the water reaching the channel.
- Sediment removed from transport and retained in snow means that snowpack sediment storage can reduce downstream sediment yield.
