# Gila Topminnow Conservation and Mine-Related Threats in Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #topminnow

## Executive Summary

This newspaper article, reproduced from a scanned page with substantial OCR damage, reports on the [[Gila Topminnow]], a small endangered fish of southern Arizona. The main issue is a dispute over whether operations at the [[Silver Bell Mine]] and a new [[Tailings Pond]] threaten the species' remaining habitat at [[Cocio Wash]]. Federal, state and mine interests disagree about the adequacy of the review. The article also describes the species' disappearance from much of its former range, its uncertain status where [[Largemouth Bass]] have been introduced, captive propagation and a reintroduction effort, and a broader concern that the [[Snail Darter]] controversy makes agencies reluctant to restock endangered fish. ## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Gila topminnow is a small fish described in the article as dwarfed in size. The article gives the female's average length as roughly 1¾ to two inches. No scientific name, family or detailed morphology is given in the surviving text.
- **Former status:** Biologists cited in the article say the topminnow was the most numerous fish in Arizona in the 1950s.
- **Current status:** The species is listed as endangered. The article notes that its disappearance from much of its historic range, including parts of the [[Gila River Basin]], remains unexplained. Jerry Burton, a [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] endangered-species biologist, is quoted on the issue.
- **Historic stocking:** The article reports that game fish were stocked in large numbers (described as millions) in an irrigation canal in southeastern Arizona over the past two decades. Burton said the topminnow "should have thrived," but it did not, and the cause is unclear. The OCR text is partly corrupted on this point.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Known locations.** Topminnows have been found at several sites:
- Cocio Wash, the habitat at the centre of the dispute, near the U.S.–Mexican border in [[Santa Cruz County]]
- Other sites named in the article: Monkey Spring, Sonoita Creek, Cottonwood Springs, Sheehy Springs, Atascosa Canyon and the [[Santa Cruz River]]
- San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, near Bylas in Gila County
- Boyce Thompson Arboretum near Superior, Pinal County
- [[Cienega Creek]], southeastern Pima County

**Predation.** At several of these sites, the status of the population is uncertain because [[Largemouth Bass]], a predatory species, has been introduced to the same waters.

**Mine-related threat at Cocio Wash.** The article describes the following:
- The [[Silver Bell Mine]] operates near Cocio Wash.
- Older tailings ponds No. 1 and No. 2 were already full and sat above the habitat.
- A new tailings pond, No. 3, was built below them. The company said it would eliminate future runoff from the older ponds.
- Spills from the older ponds were a concern. A state game-and-fish biologist, Brooks, said it was "not at all reasonable" to assume spills would not recur during a heavy rainstorm.
- The article quotes the mine spokesman as saying the company did not believe it was threatening the fish, and that the mine's operations were not affecting the topminnow.
- The article reports that the new pond might take 20 years to fill, which would make it an effective deterrent only for that period.

**Regulatory conflict.** The [[Bureau Of Land Management]] approved the third pond without obtaining the federal fish-and-wildlife regional office's review, and the approval has caused conflict between the bureau and the state and federal wildlife agencies. Brooks said the topminnow "should have been given more consideration," and that the bureau faces a choice between letting conditions continue and requiring the mine to act.

## Key Findings

1. **Mine threat is disputed.** The mine says its operations do not affect the topminnow. State and federal fish biologists disagree, arguing that the approval did not adequately consider the species, and that spill risk remains during storms.
2. **Regulatory process was contested.** The BLM approved the new tailings pond without the federal fish-and-wildlife regional office's input, creating friction between the agencies.
3. **Population status is uncertain.** Topminnows persist at several Arizona sites, but largemouth bass introductions make their survival at those sites uncertain.
4. **Causes of decline are unresolved.** Despite stocking efforts in the past two decades, the species' disappearance from parts of its range, including the Gila River basin, has not been explained.
5. **Captive propagation is ongoing.** The [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and [[Arizona State University]] maintain the species in artificial propagation programmes. Several hundred thousand fish are kept at the [[Dexter Hatchery]] in New Mexico.
6. **Reintroduction is planned.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the BLM are involved in a joint project to repopulate the species. The main obstacle is finding suitable stocking sites, since a site must have minimal competition and predation. Private waterholes may be the best option.
7. **Policy impediment.** Burton argues that the [[Snail Darter]] case, which delayed the [[Tellico Dam]] project in Tennessee, makes agencies reluctant to reintroduce endangered species where they are not currently found. He describes it as a paradox that listing a species as endangered can lead to its extinction.
8. **Conservation rationale.** Burton says the species is worth saving simply because "it's right," and that the benefits of protecting an individual species are not always predictable.
