# Humpback Chub Population Monitoring in Yampa Canyon and Black Rocks: FY 1999 Annual Reports

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Two FY 1999 annual reports from the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] address monitoring of [[Humpback Chub]] populations. Project 22-A-4 covers the Yampa River population in [[Yampa Canyon]], where electrofishing and angling captured 23 adults in two five-day trips. Project 22-A-3, part of the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP), covers the Black Rocks population in the Colorado River, where trammel netting over four four-day periods captured 298 humpback chubs. Both projects are ongoing and on track, and both report that long-term monitoring of population size, survival and recruitment is needed to guide [[Recovery Program]] management.

## Background & Description

The [[Yampa River]] humpback chub population is one of only five known populations. It is poorly understood because it lives in an inaccessible canyon reach and has been rarely collected in recent decades. Yampa Canyon is designated [[Critical Habitat]] for the species. Monitoring is intended to provide baseline status data, a response measure for management actions such as [[Non-Native Fish Removal]] and flow alterations, and parameters (population size, survival, recruitment) for life-stage models.

The ISMP was developed in 1985 to monitor [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and humpback chub across the Colorado River Basin. Its original components were spring electrofishing for subadult and adult pikeminnow, autumn backwater seining for young-of-year pikeminnow, and trammel netting for adult humpback chub in Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon. These relied on catch per unit effort (CPE). A summary of the first seven years (1986–1992) was finalized in 1994, and annual reports have followed. In 1998 the ISMP expanded to include [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Green River subbasin and began mark-recapture population estimates to supplement CPE.

Both projects are scheduled for 1998–2000 and fall under the RIPRAP Action Plan (General Recovery Program Support, Action V.A.1, "Monitor Populations").

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Yampa Canyon:** Sampling was limited to the river reach through the canyon. Electrofishing and angling were the main capture methods, and seining was not used in 1999.
- **Black Rocks (Colorado River):** Sampling was constrained by high water velocities in the main channel and violent eddies along the shoreline. Nets were set along eddy lines and rock faces, where humpback chub were readily captured.
- **Co-occurring species at Black Rocks:** The catch included [[Roundtail Chub]] (340, the most abundant chub), channel catfish, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, white sucker, black bullhead, Colorado pikeminnow, largemouth bass, smallmouth bass and black crappie.
- **Movement and tagging history:** Some captured humpback chub had been tagged earlier by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR) in Westwater Canyon and by the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW) in Black Rocks.

## Key Findings

### Project 22-A-4: Yampa River Humpback Chub Monitoring

- **Study design:** This was the second year of a three-year study to determine monitoring procedures. In 1998, 18 adult humpback chub were captured and PIT tagged. In 1999, 20 were PIT tagged, and one tagged fish from 1998 was recaptured.
- **Sampling effort:** Two five-day trips were made down the Yampa River. The first (late June to early July) used two electrofishing rafts and angling with a crew of about seven. The second (12–15 July) used one electrofishing raft and angling.
- **Catch:** A total of 23 humpback chub were captured. Twenty were PIT tagged and released, two were too small to tag, and one was a recapture. Trip 1 electrofishing yielded 12 fish (11.4 h) and angling yielded 3 (11.0 h). Trip 2 electrofishing yielded 8 fish, and angling yielded 1.
- **Population estimate:** Based on one recapture, estimates ranged from about 200 to 400 fish depending on assumptions. These are consistent with estimates proposed by [[Nesler]] (1992) when interim recovery objectives were developed. The authors concluded that a few more years of similar effort could produce a worthwhile population estimate.
- **Juvenile sampling:** Planned seining and light-trap sampling for larval and juvenile chub was not done in 1999. In 1998, juveniles were seined, but they were difficult to identify in the field.
- **Analysis and status:** The project was to consult a statistician during winter 1999–2000 to refine the sampling design for long-term trend and recruitment monitoring. The budget was reduced from $40.0K to $21.0K for FY 1999 and is scheduled through FY 2001. FY 1999 funds of $21.0K were fully expended.
- **Recommendation:** Long-range monitoring should estimate population size, survival and recruitment, not only catch data.

### Project 22-A-3: Black Rocks Population Estimate (ISMP)

- **Sampling design:** Black Rocks was sampled for four four-day periods separated by one-week intervals, from late August to mid-October 1999 (8/31–9/3, 9/14–9/17, 9/27–9/30, 10/12–10/15). Six to eight 1-inch-mesh trammel nets were set and checked every 1 to 1.5 hours.
- **Catch:** A total of 298 humpback chub and 340 roundtail chub were captured over three weeks of sampling. Thirty of the 298 humpback chub were recaptured at least once (one twice). Thirty-four were also captured in 1998.
- **Earlier recaptures:** Twenty-two of the 298 had been tagged earlier: eight by UDWR in Westwater Canyon and 14 by CDOW in Black Rocks. These were useful for growth and movement data but did not contribute to the population estimate.
- **Comparison with 1998:** Captures rose from 184 in 1998 to 298 in 1999, and recruits rose from 16 to 30. The 1998 population estimate was a mean of 1,528 fish with confidence intervals of 888–2,750. The 1999 estimate had not been completed, but the larger sample was expected to tighten the 1998 estimate. (The confidence level is garbled in the scanned source.)
- **Method evaluation:** Trammel netting was the most efficient capture method. Using other gears required stopping netting, so the report recommends trammel nets only.
- **Status and schedule:** Field work continues in FY 2000, with a final report on adult size and structure planned for FY 2001. The budget was $20,000, fully expended, and 1998 data were submitted, with 1999 data due in early 2000.

### Overall Conclusions

- Both populations are small relative to the species' recovery needs. The Yampa population is estimated at a few hundred fish, and Black Rocks at about 1,500 adults in 1998.
- Standardized, multi-year monitoring that estimates population size, survival and recruitment is needed before management objectives can be set.
