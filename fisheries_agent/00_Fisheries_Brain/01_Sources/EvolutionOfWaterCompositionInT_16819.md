# Evolution of Water Composition in the Churince and El Mojarrel Systems of the Cuatro Cienegas Basin, Mexico

#limnology #hydrogeochemistry #aquatic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This descriptive limnology study by Quinn E. Respet (Limnology, Bio 426, Spring 1996) examines how ionic and nutrient composition changes along two spring-fed aquatic systems in the [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]], Coahuila, Mexico: the [[Churince System]] and the [[El Mojarrel System]]. Water chemistry rises from headspring to terminal lake, which the author attributes mainly to [[Evaporation]]. Comparison with 1964–1966 data suggests ionic concentrations are increasing, possibly due to [[Groundwater Mining]] and surface water diversion. The author frames the results as supporting the conservation of the basin's biological preserve.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] lies in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], about 180 miles south of the Big Bend National Park border in Texas. It sits between basin-and-range compression ridges formed during the Pleistocene.
- **Geology:** Lithology is Cretaceous limestone and [[Gypsum]]. The author expected Ca²⁺, CO₃²⁻, and SO₄²⁻ to dominate the ionic composition.
- **Hydrology:** Geothermally warmed groundwater reaches the surface as headspring pools, drains through streams to saline lagunas. Groundwater is thought to derive from the [[Edwards Aquifer]]. Annual precipitation is reported as less than 200 ml, often falling in storms under 24 hours that cause flash floods.
- **Biota and threats:** The aquatic habitats support many endemic fish, mollusks, and crustaceans. Irrigation diversions from the rios over the past two decades, along with water and gypsum exploitation, are believed to raise salinity and threaten endemic fauna. The area was designated a biological preserve, but the author argues further protection is needed.
- **Study systems:**
  - *Churince:* The highly productive [[Posas Bonitas]] headspring drains through the Intermediate Laguna and Stream Sections 1–3 into the large, unproductive terminal [[Laguna Grande]]. The stream is heavily overgrown and sometimes enclosed. A "Side Pool" is an ephemerally connected cut-off pool.
  - *El Mojarrel:* A high-flow headspring (West Mojarrel) with inflow and outflow sinkholes, a short overgrown stream, and the terminal East Mojarrel lagoon. Neither system is directly affected by irrigation diversions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Evaporation as the main driver.** The dry basin produces high evaporation, which removes water and concentrates ions as flow moves downstream. The Rio Churince is longer and wider than the El Mojarrel stream, making it more susceptible to evaporation. Laguna Grande is a closed lake. The terminal Churince laguna has a maximum depth of 1 m, versus 2 m for East Mojarrel. The author notes strong afternoon winds at Laguna Grande, which likely increase evaporation.

**Chemistry of the water.**
- Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.4 mg/l (Posas Bonitas) to 8.1 mg/l (Laguna Grande), near supersaturation. This matches historical readings. Minckley et al. (1968) reported headspring groundwater at about 50% saturation.
- pH rose downstream. Historical data show circumneutral headsprings (pH 7.0–7.2), 7.3–7.8 in open lagunas, and 8.0–9.7 in highly evaporative sites.
- Mixed water types: current analysis places Churince water as a [[Sulfate-Magnesium Water Type]] with high chloride. Historic classification described Ca²⁺/SO₄²⁻ in headsprings shifting to Mg²⁺/SO₄²⁻ in lagunas. Current data show the reverse at some sites (Posas Bonitas: Mg²⁺/SO₄²⁻; Laguna Grande: Ca²⁺/SO₄²⁻).
- Ion balance was not achieved, with anion mEq/l exceeding cation mEq/l, consistent with Minckley et al. (1968).

**Nutrients and reducing conditions.** Nutrients generally declined downstream in both systems, matching reduced biotic activity. Laguna Grande is essentially devoid of biological activity, whereas East Mojarrel supports water lilies (*Nymphaea*) and fish. Bright orange-to-red pools, especially the Side Pool, suggest [[Sulfur-Reducing Bacteria]] and strongly reducing conditions. Under these conditions, phosphate can be released from sediments, which explains a marked rise in [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] in the Side Pool.

**Mineral precipitation.** Gypsum precipitates readily in the downgradient saline lagunas, forming gypsum dunes downwind of Laguna Grande. Minckley (1992) reported that continual dissolution and precipitation of calcium carbonate and gypsum regularly create new posas or enclose existing ones and streams.

## Key Findings

**1. Headwater-to-terminal concentration gradient (Churince).** Ionic concentrations increase from the headspring toward the terminal lake, but not sequentially. Cl⁻, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, K⁺, and Mg²⁺ were higher in Posas Bonitas than in the stream sections and Intermediate Laguna (except Na⁺). The Side Pool's conductivity exceeded that of Laguna Grande, indicating a highly evaporative, low-flow environment.

**2. Statistical variation among Churince sites.**
- Conductivity and chloride differed significantly between the Side Pool and Laguna Grande (P<0.01), and each differed from all other sites.
- Na⁺, K⁺, and Mg²⁺ did not vary significantly from headspring through the final stream section, but the terminal laguna differed (P<0.01 for each).
- SRP differed only at the Side Pool (P<0.001).
- Nitrate differed at the Side Pool (P<0.0001) and Laguna Grande (P<0.01).

**3. El Mojarrel statistical pattern.** Significant variation among sites was found for pH, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, and NO₃⁻-N. West Mojarrel Outflow differed significantly (P<0.05) from the West Mojarrel Inflow and East Mojarrel sites, which did not differ from each other.

**4. Headwater similarity across systems.** No significant differences were found between the Churince and El Mojarrel headsprings for any parameter tested (except chloride, as noted in the Discussion). The author concludes both headsprings likely draw from the same groundwater source. Terminal lagunas differed significantly for all parameters except SRP.

**5. Evidence of change over time (historical comparison, Churince).**
- Headspring: calcium (P<0.05) and magnesium (P<0.01) differed significantly between the current and historical data.
- Laguna Grande: every parameter tested differed at P<0.01. Historical values were higher than March 1996 values, and the author notes that this is inconsistent with the irrigation/groundwater-mining hypothesis. Timing relative to precipitation events may explain it, but rainfall data were unavailable.
- The author reports generally higher headspring concentrations in March 1996 than in the 1964–1965 samples, consistent with groundwater mining reducing the volume of water available to dissolve minerals.

**6. Ionic dominance.** In the Churince headspring, magnesium made up 57% of cations and sulfate 82% of anions. Magnesium and sulfate were also the primary cation and anion in the terminal laguna, though percentage composition shifted along the system.

**7. Correlation structure.**
- Churince: most parameters were highly correlated (P<0.01) with one another, except SRP. Ionic concentrations rose linearly with evaporative concentration, and nitrate was negatively correlated with salinity.
- El Mojarrel: fewer relationships were found. Calcium and magnesium were strongly linked (P<0.01). Nitrate was negatively related to calcium, chloride, and magnesium (P<0.05).
- The author attributes the weaker El Mojarrel correlations to possible subterranean inflows and outflows in East Mojarrel.

**8. Author's recommendations.** The author suggests sampling at least two more systems, and analyzing carbonate and silica in future work. The findings are presented as supporting evidence for halting the degradation of the Cuatro Cienegas biological preserve.
