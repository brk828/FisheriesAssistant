# Reproductive Biology and Aspects of Life History of Tiaroga cobitis in Southwestern New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 Master of Science thesis by [[Kenneth D. Britt Jr.]] at [[New Mexico State University]] examines the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, benthic cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River]] drainage of Arizona and New Mexico. Field work used electrofishing and seining at three study areas (two on the Gila River system, one on the [[San Francisco River]]) during 1979 and 1980, combined with laboratory analyses of gonads, fecundity, age and diet. The study documents spawning timing and sites, fecundity, a maximum life span of about 24 months, and the effects of severe 1978 floods on growth, food supply and condition. It also documents current distribution and argues that habitat alteration and the exotic [[Red Shiner]] are the main threats.

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is elongate, subfusiform and ventrally flattened, rarely exceeding 60 mm standard length. It has a small, terminal, highly oblique mouth and lacks barbels. Its body is olivaceous and mottled, with a black basicaudal spot usually present. Breeding males show red to red-orange coloration on the fins, mouth and head; breeding females show only yellowish markings on the fins and lower body (Minckley, 1965). The species is ecologically similar to [[Percid Darters]], especially *Etheostoma* species.

Conservation status is protective: the species is listed as Group VII in Arizona (may be jeopardized in the foreseeable future) and Group II in New Mexico (prospects for survival likely in jeopardy). Its original range was the Gila River drainage, and it has been extirpated from much of Arizona, including the Rio San Pedro, Salt River and parts of the Verde drainage. In New Mexico it remains common in riffles that have not been heavily altered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** Study Area A was on the Gila River near Cliff, New Mexico (elevation 1,350 m; substrate about 30% sand, 40% gravel, 30% cobble). Study Area B was on the East Fork of the Gila River (elevation 1,753 m; substrate about 30% sand, 30% gravel, 40% cobble). Study Area C was on the San Francisco River near Glenwood, New Mexico (elevation 1,390 m; substrate about 45% sand, 20% gravel, 25% cobble, 10% boulder).

**Hydrology and floods.** Two severe floods struck the Gila system in November and December 1978, peaking at 2,010 m³/s (25 November) and 918 m³/s (18 December) per the Gila gaging record, with the San Francisco River peaking at 357 and 581 m³/s. These floods were central to the study's interpretation of the 1979 season.

**Land use and threats.** Water diversion, irrigation, grazing, channelization and damming (a proposed dam upstream of Redrock) are the main human pressures. The thesis notes that the red shiner, which avoids turbulent flows, may be limited by periodic floods that favor native fishes.

**Invertebrate food base.** Aquatic insect larvae dominated the diet, and the 1978 floods reduced benthic macroinvertebrate abundance, linking food availability to condition.

## Key Findings

**Distribution (1979–1980 collections)**
- Loach minnows are restricted to the Gila River system in New Mexico, the San Francisco River system in Arizona and New Mexico, and Aravaipa Creek in Arizona.
- In the East Fork they extend well into the [[Gila Wilderness]].
- In the San Francisco River they occur from the Arizona–New Mexico state line upstream to about 12 km northwest of Reserve, New Mexico, and in tributaries including Tularosa and Whitewater creeks and the Blue River.

**Gonadal development and spawning**
- Gonadal development peaked in March–April. Mean GSI of females rose from 6.9 (10 March) to a maximum of 19.0 (21 April) in 1979.
- The first spawning readiness was observed on 21 April 1979. Fertilized eggs were found on 18 May and 6 June 1979 in the East Fork, and on 6 June 1979 and 13 May 1980 in the Gila River.
- Spawning occurred from late March or April into early June, depending on the year. The species is described as a **nonsynchronous, fractional spawner** whose spawning period was prolonged in 1979.
- Egg diameter analysis showed a single mode in 1979 but a recruitment mode suggests possible multiple spawning.

**Spawning sites**
- Fertilized eggs were found attached to the underside of flattened cobble on 27 occasions, usually in the shallow margins of riffles.
- Mean cobble dimensions were 13.5 × 18.1 cm. Mean water depth at egg sites was 13 cm (East Fork averaged 21 cm, Gila River 9 cm).
- Mean bottom velocity at egg sites was 19 cm/s overall (26.8 cm/s Gila River; 9.4 cm/s East Fork).
- Eggs per cobble ranged from 9 to 260, with a mean of 63. No egg sites were found in the San Francisco River.
- Eggs hatched in 6 days at about 20 °C; yolk-sac larvae measured 5 mm total length at hatching.
- Maximum daily water temperature averaged about 21 °C on days with egg sites.

**Fecundity and egg size**
- Fecundity ranged from 144 to 254 ripe eggs; mean ripe egg diameter was 1.54 mm.
- Females larger than 60 mm had significantly larger eggs (1.55–1.67 mm) than females under 60 mm (1.44–1.56 mm), a difference that the thesis links to age.

**Age and growth**
- Maximum life span was about 24 months; most individuals die after spawning in their second year.
- Age-0 fish averaged 53.0 mm total length by December 1979; age-1 fish averaged 64.7 mm.
- Maximum recorded length was 90 mm. Growth was minimal during winter.
- March 1980 total lengths were significantly greater than March 1979 in the Gila and San Francisco rivers, probably due to the 1978 floods.

**Condition and food habits**
- Food items were found in 91% of 45 fish from eleven sample dates on the Gila River.
- Chironomid larvae comprised 35.2% of food items from March 1979 to May 1980; simuliid, ephemeropteran, plecopteran and trichopteran larvae made up 29.0%, 26.0%, 5.9% and 2.5%.
- Low condition factors in early March 1979 reflected flood-reduced benthos. Food items per fish rose from 8 (10 March) to 35 (17 May) 1979.

**Effects of flooding**
- The 1978 floods reduced invertebrate food supply and possibly delayed gonadal development and spawning in 1979. They may have also shortened the spawning period and prevented a second set of eggs from maturing.
- Positively, scouring floods reduced cobble embeddedness, creating more spawning cavities. Low flows from June 1979 to May 1980 increased embeddedness and reduced spawning habitat.

**Conclusions**
- Periodic severe floods likely favor native fishes such as the loach minnow over exotics such as the [[Red Shiner]].
- The proposed Redrock dam and continued water diversion are likely to reduce loach minnow habitat and could facilitate red shiner invasion upstream.
