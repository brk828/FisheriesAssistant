# Gila River Valley as an Important Wildlife Reservoir

#ornithology #freshwater-ecology #riparian-birds #wildlife-conservation

## Executive Summary

This letter-report, written by [[Dale A. Zimmerman]] (Professor and Chairman, Department of Biological Sciences, Western New Mexico University), argues that the riparian valley of the [[Gila River]] in southwestern New Mexico is a critical wildlife reservoir. The author contends that agency studies of the proposed [[Hooker Dam]] inadequately assess scenic river values and wildlife habitat. The core concern is that tree removal, dam construction, livestock grazing and year-round shooting will destroy the [[Riparian Woodland]] that supports several rare and range-restricted birds, including the [[Gray Hawk]], [[Mexican Black Hawk]] and [[Mexican Duck]]. The author calls for a complete reevaluation of river reclamation and dam schemes proposed for Arizona and New Mexico.

## Background & Description

The document was prompted by the proposed Hooker Dam and by a report on the biological resources of the Gila River in New Mexico by [[Joseph P. Hubbard]] (1968). The author argues that Hubbard's report barely addresses birds, which the author describes as the most important recreational element of the fauna. The valley attracts amateur and professional ornithologists, and the author has received at least 200 requests for bird-finding information since 1964.

The biotic community is defined by two tree species: the sycamore, *Platanus wrightii* ([[Arizona Sycamore]]), and especially [[Fremont's Cottonwood]] (*Populus fremontii*), which the author describes as an indicator species. The author distinguishes it from *P. wislizenii* (Rio Grande Valley) and *P. angustifolia* (higher elevations). In New Mexico, *P. fremontii* is restricted to the lower Gila and San Francisco rivers and a few southwestern canyons. Associated plants include *Salix nigra* var. *vallicola* (willow), *Baccharis glutinosa* ([[Baccharis Shrubland]]), hackberry (*Celtis reticulata*), Arizona walnut (*Juglans major*), desert olive (*Forestiera neomexicana*), wild grape (*Vitis arizonica*) and western soapberry (*Sapindus drummondii*). Farther from water, the community grades into juniper (*Juniperus* spp.), pinyon and ponderosa pine, and leguminous brushland.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The riparian strip is confined to a narrow river bed in an arid to semi-arid region, so its carrying capacity for wildlife is limited. Animals concentrate there for watering, feeding, breeding and roosting, and many depend on it absolutely. Bird density is much higher in the valley than anywhere else in the region at all seasons.

The author notes that the valley contains a "Mexican element" in its avifauna, with species at the northern edge of their range. The habitat is a subtropical [[Sonoran Biotope]] association that is rare north of the Mexican border, and the Gila stand from the Hooker dam site downstream to Cliff, Gila and Redrock is described as one of the best and most accessible examples anywhere.

The author identifies four main threats:
1. Outright destruction of large trees.
2. Unrestricted, concentrated grazing that prevents floodplain forest regeneration ([[Livestock Grazing]]).
3. Irresponsible year-round shooting ([[Year-Round Shooting]]).
4. Damage to aquatic and terrestrial environments from dam construction and associated development.

The author stresses that development per se is not the problem, but that schemes involving tree removal, gunning, motorboating or disturbance would eliminate the valley's wildlife values. Mammals such as the [[Beaver]] and [[River Otter]] still occur along the banks, but the wildlife web is described as "hanging by a very tenuous thread."

The breeding-bird checklist for the Redrock to Turkey Creek reach lists roughly 104 species. Species marked as positively breeding in the valley are flagged as either largely restricted to the area in New Mexico or as having their center of abundance there. Most other species are scarce to very rare elsewhere in the state.

## Key Findings

**Rare raptors**
- The [[Gray Hawk]] (*Buteo nitidus*) apparently no longer breeds in New Mexico. The last known nesting site in the state was in a cottonwood bosque east and north of Cliff (Ligon, 1961). The species was also described as "decidedly rare" in southern Arizona by the early 1950s.
- The [[Mexican Black Hawk]] (*Buteogallus anthracinus*) numbers roughly four or five pairs along the Gila from Turkey Creek to Redrock in a typical year. This may be the entire breeding population in New Mexico, with only a few more pairs in Arizona.
- Many reported "Black Hawks" on the Upper Gila are actually [[Zone-tailed Hawk]] (*Buteo albonotatus*), and some earlier records were erroneous.
- The author predicts that the Gray Hawk could be lost from New Mexico within a decade without protection. Both hawks are shot on sight because of their "hawk" label and are conspicuous and fearless, which makes them easy targets.

**Cavity-nesting and riparian specialists**
- [[Wied's Crested Flycatcher]] (*Myiarchus tyrannulus*) needs sycamores. Approximately 15 pairs exist in the Gila Valley between Redrock and Turkey Creek.
- The [[Gila Woodpecker]] (*Centurus uropygialis*) prefers cottonwoods. Its main New Mexico range is the Gila Valley.
- The [[Elf Owl]] (*Micrathene whitneyi*) is largely restricted to cottonwood groves in the southwestern corner of the state, with its major population along the Gila.
- [[Abert's Towhee]] (*Pipilo aberti*) is apparently confined to the valley in New Mexico.

**Waterbirds and other species**
- The [[Mexican Duck]] (*Anas diazi*) breeds in the United States only in New Mexico. It bred until about ten years ago in marshy ponds near Gila and Cliff, but these sites have been destroyed. The numbers are now dangerously low, and the species is probably declining in Mexico too.
- Several herons (Great Blue, Green and Black-crowned Night) once bred near Cliff, Gila and Redrock and would return if areas were protected.
- Mourning and White-winged Doves, Yellow-billed Cuckoo and Lucy's Warbler also depend on the valley's trees.

**Visitation and tourism**
- Bird-watchers visit the valley in large numbers. The author has received at least 200 requests for directions since 1964, and the New Mexico Ornithological Society has repeatedly chosen the area for its spring field sessions.
- A visiting couple from southeast Texas suggested that part of the valley should become a National Wildlife Refuge similar to the Santa Ana Refuge.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The author judges the Gila to be the best place in New Mexico, and one of the best in the Southwest, for seeing unusual birds.
- Protection of the remaining riparian forest and brush must precede any development. Development must account for wildlife requirements, and changes must be moderate and gradual to allow adjustment.
- The author urges the [[Bureau of Outdoor Recreation]] to reevaluate all proposed river and dam schemes for Arizona and New Mexico, including the Hooker Dam, with full attention to scenic and wildlife values.
