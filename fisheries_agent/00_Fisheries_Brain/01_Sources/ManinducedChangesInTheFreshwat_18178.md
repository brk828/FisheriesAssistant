# Man-Induced Changes in the Freshwater Fish Fauna of Israel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by A. Ben-Tuvia (Department of Zoology, Hebrew University of Jerusalem), published in *Fisheries Management* (1981, vol. 12, no. 4), examines how human activity has changed Israel's inland fish fauna over roughly the previous 50 years. The author identifies three drivers: deliberate introduction and transfer of foreign and native species for aquaculture, fishery development and biological control; land reclamation; and diversion of water for domestic, agricultural and industrial use. The paper concludes that the planned introduction of exotic fishes has been economically advantageous without causing ecological disturbance, while noting that the reclamation of [[Lake Hulah]] nearly eliminated [[Tristramella Simonis Intermedia]].

The document also contains a short section of 1980 annual branch reports from two British angling and fisheries organisations. These are non-research content and are summarised briefly under Key Findings.

## Background & Description

**Native fauna.** Israel's native inland fish fauna comprises 28 species and subspecies. This count excludes the [[European Eel]], six grey mullet species (Mugilidae) and the [[European Seabass]], which are diadromous and enter coastal rivers. At least 47 species are now known from inland waters once introduced and transferred species are included (Table 1, summarised here). Approximately half of the native species, mainly Cyprinidae and Cobitidae, are of Palaearctic origin. Cichlidae and Clariidae are of Ethiopian origin. The genus *Aphanius* is considered a relic of the former Tethys Sea. Endemism is high, at about 25%.

**Categories in the checklist.** Table 1 classifies taxa as native (N), introduced from foreign countries (I), or transferred from the Mediterranean Sea (M). Native taxa include several [[Barbus]], [[Capoeta]], [[Garra]], *Noemacheilus* and *Aphanius* species. Introduced taxa include [[Common Carp]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Silver Carp]], [[Bighead Carp]], [[Black Carp]], [[Mosquito Fish]], [[Argentine Pejerrey]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and several cichlids.

**Methods and sources.** The paper is a synthesis rather than a field study. It draws on earlier surveys, government fisheries statistics (Sarid 1979; Sarig, in press), and many personal communications. No new sampling data or sample sizes are presented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water management.** The author links fish changes to three pressures:
- Introductions and transfers for aquaculture, lake fisheries and biological control.
- Land reclamation, most dramatically at [[Lake Hulah]], reclaimed in 1955 (the Discussion gives 1955; the Introduction gives 1956).
- Water abstraction for irrigation, drinking and industry, which reduces spring and stream habitats. The [[National Water Carrier]] spread several native and foreign species into new reservoirs and areas.

**Vulnerable habitats.** Small springs and streams support *Noemacheilus*, *Garra* and *Aphanius*. These are most at risk from abstraction, because altered flow can change water quality and reduce food supply. The author notes that changes in the [[Jordan River]] hydrology may also endanger the northern population of *Tristramella simonis intermedia* in [[Lake Tiberias]].

**Isolated springs.** Reproducing populations of four species are known from [[Ein Fashkha]], near the Dead Sea. Two additional species, [[Tilapia zillii]] and *Aphanius mento*, were recorded there after 1967. The author suggests *T. zillii* was probably introduced in an effort to establish fish pond culture.

## Key Findings

**1. Naturally reproducing exotics.** Four introduced species reproduce in inland waters: [[Gambusia Affinis]], [[Common Carp]], *Sarotherodon niloticus* and [[Argentine Pejerrey]]. A population of [[Rainbow Trout]] occurs in the upper Jordan River, but its reproduction is doubtful. Three Chinese carps (silver carp, bighead carp and grass carp) do not reproduce naturally and are propagated artificially at hatcheries.

**2. Lake Hulah and the decline of *Tristramella*.** The reclamation of [[Lake Hulah]] brought *Tristramella simonis intermedia* to near extinction. Before reclamation, the species was the most common fish in the lake, with annual catches of over 80 tons in some years, about 60% of the lake's total catch (Ben-Tuvia 1959). A small population survives in northern Lake Tiberias, but the author considers it endangered.

**3. Aquaculture success.**
- [[Common Carp]] was introduced from Europe in 1931–1934. Yearly yield in 1977–1979 was about 8,000 tons, roughly 60% of the fish pond harvest. Carp shows advanced domestication, including fast growth and a low food conversion factor.
- [[Silver Carp]] was introduced in 1966. Its yield was 2,648 tons in 1977 (19.6% of pond yield) and about 1,500 tons in 1978 and 1979 (17.7%), after a market shortfall. The 1979 catch in Lake Tiberias was 108 tons.
- [[Bighead Carp]] was first obtained as fingerlings in 1976. The 1979 crop was 120 tons, and about 100,000 hybrid fingerlings were introduced into Lake Tiberias in summer 1979.
- [[Grass Carp]] was introduced in 1965, mainly to control aquatic plants. The annual crop for October 1978 to September 1979 was about 80 tons.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] culture at Kibbutz Dan began in 1969 with eggs imported each year. The 1979 yield was 140 tons.

**4. Biological control.** [[Black Carp]] was introduced into two reservoirs to control gastropods and is propagated artificially. [[Mosquito Fish]] was planted in the 1920s to control mosquito larvae and is now widespread. It may compete with the native *Aphanius mento*, though no data on the nature of this competition exist.

**5. Failed or lost introductions.** The tench (*Tinca tinca*) was discontinued after slow growth in experimental ponds. Released fingerlings did not persist in the upper Jordan or Lake Tiberias. [[Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinella*) was introduced in 1956 and 75,000 fingerlings were released into Lake Tiberias in 1960–1962. It was last reported from the lake in 1974.

**6. Cichlids and mouth-brooders.**
- [[Sarotherodon Aureus]] was transferred from Lake Hulah and Lake Tiberias to fish ponds. Yields were 2,155 tons in 1977 (15.9% of pond production), 8 tons in 1978 (17.7%) and 2,665 tons in 1979. Repeated stocking raised its catch in Lake Tiberias to 100–150 tons over the last 20 years. Populations show signs of inbreeding with foreign cichlids.
- [[Sarotherodon Mossambicus]] tolerates wide ranges of salinity and temperature and starts reproducing at about 10 cm. The author warns of escape risk, despite containment precautions at Dor and Elat.

**7. Seabass and planktivores.** [[European Seabass]] was introduced from French hatcheries into inland basins. It constituted about 10% of catches in the Bardawil Lagoon, and a single 4 kg individual was captured in Lake Tiberias. The author argues the risk is low because it cannot reproduce in freshwater. The small cyprinid *Mirogrex terraesanctae* makes up about half of the Lake Tiberias catch but has low commercial value.

**8. Mugilids.** Catadromous mugilids have disappeared from several heavily polluted coastal streams. Juveniles of *Mugil cephalus* and *Liza ramada* are stocked into inland waters. Fish ponds averaged 578 tons and Lake Tiberias 191 tons in 1977–1979.

**9. Author's conclusions.** The author states that introductions have added about 400 tons a year to the Lake Tiberias catch without apparent negative effects on other stocks. He attributes this to careful planning, experiments at the Dor Fish Breeding Station and commercial ponds, and controlled stocking rates. He cautions, however, that no full guarantee can prevent accidental escapes. He calls for "rational long-range planning based on adequate biological and technological investigation" to avoid irreversible harm.

**10. Non-research content.** The final pages report 1980 annual activities of two British branches (East Anglian and Greater London and South Eastern). Topics included introductions of non-indigenous species, water quality monitoring using fish, roach breeding, and the role of water bailiffs. These do not concern Israeli fauna and are not scientific data.
