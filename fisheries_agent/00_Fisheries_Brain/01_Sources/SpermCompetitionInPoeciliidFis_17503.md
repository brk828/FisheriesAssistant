# Sperm Competition in Poeciliid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by George D. Constantz, examines [[Sperm Competition]] in livebearing fishes of the family [[Poeciliidae]]. Poeciliid males have few physical means of controlling females and control few resources vital to female fitness, which makes them appear evolutionarily weak. The author argues that sperm competition nonetheless occurs, through [[Multiple Insemination]] and [[Sperm Replacement]], and that females largely control paternity. The review proposes that these features help females produce phenotypically varied offspring in fluctuating shallow habitats. It also proposes that males respond by maximizing sexual activity, growing little after maturity, and using [[Alternate Mating Behaviors]], and that gonopodial hooks may act to traumatize female genitalia and induce temporary chastity.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Sperm competition is defined as competition between ejaculates from two or more males within one female. Insects, where it has been best studied (Parker 1970), show male traits such as flushing penises, postcopulatory guarding, and copulatory plugs. Insects have limbs for grasping females and genitalia that couple tightly, and poeciliids have neither.
- **Family:** Poeciliidae is endemic to the New World and dominant in tropical fresh and brackish waters. Familiar species include the [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*), [[Swordtail]] (*Xiphophorus helleri*), [[Platyfish]] (*X. maculatus*), and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
- **Morphology:** Poeciliids are among the smallest vertebrates, with most species under 100 mm. Males are about two-thirds the length of females. All but one species bear live young, and all have internal fertilization.
- **Female cycle:** Ovarian cycles last 30–35 days. Embryos gestate 20–30 days in the follicles. Females carry fertilizable eggs for only 1–7 days per cycle. A [[Anal Spot]] on females may signal fertility in some species (e.g., *Gambusia affinis*), and a female pheromone stimulates male sexual activity.
- **Male gonopodium:** The modified anal fin, the [[Gonopodium]], transfers sperm. Its tip bears species-specific hooks and serrae, and some males have pectoral and pelvic fins that aid copulatory aim. Males grow little after maturity: *G. affinis* males grew no more than 1 mm after gonopodial maturation (N=50).
- **Clasping exception:** *Xenodexia ctenolepis* is the only poeciliid in which males grasp females, using a right pectoral "clasper" (Hubbs 1950).
- **Sperm storage:** Sperm are massed in unencapsulated spermatophores that dissolve within 10–15 min. Sperm can be stored for 3–10 months in ovarian and oviductal folds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Poeciliids inhabit shallow, relatively predator-free non-marine waters. Examples include *P. reticulata* in springs, streams, and ditches; *X. maculatus* in flooded pastures; and *Brachyraphis rhabdophora* in isolated rainforest headwater pools. The author argues these habitats are influenced by aquatic, terrestrial, and meteorological processes, so their fish are adapted to fluctuating environments.
- **Life history:** Breeding can be year-round in the tropics or seasonal in temperate areas. Brood sizes range from one to several hundred. Fry are 1–9 mm at birth. Sexual maturity occurs at 1–11 months, and lifespan is 6 months to 3 years.
- **Resource context:** Males control neither spawning sites nor food patches, which the author links to the low level of territorial behavior in livebearers. Females provide eggs and nurture, which the author proposes limits male reproduction.

## Key Findings

**1. Sperm competition occurs through multiple insemination.**
Multiple insemination was demonstrated in six species, in five genera (*Gambusia*, *Neoheterandria*, *Poecilia*, *Poeciliopsis*, *Xiphophorus*). Reported frequencies:

| Species | Multiple insemination |
|---|---|
| *Gambusia affinis* | At least 56% of females (probably near 100%) |
| *Poeciliopsis monacha* | At least 23% of females (probably greater) |
| *Xiphophorus maculatus* | 66% (48% by 2 males, 24% by 3, 4% by 4) |
| *Xiphophorus variatus* | 42% by two or more males |
| *Neoheterandria tridentiger* | 2 of 6 broods sired by 2 males |

Mixed paternity was also reported for wild and laboratory *Poecilia reticulata* broods by six authors, but no frequency was estimated. Because these genera span three of six poeciliid tribes, the author proposes multiple insemination as a general family trait.

**2. Sperm replacement is controlled by the female.**
When *P. reticulata* females were paired successively with different male morphs, broods alternated in dominant phenotype, and the last male to mate usually sired most of the next brood. Replacement can occur on the sixth day after parturition, but not on the eighth. Sperm become undetectable in the oviduct 11–14 days after insemination (Clark and Aronson 1951). Because male poeciliids cannot grasp females or apply hydraulic pressure, the author concludes that paternal replacement is likely controlled entirely by the female.

**3. Three hypotheses for the adaptive significance of multiple insemination.**
- Larger effective population size after founder events in isolated pools (Chesser et al. 1984).
- Restoring depleted sperm stores, which prevents egg resorption and lower brood size (Boorman and Parker 1976). The author notes this explains proximate, not ultimate, causes.
- Producing heterogeneous offspring in fluctuating environments. The author considers this, along with reduced inbreeding depression, the most plausible.

**4. Mating behavior is rapid and promiscuous.**
- *Gambusia affinis* males spend about 3 s with each female.
- *P. reticulata* males display 1.4–1.6 times/min and thrust 0.1–0.7 times/min (Farr 1975).
- *X. maculatus* males perform 3.2 thrusts/min and 0.8 gonopodial swings/min (Kamrin and Aronson 1954).
- *G. affinis* males average 0.9 sexual acts/min (Martin 1975).

Copulation in *X. helleri* and *X. maculatus* can last 5.6 s, with the male and female appearing hooked together.

**5. Alternate mating behaviors arise from size-based male polymorphism.**
In *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, the largest males defend territories, display black coloration, and court females. Tiny males are light colored, flee other males, and make single "sneak" gonopodial thrusts. In *Poecilia latipinna*, large males display while small males ambush females. The author predicts that alternate mating behaviors should be most prevalent where mature males grow least.

**6. Gonopodial hooks may traumatize females and enforce chastity.**
Forceful separation can cause bleeding and tearing of female genital tissue (Clark et al. 1954; Kadow 1954). The author proposes that irritated females become unreceptive and that resulting oviducal blood clots act as [[Copulatory Plug]]s, which together minimize sperm replacement. The author calls this possibility of enforced chastity one of the most exciting questions for future study.

**7. Proposed coevolution of papillae and gonopodia.**
Peden (1972b) suggested that pointed gonopodia occur with large female papillae for efficient sperm transfer. Constantz proposes instead that long papillae deflect sharp gonopodia, improving female control of paternity.

**8. Male growth and energy allocation.**
Because males grow little after maturity, the author proposes that selection favors males that allocate all energy above somatic maintenance to sexual behavior. This predicts a negative correlation between sexual activity intensity and post-maturation growth, which had not been tested.

**9. Extension to other livebearing fishes.**
The author predicts that goodeids should show traits similar to poeciliids, since they cannot grasp females, while elasmobranchs (which clasp females with teeth and claspers) should show prolonged courtship, lengthy copulation, continued growth after maturity, and infrequent mixed broods. Preliminary observations on sharks and rays were consistent with the first two predictions.

**Conclusion:** Female poeciliids effectively control paternity through multiple insemination and sperm replacement, possibly to increase offspring heterogeneity. Male poeciliids compensate by maximizing mating effort and, in some cases, by traumatizing females to induce temporary chastity. The author frames this as an intersexual evolutionary conflict shaped by each sex's ability to manipulate the other and to control reproductive resources.
