# Bog Hole Fish Renovation Project Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document package from the [[Coronado National Forest]] (Sierra Vista Ranger District) comprises a cover letter dated 04/23/92, a project proposal dated March 9, 1992, a draft public comment letter, and a mailing list dated 03/12/92. Together they describe a proposed fish renovation of the [[Bog Hole Wildlife Area]] and nearby waters in the [[Santa Cruz Drainage]], Arizona.

The proposal would drain impoundments, treat them with a fish toxicant (Antimycin), remove non-native fish, and restock native species. The target species are the native [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Gila Chub]]. The project is a cooperative effort among the Forest Service (lead agency), the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (technical design), and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (consultation on special status species). Treatment and stocking may take up to three years. The document is preliminary and is intended to start environmental analysis and public comment.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and need.** The Bog Hole and surrounding stock ponds contain non-native fish that threaten native fishes. The proposal states that a similar action was recommended by the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (Desert Fishes Recovery Team) five years earlier, and that the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service supports the project concept.

**Key species context:**
- The [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*) was planted in the 9-acre Bog Hole pond several years earlier. It does not occur naturally in the Santa Cruz drainage. If it spread, it could damage the genetic integrity of the native [[Desert Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) through hybridization.
- Both pupfish subspecies were listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986. A recovery plan was scheduled for completion in 1992.
- [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) are documented as a serious threat to the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*). They could eliminate downstream topminnow populations and block recolonization of the upper Santa Cruz drainage.
- Bait and sportfish introductions pose hazards to native [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and topminnow populations located downstream. Topminnows also occur in [[Redrock Canyon]], a tributary of Sonoita Creek.

**Project objectives:**
1. Remove existing Quitobaquito pupfish and other non-native fish from the Bog Hole.
2. Establish a viable population of desert pupfish in the Bog Hole.
3. Eliminate non-native fish from other waters in the Bog Hole watershed.
4. Introduce Gila chub and Gila topminnow into the watershed.

Objectives 1 and 2 support a draft management plan for the Bog Hole Wildlife Area being prepared by the Arizona Game and Fish Department. All objectives are consistent with the [[Forest Plan]] standards for sensitive species (page 31), which call for maintaining and improving habitat for listed and sensitive species, re-introducing extirpated native species into historical habitat, and cooperating with affected agencies.

**Management background.** The Bog Hole Wildlife Area is managed under a cooperative agreement among the Sierra Vista Ranger District, the Coronado National Forest, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department. The impoundment was established in the 1970s to protect the Mexican duck, a species that the 1992 draft letter describes as no longer threatened. The site still provides waterfowl habitat and supports birdwatching, hunting, fishing, and bird dog trials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sensitive species beyond fish.** The spread of exotic fish also threatens other sensitive species in the watershed: the [[Huachuca Tiger Salamander]], Mexican garter snake, lowland leopard frog, and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]].

**Land use and water rights.** The project involves the San Rafael Allotment. Water rights for the Bog Hole, surrounding ponds, and the diversion dike feeding Cott Tank remain unclarified. Cott Tank lies outside the Bog Hole watershed. The livestock water needs during treatment are also an open concern.

**Related proposals.** Earlier ideas for creating more marsh habitat and using the pond as a holding tank for a downstream cienega-like wetland are separate from this renovation. They will be assessed in their own analyses if proposed.

**Earlier planning.** Discussions among agency staff and stakeholders occurred over the past two years. Key meetings were held on February 14, 1989; March 9, 1989; August 8, 1991 (draft management plan); and February 20, 1992 (renovation). The February 1992 meeting included representatives from the Forest Service, Game and Fish, and Fish and Wildlife Service. A May 9, 1990 Game and Fish renovation proposal supplied the original alternatives.

## Key Findings

**Treatment alternatives under consideration:**
- No action.
- Treat the Bog Hole and other tanks in the same watershed with [[Rotenone]].
- Treat the Bog Hole and surrounding ponds, including those draining into Redrock Canyon, with rotenone.
- Later suggestions: other toxicants such as Antimycin or chlorox; non-chemical methods such as draining or netting; closure of the area to sportfishing and other activities.

**Principal issues identified (15 items), grouped:**
- *Chemical treatment:* toxicant feasibility, safety, public acceptance, and the need for a Forest Service certified applicator.
- *Biological impacts:* effects of drawdown and treatment on sensitive species and riparian vegetation; spread of exotic fish as the Bog Hole drains; future control of exotic fishes; disposal of eradicated fish.
- *Public use:* closure of waters to sportfishing and other activities; compatibility with the draft Bog Hole management plan.
- *Legal and administrative:* clarification of water rights; completion of cultural resource and biological evaluation clearances (by the Forest Service); cumulative effects with other area projects; livestock water needs during treatment.
- One issue, treatment of water on private land opposed by permittee Bob Hudson, was dropped at the February 20, 1992 meeting.

**Evaluation criteria:**
- Effectiveness and safety of the treatment method and the need for future re-treatment.
- Impacts on the public and range permittees using the Bog Hole and nearby ponds.
- Impacts on sensitive species, other wildlife, and vegetation.
- Success in establishing a native fish refugium.

**Staffing and skills:** Design and completion of renovation requires fisheries biologists and engineers from the Coronado National Forest and Game and Fish. Toxicant application requires a certified Forest Service applicator. Biological evaluations require wildlife and fisheries biologists. Cultural resource surveys require an archaeologist, and the water rights description requires a hydrologist. The need for an [[Interdisciplinary Team]] will be decided by the District Ranger after reviewing public and agency input.

**Public involvement and timeline:**
- The proposal and draft letter were circulated for review by agency personnel, including Maintenance and Development and Game Branch staff in the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
- Comments were requested by early June 1992. A revised public letter was planned for mid-June 1992.
- A [[Feasibility Report]] will follow, based on public and agency comments, with a schedule for development and implementation to be outlined afterward.
- A field trip to the Bog Hole may be arranged if needed.

**Mailing list:** The list of potentially affected publics, dated 03/12/92, includes individual permittees, agencies, congressional offices, conservation and angling groups, the Patagonia town council, and academic experts such as W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University, fishes) and Jim Collins (Arizona State University, salamanders). Organizations suggested for addition include the Tucson Audubon Society, Michael Gregory, and fishing clubs.

**Document status:** The authors describe the project description and purpose as the basis for further analysis. Other sections, including potentially affected publics, issues, evaluation criteria, and alternatives, remain preliminary and will be refined through public and agency comment. The document was prepared by Lisa Bizios (Assistant Zone Fisheries Biologist, Phoenix) and Tom Deecken (Wildlife Biologist, Sierra Vista Ranger District).
