# Biological assessment Hoover Dam modification project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal critique authored by ichthyologist Paul C. Marsh regarding a U.S. Bureau of Reclamation Biological Assessment evaluating the impacts of proposed **Hoover Dam Modifications ("Hoover Mod")** on two imperiled fish species — the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — residing in **[[Lake Mohave]]**, a reservoir on the lower Colorado River. The author argues that the Bureau's Assessment is scientifically inadequate, lacking quantitative data on physical, chemical, and biological changes expected in the reservoir, and relying on outdated (over a decade old) and improperly cited literature. Marsh contends that the conclusion of "no impact" is unjustifiable given the significance of the Lake Mohave populations to species recovery, and calls for withdrawal and reassessment of the Biological Assessment.

## Background & Description

The source text is a policy/scientific critique letter rather than a morphological study, and does not provide direct anatomical descriptions of either species. However, relevant taxonomic and biological notes include:

- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): A cyprinid (minnow family) native to the Colorado River Basin. Historically believed to require specific substrate and current conditions for reproduction.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): A catostomid (sucker family) noted for its distinctive dorsal keel ("razorback"). Adult feeding morphology/behavior is discussed indirectly (see Ecological Niche).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Both species inhabit **[[Lake Mohave]]**, a reservoir impoundment of the Colorado River downstream of Hoover Dam. This population is described as one of the **largest remaining populations of bonytail anywhere within its range**, making it of critical conservation significance.

- **Reproductive Ecology (Bonytail)**: Although the species was historically considered to have strict substrate and current requirements for successful reproduction, the letter notes that **bonytail reproduces prolifically without human intervention in earthen ponds at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** in New Mexico. This finding challenges prior assumptions about the species' reproductive strictness regarding flow and substrate conditions.

- **Feeding Ecology (Razorback Sucker)**: The Bureau's Assessment is criticized for stating that adult razorback sucker are consistently bottom feeders. Marsh emphasizes that in **Lake Mohave specifically, the species is primarily planktivorous** — a critical ecological distinction, since changes to reservoir limnology (e.g., plankton dynamics) resulting from Hoover Dam Modifications could directly and significantly affect the population's food resources.

- **Threat Context**: Both species face potential ecological disruption from **operational changes at Hoover Dam**, including altered peaking power discharge patterns, which could affect physical (e.g., flow, temperature), chemical, and biological (e.g., plankton community) conditions in Lake Mohave. No quantitative predictive data on these changes were provided in the reviewed Assessment.

## Key Findings

1. **Inadequate Impact Assessment**: The Bureau of Reclamation's Biological Assessment lacked quantitative data on expected physical, chemical, and biological changes in Lake Mohave resulting from Hoover Dam Modification, relying instead on summary operational data (unit counts, megawattage, peaking discharge).

2. **Outdated References**: The Assessment relied on a **never-finalized 1983 draft Environmental Impact Statement (EIS)**, over a decade old at time of review, without including it in the Literature Cited section.

3. **Citation Errors**: Multiple citation errors identified, including consistent misspelling of "Minckley," incorrect literature citations (e.g., a passage wrongly attributed to Marsh and Langhorst 1989 rather than to Marsh and Papoulias or other correct sources), and exclusion of relevant, more current studies:
   - Marsh and Minckley 1987 (population estimates for razorback sucker) — *Desert Fishes Council Symposium*
   - Marsh and Minckley 1989 — *Great Basin Naturalist* 49:71–78
   - Marsh and Papoulias 1989 — *California Fish and Game* 75
   - Marsh and Langhorst 1989 — *Southwestern Naturalist* 34:188–195
   - An unpublished 1989 report to the Bureau containing relevant razorback sucker data in the lower Colorado River
   - A forthcoming major monograph on razorback sucker (**Minckley et al. 1991**, in press at time of writing) not consulted despite availability in draft form.

4. **Mischaracterized Feeding Behavior**: The Assessment incorrectly generalized razorback sucker as a bottom feeder, omitting that the Lake Mohave population is primarily **planktivorous** — a fact with direct relevance to assessing risk from altered reservoir limnology.

5. **Reproductive Flexibility in Bonytail**: New evidence from captive propagation at **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** suggests bonytail reproductive requirements (substrate, current) are less strict than previously assumed, potentially altering risk assessments tied to reservoir hydrology changes.

6. **Conservation Significance of Lake Mohave**: The reviewed populations of both bonytail and razorback sucker in Lake Mohave are characterized as uniquely large and important, potentially **critical to the species' overall recovery** under the Endangered Species Act framework. Bonytail was already federally listed; razorback sucker listing was imminent at the time of writing.

7. **Author's Conclusion**: Marsh states that, despite lacking time/resources for an independent impact assessment, he holds strong professional conviction that Hoover Dam Modification **cannot fail to impact both species**. He recommends the Biological Assessment be **withdrawn and redone** using current, complete, and properly contextualized scientific data.

8. **Organizational Concern**: The letter also raises a procedural concern that the **Native Fish Work Group** (focused on razorback sucker and bonytail recovery) should remain organizationally and temporally separate from the more contentious **Hoover Mod discussions**, to avoid disrupting collaborative recovery efforts.
