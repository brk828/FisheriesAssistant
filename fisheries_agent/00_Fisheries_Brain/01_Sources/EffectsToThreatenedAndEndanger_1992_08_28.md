# Effects to Threatened and Endangered Species from Implementation of the Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #EndangeredSpecies #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This 1992 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memorandum evaluates the ecological risk of a Bureau of Land Management (BLM) proposal — the **[[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Project]]** — to enhance non-native sport fish habitat across 42 coves and bays in **[[Lake Havasu]]**, a Colorado River reservoir spanning Mohave County, Arizona, and San Bernardino County, California. The project aims to benefit non-native sport fish species including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Crappie]] via installation of physical fish habitat structures.

Lake Havasu is also home to two federally listed endangered native fish: the critically endangered **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) and the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*). While adult individuals of both species persist in the reservoir, **no documented recruitment or reproduction** has occurred for either species in Lake Havasu. The FWS concludes that enhancing non-native predator populations could **increase predation pressure on native fish eggs and larvae**, potentially negating any incidental benefits and compromising future recovery actions. The agency requested continued informal consultation with BLM to minimize impacts to listed species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: [[Lake Havasu]] is an impounded reach of the [[Colorado River]], functioning as a reservoir with distinct littoral coves and bays (42 identified in the project area) targeted for artificial habitat structure placement.
- **Adult native fish habitat**: Persistence of adult [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in both Lake Havasu and [[Lake Mohave]] indicates that suitable physical habitat for **adult** life stages remains intact in these reservoirs.
- **Early life stage habitat deficiency**: Suitable conditions for **eggs, larvae, and juveniles** of these native species appear to be lacking or compromised — implicating a possible physical habitat deficiency as well as biological (predation) pressure.
- **Predation dynamics**: The presence of established non-native sport fish populations (predators of eggs/larvae) is identified as a **major impediment** to natural reproduction and recruitment success of both endangered native species.
- **Documented movements/occurrences**:
  - Adult [[Razorback Sucker]] from Lake Havasu were captured in the **Central Arizona Project aqueduct** in 1986.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae were documented in Lake Havasu as recently as 1986, with no subsequent recruitment confirmed.
  - Adult [[Bonytail Chub]] have been occasionally caught by anglers in Lake Havasu, but no reproduction/recruitment has been documented.
- **Historical alteration**: Colorado River habitat alteration (damming/impoundment creating reservoirs like Lake Havasu) has fundamentally altered the distribution and reproductive success of these native species.

## Key Findings

1. **No documented recruitment**: Neither [[Bonytail Chub]] nor [[Razorback Sucker]] show evidence of successful reproduction or recruitment in Lake Havasu, despite persistent adult populations.
2. **Adult habitat is sufficient; early life-stage habitat is not**: This dichotomy suggests recruitment failure is driven more by biotic (predation) than abiotic (physical habitat) factors for adults, though early life stage habitat deficiencies may also contribute.
3. **Non-native fish predation identified as primary recruitment barrier**: Existing non-native sport fish populations already exert significant predation pressure on native fish eggs/larvae in Lake Havasu.
4. **Project risk to endangered species**: The proposed habitat enhancement for non-native sport fish (via structures in 42 coves/bays) is expected to **further increase predation pressure**, potentially negating any indirect benefits to native species and adversely affecting future recovery actions.
5. **Recovery potential of Lake Havasu**: The reservoir is identified as having potential value for endangered species recovery — supporting adult broodstock, genetic diversity maintenance, ecological/life-history research, and potential rearing/growout facility siting for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
6. **Conflict between project scope and recovery opportunities**: The scale of the proposed sport fish project (covering most reservoir coves) may compromise the ability to designate areas for native species recovery efforts.
7. **Regulatory outcome**: FWS determined the project **would have an effect** on listed endangered fish, triggering a recommendation for continued **informal consultation** between FWS and BLM under the Endangered Species Act framework, rather than an immediate formal consultation or jeopardy finding.
8. **Related administrative action**: A separate no-effect determination was already issued (July 14, 1992) for shore access development at the **[[Central Arizona Project Peninsula]]**, excluding placement of fish habitat structures at that specific site.
