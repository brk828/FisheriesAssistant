# Life History and Status of the Endangered Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) of Pyramid Lake, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service research report, by G. Gary Scoppettone, Mark Coleman, and Gary A. Wedemeyer, documents a four-year life-history study of the federally endangered [[Cui-ui]], an endemic catostomid of [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. The study covered spawning migration, reproduction, larval emigration, age and growth, population size, food, and predation. The decline of the population was attributed mainly to disruption of its reproductive cycle: adults had restricted access to the [[Truckee River]] after a delta formed at its mouth in the 1930s as the lake level fell. Fish passage was partly restored by [[Marble Bluff Dam]] and the [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]] (completed 1976), and by hatchery stocking. The report concludes that the population was numerically healthy (about 187,000 adults in 1982) but dominated by a single year class, with a male-depleted sex structure.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** Cui-ui (*Chasmistes cujus*), described as the last genetically pure member of the genus *Chasmistes* in the authors' view, and ranked as a top-priority species for recovery.
- **Longevity and growth:** Fish aged from 1 to 41 years; growth virtually ceased by about age 20. Mature females grew faster and lived longer than males.
- **Aging method:** Opercle bones were validated as more reliable than scales, which underestimated the ages of old fish. Opercles were chosen over fin rays, otoliths and vertebrae.
- **Background:** The species' decline followed water diversion for the [[Newlands Project]] (completed 1905), which lowered Pyramid Lake by about 25 m between 1906 and 1982 and diverted roughly half of the Truckee River flow.
- **Study area:** Pyramid Lake is a terminal, moderately saline lake (total dissolved solids 4,900 to 5,000 mg/l in 1983), the largest remnant of pluvial [[Lake Lahontan]]. The lower 40 km of the Truckee River, the historic spawning reach, has a gradient of about 1.5 m/km.
- **Methods overview:** Gill-net sampling of the prespawning aggregation, Floy and sonic and radio telemetry, fecundity by volumetric displacement, plankton-net sampling of emigrating larvae, opercle-based aging, stomach content analysis, and mark-recapture population estimation with a modified Schnabel estimator.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** Adults spawned predominantly over gravel in riffles, at depths of 20 to 110 cm, stream velocities of 27 to 140 cm/s, and near-bed velocities of 27 to 89 cm/s. Most spawning occurred at 61 to 70 cm depth and 101 to 110 cm/s.
- **Hydrology:** Spawning runs were largest in high-flow springs. Peak upstream migration occurred at daily mean water temperatures of 12 to 15 °C, and warming temperatures stimulated migration.
- **Delta effects:** Before 1984 a delta of up to 1.5 km wide extended about 2 km upstream from the river mouth, blocking most adult passage. In 1984, after two wet years, the delta was effectively abolished.
- **Predation:** American white pelicans (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*) caused substantial adult mortality during migration; about 421 carcasses were tallied in 1982. Pelicans exploited the delta and the fishway.
- **Lake fish community:** Other catostomids, including [[Tahoe Sucker]] (*Catostomus tahoensis*), were distinguished from cui-ui larvae using a key.

## Key Findings

1. **Migration timing:** Prespawning adults gathered off the Truckee River mouth from mid-March to early June. Upstream migration lasted 48 to 59 days across the 1982–1984 runs.
2. **Residence in river:** Radio-tagged males stayed in the river 6.5 to 16.5 days and females 4.0 to 10.5 days. Migrants moved only short distances above Marble Bluff Dam, averaging 2.1 km beyond it, and most spawning likely occurred within about 5 km upstream of the dam.
3. **Sex ratio:** Males to females in the FPB run was 1.0:1.3 in 1982 and 1983 and 1.0:2.5 in 1984.
4. **Fecundity:** Ranged from 24,000 eggs (431–432 mm FL) to 196,000 (657 mm FL). A 558 mm female produced about 104,000 eggs in 1982 and 76,000 in 1983.
5. **Larval emigration:** More than 98% of downstream migrants were swim-up larvae. Emigration peaked 24 days after the adult peak in 1982 and 29 days in 1984, and occurred almost exclusively at night.
6. **Dam effects:** In 1984 survival was 99% in controls and 89 to 93% in test groups passed over Marble Bluff Dam. The authors concluded the dam was unlikely to limit the population.
7. **Age structure:** Eleven year classes were identified (ages 1 to 41). In 1983, about 92% of the offshore population belonged to the 1969 year class, 3% to other young-fish classes, and 5% to old fish (1950 or earlier).
8. **Population size:** Offshore prespawning aggregates were estimated at 187,065 (95% CI 151,883–236,451) in 1982 and 103,407 (92,270–134,161) in 1983. Biomass was about 378,432 kg and 205,573 kg, respectively.
9. **Fishway performance:** Fish passing through the fishway rose from 33 (1978) to 4,984 (1980), then fell to 47 in 1981. Only 7.4% (1982) and 5.8% (1983) of the offshore aggregate reached the fish processing building.
10. **Diet:** Adults ate almost exclusively zooplankton, mainly *Diaptomus siciloides* and *Ceriodaphnia quadrangula*, which made up 71% of total carbon. Larvae in the Truckee River fed mainly on chironomids and zooplankton; yearlings in Mud Lake Slough ate zooplankton (over 97% of food items).
11. **Management conclusions:** The population appears healthy in number but is at risk from a dominant single year class and sex-ratio imbalance, with recent recruitment described as "alarmingly low." Recovery depends on sustained access to the river and hatchery support, which provided about 7 million larvae annually from 1980 to 1984.
