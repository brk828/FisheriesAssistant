# Quitobaquito Pupfish: Systematics, Population Status, and Preservation (1976–1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted to the [[National Park Service]] under Purchase Order PX-8100-6-0215 by Boyd E. Kynard (University of Arizona, dated April 15, 1981), examines the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) of [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. Fieldwork ran from July 1976 to October 1977. The study covered water quality, population size and age structure, baseline pesticide contamination, refugia establishment, and morphological and behavioral comparison with Mexican populations. The author concludes that Quitobaquito pupfish are a distinct subspecies of *C. macularius* that should be protected in place.

## Background & Description

The report is the second of three studies on pupfish at Quitobaquito. Its aims were to:

- Document physico-chemical water quality at Quitobaquito pool and spring and at [[Williams Spring]].
- Estimate population size and age structure.
- Establish baseline pesticide data.
- Create a refugia at [[Gachado Tank]] and search for an off-monument refuge.
- Compare Quitobaquito pupfish morphology and courtship behavior with nearby populations.

The species was first described from the upper [[Gila River]] drainage in Arizona. [[Miller 1943]] judged Quitobaquito fish probably a distinct subspecies but did not run statistical tests. [[Cox 1966]] and [[Liu 1969]] reported only minor courtship differences from Salton Sea fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water quality was essentially unchanged between 1976 and 1977 and was rated excellent for pupfish. Spring flow measured 31.0 gal/min on August 5, 1976, and 29.5 gal/min on April 10, 1977.

**Population.** Population estimates used the [[Peterson Mark-Recapture Method]] with minnow traps and pectoral fin-clipping:

| Survey | Estimate (markable fish) | 95% Confidence Limits | Total estimate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 10–11, 1976 (Fall) | 2,325 | 1,667–3,333 | 4,078 | Submarkable fish were 43% of total |
| Apr 9–10, 1977 (Spring) | 1,410 | 863–2,733 | 1,428 | Submarkable fish were 1.3% of recaptures |

Winter mortality reduced abundance by 35.0%. Density in winter 1976–77 was well below that recorded in 1975 ([[Kynard 1979]]).

**Pesticides.** Mexican cotton fields were cleared in 1975, about 1 km south of Quitobaquito. Prevailing winds blow from the south and west, so drift is a concern. [[Organophosphate Pesticides]] (methyl-parathion) were detected in fish from both Quitobaquito and the [[Sonoyta River]]. Organochlorine residues ([[DDT]] and its metabolites) were also found in both populations, with the highest levels in Sonoyta fish. Water contained no detectable chlorinated hydrocarbons or organophosphates. December 1976 Quitobaquito fish contained about 0.03 ppm methyl-parathion.

**Habitat and refugia.** [[Dripping Spring]] was judged unsuitable because it is always shaded and very small. The monument therefore had only two permanent habitats, both vulnerable to wind-borne pesticides. The [[Sonoyta River]] population, which numbered in the tens of thousands, faced loss from agricultural development and water pumping.

## Key Findings

1. **Water quality:** Excellent and stable for pupfish, 1976–1977.
2. **Population decline:** Fall 1976 estimate of 4,078 fish fell to about 1,428 by spring 1977. Fish over 29 mm were nearly absent in fall 1976, and very few territorial males were seen in spring 1977. Adult mortality therefore probably occurred between the 1975 census and the 1976 breeding season.
3. **Pesticide exposure:** Pesticides were detected in fish, not in water, mud, or plants. The author suggests a pesticide kill of adults may explain the low breeding stock, but this could not be proven because no pre-1977 pesticide data exist.
4. **Gachado Tank refugia failed:** Restocked in October 1976 with 50 adults and 110 subadults, the tank dried out within six months because water was not trucked in often enough. This shows that "aquarium" populations depend on human water supply.
5. **Off-monument refuge:** [[Finley Tank]] on the Research Ranch near Elgin, Arizona (about 4,300 ft elevation, roughly 3,000 ft above Quitobaquito) was judged the most similar site. It is a shallow spring-fed pond of about 250,000 gal and maximum depth 2 m, with dense vegetation. Its colder winters are a possible concern.
6. **Morphology:** Discriminant analysis separated Quitobaquito from Rio Sonoyta (χ² = 76.05, p < 0.001) and from Pozo Caliente (χ² = 72.5, p < 0.001). Correct classification was 93.3% for Quitobaquito, 96.0% for Sonoyta, and 94% for Pozo Caliente. Differences were modest, with Quitobaquito fish having less deep bodies and more gill rakers and pectoral rays than the comparison groups.
7. **Courtship behavior:** Differences were qualitative as well as quantitative. Quitobaquito males do not display to females and engage only in orientation and contact movements. Pool populations (Quitobaquito and Pozo 1) lacked the displays used by stream populations (Rio Sonoyta and Santa Clara). The author attributes this to adaptation to pool versus stream breeding environments.
8. **Taxonomic conclusion:** Morphological and behavioral variation supports sub-specific status for each allopatric population. Quitobaquito pupfish are "certainly distinct" at the subspecies level and should not be mixed with other populations.
9. **Management:** The report recommends that the NPS give high priority to protecting the Quitobaquito population in place. Detailed management recommendations on refugia, pesticide monitoring, and population monitoring are deferred to [[Kynard 1979]].
