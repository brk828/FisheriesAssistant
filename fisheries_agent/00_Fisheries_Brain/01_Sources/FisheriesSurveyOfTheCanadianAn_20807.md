# Fisheries Survey of the Canadian and Cimarron Rivers Drainages in New Mexico (1953–1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This job completion report documents a three-year basic fishery survey (1953–1956, Federal Aid Projects F-3-R and F-9-R) of the [[Canadian River]] and [[Cimarron River]] drainages in northeastern [[New Mexico]]. The survey aimed to describe fishery environments and convert the findings into management recommendations. It covers the physical setting, water chemistry, aquatic plants, invertebrates, and the distribution and performance of roughly 31 to 35 fish taxa. The main conclusions are that lake habitat supplies most of the fishery potential, that water chemistry (especially [[Ph Levels]] and [[Alkalinity]]) and [[Aquatic Vegetation]] are the principal limiting factors, that hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]] often fail to persist over resident trout, and that access to private land is the main barrier to public angling.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The survey covered waters in Union, Colfax, Mora, Harding, San Miguel, Quay, Curry and Guadalupe counties, bounded on the north by Colorado and on the east by Oklahoma and Texas. The western headwaters drain the east slope of the [[Sangre De Cristo Mountains]], with elevations up to 13,100 feet, and descend to a plains region at roughly 3,000 to 5,000 feet. About 32 per cent of the area is forested (pinon, juniper, ponderosa pine, Douglas fir, white fir, Engelmann spruce). Average annual precipitation is about 16 inches, roughly 2 inches above the state average.

**Methods.** Data were compiled from the US Fish and Wildlife Service, US Soil Conservation Service, US Weather Bureau, Forest Service, and the New Mexico AWR Coordination Committee. Fish were sampled by [[Gill Netting]], electrofishing, seining, and hook-and-line angling. Invertebrates were collected with plankton nets, square-foot samplers, and Ekman dredges. Water quality was assessed through oxygen, alkalinity, pH, and turbidity. Fishing pressure was only spot-checked.

**Waters surveyed.**
- **Lakes:** More than 9,000 surface acres have fishery potential, about 2,000 acres of it trout water. Table 1 lists the lakes with existing or potential sport fisheries. [[Eagle Nest Lake]] (750 acres) and [[Conchas Lake]] (5,000 acres, a major warmwater reservoir) are the largest.
- **Streams:** More than 1,500 miles of stream occur in the drainage, but many are dry or intermittent. About 180 miles have immediate fishery potential, and about 270 miles are or could be trout water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Elevation gradient.** The survey identified a sharp break near 7,000 feet. Above this line, streams are rubble-bottomed, shaded, and suitable for trout. Below it, streams become wide, shallow, exposed, and sand-bottomed, with little fishery value except for off-stream impoundments. Flood scouring in the larger lowland rivers prevents significant plant growth.

**Lakes by type.** High-elevation natural basins (9,000–11,000 feet, up to five acres) receive snowmelt, have short growing seasons, and support trout. Lowland lakes are either large, shallow natural basins, which have little fishery value apart from bullhead, or large artificial impoundments, which provide most of the region's fishery potential. Lowland lakes are exposed to constant wind, have silt bottoms, reach 80°F at lower elevations, and are often turbid.

**Water chemistry.**
- *Alkalinity* rises from 30–60 ppm in mountain headwaters to 700–1,000 ppm in lowland lakes. In Salt Lake near Colmor, alkalinity near 1,000 ppm during the 1953–55 drought left trout blind, emaciated, and eventually extinct.
- *pH* is normally about 8.0 in lakes and streams. In lakes with dense vegetation, midsummer pH can reach 12.0, and values of 11.0 or more are not uncommon. These spikes cause partial or total trout kills. At [[Charette Lake]], a dense vegetation ring creates a lethal shoreline zone, though the open water is suitable.
- *Oxygen* becomes depleted in the deeper water of stratified lakes. No fish kill during the three-year study was attributed to oxygen lack.
- *Carbon dioxide* was never found to be critical, though the study design did not permit a detailed evaluation.

**Aquatic plants.** Stream vegetation is sparse. The most common rooted plant is water buttercup (*Ranunculus*), which can cover several hundred feet of stream bed and supports many invertebrates. Lake vegetation is either very abundant or absent. Potamogeton, Ceratophyllum, Myriophyllum, and Elodea occur in lakes. Dense growth is linked to the high pH problems above.

**Invertebrates.** [[Caddisflies]] (Trichoptera) are the most general fish food and are the principal food in many trout streams. Odonata, Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, beetles, and Diptera larvae are also common. Snails are abundant in vegetated waters, and mussels are locally abundant. Amphipods, water boatmen, and water striders are minor food items. No complete invertebrate list was compiled.

**Fish fauna.** Koster (1953) recorded eight families and 31 species in the area, and Table 3 lists the fishes found. One unidentified stickleback (Gasteroteidae) from Vermejo Park is the only addition. The fauna includes the native [[Cutthroat Trout]] and many introduced species, including [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], and [[Black Bullhead]]. Several rough fish, including [[Common Carp]], [[White Sucker]], and [[Carp Sucker]], are also present.

## Key Findings

**Trout**
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** are the most widely planted trout, with planting described as "extremely liberal." Hatchery rainbows often vanished within one to two months when planted over resident trout, and survival was low. Rainbows grow little in streams, so stream size depends on planting size. Excellent lake fishing occurs at [[Eagle Nest Lake]], [[Charette Lake]], and [[Maloya Lake]], where trout of three to four pounds are not uncommon. Natural spawning is insignificant.
- **[[Cutthroat Trout]]** are the only native salmonid and are highly prized. They rarely exceed nine inches in streams and have been reduced by competition and changing conditions. Where populations already exist, the survey recommends against stocking hatchery fish.
- **[[Brown Trout]]** are the most successful trout under many conditions and are the dominant fish in the Cimarron River, despite regular rainbow plantings. They spawn successfully in October and November, reach a peak length of seven to eight inches, and browns of three to five pounds were taken. Reaction against the brown among anglers was attributed largely to poor publicity.
- **[[Brook Trout]]** were planted widely and then dropped from favor. The survey recommends greater use, especially in small, high lakes and in cold stream sections with heavy fishing pressure, such as the Rio la Casa above impassable falls.

**Warmwater fishes**
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** are strictly a lake fish here, limited by low summer temperatures and turbidity. [[Conchas Lake]] supports the most important population (planted 1939), with year classes reaching nine to ten inches in their second summer.
- **[[Walleye]]** occur only in Conchas Lake, where stockings of 500,000 and 750,000 fry were made. Walleyes reach 11–12 inches in their first season, 16–18 inches in their second, and 18–22 inches in their third. One fish measured 25.0 inches.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** is the most widely distributed species in the drainage and the only game or pan fish that persists in many northeastern lakes. Individuals over ten inches are rare, but large populations were found in the Maxwell lakes and Snyder Lake, where water-level fluctuations are thought to cull the population.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** are highly prized but rarely planted because of limited hatchery capacity. Among 23 gill-net fish from Conchas Lake, lengths ranged from 9.0 to 16.5 inches, with the dominant group at 9.0–12.0 inches.
- **[[Crappie]]** have a significant fishery only at Conchas Reservoir. Thirty fish from three May 1953 catches averaged about 10 inches. Fourteen fish from September 1953 averaged 8.6 inches. Crappie in the Maxwell Lakes and Charette Lake had disappeared.
- **[[Yellow Perch]]** remain popular, but Charette Lake is now the only large water holding them.

**Rough fish and control**
- Three rough fish are common: [[Common Carp]], [[White Sucker]] (the most widespread and troublesome), and [[Carp Sucker]] (a local problem in Conchas Reservoir).
- Periodic [[Rotenone Treatment]] of trout lakes containing other species is recommended, since complete eradication is impossible because of large watersheds and intermittent streams.

**Management recommendations**
- Construct Department-owned impoundments where water rights are obtainable. [[Clayton Lake]] on Cieneguilla Creek has been completed.
- Acquire public fishing access on privately held streams, which are being posted at an "alarming rate."
- Plant catchable rainbows by a system based on surface area, water suitability, and fishing pressure.
- Stock high mountain lakes at 200–300 fingerlings per surface acre yearly, pending more data.
- Study better game and pan fish combinations for non-trout waters, since the standard bass-bluegill pairing is seldom satisfactory.
- Liberalize regulations: size limits were removed, creel limits on warmwater fish were raised, night fishing was legalized on certain waters, and the trout season was lengthened.
- Investigate artificial productivity increases in accessible trout lakes.
