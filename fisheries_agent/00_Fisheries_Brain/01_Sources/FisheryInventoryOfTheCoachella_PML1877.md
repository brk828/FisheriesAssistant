# Fishes of the Coachella Canal, Southeastern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #introduced-species

## Executive Summary

This study surveyed the fish community of the earthen [[Coachella Canal]] in [[Imperial Valley]], southeastern California, after the canal was dewatered in November 1980 during the replacement of its upper 78 km with a concrete-lined structure under the [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Project]]. Three sections were sampled with [[Rotenone Sampling]]. The canal supported 16 introduced fish species, with unusually high densities and standing crops dominated by [[Channel Catfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Sunfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]]. The authors predict that concrete lining will favor riverine-adapted catfishes while reducing centrarchids, which depend on emergent and submergent vegetation and spawning cover.

## Background & Description

The canal was built to convey Colorado River water from the [[All-American Canal]] and was operated nearly continuously after completion. Little biological data existed for irrigation canals in the desert Southwest, which are typically managed for water conveyance rather than as fisheries. Lowered water levels, standing water and the construction of earthen plugs made this dewatering a rare chance to sample a system that is normally difficult to assess. The study aimed to:

1. Document fish species composition.
2. Estimate relative abundance, standing crop and size composition.
3. Evaluate the fishery and predict impacts of canal lining.

Age and growth studies of [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] were reported separately.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sections.** Three sections were sampled in the uppermost 78 km:

- **Section A** (about 8.0 km below the All-American Canal): earthen, sandy substrate, a plunge pool below Check Drop 5, surface area 8,126 m², maximum depth 2–5 m. Sparse [[Common Reed]] grew along the banks.
- **Section B** (about 68 km below the All-American Canal): straight earthen reach, surface area 13,325 m², maximum depth 2.0 m. It had low habitat diversity, with scattered reed, rare [[Sago Pondweed]] and [[Watermilfoil]] on the sandy bottom.
- **Section C** ([[Siphon No. 2]]): a twin-barrel concrete box siphon, total surface area 1,023 m², encrusted with [[Periphyton]] and invertebrates.

**Invertebrates and plants.** The canal was considered depauperate in both invertebrate and aquatic plant diversity and density. Its dominant invertebrates were filter feeders or sediment-detritus ingesters. Periphyton and invertebrate standing crops were high on concrete surfaces, with densities of nearly 25,000 individuals m⁻² on concrete walls, about 15 times those on earthen segments.

**Regional context.** Many introduced fishes in the Southwest occupy irrigation canals and similar habitats far outside their native ranges. The lower Colorado River, Yuma Valley canals and Phoenix-area canals host comparable assemblages.

## Key Findings

**Methods and effort**

- Sections were isolated with earthen dikes and blocked with 2.5 cm mesh nets. Emulsifiable [[Rotenone]] was applied at about 2.5 µg active ingredient per litre (as given in the source). Affected fish were collected with dip nets and seines.
- A total of 9,105 fish was recovered.
- Turbidity and darkness limited second-day recovery. Second-day counts in sections A and B were used to estimate biomass. Biomass in the siphon is likely a minimum estimate.

**Species composition**

- Sixteen introduced species were recorded, including [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Redbelly Tilapia]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Sailfin Molly]].
- St. Amant et al. (1974) recorded 11 species by electrofishing, and 30 species are known from water supplying the canal.

**Abundance**

- Average density was 0.41 fish m⁻² (range 0.23–1.08 across sections). [[Channel Catfish]] dominated:
  - Section A: 0.120 individuals m⁻²
  - Section B: 0.209 individuals m⁻²
  - Siphon (C): 0.904 individuals m⁻²
- [[Channel Catfish]] made up about 80% of fish in sections B and C, and about 22% in section A.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] comprised 4.5% of fish in the siphon, at 0.049 individuals m⁻².
- [[Threadfin Shad]] made up 65.5% of the catch in the plunge pool below Check Drop 5 (0.363 individuals m⁻²) and were rare elsewhere.
- [[Bluegill]] were uniform across sections (0.13–0.32 individuals m⁻²). [[Largemouth Bass]] were the second sunfish.
- [[Common Carp]] (1.0–5.1% of fish; 0.003–0.056 individuals m⁻²) and [[Red Shiner]] (2.3–11.5%; 0.016–0.031 individuals m⁻²) were present in small numbers throughout.

**Biomass (standing crop)**

- Average biomass was 92.93 g m⁻² (range 49.37–102.00 g m⁻²).
- Section B had the lowest standing crop (about 49 g m⁻²).
- In section A, [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Common Carp]] made up over 74% of the 102 g m⁻² standing crop.
- The siphon held about 594 g m⁻², the highest value, with large fish concentrated there.

**Size structure and reproduction**

- Most species showed successful reproduction, especially [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]].
- [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] had significant numbers of small fish.
- Young [[Common Carp]] were absent, and [[Threadfin Shad]] showed no year-classes. This suggests immigration from the water-supply system and/or sporadic reproduction.
- Channel catfish in section A had modes at 26–28 cm and 32–34 cm total length, unlike the unimodal distribution (30–34 cm) in sections B and C. The authors suggested stocking by the California Department of Fish and Game as a possible cause.
- All fish were in good condition, with substantial visceral fat. No length-weight relationships or condition factors were calculated.

**Comparison with other estimates**

- St. Amant et al. (1974) estimated about 0.09 game fish m⁻², and Beaty et al. (1981) about 0.03 m⁻². The present data for the same species were 0.015 and 0.229 m⁻² in sections A and B, and 1.002 m⁻² in the siphon. Previous estimates therefore fall within the range found here.
- Electrofishing was biased toward [[Centrarchidae]], while rotenone sampling captured more catfish and shad. The authors concluded that electrofishing is biased toward sunfishes.
- Biomass in the Yuma Valley canals was 24.47 g m⁻² after a pesticide spill (Minckley 1979).
- Maximum standing crops in low-desert streams (for example, Aravaipa Creek, Bonita Creek, Eagle Creek and Tecopa Bore) ranged up to about 120 g m⁻². Estimates from the Coachella Canal therefore fall toward the higher end of these recorded maxima.

**Food habits**

- [[Channel Catfish]] in the canal fed almost entirely on small invertebrates (Marsh 1981), even though adults elsewhere are piscivorous.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] eat both invertebrates and fish. Other sunfishes rely mainly on invertebrates.
- [[Red Shiner]] feed on drifting material.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- The canal supported unusually high fish numbers and biomass, with species favored by anglers, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]].
- Unlined canals operated for continuous water delivery are a valuable fishery in a desert region with few alternatives.
- Concrete lining will change the habitat by largely eliminating emergent and submergent vegetation, which provides cover for fry, small fish and macroinvertebrates.
- Assuming concrete-lined canals provide adequate food, spawning sites (debris accumulation may create some crevices) and habitat, the fish assemblage should maintain high populations.
- [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes and bass) are expected to decline. Riverine-adapted catfishes are expected to remain important.
