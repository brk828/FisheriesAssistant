# Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) Element Stewardship Abstract

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Element Stewardship Abstract, prepared for [[The Nature Conservancy]] land stewards and compiled by Stephen P. Vives (Georgia Southern University; Arizona Field Office responsibility), summarizes the biology, habitat, threats, legal status and management needs of the [[Spikedace]], a small cyprinid endemic to the upper [[Gila River]] basin. The species now occupies only six percent of its former range. It is federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and is state-listed in Arizona and New Mexico. The principal threats are habitat modification combined with predation and competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]], especially [[Red Shiner]]. The abstract concludes that recovery potential is good only if suitable habitat is vigorously protected.

The abstract is a compilation of available information and does not endorse particular practices or products. The update date recorded at the end is 90-10-17.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Member of the tribe Plagopterini (spiny-rayed cyprinids). Related Arizona species include [[Woundfin]], [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] and [[Virgin River Spinedace]]. Non-native [[Common Carp]] and [[Goldfish]] also have spinose fin rays but are distinguishable by their longer dorsal fins (more than 11 soft rays).
- **Size:** Adults typically under 70 mm total length (TL); the largest rarely exceed 70 mm. Reported maximum: an 81 mm TL female (Anderson 1978).
- **Morphology:** Slim and laterally compressed; scales reduced to small embedded plates. Dorsal fin usually 7 rays with the first two spinose; anal fin usually 9 rays; pelvic fins adnate to the belly; eyes and mouth relatively large; no barbels; pharyngeal teeth typically 1,4-4,1.
- **Coloration:** Silvery sides with vertically elongated black specks; olive-gray to brownish dorsum; white belly. Breeding males show a golden or brassy sheen and breeding tubercles, which are better developed in males than females.
- **Sexing method (Abarca 1990):** Males have eye diameter greater than the distance from the folded pectoral fin tip to the pelvic fin origin; females have eye diameter equal to or less than that distance.
- **Larvae:** Nearly vertical mouth distinguishes them from other lower Colorado River cyprinid larvae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use**
- Occurs in flowing small to moderate streams, usually less than 1 m deep, aggregating in shear zones along gravel-sand bars and in quiet eddies below riffles.
- In Aravaipa Creek, common where gradient ranges from 5.7 to 8.2 m/km (Barber and Minckley 1966).
- In the Cliff-Valley reach of the Gila River (Propst et al. 1986): juveniles (26-35 mm TL) occupied mean depth 16.1 cm and current 16.8 cm/s; adults (>36 mm TL) occupied mean depth 19.3 cm and current 49.1 cm/s. Use of swifter water was higher June-November than December-May.
- Rinne and Kroeger (1988) in Aravaipa Creek recorded mean depth 20 cm and current 35 cm/s over gravel and pebble substrates (3-64 mm). Schools of 10 or more fish occupied deeper, slower water than solitary fish.
- Substrate stability is likely important during egg deposition and hatching (Minckley 1981). Populations in Aravaipa Creek increased after years of high flow.

**Associated fauna**
- Native associates include [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]].
- Historically associated species now extirpated from the Gila basin include woundfin, [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Life history**
- Breeding occurs over shallow (8-15 cm), sand-bottomed areas at moderate flow. Males are not territorial; females are chased by males, and eggs are presumed spawned when a female strikes the bottom within a male group.
- Breeding may be triggered by a combination of temperature and discharge. Spawning begins in March at about 19 °C and runs to June; Propst et al. (1986) identified April as peak, completed by mid-May.
- Sexual maturity at about 40 mm in both sexes, usually in the second summer.
- Fecundity: 80 to 300 or more eggs per female. Anderson (1978) sampled 29 females (38-70 mm TL, Gila River 10 km south of Cliff, NM) with 88-246 mature ova. Regression: number of ova = -152.85 + 5.61 TL (r = 0.844). Ovum diameter about 1.5 mm.
- Growth: about 47 mm TL at end of year one; 59-74 mm TL at end of year two. Longevity typically one to two years; few survive a fourth summer.
- Diet: primarily larval baetid mayflies, then other larval ephemeropterans, hydropsychid caddisflies, and chironomid and simuliid midges. Up to about 30% of diet may be emerging or adult insects (Schreiber and Minckley 1981).
- Movement of adults is unquantified. Larval cyprinids in the Gila River drift mainly diurnally (87% collected in noon or dusk samples; Bestgen et al. 1987).

## Key Findings

**Quality occurrence criteria**
- A quality occurrence must include time: all year classes (0 to 3+) present for at least 3 years to establish population stability.
- Shorter-term assessment criteria: (1) healthy fish of all age classes with reproductive activity; (2) habitat quality, including bank stability, riparian vegetation, substrate, adequate annual flow, and low abundance of non-native fishes; (3) absence of threats from water control structures, cattle grazing, logging and erosion-causing practices.

**Threats and decline**
- Local extirpations documented by 1937 (Miller 1961). Range reduced to six percent of former extent.
- Threats combine watershed change (damming, channelization, riparian destruction, downcutting, diversion, groundwater pumping) with non-native predators and competitors, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and red shiner.
- Red shiner is a likely causal factor: its inverse relationship with spikedace was first noted in 1953 (Hubbs 1954). Marsh et al. (1989) provided direct evidence of displacement in both laboratory and field settings.
- Natural flooding favors native fishes, which usually survive floods, whereas non-native fishes do not.

**Legal status**
- Proposed (FWS 1985) and listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act (FWS 1986).
- Arizona: Group 3 threatened species. New Mexico: Group 2 endangered species.

**Critical habitat**
- Proposed in Aravaipa Creek (24 km), Verde River (57 km), Sycamore Creek (1.5 km), and Gila River, NM segments (about 50 km mainstem plus West and Middle Fork sections). Final designation remained under administrative review; proposed habitat provides limited protection.
- A previously unknown population in Eagle Creek (Bestgen 1985) should also be protected.

**Recovery potential and priorities**
- Recovery is good only if suitable habitat is vigorously protected. Reestablishment into historic range is problematic until causes of decline are corrected.
- Monitoring: identify all populations (including Sonora, Mexico, and Forest Service, San Carlos and White Mountain Apache lands); monitor biannually in spring and late autumn with standardized techniques; start an immediate program in Aravaipa Creek after red shiner was discovered there in September 1990, and note increasing black bullhead abundance.
- Sampling: Minckley (1981) found 6 to 10 electroshocking passes in net-blocked areas captured 99% of spikedace; sample natural units (riffles, pools, runs, channels).
- Research gaps include competition between red shiner and spikedace under 20 mm TL, effects of habitat modification, movement patterns, and population systematics. Abarca (1989) and Marsh et al. (1989) found no evidence that competition for food occurred, though only fish over 25 mm TL were analyzed.
- Management needs (Marsh 1988) include protecting populations, controlling water and land use, maintaining natural flows, curtailing non-native stocking and building barriers, removing non-natives (including by piscicide), reintroducing populations using local stocks (e.g., Aravaipa Creek stock for the San Pedro), and closing immigration routes.
- Riparian management: cattle exclusion in Aravaipa Creek since 1973 was the only one of five Arizona riparian systems showing successful reproduction and broadleaf community dominance (Rucks 1984). Crown fire can fill riffles, so controlled burns outside spawning periods (autumn) are preferred.
- Flows: Hardy et al. (1990) recommend instream flows 10 to 20 cfs greater than current BLM permitted flows for Aravaipa Creek, which were based only on adult spikedace and loach minnow.
- Current programs: no species-specific monitoring or management program exists beyond legal protection. BLM has given riparian habitat its highest priority and planned to hire a fisheries biologist in 1990 based at the Safford district office.
