# Species Composition and Life Histories of Aquatic Insects in a Lowland Sonoran Desert Stream

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-insects

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Lawrence J. Gray]] (Arizona State University) describes the aquatic insect fauna of [[Sycamore Creek]], a lowland [[Sonoran Desert]] stream in central Arizona, sampled from July 1977 to 1979. The fauna comprised 99 aquatic insect taxa within 104 macroinvertebrate taxa. Life histories were documented for 17 taxa, with emphasis on developmental time, reproductive period and dormancy. Most taxa developed rapidly and reproduced continuously, and dormancy was rare. The author argues that hydrologic extremes, especially [[Flood Disturbance]] and drought, are the main selective pressures shaping these life histories in desert lotic systems.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Sycamore Creek rises at 2160 m in the [[Mazatzal Mountains]] and flows 65 km to the [[Verde River]] (430 m). The watershed is arid, with mean annual precipitation of 51 cm at midbasin and 23 cm at the Verde River; 55% falls in winter (November to April). Land use is mainly livestock grazing and low-density recreation.
- **Flood definition:** A flood was defined as discharge exceeding 1.0 m/s that scoured substrates and disrupted the biota. Floods occur about five times a year (range 1–16). Winter floods affect the whole basin, while summer floods (mid-July to early September) are patchy and storm-dependent.
- **Temperature:** Water temperature rarely left the 10–30 °C range. Diel extremes in the main channel ranged from 10–15 °C in February to 22–28 °C in August. Microhabitat differences of 6–12 °C were recorded between main channel and side pools.
- **Methods:** Benthic samples were collected weekly or biweekly with core samplers and dipnets (270 µm mesh) at main-stem sites (550–1040 m), supplemented by tributary, spring and light-trap collections. Developmental rates came from three approaches: laboratory rearing at 22 ± 2 °C in aerated aquaria, field "colonization trays" placed in the stream for 6–9 days, and timing of beetle immigration and emergence in ephemeral tributaries.
- **Background literature:** Few lowland Sonoran Desert species lists existed; cited comparisons include [[Bruns and Minckley]] (1980) and [[Lewis and Burraychak]] (1979). Life histories of Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Plecoptera from the region had not been published.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species composition and biogeography.** The 99 aquatic insect taxa (plus five noninsect groups) are comparable in richness to other North American desert streams: 69 taxa in Piceance Creek, Colorado, 56 in Aravaipa Creek and 48 in Pinto Creek, Arizona. The fauna is a mix of Neotropically derived southwestern taxa (e.g., mayfly genera *Leptohyphes*, *Tricorythodes* and *Thraulodes*; caddisfly genera *Chimarra*, *Culoptila*, *Helicopsyche* and *Marilia*; *Deronectes* beetles; the belostomatid *Abedus herberti*) and widespread Nearctic species (e.g., *Helicopsyche borealis*, *Tropisternus ellipticus*, *Corydalus cornutus*, *Trichocorixa reticulata*, all *Simulium* species, and probably all Odonata). The only Arizona endemic recorded is the stonefly *Mesocapnia arizonensis*.

**Taxonomic composition (Table 1 summary).** Diptera (28 taxa) and Coleoptera (31 taxa) dominated. Ephemeroptera had 9 taxa, Trichoptera 11, Hemiptera 10, Odonata 5, Plecoptera 2, Megaloptera 1 and Lepidoptera 1. The noninsect groups (5) included oligochaetes, a flatworm, leeches, a snail (*Physa virgata*) and mites. The author notes that the diptera count is an underestimate because many could not be identified to species.

**Temporary-water selection pressure.** Drought and flood regimes distinguish desert lotic insects from temperate and mesic counterparts. Mesic streams rarely experience hundred-fold discharge increases, and their armored substrates resist scour, so their faunas show few adaptations to such events. In Sycamore Creek, the rapid, scouring floods make the faunas' life histories strongly dependent on the predictability of climate and channel processes.

**Flood losses (Table 3).** Mean losses from nine floods (August 1977 to October 1979) were 86% across all taxa. Losses were 96% for Ephemeroptera, 74% for Trichoptera (*Helicopsyche mexicana* 74%), 78% for Chironomidae and 77% for Ceratopogonidae. Coleopteran larvae lost 89%, while adult beetles lost 0%. *Graptocorixa serrulata* lost 28% and *Abedus herberti* 0%.

## Key Findings

### Life histories and developmental rates

- **Mayflies (Ephemeroptera):** Four species were examined (*Baetis quilleri*, *Callibaetis montanus*, *Leptohyphes packeri*, *Tricorythodes dimorphus*). All showed egg-to-adult development in 8–14 days, continuous reproduction and no dormancy. Developmental rates of 180–310 degree-days were among the fastest recorded for the order; the previous shortest was 16–22 days for *Parameletus columbiae*, which has a year-long egg diapause.
- **Stonefly (Plecoptera):** *Mesocapnia arizonensis* appears to have an **egg diapause**. In April 1979 about 250 eggs were collected. Hatching ceased when maximum stream temperature exceeded 20 °C, and nymphs disappeared from substrates after April. Total developmental time was not determined.
- **Caddisflies (Trichoptera):** *Helicopsyche mexicana* took a total of 50 days (about 1100 degree-days), with egg hatching in 6 days, larval development averaging 33 days and pupation 11 days. *Polycentropus halidus* took 46 days (about 1000 degree-days). Five caddisfly species reproduced continuously; potential generation time was about 7 weeks, or up to seven generations per year. No caddisfly dormancy was confirmed.
- **Beetles (Coleoptera):** Thirty-one species were recorded. Developmental times ranged from 4–8 weeks. *Tropisternus ellipticus* took 53 ± 6 days, comparable to 45 days in Oregon. Reproduction was seasonal: of 10 species analyzed, six peaked in spring and two in late summer, and most had one (at most two) generations per year. None showed dormancy.
- **Bugs (Hemiptera):** *Graptocorixa serrulata* completed development in 21 days (460 degree-days) and reproduced continuously in the main channel, with potentially 17 generations per year. *Abedus herberti* took about 80 days (reported as 1440 degree-days in the text and 1200 in Table 2) and is the only known iteroparous aquatic insect in the stream; one female lived more than a year and produced four clutches.
- **True flies (Diptera):** Four chironomid species and one ceratopogonid were studied. Chironomid development took 9–16 days (200–350 degree-days), faster than previous family records. *Probezzia* completed development in 11–12 days. Small dipterans reproduced continuously and had no dormancy.
- **Horse fly (Tabanidae):** *Tabanus dorsifer* has the longest generation of any aquatic insect in the stream, about one year, including a **6-month larval diapause** deep in the substrate. Reproduction occurred in July and August.

### Dormancy

- Dormancy was largely absent. The only confirmed diapauses were stonefly egg diapause and tabanid larval diapause, both restricted to places where extreme conditions were absent (high-elevation tributaries and deep substrate, respectively).
- Dormancy is rare in desert lotic systems for two reasons: extreme temperature and discharge regimes, and flash-flood scour, which makes quiescent eggs and larvae more vulnerable than active adults.

### Adaptations to drought and flood

- **Drought:** Rapid development (in mayflies, small dipterans and *G. serrulata*) and habitat selection by ovipositing adults. Beetle adults laid eggs in deep pools that held water through larval development or in flowing main-channel segments, and caddisflies oviposited in upstream cobble riffles where groundwater reaches the surface.
- **Floods:** Behavioral avoidance (hemipteran and adult beetle escape by flight or swimming), rapid development and seasonal reproduction. Because immature stages suffer high flood mortality, aerial adults become the resistant life stage, and recolonization depends on adults that left before floods.

### Flood probability and reproductive timing

- Flood probability was calculated from 19 years (1960–1979) of discharge data from a [[USGS Gauging Station]] 17 km upstream of the creek mouth. For a given developmental time, the probability of successful reproduction equals the proportion of years without a flood during the larval period. For example, a *H. mexicana* female ovipositing on 1 January (50-day development) succeeded in 7 of 19 years (0.37).
- Mayflies and small dipterans (10–21 days) had probabilities above 0.5 year-round, so no seasonal selection was apparent.
- Longer developmental times (beetles and caddisflies) gave bimodal curves, with the highest success during spring drought and after summer rains. Nine of 10 beetle species had larvae present in low-probability periods, but peak larval abundance coincided with high-probability periods.
- Main-stem caddisflies were absent from all main-stem sites from November 1978 to April 1979, a period with several large floods. They recolonized in spring from Verde River populations, so they appeared extirpated during extreme years. Tributary and spring caddisflies (*Limnephilus* and *Marilia*) were more protected by their small contributing watersheds.

### Community and management implications

- Isolated floods have little community impact because adults are nearby to recolonize. Frequent floods may cause long-term loss of taxa with long development and high flood mortality, such as main-channel caddisflies.
- The author suggests that flood-frequency analyses like these can help identify critical life-history cues and predict evolutionarily recent, human-caused changes in stream ecosystems.

### Limitations noted by the author

- Diptera identifications were incomplete, so the 99-taxon count is an underestimate.
- The method assumed one flood does not raise the probability of another. This likely overestimates winter reproductive success, since winter soils absorb more water and additional rainfall produces more runoff.
- The method also assumed developmental times were constant through the year, though temperature strongly affects growth.
