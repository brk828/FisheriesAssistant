# Phylogenetic Relationships and Evolutionary Processes in East African Cichlid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Axel Meyer (State University of New York, Stony Brook) synthesises molecular, electrophoretic and geological evidence on the adaptive radiations of [[Cichlidae]] in the three largest East African lakes: [[Lake Victoria]], [[Lake Malawi]] and [[Lake Tanganyika]]. Each lake holds a radiation of several hundred mostly endemic species, making cichlids among the most species-rich vertebrate families. The paper tests whether each assemblage is a monophyletic species flock, examines the age and origin of each flock, and discusses the mechanisms of speciation, including [[Intralacustrine Speciation]] driven by lake-level fluctuations. It closes with a warning that the [[Nile Perch]] introduction has already caused the loss of about 200 endemic Victoria species.

## Background & Description

- **Diversity:** The cichlid faunas of the three lakes are highly diverse, with each lake containing a radiation of several hundred species, almost all endemic. The family is described as one of the most species-rich vertebrate families, and its radiations are among the most morphologically, ecologically and behaviourally diverse among vertebrates.
- **Key innovation:** A second set of jaws, the [[Modified Pharyngeal Jaws]], is functionally decoupled from the oral jaws. This is thought to allow specialisation on particular prey types and may confer a competitive advantage over fishes lacking them.
- **Parallel specialisation:** The Victoria endemic *Macropleurodus bicolor* and the Malawi endemic *Chilotilapia rhoadesi* both have highly derived dentition, jaw structures and feeding behaviour, and both prey on gastropods by crushing their shells. Molecular data later showed these convergences are not due to common descent.
- **Species flock concept:** Following [[Greenwood]], a species flock requires high endemism, monophyly and geographic circumscription.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Victoria:** The youngest of the three large lakes, formed about 250,000 to 750,000 years ago from two westward-flowing rivers (proto-Kagera and proto-Katonga) blocked by the uplifted western margin of the basin. Its area is 68,000 km², and it appears to have nearly dried out as recently as 14,000 years ago. Pleistocene connections with Lakes Edward, George and Kivu justify treating the flock as a "super-species flock".
- **Lake Malawi:** Estimated basin age of 1–2 million years; the southern end was arid only about two centuries ago. The lake is divided into two deep basins (up to 704 m).
- **Lake Tanganyika:** The oldest lake, estimated at 9–12 million years, with three extremely deep basins (up to 1470 m). It is a reservoir of old lineages and, unlike the other two lakes, its cichlids have left the lake (e.g. Lamprologus group in the Zaire River).
- **Lake-level fluctuations:** Droughts caused drops of up to 600 m in water level and split the lakes into separate basins, isolating populations that later reconnected.
- **Habitat barriers:** Rocky-habitat specialists such as [[Tropheus]] are effectively separated by long beaches or estuaries; genetic differences were found between populations only a few kilometres of shoreline apart.
- **Reproductive biology:** Most cichlids are poor dispersers, show philopatry and homing, and males defend territories for several years, all of which restrict gene flow.

## Key Findings

1. **Lake Victoria is a monophyletic superflock.** Electrophoresis showed a mean genetic distance of only 0.006 substitutions per locus. Mitochondrial DNA shows almost no variation: none in 363 bp of the cytochrome b gene and only about 2–3 substitutions in 440 bp of the control region. The flock is estimated at less than 200,000 years old, younger than the lake, supporting intralacustrine speciation.
2. **Comparison with humans:** More mtDNA variation has been documented in *Homo sapiens* than among 14 species from nine endemic Victoria genera.
3. **Lake Malawi is monophyletic.** Its two genetically distinct groups (about 200 species each, the rocky mbuna and the sand-dwelling haplochromines) share a common ancestor, possibly excepting the Rhamphochromis lineage. The flock is estimated at around 700,000 years old.
4. **Convergence, not common descent.** *Macropleurodus* and *Chilotilapia* differ by at least 54 substitutions across 803 bp, so their similarities are parallelism or homoplasy. Victoria and Malawi flocks differ by only about 5% cytochrome b divergence, compared with up to 11% among congeneric *Cichlasoma*.
5. **Tanganyika as an evolutionary reservoir.** The lake holds about 171 species in 49 endemic genera across 12 tribes, descending from at least seven ancient lineages. The Ectodini are about 3.5–4 million years old, and some lineages (Bathibatini, Lamprologini) may exceed 5 million years. Mitochondrial data suggest the Tanganyika flock may have arisen from a single ancestral lineage within the lake.
6. **Closest relatives.** The Victoria and Malawi flocks are closely related to the Tanganyikan tribe [[Tropheini]]. Non-endemic *Astatotilapia burtoni* was identified as the closest living relative of the Victoria flocks, and other riverine species (e.g. *Astatotilapia bloyeti*) are even closer.
7. **Speciation mode.** Intralacustrine, mainly microallopatric speciation is favoured for all three flocks. Physical separation of water masses is not a prerequisite for genetic discontinuity.
8. **Rapid speciation and sexual selection.** Five endemics in Lake Nabugabo (less than 4,000 years old) point to fast speciation. Colour differences among close relatives suggest a role for sexual selection, although its importance remains debated.
9. **Conservation warning.** The introduction of the predatory [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) has caused the disappearance of about 200 endemic Victoria species. The author warns against introducing non-endemic species into Lake Malawi.
