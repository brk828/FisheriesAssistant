# Ecotourism and Sustainable Travel in the Hawaiian Islands (1995 Magazine Feature)

#marine-biology #ecotourism #hawaii

## Executive Summary

This 1995 magazine feature, with a dining advertising supplement, describes the growth of [[Ecotourism]] in Hawaiʻi. It argues that visitors increasingly want meaningful, low-impact experiences of the environment and culture rather than only sun and surf, and that tourism itself has begun to stress island ecosystems. The article profiles several small operators on the Big Island, Maui and Kauaʻi, along with a large operator, [[Gray Line Hawaii]], that added cultural tours. It also reports survey data on rising ecotourism participation and ties the trend to conservation funding. The supplement covers local dining, and the last pages cover agricultural diversification and economic optimism on Maui.

## Background & Description

The article frames ecotourism as travel that is sensitive to the environment, traditions and culture of a place, with an emphasis on education and small scale to minimise impact. It traces the link between tourism and conservation to early conservationists such as John Muir. Megan Epler Wood of the Vermont-based Ecotourism Society International is quoted on this history.

Key background points:

- **Visitor numbers:** Nearly 6.5 million visitors came to the Hawaiian Islands in 1994. Tourism is the state's single largest industry.
- **National trend:** Less than 10 percent of American travelers had taken an ecotourism-related vacation four years earlier. By the previous year, the figure was nearly 40 percent.
- **Policy signal:** The first statewide Conference on Ecotourism was held in Honolulu in October 1994, sponsored by the state Department of Business, Economic Development and Tourism. Co-sponsors included the [[Sierra Club]], American Express, Sheraton Hotels Hawaiʻi, the Office of Hawaiian Affairs and the University of Hawaiʻi College of Tropical Agriculture and Human Resources.
- **Conference scope:** Panels covered natural and cultural heritage tourism, land use planning, public policy, natural resources management and community-based economic initiatives.

The article also covers the "Hawaiʻi Regional Cuisine" movement, founded in 1991 by a group of island chefs. It draws on local fish, tropical fruit and vegetables.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article describes several ecosystems and marine habitats that operators use for tours:

- **Big Island rain forest:** A native forest at about 5,500 feet on Puʻu ʻOʻo Ranch, between Mauna Loa and Mauna Kea. It has lava flows and an intact native canopy. Endemic plants mentioned include koa, ferns and Hawaiian raspberries. Native birds named include ʻelepaio, ʻapapane and ʻiʻiwi.
- **Whale and dolphin waters:** Humpback whales migrate to Hawaiian waters, mainly from December to April. Other whale and dolphin species are present year-round.
- **Reef and coastal systems:** Maui and Big Island operators take visitors to coral reefs, secluded bays and anchialine ponds. Guests learn about reef ecology and fish.
- **Maui diversity:** Tours cover valleys, forests, volcanic craters, historic lava flows and coastal areas.
- **Kauaʻi landscapes:** Sea kayak trips run along the Napali Coast and the South Shore. Hiking includes the Hanalei National Wildlife Refuge and Kōkeʻe State Park.

The article notes that Hawaiʻi has fragile ecosystems and that operators must "govern and limit our impact on the resource so we don't overuse it."

## Key Findings

### Operators and Their Practices

- **[[Hawaiʻi Forest & Trail]] (Kona, Big Island):** Run by [[Rob Pacheco]] and about a year and a half old at the time. Groups are limited to eight people. Tours are a three-hour loop hike.
- **[[Dan McSweeney's Whalewatching Adventures]] (Kona):** A 15-year-old operation. Three-hour cruises on the 40-foot *Lady Ann* carry an average of 30 visitors per trip. Tours run year-round. A portion of revenue goes to the [[Wild Whale Research Foundation]].
- **[[Maile Charters]] (Kawaihae, Big Island):** Owned and captained by [[Ralph Blancato]] and started in 1990. The 50-foot Gulfstar sloop takes up to six guests on half-day or longer charters. The boat serves as the research boat for the Oceanic Society.
- **[[Mango Mitch's Tropical Ecotours]] (Wailuku, Maui):** Started about five years before the article. Tours run from a half-day to four days and emphasise Hawaiian cultural history. Six percent of profits go to the [[Sierra Club]] and the [[Nature Conservancy]].
- **[[Kayak Kauaʻi Outfitters]] (Hanalei, Kauaʻi):** Founded 11 years earlier by brothers Micco and Chino Godinez. The company had grown to 150 kayaks, 15 employees and two stores.
- **[[Gray Line Hawaii]] (Honolulu):** The state's oldest and one of its largest tour operators. In 1993 it added two cultural tours, "The People and Cultures of Paradise" and "Hawaiʻi's Royal Heritage." Its sister company, Waipiʻo Valley Shuttle, runs 10-person vans to Mauna Kea observatories and [[Waipiʻo Valley]].

### Green Tourism Features

- **Local sourcing:** Operators use local produce, snacks, coffee and beef, and locally caught fish.
- **Conservation donations:** Some operators give a share of revenue to conservation groups. The article presents this as evidence that profit is not the only motive.
- **Cultural interpretation:** Knowledgeable drivers and guides are central to tour success.

### Recognition

- Gray Line's "Aloha in Motion" initiative won a [[Kahili Award]] from the Hawaiʻi Visitors Bureau in 1993 and 1994. The awards are given annually in 13 categories.

### Dining Supplement

- The advertising supplement describes a range of Hawaiian cuisine, from beachside breakfasts to plate lunches averaging about $6, Vietnamese and Thai dishes, and luaus.
- It lists about ten restaurants on Maui and the Big Island, including Avalon, Chico's Cantina, Gerard's, Hula Grill, Kimo's and Planet Hollywood. Each entry gives a location and phone number.
- Featured dishes include ʻopakapaka (snapper) and kalbi.

### Maui Economy and Development

- **Agriculture:** Amfac/JMB's Pioneer Mill planted 500 acres in coffee. Kaʻanapali Coffee completed a harvest of 100,000 pounds from 180 acres. It sold for $12.95 a pound at retail outlets.
- **Economic outlook:** A Bank of Hawaiʻi report said Maui's economic climate was "probably better than that of any other county in the state." A First Hawaiian Bank survey found 60 percent of respondents statewide expected the economy to improve, compared with 84 percent on Maui. Also, 52 percent statewide and 70 percent on Maui expected more jobs. Some 73 percent of Mauians expected their standard of living to rise.
- **Planned projects:** A task force is studying a four-year university. A $20 million [[Maui Ocean Center]] aquarium at Maʻalaea Harbor is expected within three years. A factory outlet mall is in planning.

### Overall Message

The article presents ecotourism as "the future for Hawaiʻi" (Blancato's words). Its overall message is that ecotourism can help the islands keep their natural and cultural heritage while growing the economy, provided operators limit their impact.
