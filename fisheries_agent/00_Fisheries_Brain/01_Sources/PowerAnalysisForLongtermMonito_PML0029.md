# Power Analysis for Long-Term Monitoring of Fishes in Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (NGTR 170, June 2000), prepared by [[Linda J. Allison]] for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], evaluates whether the proposed fish monitoring protocol can detect changes in fish community structure on the Gila and San Pedro Rivers following the completion of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). Using 1995–1998 baseline data from seven reaches and [[Program Monitor]] Monte Carlo simulations, the author finds that only relatively abundant or widely distributed species can be reliably tracked for trends under the current protocol. Rare species, including several associated with the CAP risk, cannot be assessed for abundance trends without substantially greater sampling effort.

## Background & Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]] was completed in 1993, creating the potential for transport of non-native fishes from the [[Colorado River]] into central and southern Arizona. A [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion (1994) concluded that CAP water delivery would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 2 directed the Bureau of Reclamation to establish baseline community composition and monitor impacts of non-native fishes in the Gila and San Pedro Rivers.

The monitored area covers six segments required by the Biological Opinion, sampled through seven reaches. The protocol uses single-pass electrofishing at three 200 m stations per reach, with fish identified to species and age class. This report is a second power analysis, following [[Wilson 1996]], and builds on the [[Clarkson Monitoring Protocol]] (1996). The report is funded through a [[Cooperative Agreement]] with the Bureau of Reclamation and state funds including the Nongame Wildlife Checkoff Fund and Heritage Fund.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** The Gila River (reaches 3–7, including sites near [[San Carlos Reservoir]]) and the San Pedro River (reaches 1–3, upstream of the Gila confluence) differ in assemblage composition. The San Pedro is dominated by [[Lepomis macrochirus]]-type sunfish and [[Pimephales promelas]]-type minnows, while the Gila supports more [[Cyprinus carpio]] and [[Gambusia affinis]].

**Assemblage structure.** Of the 17 species considered, 9 (e.g., [[Cyprinus carpio]], [[Catostomus insignis]], [[Gambusia affinis]], [[Lepomis cyanellus]]) can be effectively monitored for trends. Several species were detected in every reach (AGCH, AMNA, GAAF, LECY, PACL), while others were confined to a few sites (MIDO, PONI, PYOL, DOPE, LEMA). None of the four threatened or endangered species were detected during baseline monitoring.

**Sampling effects.** The current fixed-length protocol yields highly variable counts (N = 0 to 5,933 per station), and the report notes that instream habitat conditions, flow regimes, and gear differences influence capture efficiency. Recommended station lengths were debated: [[Lyons 1992]] suggests about 35 times stream width, while [[Paller 1995]] suggests 100–450 times.

## Key Findings

- **Baseline consistency.** Absolute counts were not consistent between years within most reaches or across reaches within years (chi-square tests at α = 0.05). Rank abundances showed more consistency, particularly among San Pedro reaches between years and Gila reaches between reaches within years.
- **Serial autocorrelation.** Detected for some species, with pair counts ranging from 1 to 15 year-pairs. Since autocorrelation was not incorporated into the model, the power estimates are likely overstated for those species.
- **Power under the current protocol (3 stations, 200 m, α = 0.05, 10 years).** Species with power of 80% or greater to detect trends include [[Agonistic Common Carp Relatives]]-level widespread species (AGCH, GAAF, LECY, PACL) and the abundant CAIN and CYLU. Trends of less than ±10% in MISA, PIPR, and AMME cannot be detected reliably.
- **Effort adjustments.** Single-station sampling reduces power for most species, while increasing to four stations adds little benefit. Doubling station length (400 m) improves power more than adding stations for ICPU, LECY, MISA, and PIPR.
- **Type I error.** Setting α = 0.10 raises power above 80% for CYCA, ICPU, LECY, and CYLU; α = 0.20 is needed for MISA. The author recommends evaluating the relative costs of Type I and Type II errors for each species.
- **Sample sufficiency.** Using a Poisson model, detecting a species at 95% probability requires about 1, 2, 3, or 4 stations when densities are 3.00, 1.50, 1.00, or 0.75 fish per station, respectively. Most species meet this threshold, but uncommon species do not.
- **Spatial scenarios.** The current protocol detects system-wide uniform trends, but an impact concentrated near the CAP confluence may be easier to detect on the Gila through reach-by-time interaction terms (ANCOVA). A hierarchical approach is recommended after data collection.
- **Recommendations.** Retain three 200 m stations per reach, or consider two 400 m stations if power for ICPU, LECY, MISA, and PIPR is a priority. Explore stratified sampling by habitat type to reduce variance, and consider tracking the number of occupied reaches for uncommon species rather than abundance trends, while recognizing that only seven reaches are available.
- **Multispecies analyses.** Multivariate methods (multidimensional analysis, principal components) can describe assemblage change, but their output lacks the proportional-change interpretation needed for management triggers. Inclusion or exclusion of uncommon species strongly affects measured association stability.
