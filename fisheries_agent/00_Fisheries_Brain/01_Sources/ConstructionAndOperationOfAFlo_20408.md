# Construction and Operation of a Floating Alaska Salmon Trap

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Commercial Fisheries Fishery Leaflet (No. 611, April 1968) by [[Fred W. Hipkins]] describes the history, legal framework, construction, operation and dismantling of the floating salmon trap, the most efficient fishing gear historically developed for capturing and impounding [[Pacific Salmon]]. Salmon traps were nearly extinct at the time of writing: the Alaska State Legislature abolished them in 1959, and only three traps remained in 1967, all operating on the [[Metlakatla Indian Reservation]] under Federal supervision. The leaflet is a practical manual covering the gear's components, its four-stage construction, its daily operation by trap watchmen, and the regulatory requirements for making traps inoperative during closures.

## Background & Description

**Origins.** The first salmon trap was built in Cook Inlet about 1885. It was patterned after Great Lakes [[Pound Net]] designs and modified to withstand strong tidal currents, becoming the [[Pile Trap]], which was anchored by driven piles in sandy bottoms. After about 1890, canning operations expanded into southeastern Alaska, where rocky, deep bottoms made pile traps impractical. The [[Floating Trap]] was developed for these conditions, and by the turn of the century it outnumbered pile traps nearly two to one. Oldtime canners claimed the design came from Norway, though this is unverified.

**Gear components.** A floating trap comprises seven parts: the lead (fence), frame, outer heart, inner heart, pot (crib), two spillers, and a watchman's cabin. It has three tunnels: one from the inner heart into the pot and two from the pot into the spillers. Key specifications include:
- Frame of spruce or cedar logs, about 110 by 180 feet, with logs of 36 to 42 inches butt diameter tapering to about 32 inches; usable for 5 to 8 years.
- Lead of 3- by 6-inch mesh No. 16 wire, anchored to shore by a 1¾-inch steel cable.
- Outer heart of 2- by 4-inch mesh No. 15 wire; inner heart and pot of 2- by 3-inch mesh No. 14 gauge wire.
- Spillers of creosoted cotton or manila webbing (No. 46 thread), with nylon noted as a more durable alternative.
- Watchman's cabin of about 8 by 10 feet, housing two watchmen during the season.

**Costs.** Around 1955, a heavily built trap cost about $25,000 and a lightly built one about $15,000; annual maintenance ran $5,000 to $6,500. Investments were typically recovered quickly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Target species.** Traps caught all salmon species but mostly pinks, chums and sockeyes; the species taken depended on location. A well-placed trap could catch up to 100,000 salmon in a single day, which fuelled the belief that traps caused declining runs.

**Conservation and migration.** Fishery biologists at the time did not yet know that salmon born in North American streams were intercepted on the high seas by foreign fleets; the controversy persisted for years. Regulations restricted spacing between traps, distance from rivers and spawning streams, seasons and weekly closures to let salmon reach their spawning grounds. **Regulatory history.** Referendum votes showed strong public support for abolition: 19,712 for and 2,634 against in 1948, and 20,872 to 3,946 in 1956. On November 8, 1958, the [[Secretary of the Interior]] announced phase-out of Alaskan traps except those owned by Indian tribes on reservations. Statehood transferred fishery control to Alaska in 1959, and the state legislature abolished all traps except the Metlakatla operations on the [[United States Fishery Reserve]], set aside by President Woodrow Wilson on April 28, 1916.

**Weekly closure method.** Under [[Federal Fishery Regulations]], traps had to be rendered inoperative during weekly and seasonal closures. Poles were fixed beside the pot tunnel, a draw line closed the tunnel floor, poles were painted red (right side) and green (left side), and serially numbered seals were affixed so the trap could not be fished without breaking them. Within 24 hours of season closure the inner heart wire was cut down, and spillers were raised to within 4 feet of the surface within 48 hours of a closed period. The [[Sunday Apron Law]] of 1924 motivated the lifting design of the inner heart.

## Key Findings

- **Legal prerequisites (pre-1959):** a trap required an unclaimed site, a permit from the [[Army Corps of Engineers]] for navigation, a Territory of Alaska trap license, and a notice of intent to fish filed with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Licence fee schedule (Territory of Alaska):** $1,200 for 1 to 3 traps; $1,800 for 4 to 10; $2,400 for 11 to 20; $2,800 for 21 to 50; $3,200 for 51 or more (pile or float).
- **Construction method:** traps were built in four stages: fabricating netting and webbing in a net loft; building the frame on sheltered tideland at high tide; hanging the compartments; and setting the trap by towing it to position, anchoring corners with anchors up to 7 tons, and releasing netting with rock-weighted lines.
- **Trap wire** is replaced annually because it is usable for only one season; cedar floats are reused.
- **Operation:** two watchmen, who did not know each other beforehand to discourage collusion, guarded the trap around the clock, removed debris, managed spiller tunnels, radioed catch estimates to the cannery, and assisted with brailing.
- **Brailing:** fish were driven into a spiller concentration area by workers on a skidded plank, then lifted with a brail into a fish scow. Salmon were canned within 24 hours of brailing.
- **Dismantling and storage:** wire netting was discarded after each season; webbing was reused for several seasons. Traps were stored in sheltered coves, often on leased tideland, to extend log buoyancy life.
- **Status:** traps were judged nearly extinct by 1968; the three remaining Metlakatla traps were the only operating examples in Alaska.
