## Executive Summary
This 1990 letter from C.O. Minckley to the Office of Endangered Species documents the first recorded capture of a juvenile [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) at the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] and the mainstream [[Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon]]. The finding signals a significant range expansion of this introduced predatory species upstream toward critical [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) habitat. The author expresses urgent concern that establishment of a reproducing striped bass population near the Little Colorado confluence—a key spawning and rearing area for the endangered humpback chub—would have devastating ecological consequences. The letter serves as an early warning correspondence urging immediate management action, including removal of striped bass and establishment of a refugium population for humpback chub.

## Background & Description
### Specimen Data (Striped Bass)
- **Species:** *Morone saxatilis* (Striped Bass)
- **Total Length:** 364 mm
- **Weight:** 496 g
- **Life Stage:** Juvenile
- **Capture Date:** May 1990
- **Capture Location:** Confluence of [[Little Colorado River]] and mainstream [[Colorado River]], Grand Canyon
- **Disposition:** Specimen deposited in the fish collection at [[Arizona State University]]; stomach contents retained by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Research Branch)

### Stomach Content Analysis
- Two [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) identified in stomach
- One additional unidentified prey item noted (species undetermined at time of writing)

This dietary data provides direct evidence of predation on native cyprinid fishes, reinforcing concern about trophic impacts of striped bass on the native fish assemblage of the Grand Canyon reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native species of concern:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endemic and endangered cyprinid reliant on the Little Colorado River and mainstream Colorado River eddies within Grand Canyon for spawning and recruitment.
- **Invasive predator:** [[Striped Bass]], an anadromous/euryhaline piscivore introduced into the Colorado River system, now documented expanding its range upstream into critical native fish habitat.
- **Observed distribution of striped bass (per boatman and agency reports, 1990):**
  - Readily caught below [[Havasu Creek]] (River Mile 156)
  - Captured as far upriver as **24 Mile Rapid**
  - A small individual reported in creel census data from the **[[Lee's Ferry]] fishery** (fall of previous year)
  - Documented being "caught at will" in nearly every slow eddy fished downstream from Havasu Creek during a concurrent humpback chub monitoring trip (ending 24 May 1990 at Pierces Ferry)
- **Trophic interaction:** Predation on native [[Speckled Dace]] confirms striped bass are actively foraging on small-bodied native fishes, a guild that includes juvenile humpback chub.
- **Habitat overlap risk:** Establishment of a reproducing striped bass population at or near the Little Colorado River confluence would place them in direct spatial and trophic overlap with humpback chub spawning/nursery habitat.

## Key Findings
- **First documented record** of striped bass at the Little Colorado River/Colorado River confluence in Grand Canyon (May 1990).
- Striped bass had already established a **broader-than-expected range**, extending from below Havasu Creek (RM 156) upstream to 24 Mile Rapid, and possibly as far as Lee's Ferry.
- Direct evidence of **predation on native cyprinids** ([[Speckled Dace]]) confirms trophic threat to the native fish community, including potential threat to juvenile [[Humpback Chub]].
- The author assesses this range expansion as **"only a matter of time"** before striped bass establish a breeding population near the Little Colorado River, which would be **"devastating"** for humpback chub and other native fishes, as well as the sport [[Trout Fishery]] at Lee's Ferry.
- Urgent management recommendation: **immediate removal of striped bass from the wild** and translocation to a hatchery, alongside establishment of a **safe refugium population** of humpback chub to hedge against catastrophic loss.
- Letter reflects inter-agency communication and concern, copied to representatives from [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]], [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]], [[Grand Canyon National Park]], and the [[Navajo Nation]] Natural Heritage Program—underscoring the cross-jurisdictional nature of the emerging conservation crisis.
