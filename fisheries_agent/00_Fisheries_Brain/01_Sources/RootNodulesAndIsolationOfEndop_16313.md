# Root Nodules and Endophyte Isolation on Artemisia ludoviciana

#botany #nitrogen-fixation #compositae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 Utah Academy of Sciences paper by Raymond B. Farnsworth and Max W. Hammond reports the first observation of [[Root Nodules]] on *[[Artemisia Ludoviciana]]* (Compositae), adding a new genus and family to the list of [[Nonleguminous Nodulation]] plants. The nodules were found in Utah, and a bacterial [[Endophyte]] was isolated from them. The authors argue that the nodules likely support [[Nitrogen Fixation]], though this was not directly measured. The paper is a preliminary report, and the authors say that laboratory, field and greenhouse work was still ongoing.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** Non-legume nodule research was largely dropped for about sixty years after [[Rhizobia]] were isolated by Beijerinck in 1888. Renewed interest followed the work of Hawker and Fraymouth (1951).
- **Known distribution:** Nodulation has been reported in more than 100 species of 13 genera in eight families of non-leguminous angiosperms. Eight genera in six families occur in the United States. Seven of those eight genera and five of the six families are found in Utah. *Myrica gale* (Myricaceae) has not been recorded in Utah.
- **Taxonomic note:** The authors state that *Artemisia ludoviciana* is one of five recognized *Artemisia* species in Compositae. It is less widespread and less well known than *[[Artemisia Tridentata]]*. It has a rhizomatous root system and produces new top growth each season, while *A. tridentata* has a taproot.
- **Distribution of the species:** It is native to much of the western United States and extends north to Canada, from California east to Montana and the Dakotas.
- **Reference tables:** Table 1 lists nodule-bearing non-legume genera found in the United States and Utah, including *Alnus*, *Elaeagnus*, *Shepherdia*, *Arctostaphylos*, *Ceanothus*, *Purshia* and *Cercocarpus*. Table 2 lists genera found worldwide but not in the United States, including *Casuarina*, *Coriaria*, *Hippophae*, *Discaria* and *Dryas*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Field sites:** Initial observations were made on May 28, 1967, at Milton Cemetery, about three miles northwest of Morgan, Utah. Further collections came from sites near Providence, Utah, and from Hobble Creek Canyon, east of Springville.
- **Seasonal timing:** Nodules were most evident in spring, when soil moisture and temperature were high. Nodulation was seen near mid-June at the later sites, when soils were drying out. By late June at Morgan, the soils had dried and no nodules were found. The nodules appeared to have sloughed off and decomposed.
- **Comparison species:** Root systems of *[[Artemisia Tridentata]]* subspecies *typica* and *nova* (big sagebrush and black sage) were examined at Morgan from June 15 to July 1. No nodules were observed.
- **Soil implications:** The authors suggest that nodulated sagebrush-associated plants could explain why western soils under sagebrush are considered more fertile and productive. They describe this as a conjecture.
- **Additional record:** On April 9, 1968, the authors collected nodules from the roots of *[[Opuntia]]* (Cactaceae, "prickly pear") in desert sands about 20 miles north of Delta, Utah. The authors present this as supporting evidence for their broader conclusions.

## Key Findings

**Nodule morphology and occurrence**
- Nodules on *A. ludoviciana* occurred in clusters of 8 to 20.
- Individual nodules measured about 3 to 8 mm in diameter. They were larger than those on alfalfa and clover and similar in size and shape to soybean nodules.

**Isolation and culturing of the endophyte**
- Nodules were surface-sterilized in 0.1% HgCl₂ for about 60 seconds, followed by ethanol and sterile water washes. Exposure longer than one minute caused complete loss of organisms.
- Crushed nodules were streaked on [[Media 79]] (Yeast Extract-Mannitol Agar) containing 0.3% yeast extract. Growth was profuse, with glistening, turbid colonies resembling rhizobial growth. Growth was almost negligible without yeast extract. Congo Red medium also supported profuse growth.

**Bacterial characteristics**
- Cell morphology varied with age, from coccoid to rod form.
- Coccoid cells measured 0.5 to 1.0 µm in diameter. Rods measured 1.0 to 1.5 × 0.5 µm.
- The organisms were [[Gram Negative Bacteria]] and motile, although flagellation type had not been determined.
- The authors state that the endophyte is bacterial. Most organisms isolated from other non-legumes were [[Actinomycetes]] or [[Streptomyces]].

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- The authors report that nodule-bearing plants can grow vigorously in root media without combined nitrogen, a capacity that nodule-free plants lack. They state that this supports a nitrogen-fixing role for the nodules, although the authors describe the importance of nitrogen fixation in this species as "only conjectured."
- Citing Stewart (1967), the authors state that nodulated non-legumes may contribute as much nitrogen to soils as leguminous crops. They also cite Allen and Allen (1965) for a review of the quantitative significance.
- The authors recommend that wild lands, rangelands and forests be managed with nodulated non-legumes to improve soil nitrogen. They expect more nodulated species to be found in other families and genera as interest grows.

**Ongoing work**
- Seeds were collected in fall 1967 from *A. ludoviciana* and *A. tridentata* (typica and nova). Progress was reported on propagation from seeds and root cuttings, and on inoculation with the endophyte.
