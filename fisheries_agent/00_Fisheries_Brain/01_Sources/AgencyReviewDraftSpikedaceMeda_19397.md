# Loach Minnow Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #stream-ecology #southwestern-USA

## Executive Summary

This agency review draft recovery plan addresses the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, secretive cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River]] basin. Listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act in 1984, the species has experienced dramatic range contraction from historical distributions across Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora. The loach minnow is a bottom-dwelling riffle specialist with reduced gas bladder, restricted to turbulent, rocky habitats with gravel-cobble substrates. Primary threats include habitat destruction through dewatering, impoundment, channel modification, livestock grazing impacts, and interactions with non-native fishes (particularly [[Red Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]]). The plan outlines comprehensive recovery actions including protection of existing populations, habitat restoration, research on non-native fish interactions, population monitoring, reintroduction efforts, and public education. Recovery objectives aim to restore self-sustaining populations throughout the species' historic range to justify eventual delisting.

## Background & Description

The [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis* Girard) represents a monotypic genus within the minnow family ([[Cyprinidae]]). The species has been known to science for over a century but remains poorly understood ecologically. Physical characteristics include:

- Elongated body, little compressed, flattened ventrally
- Eight dorsal fin rays, seven anal fin rays
- Approximately 65 lateral line scales
- Small, terminal, highly oblique mouth without barbels
- Non-protractile upper lip attached to snout by broad frenum
- Restricted gill openings
- Pharyngeal teeth formula 1,4-4,1
- Olivaceous coloration with dark blotching and whitish spots at fin origins
- Breeding males develop bright red-orange coloration on paired fin bases, caudal lobe, mouth area, and abdomen

The species exhibits sexual dimorphism, with breeding females developing yellowish coloration on fins and lower body. Maximum size reaches approximately 68 mm standard length (SL), though such individuals are infrequent. Standard and total lengths convert by the expression: SL = 0.84TL + 0.56 (r² = 0.99, n = 100).

**Historical Distribution**: Endemic to [[Gila River]] basin including:
- Arizona: [[Salt River]], [[White River]], [[East Fork White River]], [[Verde River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Eagle Creek]] and major tributaries
- New Mexico: [[Gila River]] (including East, Middle, West forks), [[San Francisco River]], [[Tularosa River]]
- Mexico: [[Rio San Pedro]], extreme northern Sonora (single record)

Transplant attempts to [[Sonoita Creek]] (1968) and 7-Springs Wash (1970) failed, with populations subsequently extirpated.

**Present Distribution**: Dramatically reduced. Believed extirpated from Mexico. In Arizona, persists only in limited reaches: [[White River]] (Gila County), North and East forks White River (Navajo County), [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal counties), [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], and [[Campbell Blue Creek]] (Greenlee County). Rare to uncommon except in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Blue River]] drainage. In New Mexico, found in upper [[Gila River]] including forks (Grant and Catron counties), [[San Francisco River]], [[Tularosa River]], lowermost [[Whitewater Creek]], and lowermost [[Dry Blue Creek]] (Catron County). Locally abundant in scattered reaches during 1982-1985 surveys; populations small in Whitewater and Dry Blue creeks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements**: The loach minnow is a highly specialized riffle obligate. The reduced gas bladder restricts the species almost exclusively to bottom-dwelling habits; swimming in swift water occurs only briefly as fish dart between positions. Occupied habitats characteristically feature:

- Relatively shallow depths with moderate to swift current velocity
- Gravel-to-cobble dominated substrate
- Turbulent flow conditions
- Elevations up to approximately 2200 m
- In some locations (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]]), association with dense filamentous green algae, though this is not universal

Habitat use varies ontogenetically, seasonally, and geographically. Larvae and juveniles occupy shallower, slower habitats along riffle margins compared to adults.

**Reproduction**: First spawning occurs at age I. Timing varies geographically:
- [[Aravaipa Creek]]: late winter-early spring
- New Mexico populations: late March into early June

Spawning occurs in the same riffles occupied by adults during non-reproductive periods. Sex ratios appear approximately equal. Adhesive eggs are deposited on undersides of flattened rocks in cavities open on downstream side with upstream portion embedded in substrate. Egg numbers per rock range from <5 to >250 (means 52-83 among populations). Individual female fecundity: 150-250 mature ova, increasing with body size. Mature ova diameter approximately 1.5 mm (larger in age II females: 1.55-1.67 mm vs. 1.44-1.56 mm in age I). Embryos incubated at 18-20°C hatch yolk-sac larvae in 5-6 days.

**Growth and Longevity**: Larvae approximately 5 mm at hatching. Growth rate varies with location, environmental conditions, and year class. Most rapid growth during first summer:
- Age 0: 30 to >40 mm SL by mid-summer, slightly >50 mm SL by year end
- Age I: average near 55 mm SL by end of second growing season
- Age II: maximum approximately 68 mm SL (infrequent)
- Winter growth negligible
- Longevity typically 15-24 months; exceptional individuals may reach 36 months
- Males appear to have higher survivorship than females

**Feeding Ecology**: Opportunistic benthic insectivores deriving food primarily from riffle-dwelling invertebrates:
- Larval Ephemeroptera (mayflies)
- Simuliid and chironomid Diptera (blackflies and midges)
- Seasonally: larval Plecoptera (stoneflies), Trichoptera (caddisflies), occasionally pupae or emerging adults
- Chironomids relatively more important for larval and juvenile fish
- Food diversity increases with fish size, but array typically small compared to other stream fishes
- Feeding strategy: active foraging among bottom substrates rather than pursuing drifting prey
- Feeding habits parallel seasonal changes in riffle-inhabiting invertebrate abundance

**Community Interactions**: 

*Native species co-occurrence*: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) often shares riffles but occupies mid-water column with minimal interaction. [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) frequents rapid-like habitats, grazing attached algae and microfauna. [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) co-occur, particularly with larval/juvenile loach minnow. These native associations represent millenia of coevolution.

*Non-native species interactions*: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) of all sizes move onto riffles to feed, often consuming same prey as loach minnow. Juvenile [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) feed in riffles during darkness and are notoriously piscivorous even when small, creating potential for direct predation. [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) occupies stream margins used by small loach minnow; now occurs in all places formerly occupied by loach minnow but is absent or rare where the native persists. Repeatedly implicated in native fish declines, though mechanism unclear. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) also occupies lateral habitats; demonstrated detrimental to native [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) in field and laboratory.

**Reasons for Decline**: Distribution and abundance changes directly or indirectly tied to anthropogenic river and landscape modifications:

*Direct habitat impacts*:
- **Dewatering**: From groundwater pumping, channelization, diversion, or damming; eliminates populations until flow restored
- **Impoundment**: Creates lentic habitat excluding swift-water species; downstream effects include altered flow regime, ameliorated flooding, thermal/chemical changes, elimination of organic drift
- **Livestock grazing**: Removal of watershed vegetation and riparian cover induces changes in runoff, suspended sediment, bedload; increases turbidity, clogs substrate interstices, enhances erosion
- **Mining operations**: Contribute toxic contaminants (heavy metals); 1977-1979 wastewater from Cananea Mine, Sonora killed all aquatic life in 100-km reach of [[San Pedro River]]
- **Agriculture and development**: Deliver pesticides, herbicides, fertilizers; alter hydrology

*Non-native fish impacts*: All places where suitable habitat remains but loach minnow extirpated now occupied by non-native fishes. Moderate habitat alteration alone appears insufficient to eliminate loach minnow except in severe cases. Native depletion/extirpation occurs when non-natives invade and establish, possibly contingent on specific habitat alterations (damming, overgrazing). Natural flooding may favor natives over non-natives; desert fishes effectively withstand such disturbances while non-native forms apparently do not.

Critical observation: Habitats unimpacted by human activities supporting loach minnow do not exist. Even [[Aravaipa Creek]] (no established non-native populations, seven native species including thriving loach minnow) has experienced grazing and water management perturbations.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Plan Structure**: The plan identifies nine major recovery tasks with detailed sub-actions:

**1. Protect Existing Populations**
- Survey unsurveyed/incompletely inventoried habitats (Gila drainage in Sonora, U.S. Forest Service lands, San Carlos and White Mountain Apache lands)
- Prioritize populations by protection need
- Designate critical habitat (proposed but deferred since 18 June 1987; proposal still in force providing limited protection)
- Enforce Endangered Species Act compliance, particularly Section 7 consultations
- Discourage detrimental land/water uses
- Ensure perennial flows with natural hydrograph characteristics
- Curtail non-native fish introductions
- Acquire important private lands and water rights
- Much occupied habitat already under federal/state/private protection (BLM, Defenders of Wildlife, The Nature Conservancy, NM Museum of Natural History, NM Dept. Game and Fish, National Park Service, U.S. Forest Service, Fort Apache Indian Reservation)

**2. Monitor Status of Existing Populations**
- Establish standard monitoring locations and techniques
- Determine natural variation range in absolute abundance and age-class structure
- Conduct bi-annual (spring, autumn) population estimates
- Monitor community composition and relative abundances
- Spring sampling assesses reproductive condition; autumn evaluates recruitment

**3. Research Non-Native Fish Interactions**
- Investigate direct interaction (predation, displacement) through field experiments and laboratory studies
- Examine indirect interaction mediated by other community members
- Experimental manipulations including species inclusion/exclusion among habitats
- Determine qualitative and quantitative significance

**4. Quantify Physical Habitat Modification Effects**
- Research substrate effects (siltation, armoring) on egg deposition, incubation, recruitment
- Determine velocity and depth preferenda and tolerance limits
- Establish water temperature optima, preferenda, tolerated extremes
- Assess synergistic/antagonistic interactions among parameters

**5. Enhance/Restore Depleted Population Habitats**
- Identify target areas amenable to management
- Determine necessary improvements (may include curtailing overgrazing, stabilizing erosion, altered timber management, non-native fish control)
- Implement habitat restoration; some reaches may "self-improve" if perturbation sources removed

**6. Reintroduce to Historic Range**
- Priority targets: [[Eagle Creek]] and [[San Pedro River]], Arizona (type locality); also [[Bonita Creek]]
- [[Eagle Creek]]: 13 individuals collected 1950, apparently suitable habitat, largely native fauna, but loach minnow apparently extirpated (reason unknown)
- [[San Pedro River]]: Type locality; species and 10 other natives extirpated over last 100 years due to habitat destruction and exotic fish introduction; mainstream and tributaries (Redfield Canyon, Babocomari River) have restoration potential
- Identify suitable stocks for reintroduction (preferably local affinities: upper Gila for Eagle Creek, Aravaipa for San Pedro)
- Assess habitat suitability, enhance as necessary
- Assess non-native fish status watershed-wide
- Construct barriers to prevent non-native reinvasion
- Reclaim (piscicide application) to remove non-natives
- Stock sufficient numbers for genetic heterogeneity
- Monitor success/failure, determine causes, rectify and restock as needed

**7. Determine Self-Sustaining Population Criteria**
- Define acceptable natural variation levels in:
  - Absolute numbers (via depletion sampling or mark-recapture)
  - Age-class structure (normal distribution with natural mortality diminishing successive older classes)
  - Reproduction (adequate reproductive stock, successful egg deposition/incubation/hatch)
  - Recruitment (appropriate offspring survival to reproduction)
- Establish minimum stock size below which populations cannot perpetuate
- Define suitable physical characteristics (depth, velocity, substrate, temperature)
- Define acceptable chemical characteristics (dissolved oxygen, toxicant levels)
- Maintain biological community composition and integrity; prevent exotic invasions
- Quantitative delisting criteria to be elaborated as new information becomes available

**8. Contingency Planning for Captive Propagation**
- Not currently necessary (species occupies several dispersed habitats in substantial numbers; protection probability high)
- However, conditions could change rapidly requiring hatchery program
- Preliminary investigations needed:
  - Identify wild stocks suitable for contribution without parent stock depletion
  - Develop holding and maintenance procedures/facilities
  - Evaluate propagation techniques (voluntary spawning vs. induced maturation)
  - Assess life-cycle requirements in hatchery
  - Supply individuals for augmentation, reintroduction, research, public education
  - Preserve wild populations against removal damage

**9. Information and Education**
- Public sector: local media campaigns, state-level (Arizona, New Mexico) and national exposure, assist Mexican agencies, open communication among stakeholders
- Professional sector: open information circulation, periodic exchange meetings, presentations at scientific meetings, peer-reviewed publication
- Designate central clearing house and repository for information distribution
- Public support critical to recovery success; derived from informed people

**Legal and Administrative Context**:
- Listed as threatened 28 October 1986 (Federal Register 51(208):39468-39478)
- Proposed as threatened 18 June 1985 (Federal Register 50(117):25380-25387)
- Critical habitat proposed but designation deferred; proposal continues in force
- New Mexico: Group 2 endangered species (prospects of survival/recruitment in jeopardy); protected from take except scientific collectors
- Arizona: Group 3 threatened species (continued presence could be in jeopardy); protected from take except angling or special permit
- Neither state specifically protects occupied habitats

**Recovery Objective**: Protect existing populations, restore depleted populations, reestablish extirpated populations, ensure opportunity for non-endangered self-sustenance in perpetuity. Realization constitutes justification for delisting. Plan requires modification as new information becomes available. Both non-native fish interaction and habitat modification must be addressed to achieve recovery.

**Critical Research Needs**: The scientific and management communities have not yet developed capabilities to examine extirpated areas or declining habitats and determine with certainty which factors are responsible. Separating habitat alteration effects from non-native fish impacts may not be possible as both occurred during approximately the same time period in most places.
