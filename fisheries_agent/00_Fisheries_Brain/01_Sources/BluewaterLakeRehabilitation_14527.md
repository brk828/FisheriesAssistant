# Bluewater Lake Rehabilitation: Rotenone Eradication and Trout Restocking (1955–1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This job completion report from the [[New Mexico State Game Commission]] documents a fish population survey of [[Bluewater Lake]] in August 1955, the subsequent treatment of the lake and its inflowing streams with [[Rotenone]] in October 1955, and post-treatment monitoring and [[Rainbow Trout]] restocking through May 1956. The pre-treatment survey found a population dominated by undesirable "rough" fish, mainly [[Common Sucker|white suckers]], [[Carp]] and a large number of stunted [[Yellow Perch]]. The lake was treated to remove them so it could be managed as a trout fishery. The report estimates a kill of 90% or better and recommends restricted bait use and periodic monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Location and access:** The lake lies about eleven miles east of the town of Bluewater on Highway 66, reached by a road passable by ordinary automobiles. Facilities include camp sites, cabins, a restaurant, a store, boats and bait.
- **Setting:** The lake sits at 7,357 ft in the Upper Sonoran Life Zone (pinyon and juniper). Higher elevations in the watershed fall in the Transition Zone, with ponderosa pine.
- **Dam and reservoir:** The dam was built in 1927 as a gravity-type concrete structure, 55 ft from outlet to spillway. The reservoir has a maximum storage of 50,300 acre-feet and a surface area of 3,021 acres.
- **Watershed:** The watershed covers 19.8 square miles. Its principal drainage, [[Bluewater Creek]], is intermittent, as is [[Cottonwood Creek]], which also enters the lake.
- **Dead storage pool:** A 1948 agreement between the Game Commission and the Bluewater Toltec Irrigation District gave the state a permanent dead storage pool 20 ft above the dam outlet. At that level the pool covers 320 acres and holds approximately 3,500 acre-feet.
- **Author and status:** Prepared by Earl H. Huntington for the period September 1, 1955 to May 31, 1956 and dated May 31, 1956.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry (August 1955)**
- Turbidity: 85.0 ppm (high), attributed to recent rain on the watershed.
- Total solids: 866.0 ppm.
- Total alkalinity: 114.0 ppm as CaCO₃, all as bicarbonate. Calcium 110.2 ppm, chlorides 36.4 ppm, sulphates reported as 294.02 ppm (OCR-garbled in source), fluorides 0.7 ppm, iron 0.5 ppm, hardness 439 ppm as CaCO₃, nitrates and magnesium 0.0 ppm.
- Color was rusty. Table I gives the analysis by the [[New Mexico Department of Public Health]].
- Field readings: pH 8.5.

**Thermal and oxygen conditions**
- Surface temperature was 68 °F and bottom temperature (12 ft) 65.1 °F, indicating no thermal stratification.
- Dissolved oxygen was 6.5 ppm at 12 ft, showing adequate oxygen at all depths.
- During the survey the water level was at the 12 ft mark, covering an estimated 200 acres, much of it shoal water 3 feet deep or less.

**Biota other than fish**
- [[Crayfish]] were numerous and apparently unaffected by rotenone.
- Trout in the lake fed heavily on [[Zooplankton]] and [[Chironomus Larvae]] (midge larvae). Copepods were present in February 1956.

## Key Findings

### 1. Pre-treatment survey (August 17–19, 1955)

Survey methods were [[Gill Netting]] (four 100-yard experimental nets with five mesh sizes, two overnight and two daytime sets) and [[Seine Survey|seining]] (three hauls with a 100-ft bag seine and one 30-ft seine).

- **Gill net catch (Table II):** Suckers 359, carp 18, black crappie 11, yellow perch 5, chub 3, pumpkinseed 2, rainbow trout 2 (one more seen but not recovered). Crayfish were numerous in the 1-inch mesh in shallow water.
- **Seine catch (Table III):** Yellow perch 677 (about 27 lb), suckers 29, one [[Largemouth Bass]] (3.5 lb), and small numbers of rainbow trout, crappie, carp, pumpkinseed, chub and [[Fathead Minnow]]s. Numerous crayfish.
- **Total catch (Table IV):** 704 game fish (32.7 lb, including 682 perch) versus 412 non-game fish (29.8 lb). Suckers were the largest non-game component by number and weight.
- The report says the population was over 90% undesirable fish, including white suckers, carp and a very large population of stunted yellow perch.

### 3. Rotenone treatment (October 25, 1955)

- Treatment followed the August survey. The lake was treated with 5.2% rotenone at 1.5 ppm. The higher-than-needed concentration was chosen because of turbidity.
- Ten areas were marked off with inflated rubber balloons. Areas 1–7 held roughly equal volumes; areas 8–10 held less because of shallow water.
- Ten boat crews covered the sections. Application began at 11:00 A.M. and finished at 3:00 P.M., roughly four hours.
- Five live boxes with various species were placed in the lake. Fish in boxes at 6 ft were dead by 4:00 P.M.; those at 11 ft were dead by 6:00 P.M. Boxes at 5 ft near the boat docks were alive at 6:00 P.M. but dead the next morning.
- The first fish affected was a yellow perch, seen at about 12:00 noon. Carp, bass, suckers and trout followed about an hour later.

### 4. Kill estimates (Table VI)

- Estimated total killed: 216,018 fish and 37,211 lb (as printed).
- White suckers: 28,020 fish, 26,899 lb (72.6% by weight).
- Yellow perch: 185,120 fish, 926 lb.
- Carp: 2,536 fish, 8,100 lb (large individuals; one 12 lb female carried 2.5 lb of eggs).
- Largemouth bass 266 (930 lb), rainbow trout 160 (30 lb), black crappie 106 (26 lb).
- Note: the component counts (about 216,208) and the printed percentages do not fully reconcile with the printed totals. The source is OCR-affected, so these figures should be checked against the original.
- Drifts of dead yellow perch, carp and suckers washed ashore on October 26 and 27. A second drift was checked on February 27, 1956, and was made up almost entirely of suckers.

### 5. Stream treatment

- About 30 miles of stream above the lake were treated, mainly [[Bluewater Creek]] and [[Cottonwood Creek]], in about two days. Pools were sprayed and the flowing channel was heavily treated at quarter-mile intervals.
- A 100% kill was not expected because of springs. Later shocking confirmed survivors.
- Post-treatment shocking in May 1956 found two sucker species and fathead minnows in Bluewater Creek about a mile above the lake. The report assumes these fish had already entered the lake after spring runoff. Cottonwood Creek produced no fish.

### 6. Post-treatment verification

- **February 27, 1956:** Live trout held at 4 ft for 18 hours (the OCR reads "1)" hours) were in good condition. Ice was about 6 inches thick, water 39 °F, pH 8.5. Crayfish were alive and copepods were present.
- **May 22, 1956:** Two gill nets captured 57 trout, 36 of them in shallow water. The report gives the crayfish count as unreadable (OCR).
- **May 23, 1956:** Seining with a 20-ft, ¼-inch mesh seine near the dock produced fingerling rainbow trout in excellent condition, feeding on zooplankton and midge larvae. Four fathead minnows and two crayfish were also seined.
- No yellow perch or carp were found in the lake or streams after treatment.

### 7. Trout plantings (Table VII)

- **Before treatment (five years, 1950–1955):** 84,664 catchable and 192,738 fingerling [[Rainbow Trout]].
- **After treatment (through May 31, 1956):** 13,250 catchable, 10,620 3-inch fingerlings, 6,120 5-inch fingerlings and 150,000 fry. Of these, 2,000 were fin-clipped trout averaging 5.13 inches and 0.97 oz to track growth rate.

### 8. Conclusions and recommendations

- Temperature and other data suggested the lake would be suitable for trout once undesirable species were removed. The report assumes a kill of 90% or better.
- **Fishing restriction:** No fishing with live minnows in Bluewater Lake or the streams above it.
- **Monitoring:** Periodic checks of trout growth, condition and stocking needs, plus physical and chemical water checks to manage the lake for sport fishing.
- **Fathead minnow watch:** Their numbers could rise quickly and compete with trout for food, so populations should be checked as they invade.
- **Crayfish:** They appear unaffected by rotenone, do no apparent harm, and may provide a small amount of food for trout.
