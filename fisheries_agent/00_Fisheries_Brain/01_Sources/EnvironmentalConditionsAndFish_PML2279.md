# Environmental Conditions and Fish Faunas in Low-Elevation Rivers of the Irrigated San Joaquin Valley Floor, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1984 survey of the [[San Joaquin River]], the [[Merced River]] and [[Salt Slough]] compared physicochemical conditions and fish faunas at upstream sites (above most irrigated cropland) with those in irrigated reaches on the valley floor. Monitoring ran from July 1980 to November 1981 at nine sites, using seines and backpack electrofishing. Nearly all water-quality variables changed longitudinally, mainly in response to [[Irrigation Return Flows]]. Fish assemblages differed between upstream and downstream sites, with native species concentrated upstream and introduced species dominant downstream. The authors conclude that irrigation has probably affected fish distribution and abundance, and they call for research on the toxic components of return flows.

## Background & Description

Dam construction, water withdrawals, and municipal and agricultural contamination have altered fish communities in the [[San Joaquin Valley]], but the full extent of change was unknown. Earlier work focused either on foothill streams and reservoirs above 90 m elevation or on the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]]. The low-elevation valley-floor rivers linking these two habitats had not been studied. Irrigated agriculture is the dominant land use on the valley floor, and during the irrigation season (usually March to October) return flows can make up most of the discharge of the rivers.

The study had three objectives: (i) compare the physicochemical environment of valley-head rivers with that of irrigated reaches; (ii) determine the occurrence and relative abundance of fishes at each location; and (iii) relate measured conditions to the broad habitat requirements of fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Six sites were sampled on the San Joaquin River, two on the Merced River and one on Salt Slough, at elevations of 3 to 79 m. The San Joaquin and Merced rivers rise in the [[Sierra Nevada]] above 3,500 m, fed by snowmelt and rainfall. Downstream, natural seepage and irrigation return flows supply a growing share of flow. Salt Slough arises on the valley floor and is driven almost entirely by seepage and return flows.

**Methods.** Water temperature, turbidity, discharge, dissolved oxygen, pH, total alkalinity and conductivity were measured monthly. Macronutrients and bottom-sediment particle size were measured every three months. Fish were collected with a 5.5 m seine (9.5 mm mesh) and a 30.5 m seine (12.7 mm mesh), and by backpack electrofishing at upstream sites only, since other sites were too turbid and saline. Community similarity between sites was assessed with [[Jaccard Similarity Index]], with values above 0.65 treated as significant. Relative abundance was rated as rare (0.1 to 1.0% of mean catch), common (1.1 to 7.0%), or abundant (>7.0%). The authors note that gear selectivity underrepresented burrowing species (lampreys, sculpins) and fast swimmers in deep channels (adult striped bass, chinook salmon).

**Physicochemical gradients.** Temperature was the only variable with a consistent seasonal pattern, lowest in December-January and highest in July-September. Temperature in the San Joaquin River peaked at SJR-3 and then fell from SJR-4 to SJR-5, coinciding with inflows of cooler water from Salt Slough and the Merced River. Turbidity rose sharply downstream, more than sevenfold in the San Joaquin River at SJR-4 but less than threefold in the Merced River at MR-2. Turbid inflows came from the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] and agricultural drains. Dissolved oxygen was highest at SJR-1 (111% saturation at 11.9 mg/l, according to the reported values) and fell downstream. pH ranged from 7.8 to 8.3 with no clear spatial pattern. Total alkalinity and conductivity increased from upstream to downstream. Conductivity in the San Joaquin River rose more than 30-fold (as reported) between SJR-1 and SJR-4, then roughly halved by SJR-6. Macronutrients (NH3-N, NO3+NO2-N, total N, ortho-PO4 and total P) generally increased downstream. Upstream sediments were dominated by pebbles and rocks, whereas silt and sand prevailed downstream.

## Key Findings

- **Catch:** 43,605 fish representing 33 species (excluding hybrid sunfish) from 14 families were collected.
- **Species richness:** Richness per site ranged from 17 (SJR-3) to 25 (SJR-5). Richness did not differ significantly among the nine sites (χ² = 3.55, d.f. = 4, P > 0.05).
- **Native proportion:** Native species made up a larger share of the fauna upstream (30% at SJR-1, 35% at MR-1) than downstream (12% to 23% at most downstream sites). Salt Slough held only one native species among 18. The differences were not statistically significant (χ² = 8.16, d.f. = 8, P > 0.05).
- **Community similarity:** Fifteen pairs of sites exceeded the Jaccard threshold of 0.65. The two upstream sites shared more species with each other than with downstream sites. Downstream sites grouped together, while MR-2 was similar to neither group.
- **Upstream-associated species:** [[Sculpin]], green sunfish, redear sunfish, [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Hardhead]] and [[Threespine Stickleback]] were captured mainly or exclusively upstream. Hardhead and threespine stickleback occurred only at upstream sites.
- **Downstream-associated species:** Inland silverside, white crappie, threadfin shad, fathead minnow, splittail, Sacramento blackfish, [[Tule Perch]] and striped bass were recorded only downstream. Chinook salmon appeared only in the Merced River, from October through April.
- **Dominant taxa:** Sacramento sucker, green sunfish, [[Bluegill]], redear sunfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], sculpins and Sacramento squawfish were most abundant at SJR-1 and MR-1. Introduced species (inland silverside, bluegill, largemouth bass, black crappie, threadfin shad, fathead minnow, mosquitofish) were especially abundant downstream.
- **Transitional site:** MR-2 supported both upstream and downstream elements, which the authors interpret as a transition zone between the two fauna types.
- **Interpretation:** Irrigation return flows, turbid canal inflows and reduced upstream discharge appear to have altered both the physicochemical environment and fish composition. Fishes typical of clear, cool, structurally complex foothill streams are being replaced by tolerant introduced species in warm, turbid, nutrient-rich water.
- **Recommendations:** Further work should identify the toxic components of return flows and measure their effects on fish survival, growth, reproduction and behavior, including food availability, predation and competition. Such research would support more objective decisions on irrigation drainage projects.
