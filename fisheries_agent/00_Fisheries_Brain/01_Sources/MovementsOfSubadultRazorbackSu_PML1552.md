# Movements of Sub-Adult Razorback Suckers in a Regulated Colorado River Reach, Revisited

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This interim progress report (Report 3) presents a 2000 radiotelemetry study of hatchery-reared, sub-adult [[Razorback Sucker]] released into the regulated [[Colorado River]] below [[Parker Dam]]. The study tracked the dispersal, habitat use, and potential [[Entrainment]] of stocked fish within a 23-km reach ending at [[Headgate Rock Diversion Dam]]. Twenty fish were tagged with external radio transmitters in April 1999 and released at two sites. Contact was maintained with 16 fish for up to 141 days. None of the fish was suspected of passing the dam or entering the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes Main Canal]], and no mortalities were confirmed. The authors suggest that the reach may suit stocked fish in cooler months but not in hot summers, when high water temperatures, motorized watercraft disturbance, and scarce refuge reduce its suitability.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endangered fish endemic to the Colorado River system of western North America. A large remnant population occurs in [[Lake Mohave]], a small population persists in the Green River, and scattered individuals are found elsewhere, including the lower Colorado River. The species is not known to recruit successfully in nature, so wild stock maintenance is expected to depend on aggressive [[Stocking Programs]].

A [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS 1997) on [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR) operations found jeopardy for the species. Among its Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives were reintroduction below Parker Dam and determination of entrainment risk at water management structures. No earlier razorback sucker telemetry study had specifically examined entrainment.

The study's purpose was to improve razorback sucker management by documenting dispersal and entrainment of stocked sub-adults. Cooperators included USBR, USFWS, and the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Biological Resources Division (USGS-BRD).

**Study area:** The 14.4-mile (23.2-km) "Parker Strip" reach, from Parker Dam (River Mile 192.2) to Headgate Rock Diversion Dam (RM 177.8), lies within the [[Havasu Division]]. It is strongly flowing, has few protected off-channel areas, and is one of the most heavily developed recreational areas on the lower Colorado River. Daily discharge averaged 12,550 cfs and ranged from 8,500 to 15,000 cfs during the 141-day study (30 April to 17 September 1999). Channel width is about 50 to 175 m, and depth reaches about 8 m near the dams. [[Moovalya Lake]] is a broad, slow-flowing impoundment behind Headgate Rock Dam. Shoreline modification with riprap, retaining walls, and artificial embayments is extensive.

**Methods:**
- **Source fish:** Sub-adults were raised by the [[Native Fish Work Group]] at Boulder City wetland ponds, Nevada. They were descended from wild Lake Mohave larvae collected in winter-spring 1999.
- **Transport:** Fish were trammel netted on 28 April 1999 and trucked on 29 April in water at about 13°C, dosed with MS-222 (5 mg/L) and sodium chloride to reduce stress.
- **Tagging:** Twenty fish received externally mounted transmitters ([[Radio Telemetry]]; 13 × 45 mm cylinders, 9 g in air, 25-cm antenna, 40–42 MHz, nominal battery life 90 days). Steel wires passed through the dorsal musculature. Each fish also received a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT) tag. Mounting took 1 to 2 minutes without anesthesia.
- **Release:** Ten fish were released into a lagoon at [[River Lodge Resort]] (California side, about RK 4.0 below Parker Dam). The other ten were released into a lagoon at [[La Paz County Park]] (Arizona side, RK 11.5).
- **Monitoring:** Three fixed receivers, including one at Headgate Rock Dam, one at the CRIT Main Canal intake, and one facing Moovalya Lake, ran continuously. A mobile receiver was used by boat, weekly from release until 16 July and then biweekly until 17 September 1999. Each reach was traversed at least twice per visit, day and night.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Vegetation changed through the season. Aquatic macrophytes were sparse in late April, increased through summer, and peaked around mid-July. [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) formed dense marginal beds along the lower 15 km and throughout Moovalya Lake. Quiet margins and backwaters held dense stands of giant reed (*Phragmites australis*) and cattail (*Typha domingensis*). Water clarity was about 2 m upstream and about 1 m or less downstream, where recreational watercraft disturbed softer bottoms.

**Canal system:** The CRIT Main Canal and its laterals are strongly flowing, concrete-lined channels with little habitat diversity. They were inspected by vehicle on 15 June 1999.

**Human disturbance:** Motorized watercraft, high summer water temperatures, and limited refuge were identified as stressors that may reduce the reach's suitability for stocked fish during summer.

**Comparative context:** The authors cite [[Minckley 1979]] for the lower Colorado River as a diverse and complex aquatic system.

## Key Findings

**Survival and signal retention**
- Twenty sub-adults were tagged and released. Contact was maintained with 16 fish for up to 141 days.
- Two signals disappeared immediately or the day after release, and two more were lost within 6 days. The loss causes are unknown. Possibilities include transmitter failure, radio interference near Parker Dam, fish death and removal, or other factors.
- No mortalities were confirmed. Fish repeatedly located in the same area were judged active.
- Mobile monitoring produced 196 contacts in total.
- Signals from 15 transmitters persisted to the nominal 90-day battery life (about 1 August 1999). Battery expiration was the probable cause of later contact loss.

**Movement patterns**
- Most fish initially stayed near their release sites.
- Nine fish dispersed downstream, three moved upstream, and six remained near their release sites.
- All fish stayed within the 23-km study reach.
- No fish was suspected of passing over or through Headgate Rock Dam or of entrainment into the CRIT Main Canal.

**Comparison with August 1998**
- The 1998 study found up to 65% of fish passed downstream below Headgate Rock Dam. In 1999, none did.
- The authors attribute the difference to seasonal conditions: cooler spring releases may suit stocked fish better than summer releases, which face high water temperatures, watercraft disturbance, and scarce refuge.
- They recommend stocking during cooler months to enhance repatriation success.

**Fixed-monitor performance**
- Two of three fixed monitors detected radio signals, but none could be attributed to tagged fish.
- The CRIT Main Canal monitor often detected broadband interference. Signals matching tag frequencies and pulse rates were confirmed elsewhere by mobile tracking, so these data were discarded.
- The Headgate Rock monitor detected several 40-pulse-per-minute signals that matched four fish later located upstream of the dam, near it. Other unverified signals were treated as noise.

**Fish near Headgate Rock Dam**
- Four of 20 fish (20%) were located within 1 km of Headgate Rock Dam by both fixed and mobile monitors. These fish stayed in the Moovalya Lake area for more than a month, but none crossed the dam or entered the canal.
- The authors suggest fish may have avoided entrainment, though the sample is too small for firm conclusions.

**Individual histories (selected)**
- **RL-601** (River Lodge): Stayed within 300 m of the release site, moving between the river and lagoon. It used a pond connected to the lagoon during peak releases. Its tag expired after day 92.
- **RL-11, RL-21, RL-31, RL-41:** Dispersed short distances up- or downstream and stayed within 10 km. Several were located near docks, riprap, or concrete walls.
- **RL-51 and RL-71:** Moved into Moovalya Lake. From June 1999 they were located in the new docking area of the [[Blue Water Casino]] (RK 22.0), where operators removed vegetation to allow boat access.
- **RL-91:** Repeatedly returned to the lagoon at the release site and remained there until day 141.
- **LP-701, LP-711, LP-741, LP-852:** Moved over distances of several kilometers. LP-852 was found in Moovalya Lake and later in the casino docking area.
- **LP-721:** Dispersed at least 3.75 km upstream. Contact was lost after day 22.
- **LP-872 and LP-882:** Contact was lost after days 2 and 4, respectively, and their fate is unknown.
- **LP-92:** Remained at the release site, with 15 contacts through day 141. The signal indicated activity, ruling out a dead fish or shed tag.

**Limitations and recommendations:** The small sample size limits firm conclusions. The authors recommend repeating the work with stocking during cooler months to test whether seasonal timing improves repatriation success. The report also notes that stocked fish may be exposed to [[Predation]] by introduced ictalurids, as documented in earlier work by [[Marsh and Brooks 1989]].
