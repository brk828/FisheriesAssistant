# Razorback Sucker Status, Reproduction, and Recruitment in Senator Wash Reservoir, CA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under a cooperative agreement with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (authors Anne E. Kretschmann and Laura L. Leslie, dated October 13, 2006), assesses the status of endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], California. The study combined larval light trapping, electrofishing, trammel netting, PIT-tag mark-recapture, and pectoral fin-ray aging from 2002 to 2004. Adult razorback suckers were present and spawning was observed, but no larvae, young-of-year, or juveniles were captured, and fin-ray ages showed no evidence of recruitment. The authors conclude the reservoir will not sustain a self-recruiting population, though it may serve as a grow-out site for stocked fish.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is an endangered catostomid native to the [[Colorado River]] basin. Its decline is attributed to habitat alteration, changes in water quality, competition with and predation by nonnative fishes. It was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1991, a draft recovery plan was issued in 1993, and critical habitat was designated in 1994. Remaining lower basin populations occur in Lake Mohave, Lake Mead, Lake Havasu, Senator Wash Reservoir, and the Colorado River below Davis Dam, and reservoir populations consist of old pre-impoundment adults plus hatchery-stocked fish.

Senator Wash Reservoir was completed in 1966, covers about 470 acres (57.97 hectares), and is used for pump-back irrigation storage. Water levels fluctuated up to 2 m overnight during the study. The eastern side is 7 to 15 m deep with medium to large cobble and gravel substrate, while the shallower western side (1 to 5 m) is silt-dominated with emergent vegetation ([[Typha]], [[Scirpus]], [[Tamarix]]) at its margins. Recorded water temperatures ranged from 6.6 to 31.1 °C. Non-native fishes present include [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]], and various sunfishes (*Lepomis* spp.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Razorback suckers were first studied in the reservoir by Ulmer et al. in 1980, when the population was estimated at 54 ± 22 adults. Spawning was documented and stocking began. Approximately 4,857 fish were stocked between 1987 and 1990, mostly offspring of [[Lake Mohave]] broodstock from the Niland Native Fish Ponds, plus one group reared at Blythe Golf Course Ponds from reservoir-collected eggs. Adults and larvae were found in 1999 and 2001, and the report notes three possible explanations: surviving 1985 fish were still reproducing, recruitment from earlier spawning or stocked fish, or larvae entrained from the mainstem Colorado River.

Spawning occurred from late November through early May, peaking December through March, in water temperatures of 6.6 to 25.5 °C, over mixed medium cobble and gravel with slopes of 30 to 45%. Spawning areas coincided with those identified in the 1970s and 1980s. The authors suggest that early spawning (before other species) exposes larvae to predators, and that water-level fluctuation can desiccate eggs and stranded larvae. Spawning fish were observed in water less than 2 m deep, within the zone affected by drawdown. Large-bodied predators such as [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] occur in spawning and rearing habitat, while the report notes that most predatory fish in the reservoir are relatively small.

## Key Findings

- **Spawning:** Razorback sucker spawning behavior was observed at several locations, with peak activity December through March. Ripe fish were captured during spawning; trammel net catches were highest in January and February.
- **Larval fish:** Larval light trapping (ten traps, January to May 2004) captured 271 larval fish, dominated by Common Carp (149; 55.1%) and Threadfin Shad (36; 13.3%). No razorback sucker larvae were collected.
- **Community structure:** Largemouth Bass and bluegill were the most common species in electrofishing. Carp were the most abundant species and razorback suckers the second most abundant in large-mesh trammel nets in 2004.
- **Catch rates:** Electrofishing CPUE for razorback sucker was 1.7 (SE 0.8) in 2002, 1.5 (SE 1.0) in 2003, and 0 in 2004. Large-mesh trammel netting CPUE was 1.6 (SE 0.3) in 2003 and 3.0 (SE 0.5) in 2004, a significant difference (ANOVA, F1 = 5.62, P = 0.02). No young razorback suckers were captured in small-mesh nets (CPUE 0.2, SE 0.1, in 2004).
- **Size structure:** Adults ranged from 445 to 679 mm total length, forming two length classes: 445 to 610 mm (presumed males) and 620 to 679 mm (presumed females). Fish under 520 mm might be recruits, but fin clips were not collected from this group.
- **Population estimate:** A closed-population multiple mark-recapture analysis (program CAPTURE) of 127 adults (125 tagged, 48 recaptures, 181 total captures) estimated the population at 280 fish (95% CI 212 to 400; SE 46.6). Mean capture probability was 2.22%, and the coefficient of variation was 16.63%.
- **Survival:** Based on 4,857 fish stocked and the estimate of 280 adults, approximately 6% of stocked razorback suckers survived, compared with 0.1% in the mainstem Colorado River. The authors note the 6% value is conservative because of unaccounted removals and possible pumping losses through trash racks.
- **Age:** Fin-ray ages ranged from 12 to 29 years (minimum 12, maximum 29 by the table). No fish younger than the latest stocking were aged, and no evidence of recruitment was found, although not all size classes were sampled.
- **Management implications:** Senator Wash Reservoir is unlikely to sustain a self-recruiting population because eggs and larvae are not surviving, due to predation and water-level fluctuation. The reservoir may nonetheless serve as a grow-out site for stocked fish, since most predators there are small. Suggested improvements include stabilizing water levels and providing larval refuge such as inundated artificial structures or floating vegetation, drawing on evidence that inundated vegetation in [[Lake Mead]] improves larval and juvenile survival.
