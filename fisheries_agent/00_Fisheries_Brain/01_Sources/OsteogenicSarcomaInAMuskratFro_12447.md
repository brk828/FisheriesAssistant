# Osteogenic Sarcoma in a Muskrat from an Area of High Environmental Radiostrontium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cricetidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 case report, published in the *Archives of Pathology*, describes an osteogenic sarcoma of the right tibiofibula in a wild adult female [[Muskrat]] (*Ondatra zibethica*) captured in the [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]] X-10 Area, Tennessee. The tumor was a sclerosing type with metastases to both lungs and both kidneys. Radiochemical analysis showed that the bones contained high levels of [[Radiostrontium]] (Sr-90) and its daughter [[Radioyttrium]] (Y-90). The authors judged a high probability that radiostrontium absorbed from contaminated food plants caused the tumor. The report is a case study rather than a field survey, and the document contains no fish data. Its aquatic relevance lies in the animal's semiaquatic diet, which included aquatic plants and, occasionally, fish, frogs and mussels.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** An adult female muskrat, weighing 1,325 g, was not pregnant. A second female caught nearby on March 10, 1953 carried three nearly full-term young.
- **Capture:** The animal was caught by plant guards on March 15, 1953, and was found to have an injured, largely useless right hind leg. Health physics personnel found it contaminated.
- **Natural occurrence:** Wild-animal osteogenic sarcomas are rare. Prior cases include a baboon (*Papio porcarius*) and domestic animals such as horses, cows, dogs, sheep, cats and chickens.
- **Radiation-induced precedents:** Radium-induced osteogenic sarcomas were documented in [[Radium Dial Painters]] and in rats fed radium (9 of 13 rats developed tumors, appearing 253 to 426 days after dosing). Radiostrontium-induced bone tumors were reported in rats and in mice fed an Sr-90 diet, with incidence rising with dose.
- **Species ecology:** The muskrat is a common semiaquatic mammal of most of the United States and Canada. It feeds mainly on stems and roots of aquatic vegetation, and in emergencies it eats fish, frogs, mussels and other aquatic animals. In Tennessee, breeding runs from late February to midsummer, with two to three litters per season of 1 to 11 young.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Site:** The X-10 Area of Oak Ridge National Laboratory, a long-running study area with abundant food plants that contain relatively large amounts of radioelements. The plants had persisted there for years and appeared healthy.
- **Plant bioconcentration:** Samples of cattail, wild lettuce and curly dock were compared with water in which they grew (16,200 disintegrations per minute (dpm) per gram). Cattail leaves ranged from 7,700 to 17,400 dpm/g and curly dock hearts reached 231,000 dpm/g. Plants concentrated radioelements in amounts and percentages that differed from the water.
- **Radioelement composition:** Across 12 plant samples, strontium averaged about 66% of radioactivity and rare earths about 24%. The authors estimated that about one-third of the strontium was Sr-90 and the rest Sr-89.
- **Muskrat tissues:** Bone samples showed 2,410,000 to 2,810,000 dpm/g. Soft tissues were not considered harmful. Bone, toenails and trachea carried unusually high activity. Toenails and trachea had 0.35 and 0.22 µc/g, and the left metatarsal bone had 1.0 µc/g of activity.
- **Whole-skeleton estimate:** Nearly 100 µc of radiostrontium were estimated for the whole skeleton.
- **Dose rate:** Autoradiography indicated a bone dose of at least 1.5 rep per hour (40 rep per day). Exposure duration and the exact source of the strontium are unknown.

## Key Findings

1. **Tumor type and location:** A sclerosing osteogenic sarcoma arose outside the medullary cavity of the right tibiofibula. It was grossly a 6 × 6 cm mass around the proximal bone, and it did not reach the distal end.
2. **Metastases:** Tumor masses were found in both lungs (more than half of lung tissue functionally impaired) and both kidneys (at least one-fourth nonfunctional). Some metastases occluded glomerular capillaries as emboli.
3. **Absence of radiostrontium in tumor:** Autoradiography showed no radiostrontium deposition in the neoplastic osteoid. The authors attributed this to tumor cell differentiation rather than diet, since osteoid normally takes up bone-seeking elements.
4. **Causal inference:** The authors judged it highly probable that the radiostrontium in the bone caused the sarcoma, based on experimental results and the large residual skeletal activity.
5. **Growth-plate change:** A moderate "severance" between the epiphyseal plate and spongiosa resembled changes reported in cattle and rats treated with Sr-90. No hemopoietic centers were found in the affected bone.
6. **Source and conclusion:** The muskrat ingested radiostrontium through contaminated food plants. The authors describe the case as unique because the tumor and the high environmental and skeletal radiostrontium occurred together.
