# Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This species profile from the *Arizona Riparian Council* (Vol. 8, No. 3, 1995) by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] describes the [[Razorback Sucker]], a long-lived fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has declined sharply because of dams, water diversions, and introduced [[Non-Native Predators]]. Its only sizable wild population is in [[Lake Mohave]], where larvae disappear before reaching maturity. Conservation since 1973 has relied on capturing wild larvae, rearing them in predator-free backwaters, and releasing [[Electronic Tagging|electronically tagged]] juveniles. The authors describe this as limited but positive progress, not yet a success.

## Background & Description

- **Range:** Historically occupied much of the Colorado River system, from the delta in Mexico to the Rocky Mountain headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado, including the Salton Sea, Gila, and San Juan basins. It is endemic to the Colorado River drainage.
- **Historical abundance:** Uncertain. Native Americans and settlers ate the fish, and settlers described large spawning concentrations in the Salt and Colorado rivers. A commercial fisherman was still selling razorbacks from [[Saguaro Lake]] on the [[Salt River]] as late as 1949.
- **Growth and size:** Often exceeds 1 ft in the first year and reaches about 2.5 ft as an adult.
- **Longevity:** More than 50 years.
- **Fecundity:** About 5,000 eggs per inch of body length, up to 120,000 eggs in a 24-inch female.
- **Diet:** Drifting organic material and small plants and animals from the bottom. In Lake Mohave, adults also feed on [[Plankton]] in midwater and on rocks along wave-washed shores.
- **Breeding coloration and structures:** Males develop dark backs, yellow bellies, white ventral fins, and often reddish side bands. Males and some females grow tubercles on tails and anal fins that help them grip during spawning.

### Spawning Behavior

- Spawning runs from January through early May, with timing varying by year.
- Females stay offshore feeding until their eggs swell, then move inshore. Several males flank each female, and the group stirs the substrate into a shallow depression.
- Adhesive fertilized eggs settle among stones. Females may spawn repeatedly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Larval development:** Larvae hatch within about a week and rely on yolk at first. They begin feeding at less than 0.4 inches on tiny plants and insect larvae, then move to larger plankton.

**Larval loss in Lake Mohave:** Hundreds of thousands of larvae hatch there each year, yet no evidence shows any reaching maturity. The youngest adults hatched around 1954, when [[Lake Mohave]] was filled. Larvae vanish before reaching 0.5–0.6 inches.

**Competing explanations** (tested by the authors):
1. Larvae killed passing through dam [[Turbines]]: no supporting evidence found.
2. Starvation due to absence of a particular food size or type in the reservoir: some support.
3. Predation by non-native game fish: considered the most important factor.

**Management tools:**
- [[Phototaxis|Phototactic]] larvae move toward light at night, so they can be netted by boat-mounted lights or trapped in containers.
- Captured larvae are reared in aquaria for about two weeks, then placed in predator-free [[Isolated Backwaters]] treated to remove non-native fish.
- Under good conditions, fish reach about one foot in roughly six months before release.

## Key Findings

- The razorback sucker has declined from widespread historical abundance to a single sizable wild population, in Lake Mohave.
- Non-native predators are considered the most important cause of larval loss in Lake Mohave, based on experimental tests.
- Removing larvae from the reservoir and rearing them in isolated, predator-free backwaters has produced some positive results.
- Since 1973, students, volunteers, university professors, and state and federal fisheries biologists have contributed to recovery efforts. The authors say the species "may not be doomed to extinction," but the effort is not yet a success.
- Recommended further reading: Minckley et al. (1991), "Management toward recovery of the razorback sucker," in *Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West* (University of Arizona Press, pp. 303–357).
