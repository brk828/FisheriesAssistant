# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Phase II: Native and Endangered Fish Research Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation (August 1990) is the Native and Endangered Species section of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase II Integrated Research Plan, together with several technical and budget proposals that support it. The work examines how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations affect the remaining native fish of the Colorado River in Glen and Grand Canyons, with the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] as the central species. It is organized around seven conservation measures agreed by a consultation team following the 1978 jeopardy opinion on dam operations. The plan covers taxonomy, hatchery propagation, flood-release frequency, a [[Little Colorado River]] management plan, impact research, and long-term monitoring. Proposals from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Pinetop office), [[Arizona State University]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), and a Bureau of Reclamation contract solicitation describe field and laboratory work that runs from 1990 to about 1994.

## Background & Description

**Focal species.** The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) was described by Miller (1946) from a single specimen of unknown precise origin in Grand Canyon. It is listed as endangered. Its close relatives [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) are also discussed, along with [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) are widespread natives.

**Historical status.** Before Lake Powell impounded the river, eight native species lived in Glen and Grand Canyons. Colorado squawfish, bonytail, and roundtail chub are extirpated or essentially absent from the reach. The document notes that only five razorback suckers have been collected in Grand Canyon since 1984. The fauna has grown to nearly thirty species, mostly introduced, through stocking and inadvertent introduction.

**Scope of the Native Fish Studies.** The plan is organized into four areas:
- A. Native fish studies, covering mainstem and tributary populations.
- B. Humpback chub and other endangered fish studies, including the Little Colorado River (LCR).
- C. An endangered species workshop on broader interactions.
- D. Southern bald eagle studies in Grand Canyon, including surveys of trout access to Nankoweap Creek and avian assessments.

**Governance.** The GCES Aquatic Coordination Team (ACT) integrates the work and limits overlap. Members include AGFD, the Fish and Wildlife Service, the Navajo Nation, Arizona State University, the National Park Service, GCES, and contractors. The plan lists two products: individual research reports and an integrated native and endangered species report.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes several interacting pressures on native fish below Glen Canyon Dam:

1. **Migration barrier.** The dam, together with Lake Mead and Hoover Dam, blocks migrations that some species need to complete their life cycles.
2. **Cold water.** Hypolimnial releases are perennially cold. This can limit reproduction, cause thermal shock to early life stages, and slow growth.
3. **Altered hydrograph.** "Load-following" peaking power has replaced the pre-dam seasonal pattern with daily fluctuations. Daily stage changes reach as much as 13 vertical feet. These fluctuations drain and fill backwaters every 24 hours, keep warm water from forming, and limit planktonic and benthic food for young fish. Tributary-mouth impoundments that once lasted for weeks now form and disappear daily.
4. **Non-native competitors and predators.** Introduced species may compete with and prey on natives, especially if fluctuating flows favor them.

The LCR is the key spawning and rearing area. Humpback chub congregate and reproduce in numbers mainly there. The Pinetop proposal notes that the lower river's survival appears to depend on this population. Spawning is thought to occur during or just after peak spring flows at water temperatures of about 12–23 °C (AGFD proposal, citing several sources). Travertine dams, falls, and high dissolved CO2 in spring outflows are thought to limit chub use of the upper 6 km of the perennial LCR.

### Key Points by Measure

- **Measure 1 (Gila taxonomy).** The goal is to clarify the systematic and genetic relationships within *Gila* in the basin. Objectives include mitochondrial DNA analysis, sources of variation, field characters for discrimination (including hybrids), and low-risk genetic sampling. Expected products are a taxonomic evaluation, field methods, a tissue-sampling system, and a genetic baseline for wild and hatchery stocks.
- **Measure 2 (hatchery stocks).** A basin-wide propagation and genetic management plan covers the Colorado squawfish, bonytail, humpback chub, and razorback sucker. Work includes defining short-term (1990–1992) and long-term (15-year) fish needs, facility types, and refugia design. An initial plan was completed in FY1989.
- **Measure 3 (flood frequency).** Reclamation's 1988 operating criteria aim to lower the probability of bypass releases from one in four to one in twenty years, and to keep powerplant releases limited to 31,500 cfs. The proposed work is a Monte Carlo simulation of historic forecasts and runoff, using variables such as target storage levels in [[Lake Powell]] on January 1 and July 31. The final report is due in FY1992. The document notes that high flows may help chub if they disadvantage exotic competitors.
- **Measure 4 (LCR management plan).** This plan addresses critical habitat from the LCR mouth to about 14 miles upstream at Blue Springs. It includes a basin management plan and a contingency plan for oil and hazardous substance spills. Four tasks are planned: coordination, a technical database, plan writing, and implementation agreements.
- **Measure 5 (impacts research).** Five components are assigned:
  - Habitat use in the LCR and tributaries (Fish and Wildlife Service, Pinetop).
  - Ecology and conservation biology in the LCR (Arizona State University).
  - Adult movement and habitat in Grand Canyon (Bureau of Reclamation contract).
  - Native fish studies (AGFD).
  - Synthesis of humpback chub information (Northern Arizona University).

### Pinetop Fish and Wildlife Service Habitat Proposal (Habitat Use by Humpback Chub)

- **Purpose.** Quantify humpback chub habitat use in the LCR and other tributaries, assess the potential for a second spawning aggregation, and evaluate effects of dam operations.
- **Tributaries.** Little Colorado, Paria, Bright Angel, Shinumo, Tapeats, Deer, Kanab, and Havasu creeks.
- **Study area.** 21 river km of the LCR, divided into three 7-km subreaches. Four random 1-km segments are sampled each season.
- **Macrohabitat.** 22 permanent transects at 1-km intervals, measured at low flow. Measurements include velocity (at 0.6 depth), depth, and substrate (Modified Wentworth scale).
- **Microhabitat.** Trammel, seine, hoop, and minnow-trap sampling, plus in-situ underwater observation. Densities are compared with availability using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.
- **Hypotheses.** Chub are uniformly distributed across habitats; habitat use does not vary seasonally or by age class; and habitat use does not vary across flow volumes.
- **Analysis.** Principal components analysis, followed by stepwise discriminant analysis to predict suitable habitat. The study also tests existing habitat suitability index (HSI) models, which the proposal notes are of questionable general applicability.
- **Schedule.** Reconnaissance begins autumn 1990, full microhabitat sampling starts spring 1991, a final report is due in 1994.

### Arizona State University Proposal (Ecology and Conservation Biology)

- **Logistics.** Two semi-permanent base camps (about 4 years) at the Salt Canyon confluence and about 3 km upstream of the confluence, supported by helicopter. A radio network links camps, Flagstaff, Window Rock, and the South Rim.
- **Tagging.** Humpback chub are anesthetized (MS-222), then PIT-tagged. Fish of at least 100 mm TL are PIT-tagged. Smaller fish get caudal fin clips.
- **Movement.** Two-way fish traps (1.52 cm mesh) at three locations record upstream and downstream movement.
- **Reproduction.** A "reproductive crew" searches for spawning aggregations, with a goal of documenting spawning behavior and collecting fertilized ova. This is the first documented attempt to observe humpback chub spawning in the wild. The proposal notes that spawning has never been observed in the species.
- **Early survival.** Length-frequency analysis, mark-recapture, and tetracycline marking. Laboratory thermal-shock tests cover embryos at 10–25 °C, with changes of 5, 10, and 15 °C.
- **Biotic interactions.** Predation studies on rainbow trout, common carp, and channel catfish, using starvation and feeding experiments to set digestion-rate curves.
- **Schedule.** Field work from spring-summer 1991 through 1994; annual reports due by 1 October; a draft final report by 28 February of the fifth contract year.
- **Personnel.** Co-investigators M.E. Douglas and P.C. Marsh. The Navajo Natural Heritage Program is the subcontractor, under Michael C. Tremble. Field crews total 8–19 people depending on season.

### AGFD Native Fish Studies Proposal

- **Monitoring.** AGFD has run annual monitoring since 1987, usually during May, the humpback chub reproductive period. The proposal continues this with standardized gear and extends it toward research objectives.
- **Objectives 3.1–3.9.** These cover monitoring continuity, early life stage ecology (including drift and reproductive timing from otolith daily growth rings), propagation at the Bubbling Ponds Hatchery in Page Springs, rearing habitat conditions, invertebrate and algal food resources, behavioral responses to controlled flows, age and growth analysis, otolith chemistry, and limnology in the LCR.
- **Operational limits.** Drift nets clog within about 15 minutes at flows of 400 cfs or more and become impractical above 500 cfs. Confluence drift nets are fished at mainstream flows of roughly 3,000–25,000 cfs.
- **Budget.** Year-one total is $287,415 (direct costs $266,125, indirect $21,290 at 8%). Projected year two is $259,785, year three is $272,775, and year four is $94,517.

### Bureau of Reclamation Contract Solicitation (Adult Movement and Habitat)

- **Study area.** About 464 km of Grand Canyon, with flows from about 1,000 to 31,500 cfs. Sites are the LCR confluence (RM 56–76.5), Granite Gorge (RM 76.5–156), and the lower canyon (RM 156–225).
- **Methods.** Radio telemetry and gill, trammel, and electrofishing gear. Aerial or video imagery quantifies habitat at different flows.
- **Effort.** Six trips in 1990 (one of them August reconnaissance, plus 20-day trips from September through December). In 1991–1993, 12 twenty-day trips per year, with about 60 transmitters implanted per year. No more than 10 fish per trip are implanted, and no more than 15 are tracked on any trip because of battery life.
- **Timing.** Information collection begins after a 10–12 day recovery period following implantation.

### Major Conclusions and Planned Outputs

- Native fish persistence in Grand Canyon depends on the LCR for reproduction and rearing.
- Flow management, especially reduction of high-flow bypass frequency and daily fluctuations, is central to the conservation strategy. Reclamation's 1988 criteria are the main operational change.
- Basin-wide genetic management, hatchery support, and refugia are proposed as safeguards.
- Documents are to be shared with the GCES Program Manager and Senior Scientist, the ACT, and the Section 7 Consultation Team. Raw data will go into AGFD's dBASE III fish database.
