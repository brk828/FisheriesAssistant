# Effects of Pesticides on Fish and Wildlife: A Review of Investigations During 1960

#ichthyology #marine-biology #environmental-toxicology #wildlife-management #pesticide-effects

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive 1962 report by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Circular No. 143) summarizes research findings from 1960 on pesticide effects across wildlife, sport fisheries, and commercial fisheries. The document fulfills Public Law 85-582 mandating the Service to evaluate pesticide impacts on wildlife, assist in developing safer chemicals and techniques, and inform the public. The report covers laboratory toxicology studies, enclosure experiments, and field investigations of major pest control programs including [[Spruce Budworm]] control, [[Dutch Elm Disease]] treatment, and [[Imported Fire Ant]] eradication. Key findings demonstrate widespread wildlife mortality, bioaccumulation of chlorinated hydrocarbons (particularly [[DDT]], [[Heptachlor]], and [[Dieldrin]]), and chronic effects on reproduction. The report documents pesticide residues in tissues of birds, mammals, fish, and invertebrates from treated areas, with particular concern for species like [[Woodcock]] and [[Western Grebe]]. Marine studies reveal extreme sensitivity of phytoplankton, crustaceans, and mollusks to both insecticides and herbicides at concentrations far below application rates.

## Background & Description

In 1960, pesticide sales in the United States reached $285 million at the manufacturer's level, representing a 3% increase over 1959. Total production of synthetic organic pesticidal chemicals rose 9.2% to 638 million pounds, with overall pesticidal chemical production (including inorganic compounds) approximating 1 billion pounds. [[DDT]] production reached record levels for the third consecutive year, and both 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T production exceeded all previous years. The 1960 Pesticide Handbook listed 7,851 products compared with 7,041 in 1959.

Forest treatments for spruce budworm control covered approximately 360,000 acres in 1960, considerably less than 1959. Historical data indicate an average of 1.8 million acres of forests received insecticidal treatment annually, representing less than 1% of the 640 million total forest acres. Rangeland treatments averaged 1.6 million acres per year, also a fraction of 1% of the 630 million acres of permanent range and pasture land.

Agricultural pest control represented the largest pesticide use sector. Approximately 69 million acres (15% of 465 million acres of cropland) received annual insecticide treatments, while herbicidal treatments covered about 50 million acres. The [[Imported Fire Ant]] control program continued extensively in the South, with treatment levels reduced toward year's end from single applications of 1.5-2 pounds heptachlor per acre to two applications of 0.5 pound per acre.

Urban and residential areas received intensive pesticide applications. An estimated 28% of 53 million acres in urban, industrial, and built-up areas were treated with insecticides at heavy dosages. Approximately 10 million pounds of organic arsenicals were used for crab grass control in 1959. Orchards (3 million acres) received heavy treatments on approximately 80% of acreage. Mosquito control programs treated saltmarsh areas exceeding 2 million acres.

The Fish and Wildlife Service's pesticide research budget increased from $56,000 or less in 1958 to $181,000 in 1959 and $280,000 in 1960, following Congressional authorization of $280,000 per year under Public Law 85-582. Public Law 86-279 (1959) increased authorized funding to $2,565,000, with appropriations of $615,000 in 1961 and $684,000 in fiscal year 1962.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report documents pesticide impacts across multiple ecological zones and trophic levels. In freshwater systems, [[DDT]] applications for [[Spruce Budworm]] control in Maine (217,000 acres at 1 pound per acre) and Montana (60,000 acres at 0.5-1.0 pound per acre) resulted in fish mortality and aquatic invertebrate reductions. Water samples from Montana's Swan Creek contained up to 0.01 ppm DDT within 30 minutes of spraying, with insects in drift samples containing 3.4-14.2 ppm within 1-3 hours. Trout exposed in live-cars accumulated 0.1 ppm DDT after 6-96 hours.

The [[Clear Lake]] gnat control program using [[DDD]] (also called TDE) caused chronic poisoning of [[Western Grebe]]s through bioaccumulation. Visceral fat from apparently healthy [[Largemouth Bass]] and white catfish contained up to 2,275 ppm and 1,700 ppm DDD respectively. Dead grebes contained up to 1,600 ppm in tissues. Plankton-eating fish accumulated less toxicant than carnivorous species of equal size, demonstrating trophic magnification. Fish hatched 7-9 months after the last DDD application still contained 22-25 ppm (bass) and 7-9 ppm (blackfish) in flesh samples.

At [[Tule Lake]] and [[Lower Klamath Refuges]], 307 fish-eating birds died between May 22 and June 2, 1960, from toxaphene poisoning. The probable source was a 1958 application of 2 pounds per acre on 1,500 acres of Lower Klamath Refuge. Chemical analyses showed toxaphene concentrations in bird tissues within fatal poisoning ranges: [[White Pelican]] liver (5.0-8.0 ppm), [[American Egret]] (3.0 ppm whole bird), and [[Great Blue Heron]] (2.0 ppm whole bird).

Terrestrial ecosystems showed widespread contamination. In Wilcox County, Alabama, treatment with heptachlor (2 lb/acre) and dieldrin (2 lb/acre) in March 1958 resulted in immediate [[Bobwhite Quail]] mortality and population declines. One year post-treatment, earthworms from heptachlor areas contained 0.7 ppm heptachlor epoxide; those from dieldrin areas contained 1.5 ppm dieldrin. Quail populations required two years to return to normal levels. Chemical analyses revealed 80% of 41 bobwhite quail, 90% of 20 [[Mourning Dove]]s, and 100% of 5 rails from fire ant control areas contained heptachlor epoxide in edible tissues.

[[Woodcock]] studies demonstrated progressive bioaccumulation on wintering grounds. Birds collected shortly after arrival in Louisiana in 1958 showed only minute traces of heptachlor residues, but subsequent collections showed progressive increases in both percentage of contaminated birds and tissue concentrations. By February 1960, 5 of 9 woodcock contained residues averaging 1.5 mcg/bird (maximum 3.0 mcg/bird). Immature birds from New Brunswick contained residues, suggesting contamination on breeding grounds.

[[Dutch Elm Disease]] control using DDT caused severe songbird mortality. In Wisconsin communities, robin populations declined 69-98% compared to unsprayed areas. Hickey and Hunt (1960) estimated at least 86% robin mortality on one newly sprayed area, with peak deaths occurring 3 weeks post-treatment. Other affected species included [[Starling]], [[Common Grackle]], [[Cardinal]], and [[Yellow-shafted Flicker]].

Marine and estuarine environments showed extreme vulnerability. Phytoplankton cultures demonstrated high sensitivity to herbicides, with growth inhibition at concentrations as low as 0.01 ppm for some compounds. Marine crustaceans showed differential susceptibility: stone and mud crabs experienced mortality or paralysis at 1 ppm concentrations of Sevin and endrin. Post-larval and small shrimp proved far less resistant than crabs, with younger stages more sensitive than larger specimens. Brown shrimp generally showed greater tolerance than white shrimp (48-hour median tolerance limits: endrin 1.0 vs 0.5 ppb; toxaphene 16 vs 8 ppb).

Oyster and clam larvae showed extreme sensitivity. [[DDT]] caused >90% mortality and nearly complete growth cessation at 0.05 ppm. Adult oysters exposed to 0.1 ppm toxaphene for 3 weeks experienced 50% mortality. Growth rate decreases served as the first indicator of toxicity, detectable within 24 hours. Marine fish showed species-specific tolerance variations: 48-hour median tolerance limits for endrin ranged 4-35 ppb; dieldrin 47-220 ppb; DDT 400-3,000 ppb. Juvenile [[Mullet]] (35 mm) showed 24-hour median tolerance limits of 2-3 ppb endrin in circulating water.

Contamination at the [[Rocky Mountain Arsenal]] created chronic waterfowl mortality. Three industrial lakes polluted with aldrin from an insecticide plant caused an estimated 2,000 duck deaths annually during winter and early spring at low water levels. Lake mud and inlet canal sediments contained up to 480 ppm aldrin and dieldrin. Living algae contained up to 79 ppm dieldrin; living snails up to 88 ppm. Dead ducks contained 30-64 ppm dieldrin. Ducks shot by hunters in October 1960 contained 4-39 ppm dieldrin, suggesting thousands of waterfowl acquired sublethal doses before migrating to nesting grounds.

## Key Findings

**Laboratory Toxicology Studies:**

More than 400 toxicological tests involving 27 pesticides were conducted at [[Patuxent Wildlife Research Center]] during 1960. Feeding tests established acute toxicity values for [[Bobwhite Quail]], [[Ring-necked Pheasant]], and [[Mallard Duck]]. Organophosphates showed variable toxicity: phosphamidon proved highly toxic to young quail (31 mg/kg diet causing 50% mortality in 5 days), while Dibrom and Sevin showed relatively low toxicity. Among chlorinated hydrocarbons, heptachlor epoxide demonstrated high toxicity (60 mg/kg for young bobwhite), while kepone showed moderate acute toxicity but severe reproductive effects.

Chronic feeding studies revealed profound reproductive impacts. Kepone at 50 ppm completely inhibited quail reproduction and nearly eliminated pheasant reproduction (0.7 and 0.5 viable chicks per hen vs 13.3 for controls at 25 and 50 ppm respectively). Quail reproduction was sharply reduced at 1-25 ppm kepone, with many chicks crippled or defective at hatching. Male pheasants reared on 50-150 ppm kepone failed to develop characteristic coloration, resembling adult females, with histopathological examination revealing marked testicular abnormalities and malformed sperm.

Pheasants fed 5,000 ppm Sevin, 2,500 ppm methoxychlor, or 25 ppm heptachlor produced no viable chicks. Reproductive effects persisted even when insecticide-free food was provided from the beginning of the reproductive period, indicating lasting physiological damage. Effects on reproduction were roughly proportional to the percentage of lethal dose ingested. Intermittent exposures to relatively low concentrations of several different chlorinated insecticides over short periods produced more pronounced effects than continuous exposure to greater concentrations of a single compound for longer periods.

Herbicide testing initiated in 1960 showed the amide of 2,4-D as the most toxic derivative tested, with all phenoxy herbicides appearing to act as cumulative poisons. Young bobwhite showed 50% mortality at 1,825 mg/kg for 2,4-D acetamide over 5 days, while adult mallards tolerated >35,000 mg/kg of 2,4-D dimethyl amine salt over 5 days.

**Enclosure Studies:**

Bobwhite quail kept in outdoor pens on heptachlor-treated ground demonstrated dose-dependent and exposure-route-dependent mortality. In 3 x 10-foot breeder pens, mortality varied with application rate (0.25-2.0 pounds per acre) and food/water presentation method. Greater mortality occurred when food was scattered on treated ground versus kept clean in containers. At 2 pounds per acre with food scattered on ground and contaminated water, 100% mortality occurred in both small pens (2/2 birds) and larger 1/200-acre cages (20/20 birds within 30 days). Even at 0.25 pounds per acre with food scattered and clean water after rainfall, 50% mortality occurred (2/4 birds). Clean food in containers with clean water and post-treatment rainfall resulted in no mortality at rates up to 1.5 pounds per acre, but 25% mortality (1/4 birds) at 2.0 pounds per acre.

**Field Population Effects:**

The [[Imported Fire Ant]] control program produced the most extensively documented wildlife impacts. In Wilcox County, Alabama, treatment with heptachlor (2 lb/acre) and dieldrin (2 lb/acre) in March 1958 caused immediate quail mortality and population declines. Dog censuses showed treated areas had 9 coveys with 126 quail in winter 1957-58 (pre-treatment), declining to 3 coveys with 43 quail in spring 1958 (post-treatment), compared to untreated check areas maintaining 5 coveys with 71 quail. Second-year mortality continued: 33 dead specimens were found on treated areas in spring 1959 with no further treatment, and none on untreated areas. Overwinter decline appeared greater than normal, with scarcity of [[Meadowlark]]s and [[Robin]]s noted. Populations returned to normal size during the second year after treatment (fall 1959: 10 coveys, 145 quail on treated vs 6 coveys, 88 quail on check).

In Decatur County, Georgia, whistling cock counts and covey counts on two treated areas (heptachlor 2 lb/acre, some dieldrin 2 lb/acre) and one untreated Alabama area showed quail populations low on treated areas following treatment but increasing each year, approaching normal levels in the third year post-treatment.

Kepone field trials in Alabama (3.5 grams per acre in peanut butter on 480 acres) resulted in observations of quail with tremors, subsequent deaths, and sick-appearing meadowlarks, suggesting field toxicity despite lower laboratory toxicity compared to heptachlor.

Cotton rat populations studied on 55 acres treated with granular heptachlor (1.25 lb/acre) near Gadsden, Alabama, showed slightly greater decline on the 6.7-acre trapped plot within the treated area compared to a 6.7-acre untreated area, though preliminary analysis suggested differences were not statistically significant.

Woodcock wing surveys from the 1959-60 hunting season (8,786 wings from 19 states and provinces) revealed an age ratio of 1.18 juveniles to 1 adult, with approximately 50% of juveniles and 40% of adults being males. Mortality rate approximations from long-term banding studies suggested 1959 reproductive success was nearly balanced with mortality rate. Exploratory night counts in central Louisiana on a 140-acre area treated with heptachlor (2 lb/acre) in May 1959 showed relatively low woodcock counts at season beginning, declining more sharply than on untreated fields, though dryness and unfavorable cover conditions likely contributed. Nine animals killed for analysis and four found dead included one killdeer and one pipit (dead) with traces of heptachlor epoxide, one skunk (dead) with 3.7 ppm in liver, one meadowlark (alive) with 2 ppm, and one woodcock (alive) with 1.5 ppm.

Japanese beetle control using aldrin or heptachlor (2 lb/acre) at airports and other areas resulted in widespread contamination. Specimens collected by Dr. George J. Wallace from a Michigan area treated with aldrin in 1959 showed five sparrows containing 7.7-15.4 ppm dieldrin (aldrin's epoxide), and seven other specimens (four [[Blue Jay]]s, one mourning dove, one titmouse) containing 0.5-12.1 ppm dieldrin.

Fulvous tree duck populations faced serious danger from rice seed treatments with aldrin and other toxic chemicals. Heavy mortality occurred in Brazoria County, Texas, during initial rice flooding periods, with reports of greatly reduced bird numbers.

**Aquatic System Impacts:**

Spruce budworm control in Montana's Gallatin National Forest (60,000 acres, 0.5 lb/acre near streams, 1 lb/acre elsewhere) showed rapid DDT appearance in Swan Creek: <0.002 ppm at 6 minutes post-spray, 0.01 ppm at 30 minutes, 0.002 ppm at 1 hour, then traces thereafter. Dead and dying insects peaked 1 hour post-spray, with stoneflies peaking at 30 minutes, mayflies at 1 hour, caddis and beetle larvae at 1.5 hours, and dipterans at 3 hours. Trout in live-cars showed no mortality through 96 hours but accumulated traces of DDT after 6 hours exposure and 0.1 ppm after 12-96 hours. Drift insects contained trace DDT at 30 minutes, 3.4 ppm at 1 hour, and 11.0-14.2 ppm DDE at 3 hours. Trout fed contaminated insects (3-6 hours stream exposure) accumulated 0.1 ppm DDT 20 hours after feeding began.
