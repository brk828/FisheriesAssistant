# Life History and Status of the Endangered Cui-ui of Pyramid Lake, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared by G. Gary Scoppettone, Mark Coleman, and Gary A. Wedemeyer, documents a four-year study (1981 to 1984) of the [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a federally endangered sucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. The study examined spawning migration, reproduction, larval emigration, age and growth, population size, and food. The authors conclude that the decline of the species was driven mainly by disruption of its reproductive cycle: water diversion lowered the lake and created the [[Truckee River Delta]], which blocked adult access to the [[Truckee River]] for most years after the 1930s. Restoration measures, including the [[Marble Bluff Dam]] and [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]] (completed 1976), partly restored access, but recruitment after the 1969 year class appeared sharply reduced.

## Background & Description

The cui-ui is described as the last genetically pure member of the genus *Chasmistes* (Miller and Smith 1981) and was given top priority for recovery under the [[Endangered Species Act]] listing framework. It belongs to the [[Catostomidae]] (suckers) and is a planktivorous lakesucker. Adults spend most of the year in littoral waters less than 23 m deep and gather at the river end of the lake each spring before spawning in the lower Truckee River.

Historical context:
- The [[Newlands Project]] (completed 1905) with [[Derby Dam]] diverted an average of 311 million m³ annually, about half the river flow.
- The lake level fell about 25 m between 1906 and 1982.
- Cui-ui once also occurred in [[Winnemucca Lake]], which desiccated by 1938.
- Hatchery propagation began in 1973 under the [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]], producing an average of 7 million larvae annually in 1980-84.

The 1981 study used adults confined in a natural braid of the lower Truckee River to develop reproductive data under controlled conditions, then extended the work to the main stem.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Pyramid Lake is a terminal lake of about 467 km² (1983), losing water only by evaporation. Total dissolved solids were 4,900 to 5,000 mg/L in 1983, which the authors note is lethal to incubating eggs. The lake is the largest remnant of pluvial [[Lake Lahontan]]. The Truckee River flows about 190 km from [[Lake Tahoe]] (1,898 m) to the lake (1,156 m in 1984) with a low gradient of about 1.5 m/km. Average daily flows in 1982-84 ranged from 1 to 206 m³/s.

**Delta and river access.** Before 1984 the delta extended about 2 km upstream from the river mouth. In 1983, even at a spring flow of 206 m³/s, the submerged delta contained only discontinuous channels up to 1 m deep. American white pelicans (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*) concentrated on the delta and preyed on migrating fish. After two extraordinarily wet years, the lake rose about 7 m and the channel below the dam downcut about 2 m, which abolished the delta and moved the river mouth 2 km closer to the dam.

**Habitat use.** Spawning occurred over predominantly gravel substrate in riffles. Historically, spawning ran from mid-April to late May in the lower 40 km of stream, and occasionally in the lake from May through July.

## Key Findings

### Prespawning aggregation and migration
- Prespawning adults congregated off the Truckee River mouth from mid-March to early June, with runs largest in high spring-flow years.
- Of 526 cui-ui Floy-tagged in late March to early April 1982-83, 45 were recaptured at the fish processing building (FPB), 35 to 77 days after tagging. Tagged fish remained offshore an average of 55 days.
- Eleven sonic-tagged fish stayed offshore for 6 to 8 weeks; by November 1982, 7 of the 11 were relocated, indicating high survival.
- Upstream migration lasted 48 to 59 days in 1982-84. It peaked (over 500 fish per day) when daily mean water temperatures were 12 to 15°C; migration was stimulated by rising and dampened by falling temperatures.
- Radio-tagged males remained in the river 6.5 to 16.5 days and females 4.0 to 10.5 days. Downstream return occurred mainly at night.
- Migration into the FPB through the fishway rose from 33 fish in 1978 to 4,984 in 1980, then fell to 47 in 1981; 13,804 entered in 1982 and 5,994 in 1983. In 1984 all 11,374 entering the FPB came through the river trap, and the fishway was abandoned.

### Reproduction
- Cui-ui first entered the spawning run at 380 to 420 mm, at age 6.
- Sex ratios (males:females) were 1:1.3 in 1982 and 1983 and 1:2.5 in 1984. The authors suggest the 1984 ratio is the least biased because all fish used the river route.
- Fecundity ranged from 24,000 eggs (432 mm female) to 196,000 eggs (657 mm female). A 558 mm (average) female produced 104,000 eggs in 1982 and 76,000 in 1983, paralleling a drop in relative gonad weight from 18% to 15% of body weight.
- Radio telemetry identified 15 discrete spawning areas over the three study years.
- Spawning occurred at depths of 21 to 110 cm, stream velocities of 27 to 140 cm/s, and near-bed velocities of 21 to 90 cm/s; most spawning occurred at 71 to 80 cm depth and 101 to 110 cm/s stream velocity.

### Larval emigration
- Eggs hatched in about 13 days at 10°C, with swim-up about 5 days later.
- More than 98% of plankton-net captures were swim-up larvae (10.5 to 12.5 mm).
- Peak emigration followed peak adult migration by 26 days in 1982 and 29 days in 1984.
- Larvae emigrated almost exclusively at night, primarily between 2200 and 0300 h in 1984.
- Dam passage caused modest losses: survival was 99% in controls versus 89 to 93% in test groups.

### Age and growth
- Scales were judged unreliable, as fish recaptured up to 7 years after tagging showed no increase in scale annuli. Opercle bones were validated instead by following individual year classes over time.
- Of 355 opercles collected in 1956-84, fish were 1 to 41 years old, representing 11 year classes. The oldest validated fish (28 to 41 years) are reported as the oldest validated catostomids in the literature.
- In 1983, about 92% of the offshore reproductive population was from the 1969 year class, 5% from 1950 or earlier, and 3% from other year classes.
- Males and females grew at the same rate until age 6, after which females grew faster and lived longer. Of 140 old fish sampled offshore in 1983, only 3 were male.
- Growth in length virtually ceased by about age 20.

### Population size
- Estimated prespawning aggregation size (modified Schnabel estimator): 187,065 fish in 1982 (95% CI 151,883 to 236,451) and 103,407 in 1983 (95% CI 82,270 to 134,161).
- Only 7.4% of the 1982 offshore population entered the FPB, and 5.8% in 1983, indicating the fishway was not fully effective.
- Recruitment after 1969 appeared "alarmingly low"; few cui-ui under 460 mm were seen in the FPB, although young cui-ui (13 to 350 mm) were collected in the lake in 1984-85.

### Food and feeding
- Adults in the prespawning aggregation fed mostly on zooplankton: 14 of 17 fish sampled in 1983 had food. The copepod *Diaptomus sicilis* made up 46% of organisms eaten but 71% of total carbon, and the cladoceran *Ceriodaphnia quadrangula* was also important. This contradicts earlier reports that adults stop feeding.
- Larvae (12 to 28 mm, n = 51 sampled, 42 with food) in the Truckee River ate chironomid larvae (56% of total carbon), copepods (18%), and cladocerans (15%).
- Yearlings (104 to 120 mm, n = 4) from Mud Lake Slough consumed zooplankton, which made up over 97% of food items.

### Predation
- In 1982, 427 cui-ui were lost to pelicans: 355 dropped or regurgitated along the fishway and delta (84% female, 510 to 630 mm, 1.8 to 3.1 kg) and 72 found on Anaho Island rookery.
- Pelican predation was much lower in 1983 (26 carcasses recovered) and none were found in 1984, when water depth at the delta exceeded 1 m.

### Conservation implications
- The authors attribute the dominance of the 1969 year class to unrestricted river access that year, and the earlier dominance of the 1950 year class to a 1946 spawning channel.
- Long lifespan is considered important for survival, given the scarcity of high-water years that allowed spawning access.
- Restoring reliable river access (including through the delta and fishway) and protecting recruitment are presented as the central needs for recovery.
