# Biological Opinion on Woundfin and Virgin River Chub - Beaver Dam Wash Bank Stabilization

#ichthyology #endangered-species #ESA-consultation #Cyprinidae #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued July 22, 1996, evaluates the effects of proposed bank stabilization work in [[Beaver Dam Wash]], Arizona, on two endangered fish species: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*). The [[Natural Resources Conservation Service]] (NRCS) proposed installing two lines of Kellner jacks and riprap along eroded streambanks following March 1995 flooding under the Emergency Watershed Protection (EWP) program. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service concluded the project would not jeopardize the continued existence of either species or adversely modify proposed critical habitat in the [[Virgin River]]. However, the opinion acknowledges that the project continues a pattern of piecemeal management that addresses local erosion without resolving underlying watershed-scale problems. The action is authorized with an incidental take statement and three reasonable and prudent measures addressing flow alterations, sediment control, and coordinated watershed planning.

## Background & Description

### Species Biology

**Woundfin** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) is a small, silvery minnow rarely exceeding three inches (7.5 cm) in length, characterized by a flattened head, streamlined body, and conspicuous sharp dorsal spine. The species is essentially scaleless with small bone plates in leathery skin. Listed as endangered in 1970, woundfin historically occurred in the Colorado, Salt, Verde, and Gila rivers but now persist only in the [[Virgin River]] drainage. All reintroduction attempts outside this drainage have failed. A captive population was established at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1988.

Woundfin are omnivorous, consuming filamentous algae, detrital material, tamarisk seeds, and insects. Adults and juveniles occupy runs and quiet waters adjacent to riffles with sand or sand/gravel substrates. Larvae use shallow marginal habitats with dense filamentous algae, avoiding pools containing predatory non-native species like [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*).

Spawning occurs April through July (occasionally August) over cobble to gravel substrates in swift water. Females leave pools to join males in spawning groups, then return. Deacon and Hardy (1982) demonstrated that spawning substrate selection is specific to cobble or gravel, and that spawning fails in suboptimal habitats even with adequate flows. Reduced recruitment below major diversions has been attributed to water depletions. Population recovery requires two years of favorable conditions following severe depletion (e.g., 1977 drought). Mean monthly flows exceeding 800 cfs during reproduction diminish recruitment, while average winter mortality approximates 30 percent independent of density.

**Virgin River Chub** (*Gila seminuda*, formerly *Gila robusta seminuda*) is a silvery minnow reaching 8 to 18 inches (20 to 45 cm). The species name *seminuda* refers to small, deeply embedded scales on the back, breast, and belly that may be absent in some individuals. Historic and current distribution is confined to the [[Virgin River]] and [[Moapa River]] drainages; Lake Mead construction separated these populations. A captive population exists at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

Virgin River chub inhabit deep runs or pools with slow to moderate velocities and instream cover (boulders, root snags). Larger individuals tolerate wider ranges of depth and velocity than smaller fish. The species is omnivorous with ontogenetic diet shifts: young fish feed almost entirely on macroinvertebrates, while older fish consume algae and debris. Spawning occurs April through June, but specific requirements remain poorly understood. Some evidence suggests downstream movement, but upstream movement patterns are unknown.

### Project Description

Following March 1995 flooding, NRCS proposed two bank stabilization projects under the EWP program:

1. **Kellner Jacks Installation**: Two lines of jacks along both banks downstream of Highway 91 bridge:
   - West/south bank: 870 feet beginning at downstream end of existing willows
   - East/north bank: 738 feet from willows near bridge to cottonwood trees
   - Each jack constructed of three angle iron legs welded/bolted together, laced with No. 6 galvanized wire
   - Jacks placed on 8-foot centers, attached to main cable with 1/2-inch galvanized steel cable
   - Anchored with concrete blocks (19 inches thick) using 1-inch galvanized wire

2. **Riprap Installation**: 405 feet along east/north bank at Draper residence:
   - Toe trench: 5 feet wide, 5 feet deep
   - Inner sidewall angle: 1:1 (horizontal:vertical)
   - Outer sidewall angle: 1.5:1
   - Geotextile fabric with rock fill
   - Finished slope: 2:1, approximately 10 feet total height
   - Backfill placed against existing vertical bank beneath riprap

Native [[Cottonwood]] (*Populus* sp.) and [[Willow]] (*Salix* sp.) plantings would accompany jack installations. Construction would occur on dry sand away from water, though stream diversion might be needed to minimize sedimentation if water runs adjacent to banks.

### Action Area Context

[[Beaver Dam Wash]] heads in Nevada, flows through Utah, and joins the [[Virgin River]] in Arizona. The stream is perennial but may be dewatered during low flows, especially near diversions. High water events are common and contribute to complex stream morphology. The wash is fed by springs above the old State Route 91 bridge and is affected by several diversions and [[Schroeder Reservoir]] near Nevada headwaters.

The immediate project area includes private land: a golf course on the west/south side and housing development on the east/north. Downstream areas are Bureau of Land Management public lands within the [[Virgin River Corridor Area of Critical Environmental Concern]] and Special Recreation Management Area. The confluence reach is eligible for wild and scenic river designation under recreational classification. Water quality is high and may qualify as unique water under Arizona law.

Biotic resources include mature cottonwood-willow gallery forests, wetlands, and aquatic habitats supporting native and introduced fish species. The wash has been extensively modified by human activities since the mid-1980s, including unpermitted channelization by [[Beaver Dam Golf Incorporated]] in 1989, emergency diversion dam work in 1992, and previous NRCS bank stabilization (consultation 2-21-94-F-388) following January 1993 flooding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Rangewide Species Status

Both species occupy precarious status. The [[Virgin River]] basin has been heavily modified by water diversions for agriculture and municipal use beginning in the early 1900s, storage dam construction, and habitat fragmentation from barriers. Population declines have been documented since 1984 for both species. Physical habitat degradation continued through the 1980s and 1990s.

For woundfin, the spread of invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) significantly reduces habitat value through resource competition. Because woundfin are short-lived, yearly reproductive success is crucial for population maintenance. Creation of suboptimal habitats significantly affects reproductive success. Red shiner eradication projects in the late 1980s had temporary success, but recolonization risk remains. Reintroduction efforts in historic range have failed. Recent cooperative agreements for [[Virgin River Spinedace]] recovery may provide some benefits, but extent is unclear.

Virgin River chub status has not improved since 1989 listing. Limited life history information hampers full assessment of habitat modification effects. The species occurs only in Virgin and Moapa rivers, limiting reestablishment opportunities. The Moapa River population receives no ESA protection (not listed), creating significant loss risk given increasing regional water demands.

### Environmental Baseline

The environmental baseline encompasses past and present impacts throughout the Virgin River drainage, the only remaining habitat for listed populations. [[Beaver Dam Wash]] water use and development history reflects basinwide patterns. Reservoir construction, diversion structures, and channelization have altered flows and habitat conditions.

No woundfin records exist at the project site, though records exist near the Virgin River confluence. Virgin River chub records exist below the project area and in the river. The Virgin River mainstem at and below the Beaver Dam Wash confluence provides important habitat for both species and has been proposed as critical habitat.

Baseline conditions result from complex interactions of watershed-scale activities: road and building construction, agricultural conversion, livestock grazing, dam and diversion placement, Highway 91 bridge construction (1951), channel narrowing by dikes and berms, east/south bank erosion possibly from channel relocation, flatcar bridge placement at Resort, and floods from [[Quail Creek Dam]] failure (January 1, 1989). Activities have proceeded as individual, unrelated projects without comprehensive planning or watershed-scale analysis.

High water events in 1980, 1983, January 1993, and March 1995 caused recurring damage. The 1993 event led to NRCS emergency projects including two Kellner jack lines at the Resort (consultation 2-21-94-F-388, opinion issued September 13, 1994). The March 1995 event displaced Draper residence jacks, damaged the east/north bank below the highway bridge, and affected the west/south bank north of the 1993 jack line.

### Project Effects Analysis

**Direct Effects**: Potential for direct mortality to woundfin and Virgin River chub is limited by low probability of individuals at the project site. Probability increases with water conditions and flow levels during construction. If all work occurs out of active channel without water diversion, direct effects are further reduced (assuming access from respective sides without stream crossing). If diversion is needed, direct effects include increased sedimentation, habitat and food resource changes, possible stranding mortality, and crushing/injury from machinery.

**Interrelated/Interdependent Actions**: The Resort and housing developments protected by this project may qualify under the "but for" test—without these developments, the proposed action would be unnecessary. Development effects include water diversion, runoff and return flow water quality, and past channel configuration changes. Continued development presence ensures these effects persist, potentially requiring additional future projects.

**Indirect Effects**: Extremely difficult to evaluate given complex baseline conditions. Watershed activities affecting natural hydrologic processes contribute to altered erosion and deposition patterns. Delineating which wash function changes can be attributed to the proposed project is challenging. The project will have specific effects, but existing conditions create complex patterns where discerning causation for particular events is difficult.

The most important indirect effect is continuation of piecemeal management without comprehensive planning or watershed-scale analysis. NRCS acknowledges the project does nothing to address present wash conditions but continues patching efforts focused on local conditions without considering larger system needs.

Many effects remain unknown without full-scale hydrologic assessment. The BE states riprap would reduce sediment loads by covering eroding banks, at least short-term. However, Kellner jack failure at the same location raises questions: Will failure causes affect riprap? What happens downstream of armored banks? What effects will additional bank protection have on high flow passage? The BE states projects will not improve narrowed channel conditions; jacks address low-flow erosion. What happens at high flows? Riparian tree plantings are planned, but 1995 high water removed previous plantings. Will new plantings persist?

**Cumulative Effects**: Development activities in the Beaver Dam Wash watershed and adjacent areas are unlikely to diminish. The Arizona-Nevada-Utah region experienced considerable growth in the last decade with projections indicating continued trends. Additional water demands may be placed on wash supplies, and new floodplain developments may generate additional bank stabilization proposals. These would not constitute cumulative effects if Federal involvement occurs (NRCS, Corps, EPA for section 404). Exact locations and sizes of new or expanded developments cannot be stated with certainty.

Actions are anticipated for Virgin River spinedace, though degree of benefit to woundfin and Virgin River chub is unknown.

## Key Findings

### Non-Jeopardy Determination

After reviewing current status of woundfin and Virgin River chub, environmental baseline, proposed action effects, and cumulative effects, the Service concludes the riprap and Kellner jack projects are **not likely to jeopardize the continued existence** of either endangered species. This determination is based on:

1. Low probability of direct mortality given limited species presence at project site
2. Construction primarily out of water
3. No direct effects to occupied Virgin River habitat
4. Existing Beaver Dam Wash conditions unlikely to improve but more likely to remain same or decline (amount of decline cannot be estimated)
5. Unclear magnitude of Beaver Dam Wash influences on Virgin River given present conditions
6. While both species have very limited resources to absorb new action effects, this particular action's magnitude and effect types have not reached jeopardy threshold

The opinion emphasizes that future projects will be assessed against baseline conditions in effect at consultation time, which will contain this action's effects plus all other intervening Federal, State, local, and private actions.

### No Adverse Modification of Proposed Critical Habitat

The magnitude of effects on proposed critical habitat in the Virgin River is not sufficient to warrant a finding of destruction or adverse modification. Formal conference is not required. Proposed critical habitat includes Virgin River mainstem and 100-year floodplain portions containing constituent elements. Virgin River floodplain portions extend up Beaver Dam Wash but do not reach the project area.

### Incidental Take Statement

**Anticipated Take Level**: Risk of take stems from increased sedimentation in downstream areas and potential loss of individuals if work site diversion is needed. Project completion may have long-term effects on upstream and downstream reach stability.

Because low probability exists for either species at construction area and construction occurs out of water, take from this portion is likely very low. Higher take risk occurs if live water must be diverted. Take risk exists from increased sedimentation during and immediately after construction and from toxic substance release from machinery/equipment. Actual take amount from these effects is not quantifiable.

Assessing possible suitable habitat loss in Beaver Dam Wash and Virgin River at/below confluence is much more difficult. The wash has been subject to various human activities affecting normal flows, altering meander patterns and available floodplain, and causing channel changes. The present project addresses erosion threatening human developments but does not change underlying reasons, allowing them to continue. Take attributable to proposed action is not quantifiable.

Construction-related take is likely short duration. Take from continued wash channel condition is more long-term. Subsequent flows influenced by project feature placement would continue taking into the future at some amount. NRCS states effects on future flow events would be "almost immeasurable," but this may not account for maintaining present channel conditions.

**Surrogate Take Measures**: Because of difficulty measuring project contribution to Beaver Dam Wash conditions, no specific incidental take level can be determined. The Service uses surrogate measures (similar to 1994 consultation on Resort Kellner jacks). Incidental take would be exceeded if:

1. One or more dead woundfin or Virgin River chub are observed at or below construction site
2. After subsequent high water events: (a) erosion or material deposition occurs upstream or downstream of jacks/riprap linked to water flow changes (depth, velocity, direction) caused by project features, and/or (b) new erosion or damage occurs to protected banks or to project structure integrity/operations

### Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs)

Three non-discretionary RPMs with implementing terms and conditions:

**RPM 1 - Future Flow Event Effects**: NRCS will examine this project's effects when planning future projects and give preference to solutions that do not continue or exacerbate present conditions.

*Terms and Conditions*:
1. NRCS will evaluate this project's effects on Beaver Dam Wash flow events and incorporate findings into future project planning
2. Future projects will preferentially select alternatives that do not perpetuate or worsen current wash conditions

**RPM 2 - Individual Fish Effects**: NRCS will implement measures to minimize effects on individual fish in project areas.

*Terms and Conditions*:
1. Sediment control program implementation during construction
2. If water access/diversion needed:
   - Barriers placed upstream and downstream of work area before water access/diversion to prevent fish entry after surveys
   - Complete survey by qualified biologist with State/Federal permits and species experience prior to construction
   - Native fish (including Virgin River spinedace) removed from barriered area to suitable up/downstream habitats as close to capture site as possible
   - Barriers remain until all activity completed; if diversion occurs, barriers may be removed after water diverted (provided no water access needed for construction)
   - Diverted wash reach minimized in length and lateral distance; no diversion back to original course unless new course causes downstream bank erosion or other significant problems; if removal necessary, steps a-c repeated
3. Construction water not taken directly from wash flows; all grey water disposed to prevent wash return; no equipment washing or extended parking, hazardous material storage, or related activities within Clean Water Act jurisdictional boundaries

**RPM 3 - Uncoordinated Bank Stabilization Effects**: NRCS will coordinate projects with Corps of Engineers to address watershed needs.

*Terms and Conditions*:
1. NRCS will fully coordinate this and future projects with Corps to address overall system needs rather than strictly local scale
2. Detailed report describing construction activities and terms/conditions implementation provided to Service within three months of project completion

### Additional Concerns

The opinion raises concerns about other species not formally consulted:

- **Desert Tortoise** (*Gopherus agassizii*): Proposed action states tortoises found during construction would be moved, constituting take. Without formal consultation authorizing incidental take, this action is not lawful. Tortoises must be avoided; take must not occur. Concerns exist about quarry site location and vehicle routes across occupied habitat.

- **Southwestern Willow Flycatcher** (*Empidonax traillii extimus*): Present habitat was assessed but not potential habitat. Project likely has interrelated effects downstream and possibly upstream affecting species survival and recovery through changes in potential to produce suitable habitat. Such analysis was not seen in biological evaluation.
