# Distributional Records of Two Crayfish Species (Orconectes luteus and Orconectes neglectus neglectus) in the Central Great Plains and Ozark Region

#carcinology #freshwater-biology #orconectes

## Executive Summary

This note reports new distributional records for two crayfishes, [[Orconectes Luteus]] and [[Orconectes Neglectus Neglectus]], from collections made in 1960 and 1961 in Missouri, Kansas, and Nebraska. The authors document O. luteus north of the Osage River system, where it had not previously been recorded, and O. n. neglectus at several Nebraska sites, including a new Platte River drainage record. They interpret the populations as either recent northward dispersal (or human transfer) or relictual populations left by a former, more continuous range. The authors link the restricted present-day distributions to changes in stream habitat, especially increased siltation, turbidity, and pollution in the [[Kansas River]] and [[Missouri River]] basins.

## Background & Description

**Orconectes luteus** is most common in streams draining the northern and eastern [[Ozark Plateau]] in Missouri. According to [[Williams 1954]], its range did not extend farther north than the Osage River drainage in Kansas and Missouri. [[Creaser 1933]] describes its typical habitat as clear, shallow, swiftly flowing rivers, absent from streams with muddy or sandy beds.

**Orconectes neglectus neglectus** has been localized in three areas: (1) part of the southern Ozark Plateau, (2) some tributaries of the Kansas River in the [[Flint Hills]] of north-central Kansas, and (3) extreme southwestern Nebraska, northwestern Kansas, and northeastern Colorado. Most earlier records came from rocky streams.

**Morphological variation:** [[Williams 1954]] reports that O. luteus varies considerably in chela size and shape, in the presence or absence of a second mesial carpal spine, and in the shape of the annulus ventralis across its range. The authors' specimens had typical O. luteus chelae, but 37 percent had a minute second mesial carpal spine. This spine is common in O. luteus from the Meramec, Big, Gasconade, Pomme de Terre, and Sac rivers in Missouri.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Missouri localities (O. luteus, collected October 7, 1960):** Five collections in Morgan and Moniteau counties, totaling 53 specimens. Sites were on Richland Creek, Straight Creek, and Moniteau Creek, north of the Osage River system. Water temperatures ranged from 60° to 63 °F. Water was clear at all sites, and the bottoms were predominantly limestone or chert rubble. Fringe forest shaded the pools where the species was found.

**Kansas localities (O. luteus):** Two collections, totaling 42 specimens, from Cedar Creek (Johnson County, August 14, 1960; 32 specimens) and Washington Creek (Douglas County, October 15, 1961; 10 specimens). Both streams drain into the Kansas River system. Washington Creek was the only site with appreciable flow. The other streams had little or no flow, and the species persisted in riffles and in shallow pools where flow had stopped.

**Nebraska localities (O. n. neglectus, 1961):** Five collections, totaling 44 specimens, in three drainages:
- Republican River drainage: Cottonwood Creek and Elm Creek.
- Blue River drainage: a small tributary of the Big Blue River and the Little Blue River.
- Platte River drainage: Dismal River, south of Thedford.

These streams were generally shallow and sandy, with clear, cool water supplied by nearby springs. Introduced trout can survive in Elm Creek. The authors also revisited Rock Creek (Dundy County), the first Nebraska record reported by [[Williams 1954]], and found the population still present on September 5, 1960.

**Interpretation of the O. luteus distribution:** The northern populations may reflect recent northward dispersal, possibly aided by humans, or relictual populations from a formerly wider range. The authors suggest the species dispersed when the Kansas and Missouri rivers, or their precursors, were clearer and cooler, resembling Ozarkian streams. Since the [[Kansan Glaciation]], the Kansas River appears to have shifted headwater-first westward, from a rubble-bottomed stream draining the Flint Hills to a sandy, turbid Great Plains river (Frye and Leonard, 1952). Human activities have since increased siltation, turbidity, and pollution. These changes may have isolated O. luteus in tributaries where suitable habitat persists.

**Interpretation of the O. n. neglectus distribution:** The new records fill part of the gap between the Flint Hills and Nebraska populations. The authors support [[Williams 1954]] in proposing that the species once occupied a continuous range through a former central Kansas drainage system. Higher temperatures, turbidity, or pollution may have restricted it to a few cool, clear, unpolluted streams in western Kansas and Nebraska. The Dismal River record is the only known Platte drainage record since [[Engle 1926]] reported the species from the South Platte River at Julesburg, Colorado.

## Key Findings

- **Range extension for O. luteus:** The species occurs north of the Osage River system in Missouri, a range limit not previously documented. It is also recorded from the Kansas River system. Total collections: 95 specimens (53 from Missouri, 42 from Kansas).
- **Habitat of O. luteus:** Populations occurred in clear, shallow water over rubble, in riffles and shaded pools, including where flow had ceased. Pools with little or no flow were common, which departs from the swift-flow habitat described by [[Creaser 1933]].
- **Morphological variation in O. luteus:** 37 percent of the specimens had a minute second mesial carpal spine, a trait with a high frequency in several southern Missouri river populations.
- **New Nebraska records for O. n. neglectus:** 44 specimens from five sites in the Republican, Blue, and Platte drainages. The species tolerates shallow streams with sandy bottoms, which differs from the rocky streams where it was previously most recorded.
- **Bridging a distributional gap:** The O. n. neglectus records connect the Flint Hills and western Nebraska populations, supporting a former continuous range through a central Kansas drainage system.
- **Persistence of a historic site:** The Rock Creek population (Dundy County, Nebraska) was still present in 1960.
- **Conservation implications:** Siltation, turbidity, and pollution in mainstream rivers may be limiting these crayfishes to isolated tributaries. Surviving populations depend on cool, clear, and unpolluted streams.
