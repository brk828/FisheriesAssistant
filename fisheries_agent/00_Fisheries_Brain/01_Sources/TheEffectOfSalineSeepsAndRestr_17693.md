# Saline Seeps and Restricted Light Shape Phytoplankton Succession in Chevelon Creek, Arizona

#phycology #bacillariophyta #phytoplankton #limnology

## Executive Summary

This study examines how saline groundwater seeps and light limitation in a deep, narrow canyon influence the seasonal dynamics of [[Phytoplankton]] in [[Chevelon Creek]], a desert stream in north-central Arizona. Over a two-year monthly sampling program along the lower 10.5 km of the creek, the authors found phytoplankton densities to be exceptionally low at all sites, with [[Bacillariophyta]] (diatoms) dominant. Specific conductance rose from dilute spring flows to about 3600 µmhos/cm during baseflow, driven mainly by NaCl from seeps. A marked downstream succession of three centric diatoms ([[Thalassiosira Pseudonana]], [[Chaetoceros Muelleri]], and [[Rhizosolenia Minima]]) was documented. The authors attribute it to differing ionic tolerances, division rates, light utilization, and possibly [[Allelopathy]].

## Background & Description

Chevelon Creek is a small tributary of the [[Little Colorado River]] that flows through steep-sided canyons cut into [[Coconino Sandstone]]. The arid region of the southwestern USA contains many flowing-water systems that have been little studied. The authors note that desert streams tend to be more autotrophic than mesic streams because riparian canopy is reduced, but light can be strongly reduced in deeply incised canyons.

The study area lies in a rain-shadow created by the [[Mogollon Plateau]], with annual precipitation of about 18.7 cm at Winslow, Arizona. Most rain falls in July through September, and the vegetation is desert grassland. Three sites were established along the lower reaches:

- **Site 1:** 1510 m elevation, bounded by sheer Coconino Sandstone walls 15–30 m high.
- **Site 2:** 7.3 km downstream of Site 1, about 1 km below the canyon mouth, elevation 1500 m, surrounded by eroded sandstone knobs and [[Salt Cedar]]-stabilized dunes.
- **Site 3:** 3.5 km below Site 2, about 4.5 km below the canyon mouth, elevation 1490 m, on low alluvial mounds with salt cedar.

Geologically, the Moenkopi Sandstone is impermeable and sits unconformably on the Coconino Sandstone. Groundwater trapped above the underlying [[Supai Formation]], which contains evaporites, is released where the canyon cuts the water table, creating seeps that become more concentrated downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regimes.** Two seasonally predictable regimes operate. Storm-driven high flows (peak commonly about 175 cfs in late spring, with flows above 1000 cfs for several days at times) dilute the system. Maximum recorded discharge was 25,300 cfs on 19 January 1952. Long [[Baseflow]] periods, sustained by seeps, occur from mid-June through mid-November, when only the lower 20–25 km of channel contributes flow. Baseflow sometimes approaches 1 cfs.

**Chemistry.** During baseflow, specific conductance rose from a minimum of 50 µmhos/cm to 3600 µmhos/cm at Site 2 over about 90 days. Chloride at Site 3 reached 900–1100 mg/L, roughly tenfold Site 1 values. Sodium was 685 mg/L at Site 3 versus 88 mg/L at Site 1. Alkalinity fell from above 210 mg/L in summer to below 20 mg/L during winter–spring dilution. Specific conductance dropped below 0.5 mmhos/cm during flood periods.

**Physical conditions.** Water temperature ranged from 1 °C in winter to 26 °C in summer. pH ranged from 8.0–8.3 during summer and fall baseflow to below 6.0 during winter–spring floods. Dissolved oxygen exceeded 10 mg/L in winter–spring and fell below 7.0 mg/L in summer and fall.

**Light.** Within the shaded canyon section (canyon less than 16 m wide, walls over 60 m high), surface indirect light was commonly 90% lower than outside the canyon, typically below 800 ft-c. No streambed portion received more than 3 hours of direct light.

**Residence time.** Estimated residence time during baseflow at the upper reach was 2–4 days. The authors cite [[McGaha and Steen]] (1974), who suggested that 9–20 days of minimum flow time is needed for appreciable plankton development.

**Nutrients.** Nitrate-nitrogen was scarce (mean 0.03 mg/L, S.D. 0.014). Phosphate-phosphorus averaged 0.09 mg/L (S.D. 0.074). N:P ratios were generally low (0.09–1.2). Silica was never considered limiting, with concentrations above 2.0 mg/L year-round and seep inputs of 7–11 mg/L.

## Key Findings

### Phytoplankton density and composition

- Phytoplankton densities were exceptionally low at all sites. Maxima of 1.6–2.0 × 10⁴ cells/L occurred in August and September during baseflow. Winter minima were below 200 cells/L.
- Bacillariophyta were the predominant group. Chlorophyta were much less abundant (below 500 cells/L), and Euglenophyta were below 100 cells/L.
- A brief blue-green algal dominance occurred in August–September in both years, with [[Chroococcus]] spp. making up more than 80% of the cyanophytes.
- The authors identified 73 genera and 249 species, but fewer than 10% were considered true plankton. High richness was attributed to habitat variation along the conductivity gradient and numerous seeps.

### Species distribution along the gradient

- At the upstream Site 1 (conductivity 0.80–1.0 mmhos/cm), [[Stephanodiscus Invisitatus]] and [[Stephanodiscus Tenuis]] were most abundant from June to November. Their densities fell progressively at downstream sites where conductivity was commonly above 3.0 mmhos/cm.
- [[Aulacosira Distans]] var. *alpigena* showed a similar preference for conductivity below 1.0 mmhos/cm, but appeared at Site 1 only briefly.
- Mesohalobous pennate diatoms (e.g., *Entomoneis alata*, *Bacillaria paradoxa*, *Nitzschia microcephala*) were common in sediments at Sites 2 and 3 and were dislodged into the water column at low densities (below 500 cells/L). Oligohalobic pennates (e.g., *Cymbella affinis*, *Gomphonema parvulum*) persisted in low numbers along the entire gradient.
- Green algae included [[Ankistrodesmus Falcatus]] and several [[Scenedesmus]] species, with *S. quadricauda* peaking at Site 1.

### Centric diatom succession (Sites 2 and 3)

- *Thalassiosira pseudonana* appeared in early May when specific conductance exceeded 1.0 mmhos/cm, and dominated until early July. It appeared at Site 1 at densities below 100 cells/L.
- *Chaetoceros muelleri* became dominant in early July as conductance rose above 2.5 mmhos/cm, and held dominance until the end of August.
- *Rhizosolenia minima* became dominant at the end of August and remained until late October. It reached 8.9 × 10⁵ cells/L.
- The sequence was observed qualitatively over four years and quantitatively over two.
- Cell numbers were significantly higher at Site 3 than at Site 2, possibly due to longer residence time and greater light availability.

### Light and primary production

- The average annual cell number at Site 2 (within the canyon) was 2.3 × 10³ cells/L, less than half that at Site 1. At Site 3 (open channel), it was about 65% higher than at Site 2, reported as 3.6 × 10³ cells/L.
- Net primary production was low at both the canyon and open sites (below 13 mgC/m²/hr). Canyon production was commonly 2–3 times lower than at Site 3. A single comparison on 25 June gave production at Site 1 about twofold higher than Site 3 (19.7 mgC/m²/hr).

### Mechanisms proposed for succession

- **Ionic tolerance and division rates:** *T. pseudonana* (mean cell diameter about 4.5 µm) divided at 2.3–2.6 divisions/day, faster than *C. muelleri* (about 10.5 µm, 1.8–2.2 divisions/day) at 15–25 °C. Laboratory *C. muelleri* divided below 1.0 divisions/day at conductivities below 2.0 mmhos/cm, giving the smaller opportunist a short-term advantage at the onset of baseflow.
- **Light:** Daily solar radiation fell about 75% from summer solstice levels by late summer to early fall. In laboratory cultures, *C. muelleri* outcompeted *R. minima* at high light (cell ratio 45.8 at 900–1100 ft-c) but not at low light (ratio 2.7 at 150–250 ft-c). *R. minima* division rates were 0.51 (high light) and 1.12 (low light) divisions/day, versus 1.96 and 1.43 for *C. muelleri*.
- **Allelopathy:** Preliminary cultures suggested allelopathic interaction between *T. pseudonana* and *C. muelleri*, consistent with [[Sharp et al. Allelopathy Study]] (1979) on marine diatoms. The authors call for further controlled tests.
- **Temperature:** *R. minima* disappeared in early October 1978 when temperatures fell below 15 °C. It persisted longer in 1979 during an extended warm period. The authors suggest it will persist until temperatures drop below 15 °C or a winter spate dilutes waters below 2.0 mmhos/cm.

### Ionic prediction equations

The authors derived linear regressions of major anions against specific conductance (n = 67), useful for characterizing other saline streams:

- Cl⁻: y = −55.6 + 298.6X (r = +0.987)
- SO₄²⁻: y = 33.7 + 38.6X (r = +0.828)
- HCO₃⁻: y = 49.6 + 46.1X (r = +0.898)

Here X is specific conductance (mmhos/cm at 25 °C). The published regression constants are OCR-affected, so verify them against the original article before use.

### Conclusions

- Low phytoplankton densities in Chevelon Creek reflect the combined effects of short residence time, canyon light limitation, and the ionic gradient. The authors suggest this pattern may be typical of shallow desert streams that flow through light-limited canyons.
- Several interacting factors (ionic tolerance, division rate, light use, and possibly allelopathy) appear to drive the seasonal diatom succession. Laboratory and field evidence support that some, if not all, of these mechanisms operate.
