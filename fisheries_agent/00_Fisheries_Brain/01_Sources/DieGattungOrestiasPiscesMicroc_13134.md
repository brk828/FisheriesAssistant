# Orestias Speciation in the Lake Titicaca Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 paper by [[Wolfgang Villwock]] examines how the endemic killifish genus [[Orestias]] (Cyprinodontidae) diversified in the high-altitude [[Altiplano]] of Peru and Bolivia. The author reconstructs the geological and climatic history of the region, describes the present-day distribution and ecology of the genus, and evaluates two speciation models. He concludes that [[Geographic Isolation]] alone is an unsatisfactory explanation for the observed pattern, and favours [[Intralacustrine Speziation]] within an ancient, once-connected lake, driven by ecological niche differentiation. The argument is explicitly hypothetical; the author states that a firm answer requires genetic analyses such as crossing experiments.

## Background & Description

- **Study group:** The genus [[Orestias]] consists of small, endemic, oviparous fishes. The author notes they are naturally free of competitors across much of their range. Males of the common small form are yellow-bellied on the ventral side, reported as the only known case in an Orestias species where the sexes can be told apart at first sight.
- **Study area:** The Altiplano lies at about 4000 m, bounded by the Eastern and Western (Coastal) Cordilleras. The [[La Raya Range]] divides the genus's distribution into two major areas. The paper focuses on the southern Altiplano, which is better documented geologically.
- **Field basis:** The author took part in a four-month collecting expedition in 1960 led by Professor Kosswig (Hamburg). He also examined type and other material from the British Museum (Natural History), the Paris Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle, and the Bavarian State Ichthyological Collections in Munich.
- **Species treated:**
  - [[Orestias Agassii]]: the most widespread form, usually small, occurring in all shallow-water areas. The author includes the larger "agassii" populations (such as O. agassii tschudii) in one species complex, distinguished mainly by final size and timing of sexual maturity.
  - [[Orestias Luteus]]: a bottom-dwelling predator, mainly eating mollusc and ostracod shells. It occurs in the Titicaca reed belt and in some peripheral lakes (Arapa, Umayo, Checayani).
  - [[Orestias Cuvieri]]: the largest Titicaca species, about 30 cm total length (TL). Preliminary gut-content data suggest a predatory, "pike-like" habit. Its numbers fell after salmonids were introduced in 1937, and it was not recorded in 1960.
  - [[Orestias Pentlandii]]: about 20 cm TL, apparently a planktivore including fish larvae, with a "herring-like" habit.
  - Two to three further small, shore-dwelling species were noted, one with fully scaled body and others with large eyes. Their names were withheld pending synonymy checks.
  - [[Trichomycterus Rivulatus]]: a catfish (described in the source as Siluridae) that co-occurs with Orestias but is considered unlikely to affect them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Palaeogeographic history.** The paper summarises [[Moon 1939]] for the sequence of lakes:

1. Pliocene: a lower, subtropical "Ur-Altiplano" with extensive lakes and floodplains.
2. Late Pliocene to early Pleistocene: uplift raised the Altiplano to about 4000 m, extinguishing the earlier fauna except for survivors. The author argues the Orestias ancestor must already have been present in the uplifted waters, since the genus has poor swimming ability.
3. Pleistocene: progressive aridity produced [[Lake Ballivian]], which covered most of the southern Altiplano, then split into the Ur-Titicaca lake, [[Lake Minchin]] and possibly [[Lake Reck]].
4. Postglacial: continued drying shrank these lakes to today's remnants: [[Lake Titicaca]], [[Lake Poopo]] (brackish) and the [[Saladas de Uyuni]] (salt flats). The author notes that Titicaca may follow the same fate unless an outflow forms through erosion.

**Lake Titicaca.** The lake covers about 8000 km² with a maximum depth of about 280 m. It has a varied shoreline: extensive shallow zones with rushes (*Scirpus*-type belts), submerged algae and plants such as *Potamogeton*-type and *Myriophyllum*, plus steep shores with depths of 20 m or more and rocky bottoms. Smaller lakes (Arapa, Umayo, Lagunillas, Checayani) are either isolated or linked to the main lake. Only the larger ones reproduce the lake's ecological conditions. The author describes the Moho area as having steep, rocky shores.

**Ecological partitioning.** The Orestias species occupy distinct niches:
- Shallow littoral shores: small O. agassii.
- Reed belt outer edge and deep water: O. luteus (bottom predator).
- Open bays: O. cuvieri (predator) and O. pentlandii (planktivore).
- Steep rocky shores: a scaled, shore-dwelling species.

**Other fauna.** [[Hyalella]] is an amphipod genus that the author cites as showing endemism in Titicaca. Citing Brooks, he notes that Hyalella is the only amphipod of continental South America, with several endemic species in the lake (the count is garbled in the scanned text).

## Key Findings

1. **Morphological pattern:** Apart from the widespread, polymorphic O. agassii complex, the main lake holds greater species diversity than any other basin. Only O. luteus is shared with peripheral lakes, where it finds conditions similar to the main lake.
2. **Non-random distribution:** Titicaca species show adaptations to specific habitats rather than random dispersal across the lake.
3. **Rejecting pure geographic isolation:** The author finds it implausible that isolated, ecologically similar lakes (including the northern Altiplano and the Lagunillas) would each produce only one of the same forms. Postglacial isolation is too recent for it to have driven speciation, which must have been complete before the lakes became separated.
4. **Favoured model, intralacustrine speziation:** A single ancestral population, probably in the early Pleistocene [[Lake Ballivian]], occupied a large, ecologically varied lake. Adaptive radiation into different niches (bays, river mouths, shallow shores, steep rocky shores) was followed by reduced gene flow within the lake, producing species.
5. **Ongoing process:** The large O. agassii forms in Titicaca are presented as possible examples of ongoing intralacustrine speciation, since they differ from small O. agassii in size and maturity timing and occupy a distinct niche (dense rush beds).
6. **Subspecies and gene flow:** Geographic isolation may still matter for subspecies differentiation, and gene flow through connected shallow waters may explain the conservative "basic type" of O. agassii.
7. **Limitations and next steps:** The author states that a conclusive answer requires genetic analysis, such as crossing experiments like those applied to [[Anatolian Cyprinodontids]] by Kosswig and colleagues. The author notes these investigations are planned.

**Comparative reference:** The author draws a parallel with the [[Baikal Lake]] fauna, which he cites as a classic example of intralacustrine speciation, and suggests the Titicaca genus might be interpreted similarly.
