# Water Weeds: Their Value and Control

#aquatic-botany #algae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1940 extension article by C. J. D. Brown of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]], [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]], argues that aquatic plants and algae are essential to fish production, yet can become a nuisance that justifies control in limited circumstances. It covers the role of [[Algae]] in the [[Food Chain]], the zonation of higher aquatic plants, the relationship between [[Aquatic Vegetation And Fish]], and the risks and limits of [[Chemical Weed Control]] and [[Mechanical Weed Control]]. The author concludes that weed eradication is justified in a fisheries program only for small, weed-filled fish-producing ponds and lakes, and that chemical methods are not yet safe enough for general use.

## Background & Description

The article distinguishes two broad groups of aquatic vegetation:

- **Algae:** one-celled plants that form "water slime" or "pond scum." More than half of the aquatic plant kinds in Michigan are too small to see with the unaided eye. They occur from the surface into fairly deep water in most Michigan lakes, but become a problem only under favorable conditions.
- **Higher aquatic plants ("water weeds"):** leafed, stemmed and rooted plants. The article notes that at least a hundred kinds are common in Michigan lakes and ponds.

The higher plants are grouped by growth habit into three zones that together form a lake's plant community:

| Zone | Examples | Typical depth |
|---|---|---|
| Emergent | Cattails, bulrushes | Shallow water, standing above the surface |
| Floating | Yellow and white water lilies | About 3 to 12 feet |
| Submerged | Common pondweed, eel grass, water milfoil, coontail | Seldom reaches the surface; down to about 20 feet |

Some plants, such as coontail and water milfoil, are seldom rooted and depend mainly on nutrients dissolved in the water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nutrients and soil:** Plant growth depends on soil fertility. Lakes and streams rich in plant foods are usually found in good agricultural districts or drainage areas with fertile topsoil.
- **Light and depth:** Plants need sunlight, so growth is limited by depth. In ordinary lakes the lower limit is about 20 feet, but it depends on turbidity. Very clear water may support plants to 40 or 50 feet, while very turbid water often excludes vegetation. In average natural waters the most abundant beds occur between 2 and 12 feet.
- **Substrate:** Sand bottoms in wave action prevent plant establishment because moving sand destroys young plants. This explains the near absence of vegetation along the shoals of larger Michigan lakes.
- **Lake aging:** Lakes with vegetation throughout are generally shallow, muddy and old. Organic deposits from decaying plants gradually fill the basin, and many extinct lakes now appear as marshes or bogs along Michigan highways. Photographs in the article show a lake being overtaken by encroaching shoreline vegetation and a former lake bed used as cornfield.
- **Algal blooms:** Dense algal masses can choke small lakes and ponds, produce a strong odor and release poisonous substances when they decay. Fish and livestock have died from this poisoning.

## Key Findings

**Algae and the food chain**
- Algae are essential to fish production. Although seldom eaten directly, they transform elements into usable substances and are the food of fish food organisms.
- Copper sulphate is the best-known algicide. A concentration of 0.3 to 0.5 parts per million is considered safe under most circumstances. Water must be studied before treatment, because a dose that kills algae in hard water can kill fish in soft water.

**Aquatic plants and fish**
- Most panfish live in or near weed beds, which provide cover and more abundant food.
- Studies on certain small Michigan lakes found food organisms "many hundreds times" more numerous in submerged weed beds than in equal areas without weeds.
- Some fishes spawn on plant leaves and stems.
- Food organisms increase in almost direct proportion to vegetation density. Very dense weed beds, however, reduce space for fish, can lower oxygen to dangerous levels during dark or cloudy periods, and make angling impossible, so the legal fish cannot be harvested.

**When weed removal is justified**
- Weed eradication can be justified in a fisheries program only on ponds or small lakes that are known to produce fish and fill with weeds early in the season.
- Eradication is desirable in hatchery ponds to safeguard stock.
- Removing weeds from bays of lakes with large open water is questionable for fish production.
- Where swimming and boating are more important than fishing, complete removal may be justified. Restricting control to swimming beaches and boat paths will probably not seriously affect fish production. Decisions should serve the greatest public interest, not the desires of a few.

**Chemical control**
- Chemicals toxic enough to kill plants are also toxic to fish and other organisms.
- Under Michigan law, chemicals may be used only under competent supervision and with Conservation Department permission. Treatment of public waters by laymen is prohibited.
- Private ponds may be treated at the owner's discretion. Treating streams that cross private property is dangerous because poison washes downstream. The article reports cases where vegetation and fish were killed a mile or more below the treated section.
- Sodium arsenite has been used against common pondweed, coontail and waterstar grass. One author recommends a solution of 1 part per million of arsenous oxide, made by mixing one gallon of concentrate with 64,082 gallons of water, applied by pressure sprayer. Sodium arsenite is also dangerous to humans and livestock and can sterilize soil for a long time.
- Results vary with minerals and organic matter in the water, so even experts cannot predict outcomes reliably. The author states that chemical control is still experimental and does not recommend its use until further studies are made.

**Mechanical control**
- **Weed saw:** A serrated blade worked from the bank severs stems, and cut plants are dragged ashore. Reported effectiveness varies, probably depending on plant type and density.
- **Mower-type cutters:** Boat- or raft-mounted underwater cutters are costly. Some users reported success, while others said cutting spread the weeds. Results depend on weed type, density and cutting frequency.
- **Dredging:** Very effective when water is deepened enough to prevent regrowth, and the only solution for lakes filled with organic deposits. It is very expensive and practical only for waters of very high recreational value.
- **Hand tools:** Scythes and rakes work for private docks and small beaches but require several cuttings each season.

**Overall conclusion**
The author views water plant eradication as a balance between fish production and public recreation. Control should be limited, supervised and matched to the specific water body, because no single chemical or mechanical method has proven satisfactory for all situations.
