# Species-Area Relationships for Stream Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

Angermeier and Schlosser (published in *Ecology*, Vol. 70, No. 5, October 1989) test whether species-area relationships among stream fish assemblages reflect habitat size itself or mainly passive sampling of more individuals. They sampled riffle and pool habitats in small streams in [[Minnesota]], [[Illinois]], and [[Panama]], comparing [[Habitat Area]], [[Habitat Volume]], [[Habitat Complexity]], and [[Number of Individuals]] as predictors of [[Species Richness]]. Habitat volume generally outperformed area, and the number of individuals was usually the strongest single predictor. The authors conclude that species-area patterns are largely sampling phenomena in these systems, but that habitat structure still matters for fish distribution and abundance, particularly in the more stable Panama streams.

## Background & Description

The study addresses two long-standing questions in community ecology: why species richness and habitat size are positively related, and what causes variation in the slope, intercept, and scatter of species-area relationships. Three hypotheses are reviewed:

- **Island biogeography** ([[Island Biogeography Theory]]): immigration and extinction rates depend on island size; weak fit for habitat patches that do not support self-sustaining populations.
- **Habitat heterogeneity**: larger patches contain more habitat types and niches.
- **Sampling effect**: larger areas hold more individuals and so sample the regional species pool more completely ([[Passive Sampling Hypothesis]]).

Streams are argued to be a good system for this test. Riffles and pools are easily delimited, and fish can be counted accurately. Each patch is treated as an "archipelago" element, though fish move freely among patches, unlike true islands.

Study sites:

- **Minnesota**: Sucker Creek and Gould Creek, headwaters of the Mississippi River; dominated by cyprinids; sampled August 1983 and 1984.
- **Illinois**: Jordan Creek and Range Creek, headwaters of the Vermilion and Embarras rivers; rich faunas of cyprinids, centrarchids, catostomids, and percids; sampled August 1979 and 1980.
- **Panama**: Quebrada Juan Grande, Rio Frijolito, and Tower Creek, headwaters of the Rio Chagres in [[Parque Nacional Soberanía]]; fauna dominated by characids, loricariids, and poeciliids; sampled January 1980 and March 1981.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology**: Minnesota streams peak in spring (April–May) with variable summer flows. Illinois streams peak in spring with low, variable late-summer flow. Panama streams follow a seasonal rainfall pattern, with the driest months January through March receiving less than 5% of annual precipitation.
- **Habitat measurement**: Depth, current, and substrate were recorded along transects. A [[Habitat Complexity Index]] was built from the Shannon-Wiener index applied to unique depth–current–substrate configurations.
- **Sampling gear**: Minnesota and Jordan Creek used electrofishing (two passes with a 7-m electric seine). Range Creek and Panama pools used bagseines (three passes), and riffles were sampled by kicking through substrate upstream of a net. The authors estimate that efficiencies of the two methods were within 10–20% of each other.
- **Harsh seasonality**: Minnesota streams face severe winters, and few fish overwinter in small streams; fish apparently recolonize each spring from downstream reaches or connecting lakes. Most Illinois and Panama fishes appear to be year-round residents.
- **Reproductive window**: All Minnesota species spawn between mid-May and July, while Panama species show diverse reproductive periodicity.

## Key Findings

1. **Volume beats area.** Site volume was a better predictor of species richness than site area in six of seven streams. Jordan Creek, Illinois, was the exception. Sucker Creek and Jordan Creek showed significant year effects regardless of the size measure.
2. **Individuals beat volume.** Number of individuals was the stronger correlate in 16 of 19 cases (Table 3). Multiple regression confirmed that the number of individuals was consistently significant across streams, whereas volume was significant only in the two Illinois streams once individuals were accounted for.
3. **Complexity adds little.** Habitat complexity did not contribute significantly to any multiple regression model. It was correlated with species richness only in Rio Frijolito and Tower Creek, and it was not correlated with volume in Range Creek or the Minnesota streams.
4. **Regional contrast.** Slopes of species–volume relationships did not differ among regions, but Panama sites held 1–2 more species per unit volume (P < .05). In Panama, pools held about two more species per unit volume than riffles.
5. **Species-level abundance.** Only 7 of 79 species tests (9%) in Illinois and Minnesota showed positive abundance–volume correlations, compared with 20 of 57 tests (35%) for Panama species. Mean variance explained by volume was 78% in Panama versus 45% for temperate species (ranges 41–93% and 35–61%).
6. **Interannual stability.** Relative abundances were strongly concordant between years in all streams except Gould Creek (P < .01). Concordance was higher in pools than riffles. In Minnesota, the share of species captured in only one of two years was high, and correlations were weakest.
7. **Density–richness relationship.** Total fish density was positively correlated with richness in Minnesota pools, uncorrelated in Illinois pools, and negatively correlated in Panama pools, consistent with density compensation.
8. **Central hypothesis.** Annual reproductive variability combined with harsh winters keeps Minnesota assemblages in near-continual imbalance with their habitat. Immigration–extinction and recolonization dynamics are thought to play a larger organizing role in Minnesota and Illinois than in Panama. The authors caution that the interpretation is a hypothesis awaiting experimental testing, since similar population dynamics can arise from different coexistence mechanisms.
9. **Rifle fishes.** Rifle species appear to be largely opportunistic rather than specialized, as shown by large year-to-year variation in rank abundance. Rifles present energy costs, exposure risks, and fewer trophic opportunities than pools.
