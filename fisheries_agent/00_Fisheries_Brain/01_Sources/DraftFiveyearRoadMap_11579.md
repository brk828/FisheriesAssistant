# Five-Year Road Map for Recovery Implementation Program, Rare and Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #recovery-program

## Executive Summary

This draft document presents a comprehensive five-year road map (dated June 29, 1990) for the Recovery Implementation Program targeting four rare and endangered fish species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The geographic scope encompasses the historic range of these species above [[Lake Powell]], excluding the [[San Juan River]] pending separate management planning. The program establishes an overarching goal to achieve self-sustaining populations of all four species and protect their essential habitats through coordinated management activities including flow regulation, habitat enhancement, genetic conservation through refugia, controlled propagation, nonnative species management, and public education.

## Background & Description

The Recovery Implementation Program addresses four native fish species facing extinction in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Each species exhibits different conservation status requiring tailored approaches:

**[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: The largest cyprinid in North America, historically widespread throughout the basin. The [[Yampa River]] and upper [[Green River]] system currently support the most viable populations with evidence of successful reproduction. The [[Colorado River]] mainstem population between [[Lake Powell]] and Rifle, Colorado shows inadequate recruitment with rare juveniles, indicating a declining adult population.

**[[Razorback Sucker]]**: An endangered catostomid exhibiting negligible to nonexistent recruitment in wild populations. Remaining populations consist almost exclusively of large, old adults. The last verified juveniles from the entire Upper Colorado River Basin were collected from the [[Colorado River]] near Moab, Utah in 1962-1964. Collection numbers have declined dramatically: 52 adults (1979-1981), 23 adults (1982-1985), and only 7 adults (1986-1988).

**[[Humpback Chub]]**: Populations exist in several concentration areas including [[Black Rocks]]/[[Westwater Canyon]] (the largest and believed stable), [[Dinosaur National Monument]], [[Desolation Canyon]]/[[Gray Canyon]], and [[Cataract Canyon]]. Specimens have been collected sporadically from other locations including Professor Valley, Elephant Canyon, and various river miles on the Green, Yampa, and Little Snake rivers.

**[[Bonytail Chub]]**: On the verge of extinction in the wild with no evidence of self-sustaining populations. Captive stocks from [[Lake Mojave]] are being maintained, but immediate action is required to prevent complete extinction and preserve remaining genetic material.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies multiple anthropogenic factors contributing to species decline:

**Hydrological Alterations**: Large water development projects and dam operations (particularly [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and the [[Aspinall Unit]]) have fundamentally altered natural flow regimes. The program emphasizes maintaining high spring flows to flood lowlands, provide migration cues, and create/maintain habitats; gradually declining summer flows for growth and survival of young; low stable flows in late summer and autumn; and stable winter flows through ice breakup.

**Habitat Fragmentation**: Dams and diversion structures have isolated population segments, preventing genetic interchange and blocking access to essential spawning and rearing habitats. Seasonal concentrations of squawfish in dam tailwaters suggest thwarted migration attempts. Specific barriers identified include Redlands, Taylor Draw, and Price Stub diversions.

**Nonnative Species**: Introduced fishes negatively impact native species through competition and predation. Specific concerns include [[Northern Pike]] impacts on squawfish in the [[Yampa River]] and various exotic species throughout the basin requiring management through flow regulation and direct control.

**Sediment Dynamics**: Flow regulation alters sediment supply and transport capacity, potentially degrading spawning substrate (cobble bars), filling pool/riffle habitat, and eliminating backwater nursery areas through bar/island consolidation with stream banks.

**Contaminants**: Both catastrophic events (oil spills) and chronic contamination from pesticides and heavy metals in the upper [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], [[Dolores River]], and Grand Valley portion of the [[Colorado River]] pose threats requiring assessment and remediation.

**Recruitment Failure**: Early life stage mortality represents a critical bottleneck across all species, particularly evident in razorback sucker where no juveniles have been documented in decades despite presence of spawning adults.

## Key Findings

### Species-Specific Recovery Goals and Priorities

**Colorado Squawfish**:
1. Maintain and enhance wild populations in the [[Yampa River]] (the "working system" providing baseline knowledge), [[Green River]] (largest population numbers), [[Colorado River]] (declining population requiring habitat enhancement), and [[White River]]
2. Capture and secure individuals representative of presumptive genetic populations in refugia before remnant stocks are extirpated
3. Establish or augment populations through natural reproduction, experimental augmentation following Propagation Plan protocol, and potential reintroduction to currently unoccupied habitat

**Razorback Sucker** (highest urgency given near-complete recruitment failure):
1. Immediate priority to capture and secure genotype representatives (30 adults per population if possible) before genetic material is permanently lost
2. Enhance existing captive broodstock (FY 88-95) and determine modes of reproduction, including mechanisms of migration, olfaction, rearing, and recruitment
3. Identify reasons for reproduction/recruitment failure and promote recruitment through habitat management, particularly in [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]] systems and experimental augmentation in the [[Colorado River]]

**Humpback Chub**:
1. Protect and conserve populations in primary recovery sites: [[Black Rocks]]/[[Westwater Canyon]] (largest, stable population), [[Yampa River]]/[[Green River]] systems, and [[Cataract Canyon]]
2. Define wild populations using measurable habitat descriptors and develop predictive habitat vs. flow relationships
3. Provide refugia protection for low-concentration populations and establish self-sustaining populations in currently unoccupied habitat

**Bonytail Chub** (most endangered, requiring immediate action):
1. Capture, transport, and hold ALL suspected bonytail encountered; finalize/reinstitute capture/transport protocol
2. Maintain existing captive stocks and genetic material through implementation of Propagation Plan
3. Establish self-sustaining populations through reintroduction at identified sites including [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (begun FY 89), [[Cataract Canyon]], and [[Desolation Canyon]]/[[Gray Canyon]]

### Water Acquisition and Flow Management Priorities

The program identifies water acquisition as critical for long-term habitat protection within state water law frameworks:

**[[Yampa River]]**: Acquire water rights using Phase II flow recommendations (FY 90+); develop and implement Phase III studies (FY 91+); Service, [[Bureau of Land Management]], and [[The Nature Conservancy]] should execute agreement on [[Cross Mountain Ranch]] water rights upon securing purchase option (FY 90)

**[[Little Snake River]]**: Execute Cooperative Agreement between Service and State of Wyoming for technical evaluation of conditional water rights (by August 1, 1990); prepare work plan for developing acquisition/appropriation options (FY 90)

**15-Mile Reach** (Colorado River): Make purchase of existing absolute water rights convertible to instream flows the Recovery Program's first priority (FY 91+); initiate water availability study (FY 90-91); decide on filing for instream flow appropriation based on species needs (FY 90); evaluate leasing water from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] or upstream sources

**[[Green River]]**: Re-regulate [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases (FY 91) through exploring options, developing operational criteria, implementing changes, and conducting annual meetings between Service and [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to refine recommendations

**[[White River]]**: Identify habitat and life history requirements through work plan development (May 1990), literature review (FY 91), and research (FY 91+) before developing flow recommendations

### Genetic Conservation and Propagation Strategy

The program emphasizes genetic diversity conservation through multi-faceted approach:

**Refugia Development**: Identify and secure at least one refugium by April 1, 1990; identify additional facilities (FY 91); secure additional facilities (FY 92). Develop criteria and priorities for removal of fish from wild populations by March 1, 1990.

**Genetic Assessment**: Inventory and genetically assess both captive stocks and wild stocks using standard biochemical and molecular techniques (begun FY 89-90). Priority assessment areas include Yampa, Green, and Colorado rivers (inventory FY 90-91; genetic assessment FY 91-92).

**Broodstock Development**: Begin development of broodstock for each identified population (FY 92-93) following Propagation and Genetics Management Plans protocol. Manage refugia populations based on genetics characterization results (beginning FY 95).

**Stocking Protocol**: All augmentation and reintroduction must follow Propagation Plan procedures including: identifying potential sites based on habitat quality/quantity; establishing criteria for stocking location, timing, and fish size; developing culture methods to conserve genetics; producing genetically compatible fish; marking and stocking fish; and evaluating results through monitoring.

### Cross-Cutting Program Elements

**Information and Education**: Generate public support through newsletter production (March and September annually), full-color brochure distribution, addition of rare fish photos to state fishing regulations, development of displays for high-use areas, 20-minute videotape production (FY 91), and coordination with Project WILD educational programs. Conduct baseline survey of public awareness (FY 90) with follow-up surveys to evaluate effectiveness.

**Nonnative Fish Management**: Develop procedures and agreement with Utah and Wyoming for Service review of state proposals to stock nonnative fishes (FY 90); identify species impacting rare fishes and nature/degree of impacts; explore opportunities to eliminate nonnatives including [[Northern Pike]] from [[Yampa River]] (FY 91-92).

**Sportfishing Management**: Develop Upper Colorado River Basin sportfishery management policy addressing stocking, incidental take, and law enforcement in coordination with state and federal managers (FY 91); assess magnitude of incidental take and identify problem areas (FY 90+); evaluate feasibility of promoting [[Colorado Squawfish]] as sportfish including monitoring of [[Kenney Reservoir]] experimental fishery (FY 91-95).

**Monitoring and Evaluation**: Evaluate efficacy of Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program (ISMP) by clearly defining objectives, evaluating effectiveness, and modifying as appropriate (FY 90); incorporate non-lethal genetic sampling (FY 92); determine effects of ISMP on rare fish survival, growth, and reproduction (FY 91); develop training program on rare fish sampling and handling procedures (FY 91).

**Data Management**: Coordinate data management activities across federal, state, and private agencies; evaluate existing program effectiveness and continue or modify as appropriate (FY 90).

**Sediment Management**: Determine substrate characteristics necessary for successful reproduction (FY 91+); determine flows necessary to prepare and maintain spawning substrate (FY 93+); monitor changes in habitat characteristics (FY 90+); develop sediment models for sensitive reaches (FY 93+).

**Contaminants**: Review Yampa oil spill report and determine Recovery Program role in catastrophic event response (FY 91); review Service reports on effects of pesticides and heavy metals in upper [[Green River]] (FY 92) and [[Gunnison River]]/[[Dolores River]]/Grand Valley [[Colorado River]] (FY 92); identify information gaps and make recommendations (FY 92).

**Recovery Goals**: Review existing Recovery Plans and translate goals for Recovery Implementation Program use (FY 91); develop quantifiable recovery goals for downlisting/delisting species (beginning FY 90); refine Road Map and prioritize tasks annually through meetings, outside review, and revision (FY 90+).

### Implementation Timeline and Priorities

The Summary Table categorizes 200+ specific tasks by Recovery Target Element: Habitat Management (HMG), Habitat Development (HMD), Stocking (STK), Nonnatives and Sportfishing (NNA), Research/Monitoring/Data Management (RMD), and Other (OTH). Priority rankings guide annual work planning.

Immediate priorities (FY 90) include: securing first refugium (April 1, 1990); developing fish removal criteria (March 1, 1990); executing [[Little Snake River]] Cooperative Agreement (August 1, 1990); finalizing fish passage report; implementing 15-mile reach flow recommendations; acquiring [[Yampa River]] water rights; and initiating multiple baseline assessments.

The program explicitly recognizes that listed tasks exceed five-year capacity, requiring annual prioritization with input from researchers and involved parties. The Road Map will be reviewed, revised, and tasks re-prioritized annually to maintain adaptive management flexibility while progressing toward quantifiable recovery goals.
