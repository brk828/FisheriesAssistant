# Changing Chemical and Biological Conditions in Oneida Lake, New York

## Executive Summary
This paper (Dence & Jackson, 1959) presents a longitudinal (1927–1958) limnological and ichthyological case study of [[Oneida Lake]], New York, documenting dramatic increases in total alkalinity (from ~13.5 ppm to ~82–88 ppm, a ~424% increase), a shift in dominant phytoplankton from diatoms to blue-green algae, a >500% increase in seston organic matter, and major restructuring of the fish community. Key drivers of faunal change include anthropogenic modification of the [[Caughdenoy Dam]] (replacement of an impermeable concrete dam with a permeable Painter-type gate dam in 1951), which permitted new invasive/exotic species — [[Gizzard Shad]], [[White Perch]], and [[Freshwater Drum]] (sheepshead) — to colonize the lake via the Barge Canal system. These invasions coincided with declines and later recoveries of native sport fish such as [[Walleye]] (pikeperch) and [[Yellow Perch]], while historically important species like [[Tullibee]] and [[American Eel]] collapsed due to overharvest, predation, and canal lock barriers.

## Background & Description
The paper is ecological/limnological rather than morphometric, but provides some size/condition data relevant to fish biology:
- **Pikeperch (Walleye, *Stizostedion vitreum*)**: Fish stripped for eggs in March 1955 were noted to be uniformly "fat," indicating high prey availability (linked to gizzard shad abundance).
- **White Perch (*Roccus americana*)**: Locally-caught specimens by anglers exceeded 0.5 lb, though most net-caught individuals weighed less — suggesting a population skewed toward smaller/younger fish during the initial invasion phase, consistent with rapid recruitment and high juvenile survival.
- **Tullibee / Oneida Whitefish (*Coregonus artedi tullibee*)**: Once commercially harvested at 4–5 tons/year (1914), later reduced to rarity; a single specimen recaptured in 1958 after years of absence from seine catches.
- **Sea Lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*)**: Presence inferred indirectly via scarring on dead host fish rather than direct specimen morphology; large lamprey-scarred fish carcasses were historically common.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Chemical Environment
- Total alkalinity (as CaCO₃) near Cleveland, NY rose from an average of **13.5 ppm (1927)** to **~82–88 ppm (1946–1954)** — a 424% increase.
- Higher alkalinity correlates with harder water, reduced free CO₂, altered iron availability, and shifted nitrate-phosphate ratios — all bottom-up drivers of biotic change.
- The Oneida River below Brewerton showed comparatively modest alkalinity change (78 → 89 ppm), buffered by inflows from Big Bay and Chittenango Creek.

### Primary Producers
- **1927**: Diatom-dominated phytoplankton (*Fragilaria*, *Tabellaria*, *Asterionella*, *Stephanodiscus*) per [[Muenscher (1928)|Muenscher]].
- **1950s**: Shift to blue-green algae dominance (*Gloeotrichia*, *Aphanizomenon*, with reduced *Microcystis* and *Anabaena*), consistent with limnological literature linking diatom dominance to alkalinity <25 ppm and blue-green dominance to 45–125 ppm.
- Algal blooms (e.g., *Microcystis*/*Rivularia*, 1942; *Microcystis*, 1944) caused beach fouling and fish kills upon decay — an early eutrophication signal.
- Seston organic matter increased from **2.25 mg/L (1927)** to **12.7 mg/L (1954)** — a 564% increase, reflecting both phytoplankton and inferred zooplankton increases.

### Habitat Structure
- Littoral **aquatic macrophyte ("weed") beds**, once extensive in the western lake basin ([[Baker 1916|Baker]]), have substantially declined due to water-level fluctuation and shoreline residential development (cottage count increased ~50% in 20 years, to ~6,000).
- Loss of macrophyte habitat is hypothesized to negatively affect invertebrate fauna (mollusks, mayflies), which underpin fish food webs — though the authors note this remains unconfirmed.

### Connectivity & Invasion Pathway
- The 1909 **impermeable concrete dam at Caughdenoy** (Barge Canal system) blocked upstream fish passage, contributing to the collapse of the historically major commercial [[American Eel]] fishery (41,635 lbs harvested in 1913 vs. near-absence by the 1950s).
- Replacement with a **permeable Painter-type gate dam (1951)** reopened upstream passage, enabling colonization by [[Gizzard Shad]], [[White Perch]], and [[Freshwater Drum]] via the Oneida-Oswego River/Barge Canal corridor from Lake Ontario.

## Key Findings
- **Alkalinity increase (424%)** from 1927–1954 fundamentally altered water chemistry and correlates with a phytoplankton community shift from diatom- to blue-green algae-dominance.
- **Seston (organic matter) increased 564%** over the same period, indicating broad productivity increases.
- **Tullibee ([[Coregonus artedi tullibee]])** collapsed from a 4–5 ton/year commercial harvest (1914) to near-extinction by 1932, potentially linked to predation pressure from [[Sea Lamprey]]; a reciprocal decline in lamprey scarring incidence followed, with both showing signs of resurgence by 1957–58.
- **Atlantic Salmon** likely extirpated from Oneida Lake by the early 1900s (last historical record 1842), illustrating natural extinction dynamics.
- **American Eel** fishery collapse (41,635 lbs in 1913 → near absence) attributed directly to the impassable 1909 Caughdenoy dam blocking eel migration through canal locks.
- **Dam permeability change (1951)** acted as a keystone anthropogenic event enabling three exotic/invasive species to establish: [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), [[White Perch]] (*Roccus americana*), and [[Freshwater Drum]]/sheepshead (*Aplodinotus grunniens*).
- **Gizzard Shad boom-bust dynamic**: First appeared in Oneida Lake ~1954, coincided with the poorest [[Walleye]] (pikeperch) fishing in 50 years (1954–55), but by 1958 the shad population had nearly vanished, suggesting a migratory/transient population rather than an established resident one. A single pikeperch was estimated (per Forbes 1880) to consume ≥600 gizzard shad/year.
- **Walleye (Pikeperch) recovery**: Improved markedly in 1956–58, possibly due to a "buffer effect" of abundant gizzard shad as alternative prey, reducing predation pressure on juvenile walleye.
- **White Perch** invasion progressed stepwise through the watershed: Cross Lake → Onondaga Lake → Oneida Lake (first notable abundance, April 1955), with evidence of high juvenile survival driving rapid population growth.
- **Freshwater Drum (Sheepshead)** was the most recent invader, first noted in the pikeperch seine in Spring 1957.
- **Yellow Perch** fishing quality declined steadily from the early 1930s to 1948, then improved significantly from 1948 onward in both catch numbers and fish size — potentially benefiting from the presence of exotic competitor/prey species rather than being harmed by them.
- Several other regional species (**Lake Sturgeon, Longnose Gar, Mooneye, Muskellunge, Rainbow Trout**) were noted as present in the broader watershed but failed to establish populations in Oneida Lake proper.
- The study frames Oneida Lake as a dynamic ecological system undergoing continuous change, referencing [[Mozley (1954)]]'s characterization of the lake as "one of the most productive lakes on any continent," with the authors calling for continued longitudinal monitoring (proposing a future comparison point in 1981).
