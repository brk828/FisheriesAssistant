## Executive Summary

This is a scientific exchange between [[Stuart G. Fisher]] and [[Barbara L. Peckarsky]] published in *Can. J. Fish. Aquat. Sci.* (1987), debating whether [[Ecological Succession]] occurs in typical New England woodland streams following substrate disturbance. Fisher (1983) had originally hypothesized that, unlike desert streams subject to catastrophic flash floods ([[Desert Stream Ecosystem|Fisher et al. 1982]]), New England streams — where flooding is more frequent and biota are pre-adapted — would **not** show strong site-specific succession. Peckarsky's 1986 study, which denuded natural substrates in a small woodland stream and tracked colonization by aquatic insects over 48 days, found that elapsed time since denudation significantly predicted insect abundance and richness, leading her to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that succession *does* occur.

Fisher's letter challenges this conclusion on grounds of **spatial scale** (the denuded patch is far smaller than the scale at which flood disturbance and true ecosystem-level succession operate) and **falsifiability** (the experimental design makes the null hypothesis functionally untestable, since recolonization of small patches by mobile organisms is a near-certainty). Peckarsky's reply defends the ecological relevance of **micro-scale disturbance** (rock-rolling, patch formation) as a legitimate and independently meaningful scale of study, drawing analogies to gap dynamics in forests and the marine rocky intertidal, while conceding that her study's temporal scope (a single 48-day autumn period) was too limited to properly test for seasonal effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Lotic freshwater systems — specifically contrasted between:
  - [[Desert Stream Ecosystems]] (e.g., Sonoran Desert streams) — characterized by catastrophic flash flooding, high biotic mortality, scoured fine-gravel substrates, and pronounced post-flood succession (per [[Fisher et al. 1982]]).
  - [[Temperate Woodland Streams]] (New England / New York) — characterized by more frequent, less catastrophic flooding; substrates and biota purportedly pre-adapted to disturbance, yielding lower flood-associated mortality.

- **Disturbance regime:** Central ecological driver is [[Flood Disturbance]] and substrate scouring, which resets benthic community composition. Disturbance is discussed across a hierarchy of spatial scales (per [[Sheldon 1984]]):
  - **Micro-level** — individual rock/substrate patch disturbance (e.g., rock-rolling; [[McAuliffe 1983]], [[McAuliffe 1984]]).
  - **Meso-level** and **Macro-level** — larger reach- and catchment-scale disturbances, including catastrophic flash floods.

- **Community/trophic context:** Focal organisms are **benthic macroinvertebrates / aquatic insects**, examined via colonization dynamics onto denuded/enclosed natural substrates. Analogous disturbance-recovery dynamics are drawn from terrestrial ([[Forest Gap Dynamics]]) and marine ([[Rocky Intertidal Patch Dynamics]], per [[Paine and Levin 1981]]) systems, suggesting cross-ecosystem generality of patch-scale disturbance theory.

- **Independent variables affecting colonization:** water depth, current velocity, water temperature, time since disturbance, and season — evaluated via correlation/partial correlation and ANOVA to determine which best predict biological state variables (insect abundance, richness, and by extension possible chlorophyll/algal proxies).

## Key Findings

- **Original hypothesis ([[Fisher 1983]]):** Site-specific succession is less likely in New England streams than in desert streams because higher flood frequency, biotic pre-adaptation, and greater substrate stability reduce flood-driven mortality.

- **Peckarsky (1986) empirical result:** In a 48-day autumn experiment using denuded substrate patches in a New York woodland stream:
  - Time since denudation **significantly** predicted number and species of colonizing aquatic insects.
  - Depth change was **marginally significant**.
  - Temperature change, velocity change, and season were **not significant** predictors.
  - Two-way ANOVA: time since disturbance had a **highly significant** effect on total abundance/species richness; season (cage retrieval date) was **nonsignificant**; season × time interaction was **nonsignificant**.
  - **Partial correlation** analysis: once abiotic factors (depth, velocity, temperature) were statistically controlled, the association between colonization and time since disturbance became **nonsignificant** — indicating the time effect was largely mediated by covarying abiotic regime change, not a "pure" successional/temporal process per se.

- **Fisher's critique (Reply/Comment):**
  1. **Scale mismatch:** The denuded patch scale is smaller than the scale at which flood disturbance and true ecosystem-level succession act; the manipulation caused no whole-stream mortality and did not alter the pool of potential colonists, so it cannot properly test the original ecosystem-scale hypothesis.
  2. **Unfalsifiability concern:** Given a "sea of mobile organisms" surrounding a small denuded patch, recolonization is virtually inevitable, making the null hypothesis (no succession) untestable at this scale.
  3. **Reframed hypothesis proposed:** "Time since flooding is an insignificant variable [in predicting biological state variables]" — to be tested via large-scale, multi-variable partial correlation analysis conducted over at least an annual cycle (to properly capture potential seasonal effects), since Peckarsky's short 48-day window risked a **Type II error** regarding season.

- **Peckarsky's rebuttal:**
  1. Defends the substrate-patch ("micro-level") as an ecologically legitimate and independently important scale of disturbance (rock overturning is a real, frequent, non-catastrophic disturbance mechanism), not merely a scaled-down proxy for flood-level disturbance.
  2. Draws cross-system analogies: patch dynamics parallel [[Forest Gap Dynamics]] and marine intertidal "patch birth" ([[Paine and Levin 1981]]), and cites [[Sousa 1984]] on the general importance of small-scale disturbance to population mortality.
  3. Agrees disturbance and succession operate meaningfully across **multiple spatial and temporal scales** simultaneously — micro, meso, and macro — and that no single scale invalidates findings at another.
  4. Concedes the study's **limited temporal scale** (single season, 48 days) constrains conclusions about seasonal effects, aligning with Fisher's critique on that specific point.
  5. Clarifies that her conclusions should be understood as scale-limited (micro-level colonization/redistribution dynamics), not a claim about ecosystem-wide succession following catastrophic flooding.

- **Broader methodological point:** Both authors agree that a **manipulative field experiment at the whole-ecosystem scale** (needed to rigorously test the macro-scale succession hypothesis) is likely impractical, expensive, or impossible, favoring large-scale correlational/partial correlation approaches as a practical (if less powerful) alternative to controlled experimentation.

- **Conceptual contribution:** The exchange highlights that ecological hypotheses about [[Ecological Succession]] and [[Disturbance Ecology]] are inherently **scale-dependent**, and that apparent contradictions between studies can arise not from flawed methodology but from mismatches between the **spatial/temporal scale of disturbance** and the **scale of the experimental design** used to test it.
