# An Annotated Checklist of the Freshwater Fishes of Texas, with Keys to Identification of Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This supplement to *The Texas Journal of Science* (Vol. 43, No. 4, 1991) presents an annotated checklist and dichotomous identification keys for the fishes of Texas freshwaters. The authors, [[Carl Hubbs]], [[Robert J. Edwards]], and [[Gary P. Garrett]], report 45 families and 247 species, of which 169 are native freshwater species. The checklist also covers estuarine and marine taxa that enter freshwater, and it records extinctions, extirpations and conservation status. The document is a taxonomic reference rather than a single-study report, so its content is a species inventory, keys, and status notes.

## Background & Description

The journal issue is published by [[Texas Academy of Science]] and includes general membership, subscription and affiliation information, which is not ecological content. The checklist itself has the following components:

- **Scope:** Forty-five families and 247 species known from Texas freshwaters.
- **Composition:** More than two-thirds of the 247 species are recognized as freshwater fishes; the remaining 78 are estuarine or marine species that occur in low-salinity habitats.
- **Keys:** Dichotomous keys to families and species, adapted from the work of [[Carl Hubbs]] (1982) for ichthyology students at the University of Texas.
- **Species accounts:** Each entry gives range, Texas distribution, habitat and conservation status (Freshwater, Estuarine, Marine, Introduced, Special Concern, Threatened, Endangered, Extirpated, Extinct).
- **Conservation categories used:** *Special Concern* (reduced abundance or range, peripheral to Texas, or insufficient data), *Threatened* (likely to become endangered), *Endangered* (requires assistance to avoid extinction).

The historical Texas fauna count rose from 154 species (Evermann and Kendall, 1894) to 247 in this checklist, reflecting both new recognition of taxa and documentation of introductions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeographic setting.** Texas has high fish diversity because of its large area, many discontinuous drainages, and varied physiography. It lies in a broad transition zone between several major zoogeographic provinces of North America, and it contains a large number of distinctive biotic zones ([[Edwards et al. 1989]]). Salinity boundaries are not sharp: the Pecos, Brazos and Wichita rivers have salinities higher than those of the tidally influenced Sabine Lake.

**Human impacts.** The authors state that a large number of freshwater fishes have been adversely affected by human activity. Approximately 20 percent of Texas freshwater fishes are of conservation concern. In the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] region (west of the Pecos River), about half of the native fish species are of conservation concern. The authors agree with [[Williams et al. 1989]] that water quantity (too much or too little, as in dewatering), habitat quality, and the introduction of exotic species are the major components of the problem.

**Extinctions and extirpations.** Of the native species, five are extinct (*Notropis orca*, *Notropis simus simus*, *Cyprinella lutrensis blairi*, *Gambusia amistadensis*, *Gambusia georgei*). Three appear to be extirpated from Texas: *Oncorhynchus clarkii virginalis* ([[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]), *Hybognathus amarus* ([[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]]), and *Gambusia senilis* ([[Blotched Gambusia]]). These losses amount to more than five percent of the strictly freshwater species native to Texas.

**Legal and listed status.** Six species are listed as threatened or endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1989), and 27 more by the [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Department]]. The [[Texas Organization for Endangered Species]] (1988) list includes 35 species. The authors' own list contains 47 species of concern.

**Trophic and habitat notes.** Many estuarine and marine species, such as menhaden, shads, anchovies, mullets, drums and gobies, are recorded as entering lower coastal streams. Several species, such as the [[Bull Shark]] and [[Smalltooth Sawfish]], enter coastal streams only short distances.

## Key Findings

- **Richness:** 247 species in 45 families; 169 native freshwater species; 78 estuarine or marine species.
- **Taxonomic changes:** *Fundulus kansae* is a synonym of *F. zebrinus*, which retains the common name plains killifish. *Dionda serena* and *D. argentosa* were split from *D. episcopa*, and the authors propose the common names Nueces roundnose minnow and manantial roundnose minnow.
- **New records:** *Heterandria formosa* (least killifish) was recorded in the Sabine River, extending its range.
- **Extinct taxa:** *Notropis orca*, *N. simus simus*, *Cyprinella lutrensis blairi*, *Gambusia amistadensis* and *G. georgei*. The *G. amistadensis* population was lost when Goodenough Springs was inundated by Amistad Reservoir in 1969, and the captive cultures were eliminated by contamination with western mosquitofish before 1983.
- **Extirpated taxa:** *Oncorhynchus clarkii virginalis*, *Hybognathus amarus*, and *Gambusia senilis* in its Texas range (formerly Devils River; now only in the Rio Conchos Basin of Chihuahua, Mexico).
- **Endangered species highlighted:** [[Devils River Minnow]] (*Dionda diaboli*), [[Big Bend Gambusia]] (*Gambusia gaigei*), [[Clear Creek Gambusia]] (*G. heterochir*), [[Widemouth Blindcat]] (*Satan eurystomus*), [[Toothless Blindcat]] (*Trogloglanis pattersoni*).
- **Threatened species highlighted:** [[Mexican Stoneroller]], [[Proserpine Shiner]], [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*), [[Bluehead Shiner]], [[Rio Grande Shiner]], [[Taillight Shiner]], [[Sharpnose Shiner]], [[Pecos Gambusia]], and [[Graham's Darter]].
- **Special Concern:** [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (now found only in the Red River below Denison Dam), [[Blue Sucker]], [[Guadalupe Bass]] (the official state fish of Texas), and several marine or estuarine taxa.
- **Introduced species:** Examples include [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Rudd]], [[Rio Grande Cichlid]], [[Mozambique Tilapia]], [[Armadillo del Rio]] (*Hypostomus* sp.), and [[Striped Bass]] (widely stocked into reservoirs, replacing white bass).
- **Keys:** Dichotomous keys cover families and species, including a separate key for the Cyprinidae (minnows) and for darters, sunfish and other large groups.
