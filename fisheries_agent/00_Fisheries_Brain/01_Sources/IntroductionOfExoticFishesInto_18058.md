# Introduction of Exotic Fishes into Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by E. J. Crossman traces the deliberate introduction of exotic fishes into Canada during the late 1800s and early 1900s. It argues that fisheries managers have long used introductions as a management tool, and it uses the [[Common Carp]] case in Ontario as a cautionary example. Carp were imported to supply a cheap food fish after the failure of efforts to restore [[Atlantic Salmon]] in [[Lake Ontario]]. Within about nineteen years, the Ontario fisheries authority judged the introduction a failure.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** Crossman (1968) divided fisheries management into eras defined by the main tool used: law enforcement, regulations, hatcheries, introductions (roughly 1850-1900s), and, by 1968, eradication.
- **Canadian introductions:** In the late 1800s Canada followed the [[United States]] in importing foreign fishes. Most were sport fishes, such as the [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), meant to widen angling options.
- **Common carp:** One of the earliest introductions. Carp were brought into Ontario to provide "a cheap article of food."
- **Key figure:** [[Samuel Wilmott]], described as the father of Canadian [[Fish Culture]], failed to rehabilitate Atlantic salmon in Lake Ontario. He then turned to carp, hoping they would be "welcomed to the poor man's table."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The chapter presents exotic introductions as a response to human-caused loss of native fish populations. The carp introduction was intended to replace the Atlantic salmon, which had been extirpated from Lake Ontario. The document does not give ecological data such as population sizes, habitat measurements or water chemistry. Its evidence is historical and policy-based.

## Key Findings

- Canada's introductions of exotic fishes began in the late 1800s, following US practice.
- Most early introductions were sport fishes, including [[Brown Trout]].
- Carp were introduced into Ontario as a cheap food source, a goal the chapter presents as a motive rooted in socioeconomic needs.
- Wilmott's attempt to rehabilitate Atlantic salmon in Lake Ontario failed, and he shifted to carp.
- By the Ontario Department of Fish and Game's assessment, within nineteen years of introduction, "the promiscuous introduction of carp on this continent had been attended with nothing but evil results."
- The chapter identifies the carp introduction as the first Canadian attempt to offset human extirpation of one fish population by introducing an exotic species. Crossman notes the exotic remains in Canadian waters today.
- Crossman's broader argument is that introductions were one of several successive management "ages," and that such introductions carry lasting risks, a theme linked to [[Exotic Species Introduction]] and [[Fisheries Management]].
