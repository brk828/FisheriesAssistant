# Freshwater Mollusks Collected by the United States and Mexican Boundary Surveys

#malacology #Gastropoda #Bivalvia #freshwater-mollusks #historical-survey

> **Note on scope:** This source is a malacological (mollusk) systematics and biogeography paper. Fish are mentioned only incidentally (as bycatch/food items collected by 19th-century survey naturalists). The structured note below is adapted accordingly, treating **freshwater Gastropoda and Bivalvia** as the primary taxa of interest, per instructions to tag and frame the note faithfully to the source's actual content.

## Executive Summary

This paper by Dwight W. Taylor (1967), published in *The Veliger*, is a historical and taxonomic review of freshwater mollusk collections made during the two U.S.–Mexico Boundary Surveys (1849–1855 and 1891–1894). Taylor traces and relocates the **type localities** of numerous freshwater snail and mussel species described by 19th-century malacologists (chiefly Augustus A. Gould), corrects earlier locality errors (notably those of [[William Healey Dall]], 1896), and compiles a definitive locality-by-species table for the collections made by military surgeon [[E. A. Mearns]] during the second survey. Although titled around mollusks, the paper is of incidental interest to ichthyologists because it documents some of the earliest **fish records and fish-collecting activity** in remote streams of the Sonoran/Chihuahuan Desert borderlands, including a documented catch of trout in a tributary of the Rio Casas Grandes (Chihuahua) in 1852.

## Background & Description

- **First Boundary Survey (1849–1855):** Led by Commissioner J. R. Bartlett, with Dr. T. H. Webb (Surgeon and Secretary) responsible for natural history collections, including mollusks. Botanist George Thurber also incidentally collected shells (e.g., on herbarium sheets).
- **Second Boundary Survey (1891–1894):** Mollusks were collected by [[E. A. Mearns]], the expedition's medical officer, who kept detailed, well-documented locality records — in contrast to the poorly documented first survey.
- Taylor's methodology combines archival narrative accounts (Bartlett 1854; Mearns 1907), museum specimen re-examination (ANSP, MCZ, USNM), and modern geographic quadrangle referencing (International Map of the World grid system) to pinpoint historical type localities with precision.
- The paper catalogs and re-identifies type material for taxa including:
  - [[Lampsilis bracteata]] and [[Quadrula petrina]] (Llano River, Texas)
  - [[Physa virgata]] and [[Physa humerosa]] (Gila River / Colorado Desert region)
  - [[Biomphalaria gracilenta]] (Colorado Desert / Cienega Grande)
  - [[Uniomerus tetralasmus manubius]] (Puntiagudo, Nuevo León)
  - [[Bythinella palomasensis]] (Palomas Lakes, Chihuahua)
  - [[Anodonta californiensis]] and [[Anodonta dejecta]] (Colorado River drainage)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitats sampled** span a strong aridity/permanence gradient: perennial rivers (Rio Grande, Colorado River, Gila River, Santa Cruz River), desert springs (Ojo Caliente, Chihuahua), ephemeral desert lakes and sloughs (Palomas Lakes, Laguna Station on New River), and small tributary creeks (Las Moras Creek, Elm Creek).
- Several localities (e.g., Monument 219, "Salt Creek, W. side of Colorado Desert") yielded only **subfossil or bleached/stream-drifted shells**, indicating former wetter conditions or paleohydrological connections in what are now desert basins — relevant to broader questions of Pleistocene-Holocene aquatic habitat contraction in the Sonoran/Chihuahuan Desert.
- The single explicit **fish reference** in the source: at "Ojo Caliente," Chihuahua, a warm spring (approximately the same temperature as that of the atmosphere) draining via a small creek into the Rio Carmen, Bartlett's 1852 account notes "some fish were taken and preserved for specimens" — indicating this desert spring-and-creek system supported viable fish populations in the mid-19th century, a useful historical baseline for desert-fish biogeography in the region (comparable to work on [[Desert Pupfish]] and other Chihuahuan Desert endemics).
- A second incidental fish reference: near Janos, Chihuahua (a tributary of the Casas Grandes/San Miguel River), Bartlett's party in 1852 "caught a sufficient number of trout to give us all a meal" while also collecting unionid mussels — an early, undocumented record of trout occurrence in this Chihuahuan drainage, notable because no scientific record of either the fish or the mollusks from this locality was subsequently published.
- Freshwater mollusk assemblages (particularly Unionidae and Planorbidae) are used here as environmental/historical indicators, paralleling how fish assemblages are used to reconstruct historical river connectivity and desert stream permanence in [[Aquatic Ecosystems Of The Sonoran Desert]].

## Key Findings

- **Type locality corrections:** Multiple previously vague or erroneous type localities (e.g., "near San Pedro, California" for *Sphaerium nobile*, actually likely San Antonio, Texas) are geographically resolved using survey itineraries and period maps.
- **Table 1** presents a comprehensive locality × species occurrence matrix for 29 mollusk taxa across 18 of Mearns' numbered survey stations (Fort Worth, TX to Laguna Mountains, CA), representing the most complete synthesis of the second Boundary Survey's freshwater mollusk fauna.
- Identification errors in Dall's (1896) report are corrected throughout, including misattributed localities and species-level misidentifications (e.g., specimens labeled *Planorbis tumidus* re-identified as *Helisoma tenue sinuosum*).
- Documents desert freshwater habitats (springs, playas, sloughs) that have since been substantially altered or eliminated by 20th-century agricultural and irrigation development, making this historical baseline valuable for contemporary aquatic conservation and biogeography research in the borderlands region.
- Two direct, if minor, historical fish records are preserved: (1) unspecified fish taken and preserved near Ojo Caliente, Chihuahua (1852), and (2) trout caught for food in a tributary stream near Janos, Chihuahua (1852) — both undocumented elsewhere in scientific literature, underscoring the paper's incidental value as a historical source for desert fish distribution.
