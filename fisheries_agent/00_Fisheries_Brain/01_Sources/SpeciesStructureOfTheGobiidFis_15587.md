# Species Structure of the Gobiid Fish *Gillichthys mirabilis* from Coastal Sloughs of the Eastern Pacific

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by George W. Barlow, published in *Pacific Science*, examines morphological and meristic variation in [[Gillichthys Mirabilis]] across its range from central California through Baja California, the [[Gulf of California]], and the introduced [[Salton Sea]] population. About 4,000 specimens were examined, and the analysis draws on fin counts, vertebral counts, head and jaw measurements, squamation, and asymmetry. The author concludes that populations show genetic divergence intertwined with phenotypic plasticity, with the northern Gulf populations differing most. Subspecific names were deliberately withheld, and the author judged that no group of populations is evolving into a distinct species. The Salton Sea fish are described as aberrant, probably reflecting developmental disruption from the saline water.

## Background & Description

The study addresses the biogeographic question of whether outer-coast species in the upper [[Gulf of California]] differ from their Pacific counterparts. *G. mirabilis* was the primary focus, with the closely related and restricted [[Gillichthys Seta]] used as comparative material. The author resolves a taxonomic dispute by supporting Starks and Morris (1907) in treating *G. detrusus* Gilbert and Scofield as a synonym of *G. mirabilis*. Earlier, *G. mirabilis* from the San Diego area was introduced into the Salton Sea in 1930, where it became established.

Methods followed [[Hubbs and Lagler Meristic Methods]], with one exception, the anal fin height, which was defined by the author. Specimens were held at the University of California, Los Angeles Fish Collections. Statistical tests (F-test, covariance, t-tests, correlation) used a 5% critical level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and salinity.** The typical habitat is the intertidal coastal slough with extensive mud flats, sometimes a swampy lagoon at the mouth of a dry river. Hypersaline conditions are common. The maximum recorded salinity was 82.5 ‰ at "El Marino," north of San Felipe. Fish usually are absent from fresh or slightly brackish water.

**Temperature.** Air temperatures were used as a proxy, since surface water temperatures in backwaters correlate with them. Air temperatures rise north to south on the Pacific Coast, especially in summer. In the Gulf, the summer gradient reverses slightly. The tidal range increases northward to about 10 m at the head of the Gulf, producing longer pond phases and greater water temperature fluctuation. Summer air temperatures exceed 30 °C in the Gulf and Salton Sea areas, while San Diego and San Francisco remain moderate. The Salton Sea climate is more extreme than that of the upper Gulf.

**Dispersal.** Pelagic larvae are the most likely route of genetic exchange. Postlarvae of *G. mirabilis* are more heavily pigmented at 8–12 mm than those of related genera ([[Quietula]], [[Clevelandia]], [[Ilypnus]]), suggesting shorter pelagic life and reduced dispersal.

**Reproduction.** Spawning begins in January throughout the range. In the Salton Sea it runs from January to May or June. Females lay 4,000–9,000 eggs per spawning. Salton Sea ovary evidence suggested 2–3 spawnings per season at intervals of 40–50 days, implying roughly 8,000–27,000 eggs per female per season. Fish reach maturity by the end of their first year at a standard length of 100–140 mm.

## Key Findings

- **Range and status:** *G. mirabilis* occurs in coastal sloughs from central California to near Cabo San Lucas, and in the central and northern Gulf. A break in distribution near Cabo San Lucas separates Gulf and Pacific groups.
- **Head shape:** Gulf fish have more depressed heads, which was a key reason for the earlier description of *G. detrusus*. Salton Sea fish have the shortest heads.
- **Jaw and fins:** Upper jaw length is greatest in Gulf fish and shortest in Salton Sea fish. Anal fin height is highest in Gulf populations, with the Salton Sea lowest. A deviant sample from an isolated salt pond north of San Felipe had low fins, resembling Pacific fish (n = 10).
- **Dorsal fin spacing:** Greatest in the upper Gulf (1.0–1.8 times eye diameter), versus 0.7–1.1 in central California and central/southern Gulf. In the Salton Sea, spacing is under one eye diameter.
- **Squamation:** Gulf fish have larger scales and more ventral scaling (about 80% of Bahia Cholla fish versus about 10% at Guaymas and 30% at Bahia Agiabampo). Salton Sea fish have reduced scalation.
- **Meristic clines:** Dorsal and anal ray counts decrease from north to south on the Pacific Coast but increase from north to south in the Gulf. The pectoral ray trend is reversed. The fin index (segmented dorsal plus anal rays divided by pectoral rays, ×100) decreases north to south on the Pacific Coast and increases south in the Gulf. Typical values: about 95 for Gulf populations and 82 for [[Gillichthys Seta]] in the upper Gulf.
- **Variance:** Variance of fin ray counts increases with latitude (r = 0.848 for first dorsal fin variance), and vertebral variance shows a similar positive trend.
- **Correlations:** Within a population and year class, median and pectoral fin counts are not correlated. Between populations, the pectoral count is negatively correlated with the second dorsal count (r = −0.737) and anal count (r = −0.659).
- **Asymmetry:** Pacific Coast and Salton Sea populations average about 30% total pectoral asymmetry versus about 20% in the Gulf. Dextral and sinistral distributions are consistent with chance.
- **Vertebrae:** The mode is 32 in all samples. Only the Salton Sea mean (32.17) differs significantly from 32.
- **Annual variation:** Bahia Cholla year classes from a cold winter (1949) had higher median fin counts than a normal year (1954), with the second dorsal fin higher by about one ray. Fin variances did not differ between years.
- **Salton Sea peculiarities:** All fin and vertebral counts are higher; supernumerary spines occur (second dorsal 40%, 13%, and 33% across three years); axial deformities peak in autumn (up to 53% in October 1955, small sample); and otoliths are coarser and rounder. Fin index still matches the parental Pacific stock.
- **Interpretation:** Salton Sea abnormalities are attributed mainly to the saline water chemistry disrupting development rather than temperature, as the Salton Sea fish were introduced from about 500 founders. Gulf-Pacific differences are attributed mainly to genetic divergence and physical separation, with temperature effects on meristics noted.
- **Taxonomic conclusion:** No subspecies are named. The author considers the geographic variants to reflect adaptive differences within one widely distributed species.
