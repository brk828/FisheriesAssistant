# Some Influences of Domestication upon Three Stocks of Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares a [[Domestic Brook Trout Stock]] (about 90 years of hatchery selection), a [[Wild Brook Trout Stock]] from [[Honnedaga Lake]], and a [[First Generation Brook Trout Stock]] reared from eggs under identical hatchery conditions at the [[Cornell University Experimental Fish Hatchery]]. Laboratory tests and limited field trials in a stream and two ponds examined physiology, behavior, and survival. Domestic fish were tamer, less wary, more tolerant of accumulated metabolites in some tests, and grew faster, while wild fish showed greater stamina, lower temperature-related mortality, and better survival in natural waters. The author concludes that artificial selection for fast growth and early maturity can produce hidden behavioral and physiological changes that may reduce fitness once fish are released into natural environments.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with [[Livingston Stone]]'s 1873 book *Domesticated Trout*, which anticipated that domestication could alter trout instincts. The author notes that intentional breeding in hatcheries has targeted faster growth, earlier maturity, greater egg production, earlier spawning, and disease resistance. Unintentional selection also occurs, as fish that handle easily or have a "nice shape" are favored.

The three stocks were:
- **Domestic:** Brook trout reared in hatcheries for approximately 90 years, with brood stock chosen from the best 2-year-olds. After 20 years the spawning date had advanced by 15 days.
- **Wild:** Fish from [[Honnedaga Lake]] in the southeastern Adirondack Mountains. The lake has total alkalinity under 2 p.p.m., a pH near 5, and a surface temperature that rarely reaches 70° F. Brook trout are the only native fish. Honnedaga trout are slow-maturing and long-lived, with 2- to 6-year-old fish not unusual and 2- to 3-pound fish common among spawners.
- **First generation:** Hatchery stock that spent one year in Long Pond near Brandon, New York, before spawning. Genetic origin was unknown; this lot served as a reference.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Rearing was done in adjacent troughs with lots rotated every 2 weeks to limit differences in light, waste accumulation, and human disturbance. Water temperature was usually within 1° F and never varied more than 2° F. Domestic eggs were reared at slightly higher temperatures in another hatchery before transfer, so temperature was manipulated to equalize hatching. Mortality from egg take to about one year was high in all lots: 93% for domestic, 62% for first generation, and 64% for wild. The author attributed much of this to poor water quality at the Cornell hatchery.

Field work used two contrasting habitats: a severe stream (the outlet of East Lake, Adirondack League Club Preserve) and a mild pond (Laramie Pond, Brandon Preserve). Both were stocked more heavily than normal to intensify competition.

## Key Findings

**Growth and morphology**
- After one year in hatchery conditions, domestic fish averaged 5.2 inches and wild fish 3.6 inches.
- Growth was similar for the first 80 days, then diverged.
- Condition factors measured on November 15 were 54.05 (domestic) and 38.58 (wild); wild fish were visibly slimmer.
- Wild fish had larger heads relative to body size than fast-growing fish, as shown by head-to-body ratios of 2.880 (domestic yearlings) and 2.676 (wild yearlings).

**Behavior**
- Domestic fish were tamer and showed little fright. Wild fish reacted strongly to shadows and hands in the water.
- In a tall aquarium, 50% of domestic fish and 26% of wild fish were in the top five positions (chi-square = 14.36, P < 0.01); first-generation fish resembled domestic fish.
- In hiding tests, domestic fish did not conceal themselves, while wild fish quickly sought cover.

**Physiology and stress tolerance**
- In metabolite tests, 51% of domestic, 50% of first-generation, and 20% of wild fish died (chi-square = 12.56, P < 0.01).
- Under a rise to 80° F over 5 hours, mortality was 69% for domestic, 42% for first-generation, and 25% for wild fish (chi-square = 15.76, P < 0.01).
- Domestic fish had higher and more saturated body fat. Wild fish had less fat than domestic fish when fed the same hatchery diet, a difference the author links to differential utilization of food.

**Stamina**
- In 1,522 individual swimming trials to exhaustion, wild fish had greater stamina than domestic fish across the entire size range tested. First-generation fish generally fell between the two.

**Survival in East Lake Outlet (73 days, stream)**
- 500 domestic (mean 2.41 in) and 500 wild (mean 2.04 in) fish were stocked on June 7, 1958.
- Estimated survival: 20% domestic (102 fish) and 33% wild (167 fish) by DeLury estimate.
- Wild fish outnumbered domestic fish in seven of eight sections.
- Length increase: wild 0.48 in; domestic 0.34 in.
- About 10% of domestic fish swam into rocks or sand bars or were confused by the new surroundings. This was not seen in wild fish.

**Survival in Laramie Pond (108 days, pond)**
- 625 fish each of domestic, wild, and backcross (brook trout × lake trout hybrid backcross to brook trout) stocks were planted June 16, 1958.
- Recovery survival: 65% wild, 62% backcross, 43% domestic.
- Length increase was similar: 2.5 in (domestic), 2.1 in (wild), and about the same for backcross.
- Twelve of 105 domestic fish examined were mature males, with many males maturing at age 0+.
- When returned to the hatchery, domestic fish fed immediately. Wild fish did not feed well for 4 days.

**Discussion themes**
- Morphological, physiological, behavioral, and genetic factors interact, so cause and effect are hard to separate.
- Artificial selection for fast growth and early maturity may be associated with shorter lifespan, as domestic stock rarely survived beyond 3 years in some Adirondack ponds (Hatch, 1959).
- Hatchery environments relax natural selection, so marginal individuals survive and pass on their genes.
- The author concludes that hatchery-produced fish are adapted to hatchery ponds rather than streams or lakes, so their adaptive traits can be of little value or even harmful once they are released.
