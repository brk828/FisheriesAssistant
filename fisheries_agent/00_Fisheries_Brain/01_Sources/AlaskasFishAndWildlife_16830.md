# Alaska's Fish and Wildlife (Fish and Wildlife Service Circular 17, 1953)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service circular, written by Regional Director Clarence J. Rhode and Will Barker, is a regional field guide to Alaska's fishes, mammals and birds. It describes the Territory's five natural divisions (Southeastern Alaska, the Gulf Region, the Interior, the Arctic Shelf and the Aleutians) and the Pribilof and Nunivak Islands. Fisheries are its central theme: commercial fisheries, led by [[Pacific Salmon]], are described as the foundation of the Territory's economy, and the sport fishes are covered in detail. The document also covers furbearers, marine mammals, waterfowl and other birds, the management framework under the [[Alaska Game Law]], licence fees, and checklists of species. It is a descriptive and management overview, not a research study, and it contains no new experimental data.

## Background & Description

**Scope and authorship.** The circular was produced by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and printed in Washington in 1953. It supersedes Circular 3, *Birds and Mammals of Alaska* (1942). The price was 25 cents.

**Physical setting.** Alaska, with 586,400 square miles, is described as about a fifth the size of the 48 States. Its coastline totals 33,000 miles, with more than 3,000 islands, 1,100 of them in the Alexander Archipelago. The 1950 population was about 129,000, roughly one person per 5 square miles. The text stresses that Alaska is not uniformly frigid: temperatures range from 100°F at Fort Yukon to −70°F at Tanana, and glaciers occur in the south, southwest and southeast, where heavy precipitation and high mountains allow them to form.

**Regions covered.**
- **Southeastern Alaska (the Panhandle):** mild and wet, with annual rainfall averaging 99 inches (150 inches at Ketchikan, 60 at Haines). Forests are mainly western hemlock and Sitka spruce.
- **Gulf Region:** drained by the Copper and Susitna Rivers, including the Kenai Peninsula and Kodiak Island, and described as the Territory's most important game region. Precipitation averages about 35 inches.
- **Interior:** the Yukon and Kuskokwim valleys, with cold dark winters, continuous summer daylight, and annual precipitation of about 13 inches.
- **Arctic Shelf:** north of the Seward Peninsula and Brooks Range, with 6 to 8 inches of annual precipitation and permafrost-limited soil thaw.
- **Aleutian Islands:** treeless, fog-bound volcanic islands with about 60 inches of annual precipitation, extending about 1,100 miles westward.
- **Pribilof Islands:** five volcanic islands, the main fur seal breeding grounds.
- **Nunivak Island:** the second-largest island in the Bering Sea, now a national wildlife refuge.

**Institutional framework.** The Territorial Legislature, created in 1912, governed residents. Fish and wildlife were managed by the Alaska Game Commission and the Fish and Wildlife Service, with the Territorial Department of Fisheries conducting scientific investigations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Marine and anadromous fishes.** All Pacific salmon are anadromous: they return from the ocean to the streams of their birth to spawn and, unlike Atlantic salmon, die after a single spawning. Stock depletion is a concern. The document notes that "so much gear has been added to the fishing fleet since World War II that salmon runs in most areas have shown distinct signs of depletion," and that conservation must match increasing commercial effort.

**Freshwater habitat.** Coastal streams are described as short, swift and clear, flowing through wooded valleys. Inland clear-water streams rise from snow-fed mountain lakes, while large rivers draining glaciers are silt-laden. Most tributaries of the large rivers are clear and hold substantial game fish populations, except near cities.

**Predator-prey and food-web relationships.** Several entries describe trophic links:
- [[Dolly Varden]] are accused by packers of eating salmon eggs and young, but studies "indicate that it usually eats drifting eggs that would be lost anyway."
- [[Lake Trout]] feed on migrating salmon fry and fingerlings near lake outlets, and on mice and lemmings that fall into the water.
- Killer whales prey on fur seal pups and on other warm-blooded sea animals.
- Sea otters use kelp beds for protection from killer whales, their only enemy at sea.
- Fur animal populations, including lynx, marten and fox, rise and fall with cycles of small rodents, snowshoe hares and lemmings.

**Habitat-driven wildlife trends.** Natural and human factors shape populations. [[Reindeer]] declined from about 600,000 in 1936 to about 20,000, attributed to depleted winter ranges, wolf predation, poor herding, excessive butchering and mixing with migratory caribou. Muskrat losses are mainly caused by deep icing of shallow ponds. Moose populations fluctuate over long periods in response to habitat change.

**Pollution and introductions.** Introduced species are noted, including the house mouse, Norway rat, blue fox and reindeer, as well as transplanted [[Bison]], [[Musk Ox]], wapiti and eastern brook trout.

## Key Findings

**Fisheries and economic importance**
- Commercial fisheries are described as yielding products of "greater value than any other natural resource." Fishing contributes over one-half of Territorial tax revenue and employs about 30,000 persons, almost a fourth of the population.
- Canned salmon makes up 80 to 90 percent of the commercial fisheries output. Gold mining is often assumed to lead the economy but is overshadowed by fisheries.
- Five salmon species are taken: [[Chinook Salmon]] (king), [[Coho Salmon]] (silver), [[Sockeye Salmon]] (red), [[Pink Salmon]] (humpback) and [[Chum Salmon]] (dog).

**Salmon details (as given)**
- Kings: average about 23 pounds, occasionally up to 100 pounds; reach maturity at 3 to 7 years.
- Coho: may reach 36 inches and 30 pounds, but average 24 inches and 9 pounds; mature at about 3 years.
- Sockeye: 15 to 20 inches, average 6 pounds, most common age 5 years. [[Bristol Bay]] is "the greatest producing area of this species in the world."
- Pinks: average about 18 inches and 4 pounds. Southeastern Alaska produces an average annual pack of 2,000,000 cases.
- Chum: 5 to 45 pounds, average commercial catch 8 pounds, the most widely distributed salmon.

**Halibut and shellfish**
- [[Pacific Halibut]] is the largest flatfish, weighing up to 600 pounds but averaging less than 50. A 7-foot fish may be 50 years old. Most U.S. halibut comes from Alaska, and catches are regulated by quota under an international treaty with Canada.
- Shellfish include razor clams (Copper River flats, Cook Inlet and Shelikof Strait), shrimp (mainly Wrangell-Petersburg), and a single commercial oyster bed near Ketchikan, which requires transplanted spat.

**Sport fishes**
- Freshwater game fishes include rainbow, steelhead, cutthroat, lake and Dolly Varden trout, grayling, northern pike and sheefish.
- Dolly Varden are "the most abundant trout in Alaska" and are often underrated as sport fish, despite being "every bit as much fight" as eastern brook or lake trout.
- [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]] is described as "one of the finest game and food fishes" and reaches up to 25 inches.
- [[Grayling]] (*Thymallus signifer*) grow up to 23 inches and are notable for their large dorsal fin and aggressive surface feeding on flies.
- [[Sheefish]] (inconnu) are described as "found nowhere else under the American flag"; they average about 20 pounds and occasionally reach 85 pounds.
- In 1931 [[Eastern Brook Trout]] were transplanted into barren lakes near Ketchikan, Juneau and Skagway. The source reads "1981", which is impossible for a 1953 publication and is likely a scan error.
- Southeastern Alaska's [[Chatham Strait]] is named as "the finest" area for silver salmon sport fishing.

**Marine mammals**
- [[Alaska Fur Seal]] is "the world's largest and most valuable fur-seal herd," returning to the Pribilofs each summer. The annual take is about 65,000 pelts, split 80 percent to the United States and 20 percent to Canada under a 1942 provisional agreement. The U.S. share nets about $1,500,000 a year.
- [[Sea Otter]] was nearly exterminated; about 150,000 pelts were taken in the Russian period. Killing was prohibited in 1911, and the population is rebuilding under Fish and Wildlife Service protection.
- Walrus may not be killed except by natives for food and clothing, or by others for emergency food. Ivory may only be exported as finished handicrafts.
- Whales: the blue whale is "the largest mammal that has ever lived," and the bowhead is recovering from whaling.

**Large mammals**
- [[Brown Bear]] (the Alaska brown bear and grizzly): top weight up to 1,600 pounds. Shiras brown bears are nearly coal-black, while Kodiak bears are the largest.
- [[Polar Bear]]: males weigh 700 to 1,600 pounds; females about 700 pounds.
- [[Black Bear]]: 200 to 300 pounds, occasionally 500 pounds. The rare glacier (blue) bear is confined to a range between Lynn Canal and Cape St. Elias.
- [[Dall Sheep]]: the only wild white sheep; a new "three-quarter-curl" horn regulation protects younger rams.
- [[Mountain Goat]] and [[Moose]] (the Kenai moose is the largest of its kind; bulls can exceed 1,400 pounds).
- [[Caribou]]: "the most abundant big-game animal" in the Territory, though numbers have declined sharply.
- [[Sitka Black-Tailed Deer]]: the only true deer in Alaska, with a vertical migration up to 2,500 feet.
- Introduced [[Musk Ox]] on Nunivak Island: 76 in 1951, founded from 34 animals brought from Greenland in 1930.
- Bison: 6 males and 17 females transplanted to Big Delta in 1928, increasing to 350.

**Furbearers**
- Trapping supports hundreds of Indians, Eskimos and Aleuts. Around 15,000 natives depend on the fur industry, which returns about $2,500,000 a year from pelts.
- Mink: the most valuable annual pelt harvest; Alaska exports about 40,000 pelts annually.
- Muskrat: about 144,000 skins a year, worth about $216,000.
- Beaver: about 20,000 skins, worth about $300,000 annually.
- Other furbearers include marten, land otter, weasel, wolverine, red and arctic foxes, wolves, coyote and lynx.

**Birds**
- Alaska is important for continental waterfowl. The Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta is described as "perhaps the largest waterfowl-nesting area on the continent."
- The Arctic tern makes "one of the longest migrations known."
- The trumpeter swan, "once on the verge of extinction," has been found wintering in southeastern Alaska.
- The Aleutians support large seabird colonies, while the Pribilofs host about 100 species, 20 of which breed there.

**Regulations and licences (1953 fees)**
- Resident trapping, hunting and fishing: $3; resident hunting and fishing: $2; resident fishing: $1.
- Nonresident general hunting: $50; small game: $10; sport fishing: $2.50.
- Alien special (hunting, trapping, firearms possession): $100; alien sport fishing: $2.50.
- Migratory waterfowl stamp: $2 (ages 16 and over). Local sport-fishing stamp: $1.
- Nonresidents and aliens must hunt big game, or photograph brown or grizzly bears, with a licensed, registered guide.
- The Alaska Game Commission has five members (four residents, one the Regional Director). It meets annually to set seasons, bag limits and sanctuaries.
- Fifteen national wildlife refuges provided protection for sea otter, bear, moose, seabirds and waterfowl, including the [[Kenai National Wildlife Refuge]] and the [[Kodiak National Wildlife Refuge]].

**Checklists.** The circular provides representative lists of about 40 to 50 better-known fishes (including about 20 rockfish species and many sculpins, flounders and sculpins), numerous mammals, an extensive bird checklist covering all recorded species, 30 tree species (including Sitka spruce and western hemlock), and principal shrubs. These are summarized here rather than reproduced in full.
