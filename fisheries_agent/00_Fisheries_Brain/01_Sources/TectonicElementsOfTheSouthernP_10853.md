# Tectonic Elements of the Southern Gulf of California: Summary

#marine-geology #plate-tectonics #sedimentation

## Executive Summary

This summary by [[Jeffrey W. Niemitz]] (Dickinson College) and [[James L. Bischoff]] (U.S. Geological Survey) reports geophysical mapping of the tectonic structure of the southern [[Gulf of California]]. The authors use sediment-thickness patterns, derived from [[Seismic-Reflection Profiling]], to locate the presently active [[Pacific–North American Plate Boundary]]. They conclude that sediment patterns are a useful tool in tectonically complex, nearshore settings where [[Magnetic Anomaly Analysis]] is inconclusive. The paper is a summary of a fuller article in the *Geological Society of America Bulletin* (v. 92, p. 101–104, March 1981). It contains no biological data.

## Background & Description

The Gulf of California is a series of linear, en echelon fault scarps offset by deep basins. The authors attribute this pattern to [[Plate Tectonics]]. The gulf and the diffuse [[San Andreas Fault]] zone are interpreted as the Pacific–North American plate boundary, lying between the [[East Pacific Rise]] to the south and the [[Mendocino Fracture Zone]] to the north.

Unlike mid-ocean spreading centers, which appear as linear bathymetric ridges, the gulf's plate-separation loci are deep, rectilinear basins. The authors attribute this to two factors:

- Elevated continental crust lies close to the zone of plate separation.
- Rapid terrigenous and biogenous sedimentation forms sediment wedges.

Two fault categories are defined:

- **Active faults:** traces that displace surficial sediments and indicate the most recent plate motion.
- **Relict faults:** traces that do not displace surficial sediments but show offset at depth. Examples were observed east and west of the [[Pescadero Basin Complex]], in the Carmen and Farallon basins.

Conventional [[Magnetic Anomaly Analysis]] (as applied by [[Heirtzler et al. 1968]]) is not useful here. Symmetric anomalies across the inferred accretion centers within the gulf are absent (Larson and others, 1972). Anomalies across the East Pacific Rise at the gulf mouth indicate an average plate separation of 60 mm/yr over the past 4 m.y. (Larson, 1972).

The study uses several detailed geophysical surveys of the southern gulf province (Figure 1, trackline coverage map). The authors focus on how small-scale features relate to zones of lacking sediment accumulation, which may mark active faulting. The work builds on earlier surveys of the [[Northern Gulf of California]] and central gulf provinces (Henyey and Bischoff, 1973; Bischoff and Henyey, 1974).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address biological communities, fish, invertebrates, plankton, or water chemistry. Its environmental content concerns sediment supply and distribution:

- **Sediment sources and processes:** Sediment derives from rivers and from high biological productivity. Turbidity flows channel shelf sediment to basin floors. Localized tectonic damming can inhibit transport, as at La Paz Bay on the western margin. Tidal currents and bottom scouring are important in narrow transform-fault channels, as previously noted in Salsipuedes Channel.
- **Sedimentation rates:** Combined river and productivity inputs produce very high accumulation in the [[Guaymas Basin]] of the central province. Where pelagic, river-derived sediment dominates, as in the [[Mazatlan Basin]], rates fall by one to two orders of magnitude.
- **Sediment character:** Sediment accumulation in deep basins is probably episodic (turbidites and mass-debris flows) rather than a steady rain of material. Relative thickness and abrupt thickness changes matter more than absolute thickness.
- **Coastal sediment wedges:** The Mexican "protogulf" shelf along Sinaloa is thickly sedimented. Basins offshore of river mouths, such as the Rio Fuertes at the head of the Farallon Basin, show little accumulation.

## Key Findings

1. **Sediment-based mapping of active faults:** An isopach map from seismic-reflection profiles shows little or no sediment coincident with inferred active fault traces. Elongate, anomalously thick sediment wedges coincide with inferred relict faults.
2. **Basin–flank paradox:** Deep basins are largely sediment-free, while basin flanks are thickly sedimented. Northwest of the Pescadero Basin complex, about 800 m of sediment lies adjacent to areas with no discernible accumulation.
3. **Plate separation rate:** Symmetric anomalies at the East Pacific Rise mouth indicate an average plate separation of 60 mm/yr over the past 4 m.y., while anomalies within the gulf are absent.
4. **Relict structural trends:** The Pescadero Basin complex contains relict and active fault traces subparallel to the dominant northwest–southeast trend of the major transform faults. These traces converge and diverge, resembling the [[Southern California Borderland]] strike-slip structures.
5. **Fault abandonment model:** Transform faults most orthogonal to spreading centers tend to persist. Subparallel faults are less likely to persist and are often abandoned when accretion axes shift. Rapid sedimentation can then bury abandoned fracture zones, erasing their bathymetric expression. Figure 2 illustrates this rifting and rotation of [[Baja California]].
6. **Sedimentation versus spreading:** Basin development from sea-floor spreading must outpace burial, or basins would fill. Sediment thickness alone does not capture the extension–accumulation relationship, which cannot be simply described by the sedimentary slope model of [[Sharman 1976]].
7. **Conclusion:** Sediment-thickness patterns can help reconstruct plate-tectonic history where magnetic methods fail. Rapid sedimentation can preserve relict tectonic elements without burying active accretion loci. The authors describe the southern gulf as a complex, transitional stage of [[Passive Margin]] ocean-basin development.
