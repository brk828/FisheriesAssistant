# Peces de la Cuenca Geohidrológica del Río Bravo: Atlas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1995 atlas, produced by [[Salvador Contreras-Balderas]], [[María de Lourdes Lozano-Vilano]] and [[María Elena García-Ramírez]] of the Laboratorio de Ictiología, Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas, Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León, is the first comprehensive distributional summary of the freshwater fishes of the Río Bravo basin (the Provincia del Bravo) in Mexico. It is based on 36 years of collections housed in the UANL ichthyological collection plus published literature. It covers 145 numbered species entries, several of them with multiple nominal forms, and excludes undescribed taxa whose data are withheld to protect researchers and threatened populations, as well as invasive marine and brackish species (listed separately in an appendix).

Each species entry gives the current scientific and common names, type locality, maximum size, systematics, general and regional distribution, conservation status (IUCN-style categories and NOM-059-ECOL-1994), and additional references, accompanied by a photograph and distribution map.

## Background & Description

The authors state that the Río Bravo region of northern Mexico received little attention from Mexican biologists until the North American Free Trade Agreement and the 1992 Rio de Janeiro biodiversity agreements brought the border to the forefront. The UANL ichthyology group began with a bachelor's thesis on the fishes of the Río San Juan, Nuevo León, and the senior author later completed a doctoral dissertation at Tulane University on the *lutrensis-formosus* complex (now *Cyprinella*).

The atlas was funded by a series of institutions over time, including Banco de México (1964), Comisión Nacional Consultiva de Pesca (1967–68), a CONACYT–UANL–Nuevo León tripartite committee (1975), U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service (1982), CONACYT (project ICECBNA), and finally CONABIO (1993–95), which financed computerization of the collection database. A list of more than 100 collectors is provided, most of them UANL students, with the base map supplied by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University).

Conventions used: scientific names updated, common names given for convenience (not guaranteed identifications), standard length (LP) and total length (LT) in millimetres or centimetres, author-year citation style, and a glossary defining endemism levels (punctual, basin, regional), native, introduced, invasive, and NOM categories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Río Bravo basin is a semi-arid system with a high proportion of endemic and threatened fishes, many confined to isolated springs and endorheic basins such as the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], the Parras basin, the Nazas–Aguanaval complex, the Samalayuca complex, and the Laguna de Bustillos and Mezquital systems.

The document repeatedly identifies the main threats: water over-extraction and drought, damming (for example [[Amistad Reservoir]], Falcón, Marte R. Gómez, Rodrigo Gómez), salinization of the lower river, pollution, and the spread of introduced species such as [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Guppy]], and aquarium-derived [[Swordtail]] and platy populations. Several species, including [[Stypodon signifer]] and [[Gila sp. A]], are considered extinct or probably extinct; others such as [[Cyprinella alvarezdelvillari]] and [[Cyprinodon ceciliae]] have been reported lost after catastrophic floods or spring drying.

Notable habitat-level observations include: the Cuatro Ciénegas springs harbor several endemic pupfishes and minnows; the lower river receives marine and brackish invaders (about 60 species listed in an appendix, including snappers, croakers, gobies and flatfishes); and hybridization between native and introduced forms (e.g., *Gambusia krumholzi × affinis*, *Catostomus bernardini × leopoldi*) is documented.

## Key Findings

- **Coverage:** 145 species treated in detail across 19 families; the full checklist appears in the document's index.
- **Gars:** *Lepisosteus osseus* was abundant until the 1960s and has declined since; *Atractosteus spatula* is now rarely seen, though it was common in fishermen's nets in the 1950s–60s; *Anguilla rostrata* has been extirpated upstream of major dams.
- **Introduced species:** Common carp, goldfish, grass carp, rosy barb, cherry barb, golden shiner, rainbow trout, green sunfish and others are recorded as introduced, several with unknown or local impacts.
- **Threatened and extinct taxa:** Numerous entries are rated Endangered (En Peligro) or Threatened (Amenazado). Examples include [[Notropis orca]] (probably extinct, last collected 1975), [[Notropis saladonis]] (probably extinct after 1982), [[Cyprinodon ceciliae]] (extinct 1989–1990), [[Cyprinodon longidorsalis]] (extinct 1993–1994), and [[Megupsilon aporus]] (extinct 1992–1994).
- **Species-level discoveries:** Several species are described as new or undescribed in the atlas, including *Astyanax* spp. from Cuatro Ciénegas and Mayrán, *Gila* spp. from several endorheic basins, and *Cyprinella* sp. from the Río Salado.
- **Hybrids and misidentifications:** Eight hybrid or erroneous records are listed, including *Xiphophorus kosszanderi* and *X. roseni*, both interpreted as spontaneous hybrids between native *X. couchianus* and introduced species.
- **Unverified records:** Two taxa, *Lepisosteus platostomus* and *Couesius adustus*, are reported in the literature without authentic voucher records.
- **Marine and brackish invaders:** An appendix lists over 60 marine or estuarine species recorded as recent invaders of the lower Río Bravo, favored by anthropogenic salinity changes.
- **Authors' stated aims:** The atlas is presented as a synthesis rather than a definitive treatment, intended for biosystematists, ecologists, fisheries managers, planners and conservation NGOs, and the authors invite certification of identifications and deposit of voucher material in collections.
