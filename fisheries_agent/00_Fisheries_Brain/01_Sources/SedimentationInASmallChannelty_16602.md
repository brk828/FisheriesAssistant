# Sedimentation in a Small Channel-Type Reservoir: Ashland Lake, Missouri

#limnology #reservoir-sedimentation #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the International Association of Scientific Hydrology Symposium on Lakes and Reservoirs (Lake Garda, Italy, 1966), by H.G. Heinemann, D.L. Rausch and R.S. Campbell, reports a multi-survey study of sediment accumulation in [[Ashland Lake]], a small [[Channel-Type Reservoir]] in central Missouri. The lake was built in 1937 and surveyed in detail in 1949, 1951, 1955 and 1962. The authors aimed to provide design criteria for conservation structures in hilly terrain, where channel-type reservoir sedimentation data were previously scarce.

The study found that the vertical and horizontal distribution of sediment stayed fairly constant between surveys, that sediment particle size varied with distance from the dam, and that sediment volume-weight increased with depth. A regression equation relating volume-weight to sediment depth and percent clay explained 82% of the variation. Water-quality measurements showed strong thermal stratification from May through October and high turbidity during spring overturn. Cation concentrations were too low to flocculate clay particles effectively.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and context.** Soil and water conservation agencies needed methods to estimate reservoir sediment deposition: total volume, volume-weight (dry weight per unit volume in place, usually g/cc) and spatial distribution. Detailed sedimentation data for small channel-type reservoirs were not readily available. The authors cited [[Sprabarry Summary of Reservoir Sediment Surveys]] (USDA Misc. Pub. 964, 1964), which summarized U.S. surveys through 1960, as the existing national reference.

**Reservoir and dam.**
- Built in April 1937 by the former Resettlement Administration, a federal agency.
- Earthfill dam with a concrete core wall, about 12 m high; concrete drop-chute spillway.
- Storage began June 1937; reservoir filled by February 20, 1938.
- Original capacity: about 346,000 m³ (figure 2 gives 345,972 cu m).
- Surface area at spillway elevation: 7.22 ha.
- Original depth: 12.2 m.
- Main body about 1.1 km long, average width about 56 m. Channel side slopes are steep and wooded above the waterline.

**Watershed.**
- Drainage area: 979 ha; about 8 km long; average width 1.2 km; total relief 27 m.
- Soils are dominantly silt loam and highly erosive; sheet and gully erosion have been severe.
- Located in the 970 mm average annual rainfall zone. Precipitation data came from [[Fulton Missouri Weather Station]], 22 km away.

**Survey methods.**
- Surveys followed the techniques of [[Heinemann and Dvorak Volumetric Survey Procedures]] (1965).
- The survey used the spillway contour, 16 established ranges across the main body and tributaries, and 3 additional ranges on the main stream above the headwaters. Depth was sounded along the water ranges, and plane table, alidade and level were used for emergent delta areas.
- 1949, 1951 and 1955 volume-weights came from split-tube and tubular volumetric samplers.
- 1962 volume-weights came from a [[Gamma Density Probe]] (density probe readings at 17 locations on the ranges) and a [[Volumetric Piston-Type Sampler]].
- Piston sampler: interchangeable barrels 1 and 2.7 m long, 7.30 cm diameter. A 10.2 cm sample was taken about every 30 cm of core. 57 samples were taken from 13 locations.

**Laboratory analyses.**
- Particle-size distribution by the hydrometer method.
- Specific gravity (particle density) by pycnometer bottles; organic matter was not removed beforehand.
- Spectrographic analysis of 6 samples to identify elements affecting gamma-ray absorption.

**Water studies.**
- Physical and chemical studies covered 12 months in 1952–1953 (sampling every two weeks) and 1962–1963 (monthly).
- Stations were over the deepest water at range 1 and range 11.
- Temperature readings at 0.3 m intervals; chemical samples at 1.5 m intervals.
- Turbidity was measured with a USGS turbidity rod and expressed in ppm of suspended matter.
- Methods followed the [[American Public Health Association Standard Methods]] and [[Welch Limnological Methods]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.**
- Two stratification periods: winter (under ice) and summer, separated by spring and fall overturns.
- Winter stratification varies with ice duration. Ice lasted less than 4 weeks in 1952 and 1953, but more than 10 weeks in 1962 and 1963.
- Summer stratification is established by July and continues through October. A warm surface layer 2 to 3 m deep sits above cold, stagnant water.
- Fall circulation is usually established by November. Spring circulation follows ice melt and usually extends into April.
- The authors state the thermal regime is similar to other reservoirs in the north temperate region.

**Turbidity and water clarity.**
- Turbidity ranged from 5 to 40 ppm from June through February.
- Clearest water (average 10 ppm) occurred under ice cover and during summer stagnation.
- Spring overturn, coinciding with spring rains, produced a maximum of 200 ppm.
- Turbidity correlates with heavy runoff. Ashland Lake is classed with the more turbid Midwestern reservoirs.

**Flocculating agents (mineral content).** Measured at 0 m and 8 m depths seven times during the 1962–1963 study:
- Calcium: 9 to 58 ppm
- Magnesium: 3.8 to 15.5 ppm
- Sodium and potassium: each less than 7 ppm
- Soluble iron: 0.3 to 14.4 ppm
- The authors conclude that cation concentrations are too low to be effective in flocculating clay particles.

**Sediment transport.** Deposition is controlled by inflow velocity. In the delta, most inflow stays in the channel. During overbank flow, sediment is deposited on the channel sides. Where the channel becomes poorly defined (around range 13), flow spreads, velocity and turbulence fall, and sand and coarser suspension settle rapidly. Finer silt and some clay move toward the dam, consistent with [[Stokes Law]].

## Key Findings

**Sediment accumulation and capacity loss**
- By the 1962 survey, 34% of the original capacity was lost to sediment.
- The lower 40% of original depth contained 10% of original capacity.
- By 1962, 24% of total sediment (1937–1962) was distributed below that depth, and 84% of the capacity below it was replaced by sediment.
- The capacity in the lower 25% of original depth was lost by 1962.
- The 60–80% depth zone holds about one-third of total accumulation.
- The vertical and horizontal sediment distribution stayed relatively constant across all four surveys.

**Bottom gradient**
- The lower half of the reservoir stayed approximately at its original channel gradient.
- The low point on range 14 and above reached a maximum by 1949.
- The channel through the delta stayed constant through 1955, then became narrower and deeper by 1962.

**Particle size**
- Sand and silt were highest near the headwaters and decreased toward the dam.
- Clay (<4 µm) was lowest at the headwaters and highest near the dam.

**Volume-weight**
- Volume-weight increased with sediment depth, roughly in proportion to the logarithm of depth.
- Sediment above low water (range 13) was denser than constantly submerged sediment.
- Volume-weight was highest in the delta, where sediment is exposed at low water (about 1.2 m below spillway elevation) and clay content is lowest.
- Volume-weight decreased as percent clay increased. The correlation with clay was weaker than that reported for [[Sabetha Lake]], Kansas.
- The volumetric sampler gave consistently lighter values than the gamma probe. The gamma probe was judged more accurate and reproducible.

**Regression model.** The best predictive equation was:

1/(w/v) = 0.824 − 0.32 log D + 0.0019 C

- w/v is dry volume-weight (g/cc), D is depth in sediment, and C is percent clay (<2 µm, as the text reads "< 42").
- Coefficient of determination: 0.823 (82% of volume-weight variation explained).
- Partial correlation of w/v with log D: −0.899. With C: 0.527.
- The authors state that the equation is derived from the consolidation theory of [[Taylor Fundamentals of Soil Mechanics]].

- The authors caution that sediment weight is only as accurate as the volume-weight and volume data. Because the first three surveys used small-diameter core samplers that penetrated only the upper sediment, the weights from those surveys were not compared critically.

**Water chemistry and stratification**
- Ashland Lake is strongly stratified from May through October. During this period circulation is confined to the upper 3 m (about one-third of the lake).
- This stagnation reduces the reservoir's ability to trap sediment during periods of small outflow.
- Spring and fall overturns coincide with the highest turbidity. The lake stays highly turbid after rainfall and clears slowly during stagnation periods.

**Conclusions for design**
- Sediment volume alone is an inadequate measure of accumulation because of consolidation. Weight-based accumulation is the more reliable basis for estimating the "true" rate.
- Accumulation rates vary with precipitation, land use, surface condition during rainfall and trap efficiency. Only precipitation data were available to the authors.
