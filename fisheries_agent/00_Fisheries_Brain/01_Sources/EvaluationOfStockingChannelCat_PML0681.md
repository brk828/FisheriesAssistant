# Evaluation of Stocking Channel Catfish in Kenney Reservoir, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife (CDOW) draft final report evaluates whether [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) can provide a sportfishery in [[Kenney Reservoir]], a mainstem impoundment on the [[White River]] near Rangely, Colorado. About 3,000 Age 1 channel catfish were stocked in 1992 and fin-clipped for identification. Sampling ran from 1993 to 1994, with additional work in 1995, 1996, and 1997. Objectives were to assess survival, growth, and retention in the reservoir, and downstream escapement into the White River. The White River below the dam is occupied habitat for the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and concern about nonnative catfish threatening its recovery shaped the study.

Of 278 channel catfish collected from the reservoir, 259 were stocked and 19 (7%) were wild. Estimated survival was 63% per year for 1992–95. Growth was good and comparable to other regional waters. None of 404 catfish sampled downstream in the White River were stocked fish, indicating very low escapement. The authors concluded that channel catfish appear suitable as a sportfish and recommended limited, guideline-based stocking.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Channel catfish is a widely valued sportfish in the United States, popular in western waters that do not support trout. It is not native to the White River; the first recorded stocking was in 1907.
- **Reservoir:** Kenney Reservoir was created in 1984 by [[Taylor Draw Dam]]. It is about 10 km long, covers 243 ha (600 acres), has a maximum depth of 15.2 m, and retains water for about two days. The run-of-the-river hydroelectric facility outlet is a penstock at 15.2 m depth, fitted with a non-removable trash rack.
- **Prior management:** The 1986 Lake Management Plan aimed at a sportfishery based on rainbow trout and Colorado pikeminnow. Rainbow trout moved in and out of the reservoir, and most stocked pikeminnow disappeared within a year, with large numbers escaping over the spillway. The pikeminnow sportfishery attempt was judged unsuccessful (Elmblad 1997).
- **Other species:** Black crappie became a limited reservoir sportfishery and have been found below the dam. Their predatory nature raised concern for endangered river fish.
- **Sterile fish:** Sterile channel × blue catfish hybrids were originally proposed, but blue catfish were not introduced to the Colorado River basin. The Colorado Division of Wildlife (CDOW) could not produce sterile triploid channel catfish, so the 1992 stocking used fertile fish.
- **Stocked fish:** Mean TL at stocking was 195 mm and mean weight 61 g.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir lies in critical habitat for the endangered Colorado pikeminnow. Adults still occur in the White River up to Taylor Draw Dam, but are no longer found in the reservoir or upstream. A wild channel catfish population already exists in the White River below the dam, and the authors state that it appears to be naturally reproducing (though this is later judged unlikely within the reservoir).

Reservoir conditions were described as unfavorable for many species: Martinez (1986) characterized the river as coolwater/warmwater, subject to extreme discharge and heavy silt loads. Channel catfish tolerate the turbid conditions of both the White River and the reservoir. Prey was abundant: fathead minnows comprised 45–90% of the fish community from 1984–1990, and crayfish were common. Growing season in the area is 105–130 days.

The hydroelectric plant began operation in June 1993, the same year the study started. Fish escaping through the penstock may suffer turbine and pressure injuries. Spill over the spillway occurs only during spring runoff or heavy precipitation.

## Key Findings

### Sampling Effort and Catch
- Total sampling effort 1993–1997: 4,431.8 hours. Reservoir effort was 3,042 hours; river effort was 1,390 hours.
- Total channel catfish catch 1993–1997: 682 (278 reservoir, 404 river).
- Reservoir gear: gill and trammel nets were most productive; electrofishing and trotlines were dropped after 1993 because of low catches.
- Catch per gill/trammel net hour: 0.14 (1993), 0.15 (1994), 0.10 (1995), 0.07 (1996), 0.23 (1997).

### Survival, Growth, and Retention
- Stocked fish recaptured in the reservoir: 3% (1993), 9% (1994), 14% (1995); none in 1996–1997.
- Estimated stocked population: 757 fish in 1995 (Schnabel estimator), 25% of the 3,000 stocked, giving a **63% annual survival rate** for 1992–95. This estimate is likely low because few recaptures were available.
- Survival is comparable to other sportfisheries: [[Wyoming Reservoirs]] (about 75% assumed) and Lake Sharpe, South Dakota (50–70%).
- Growth: measured mean TL was 195, 213, 266, 360, 440, and 480 mm for Ages 1+ through 6+. Growth was comparable to or better than other waters, with Oklahoma and Colorado waters used as comparisons in Table 5.
- Mean measured length rose 53 mm (1993–94), 94 mm (1994–95), 80 mm (1995–96), and 40 mm (1996–97). Mean weight rose from 87 g to 392 g across the same periods.
- Wild fish: 19 unmarked fish (7% of reservoir catch), 230–715 mm TL (mean 445 mm). These were probably trapped when the dam was built or illicitly stocked. Using the stocked-to-wild ratio, the original wild population was estimated at about 220, roughly 46% of the reservoir channel catfish population.
- Only four fish under 300 mm were taken in five years, so natural reproduction in the reservoir is considered unlikely.

### Escapement
- No stocked fish were found in 404 channel catfish sampled downstream in the White River. One wild fish was recaptured there.
- One stocked fish was taken in the White River about 11 km upstream of the reservoir (1995). Two fish from the reservoir (one wild, one stocked) were recorded upstream.
- In 1998, after the draft was circulated, a marked stocked fish (551 mm TL, 2,250 g) was caught below the reservoir with a gash, suggesting injury from escapement. This capture is not included in the report.

### River Population Estimates
- Electrofishing catch per effort in 1994: 13.8 fish/km, expanding to about 2,832 fish (55 fish/km) at a 25% catch efficiency. At a 10% efficiency, the estimate was 7,107 fish (138 fish/km).

### Recommendations
The report recommended amending the Kenney Reservoir LMP and beginning review under the Nonnative Fish Stocking Procedures (NNFSP). Main guidelines:
1. Stock biennially at a low rate (1.2 fish/ha) to maintain growth.
2. Adipose-clip all stocked fish.
3. Release fish at the upper reservoir end, away from the outlet and spillway.
4. Sample the White River below the reservoir at five-year intervals for escapement.
5. Periodically sample the White River above the reservoir.
6. Allow transplanting of channel catfish removed from critical habitat, with all fish marked.
7. Discontinue stocking if downstream escapement develops or the upstream fish community changes unusually.

### Peer Review and Policy Context
- Reviewers from the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) raised concerns. A consultant reviewer (William Davis, EcoPlan Associates) opposed stocking, arguing that the RIP is spending heavily to remove channel catfish as a major threat to native fish, and recommended removing the phrase "propose stocking" from the recommendations. The FWS reviewer (Bob Burdick) recommended a Conclusions section and asked whether adipose fin clips persist, since regeneration was unknown.
- The 1996 Colorado Wildlife Commission approved Procedures for Stocking Nonnative Fish in the Upper Colorado River Basin, under which channel catfish stocking in Kenney Reservoir would require individual RIP review.
- Conclusion: channel catfish growth and survival were good, retention appeared good, and the potential impact to endangered fish appeared minimal. Stocking "warrants further consideration."

### Earlier Proposal (FY-1991 Scope of Work)
An FY-1991 proposal aimed to evaluate Colorado pikeminnow and sterile catfish as sportfish, with a budget of roughly $40,000–41,000 per year (about $93,000 total across FY-91 to FY-93, as listed). The FWS had previously opposed nonnative warmwater stocking unless the fish were sterile or remained in the reservoir.
