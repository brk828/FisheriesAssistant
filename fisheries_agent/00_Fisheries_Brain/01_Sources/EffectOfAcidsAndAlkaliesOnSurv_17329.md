# Effect of Acids and Alkalies on Survival of Bluegills and Largemouth Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Technical Paper (No. 42, October 1969) reports a laboratory and field study by [[Anthony Calabrese]], based on his 1962 M.S. thesis at [[Auburn University]]. The study tested whether rapid changes in water pH could selectively kill [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) in farm-pond populations while sparing [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Acids and alkalies were tested in aquariums (tap and pond water) and in plastic-lined pools (stream water), across a pH range of 3.3 to 11.2. The author concluded that [[PH Manipulation]] is impractical as a method for selectively thinning bluegills from bass-bluegill populations.

## Background & Description

Selective chemical removal of unwanted fish or size classes is an established tool in [[Farm Pond Management]]. Many compounds had already been screened for toxicity to bluegills and bass, including antimycin, MS-222 and organic pesticides (Applegate et al., 1957; Burdick et al., 1964; Callaham and Huish, 1967; Marking, 1967; Mulla et al., 1967; Walker et al., 1964). This study asked two questions:

1. What pH levels are lethal to largemouth bass and bluegills?
2. Does fish size influence pH tolerance?

Toxicity of strong alkalies is generally attributed to hydroxyl ions and that of strong acids to hydrogen ions, acting mainly on the external surfaces of fish, particularly the [[Gill Damage]] they cause (Doudoroff, 1957; Ellis et al., 1948). Westfall (1945) proposed that acid altered gill permeability and interfered with respiration and excretion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Aquarium tests used 40-liter aquariums with three to five replicates per concentration and one to three untreated controls. Tap water was passed through activated charcoal to remove chlorine. Pond water came from a small pond at the Auburn station. Water was aerated for 24 hours before testing. Stocking followed a guideline of no more than 2 g of fish per liter (Doudoroff et al., 1951), with 10 to 25 fish per aquarium. Tests normally ran 24 hours; three ran 48 hours.

Field-style tests used five vinyl-lined pools (2.7 m diameter, 0.76 m deep, about 3,743 to 3,890 liters of pumped stream water). Each pool had two controls and two or three replicates per concentration. Pools held 25 to 50 bluegills, 4 small bass and 2 large bass, with large bass isolated in wire baskets to prevent predation.

**Water chemistry.** Tap and pond waters were chemically similar, though pond water had lower total hardness. Stream water varied more widely, and it contained carbonates in some tests.

| Parameter | Tap water (aquarium) | Pond water (aquarium) | Stream water (pools) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°F) | 69–75 | 70–77 | 42–95 |
| CO₂ (ppm) | 1.1–4.1 | 2.3–4.9 | 0.0–2.8 |
| Carbonates (ppm) | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0–21.0 |

**Buffering.** Buffering capacity, driven by CO₂, bicarbonates, carbonates and hardness, strongly influenced how far pH moved at a given chemical dose. This is a central reason the results were so variable.

**Chemicals.** Hydrochloric acid (HCl), acetic acid, sodium hydroxide ([[Sodium Hydroxide]]) and [[Calcium Hydroxide]] were used at U.S.P. or A.C.S. grade. HCl is a strong acid that drops pH quickly. [[Acetic Acid]] is weak, partially ionizes and buffers, so its pH stays near one level for longer.

## Key Findings

**Acid toxicity: HCl**
- In tap water, all bluegills and bass died at 25.9 and 22.2 ppm HCl (pH 3.4 to 3.6). In a separate concentration, 74% of bass but no bluegills died at 18.5 ppm (pH 3.8).
- The 24-hour [[Lethal Concentration 50]] (LC50) values were 23.0 ppm (pH 3.6) for bluegills in tap water and 18.0 ppm (pH 3.7) in pond water.
- Bass LC50s were 16.0 ppm (pH 3.9) in tap water and 23.0 ppm (pH 3.7) in pond water. Bass were more susceptible in tap water and about equally tolerant in pond water.
- In pond water, bass showed a delayed pH drop and began dying only after pH fell to about 3.7.
- Moribund fish were sluggish and covered in thick mucus.

**Acid toxicity: acetic acid**
- All bluegills and bass died at 200 to 225 ppm (pH 4.0).
- At 125 to 150 ppm (pH 4.2), 42.5% to 97.5% of bass died, but these figures came from 48-hour tests.
- Both species shared an LC50 pH of 4.2. Table 10 lists about 130 ppm for small bass and 185 ppm for bluegills.
- Acetic acid toxicity was slow, and a high proportion of fish died within 1 hour at 300 ppm (pH 3.9) and above.

**Acid toxicity: plastic-lined pools (HCl)**
- Two bluegill tests gave variable results, with pH differing by up to 1.0 unit at doses of 20.7 ppm and below.
- The estimated LC50 was 20.0 ppm (pH 4.1), which did not agree with the aquarium results. At 28.2 ppm, both tests reached pH 3.5 and 77.8% of bluegills died, which was comparable to aquarium data.

**Alkali toxicity: sodium hydroxide**
- Bass were more sensitive than bluegills to sudden increases in pH. At 35 ppm (pH 10.3), 57.5% of bass but only 7% of bluegills died.
- All bass died at 65 ppm (pH 10.9), while only 28.8% of bluegills died at the highest concentration.
- Bass began dying 2 to 4 hours after treatment and bluegills 6 to 12 hours after.
- The LC50 values were 31.7 ppm (pH 10.3) for bass and 76.6 ppm (pH 10.9) for bluegills.

**Alkali toxicity: calcium hydroxide**
- Tap water: LC50 of 38.3 ppm (pH 10.5) for small bass and 64.5 ppm (pH 10.9) for bluegills.
- Pond water: LC50 of 31.0 ppm (pH 10.3) for small bass and 60.0 ppm (pH 10.8) for bluegills.
- Stream water: LC50 of 40.0 ppm (pH 10.8) for small bass and 45.8 ppm (pH 10.9) for bluegills.
- Large bass (one test only) had an LC50 of 55.0 ppm (pH 11.0). The author states these results are not conclusive.
- Death generally began 2 to 6 hours after treatment, and fish died faster in tap water than in pond water.

**Size and species comparison**
- Small bass were slightly more sensitive than bluegills to alkali in aquariums and pools.
- Large bass appeared more tolerant than small bass or bluegills, but this rests on a single test.
- Within the bluegill size range tested (2.5 to 12.7 cm), size had no apparent relation to pH tolerance.

**Variability and comparison with other studies**
- No single LC50 could be assigned to either species because results varied greatly between tests. One Ca(OH)₂ confidence interval was reported as ±62.6%.
- Stiemke and Eckenfelder (1947) gave a lethal pH for bluegills in HCl of 3.1 to 3.2 in spring and 3.7 to 3.8 in winter, suggesting seasonal or water-quality effects.
- Calabrese's NaOH bluegill LC50 (76.6 ppm, pH 10.9) closely matched Trama (1954).
- Sanborn (1945) reported no effect on bass at 50 ppm NaOH (pH 10.4), which disagrees with these results.

**Field limitations**
- Buffering power varies with time of day and depth. Deeper water needs more alkali, which could create lethal pH levels near the surface.
- Photosynthesis during the day shifts pH and carbonate chemistry.
- Some acids appeared to be toxic at certain concentrations regardless of pH.

**Conclusion**
- Selective removal of bluegills using pH manipulation is not feasible. The species differ only slightly in tolerance, test results are highly variable, and field buffering is unpredictable.
- Tests were mostly limited to 24 hours. Longer exposure (for example 96 hours) could have produced higher death rates, but the author judged that pH would drift too much over that period.
