# Proposed Introduction of Rainbow Smelt into Lake Powell: Expert Review Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of expert review letters and comments by an Arizona State University zoology professor (1988–1989) on the Utah Division of Wildlife proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]]. The reviewer opposes the introduction. The main concerns are the likelihood of downstream escape into the [[Colorado River]] system, direct predation and competition with native and [[Endangered Species Act]]-listed fishes such as the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and the irreversibility of introductions. The reviewer also criticises the proposal's interpretation of literature and the management history of [[Striped Bass]] in the basin.

## Background & Description

The correspondence consists of three parts:

- **Letter to the National Park Service (14 December 1989)**, addressed to Mr. John P. Ritenour, Chief of Resource Management at Glen Canyon National Recreation Area. It summarises the reviewer's position and recommends excluding smelt from the lower Colorado River.
- **Detailed review (4 February 1988)**, addressed to Mr. Wayne Gustaveson of the Utah Division of Wildlife. It reviews the smelt proposal and two supporting documents: an assessment of storage enhancement for game fish and a 1987 striped bass report.
- **Comments on the August 1989 proposal**, a multi-page critique covering the literature base, life history, thermal ecology, potential impacts and expected behaviour of smelt in Lake Powell.

The reviewer describes smelt as a high-level carnivorous species that reaches about 14 inches in total length and can become very abundant. Its native range is temperate eastern North America, and the species is considered a lacustrine or oceanic fish in that range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir fishery development.** The reviewer argues that Lake Powell, which filled and spilled in 1983, has not yet stabilised. Fish populations have followed a boom-and-bust pattern common in North American reservoirs. Walleye appeared unplanned, kokanee were stocked in 1963 (600,000) and 1964 (35,000), and threadfin shad were introduced as a prey species. Striped bass were then introduced as a piscivore, and largemouth bass and black crappie declined after 1979. The reviewer notes that the reported 0.4 fish per hour average angler take has held for over two decades.

**Thermal ecology.** The proposal cites a Wyoming feasibility review (Wiehers 1980) giving a temperature limit of 21 to 26°C. The reviewer notes that smelt spawning has been recorded at 18.3°C, 21°C and an average of 16.5°C for hatching. Surface temperatures at Roosevelt Lake seldom exceed 28–30°C, and only for a few weeks each year. Lake Powell surface temperatures are similar or slightly cooler.

**Downstream and transfer pathways.** The reviewer considers downstream establishment in [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]] and possibly [[Lake Havasu]] highly likely. Other routes include the [[Central Arizona Project]] canal to central Arizona reservoirs, the Los Angeles intake to California, and illicit bait transfers. The reviewer argues that fishes "do not read the books" and points to striped bass spawning in reservoirs, walleye appearing in Lake Powell and Lake Mead, and tilapia surviving in cold water.

**Habitat overlap with native fishes.** Humpback chub concentrate in the [[Little Colorado River]] mouth and in Grand Canyon. Bonytail chub persist as large adults in Lake Mohave, where augmentation is ongoing using hatchery fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Larval razorback suckers are common in Lake Mohave in early spring. Recruitment in Lake Mohave is minimal, and a recovery effort has run for nearly a decade.

## Key Findings

1. **Escape is probable.** Smelt will almost certainly establish in Lake Powell, invade downstream into Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, and move through the Colorado and San Juan rivers. The reviewer rejects the proposal's assumption that thermal barriers will contain them.
2. **Native fish impacts are unacceptable.** Smelt will "certainly eat" native fishes and compete with them for food and space. The reviewer expects smelt to feed on young humpback and bonytail chub and on larval and surficial razorback sucker eggs.
3. **Legal constraints apply.** Federal funding and federal ownership of Glen Canyon Dam would likely require consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the Endangered Species Act, plus an Environmental Impact Statement.
4. **Literature is incomplete and inconsistent.** Of 147 citations in the proposal, only 41 are gray literature. Most life-history references come from eastern North America, and western introductions (Nebraska, South and North Dakota, Colorado) have not been running long enough for objective evaluation. The proposal relies on personal communications and contains internal contradictions on temperature and spawning.
5. **Striped bass precedent.** Striped bass introduced below Davis Dam and into Lakes Mead and Powell followed a sequence of trout fishery collapse, shad depletion and fishery collapse. The reviewer argues that smelt would follow a similar path.
6. **Irreversibility.** Once established, species cannot be eradicated. The reviewer concludes that the risks are unacceptable and recommends exclusion of smelt from the lower Colorado River system.
