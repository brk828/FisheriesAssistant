# No Time Left--Arizona's Native Fishes, Going, Going, Gone!

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #conservation-biology #arizona-native-fish

## Executive Summary
This paper by W.L. Minckley documents the catastrophic decline of Arizona's native fish fauna over the preceding 50 years. Of 33 originally present fish species/kinds, 25 are officially listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as [[Endangered Species]], [[Threatened Species]], or candidates for listing, and one species is already extinct. Arizona is identified as the first U.S. state where essentially the entire native fish fauna faces extinction. The decline is attributed to cumulative anthropogenic habitat degradation—timbering, livestock grazing, water development, and the introduction of non-native species—rather than any single cause. The author argues for habitat management and regulation of non-native species introductions as the primary path to preventing further extinctions.

## Background & Description
This is a conservation/ecosystem-focused paper rather than a morphological study; no specific anatomical, meristic, or morphometric data for individual species are provided. Key taxonomic notes include:
- **33 kinds of fishes** originally native to Arizona.
- **29 of 31** (non-marine) native species are **endemic** to western American rivers, occurring nowhere else in the world.
- **2 species** are marine strays — euryhaline/marine fishes from the [[Gulf of California]] that historically entered Arizona by swimming up the [[Colorado River]].
- **1 species** is confirmed **extinct**.
- **5 species** were recently recommended as new candidates for federal listing at time of writing (1993).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Native Arizona fishes are tied to specific historical hydrological and geomorphological conditions of desert stream systems, described in detail as a baseline against which degradation is measured:

- **Historical (undisturbed) watershed conditions:**
  - Slow runoff from intact vegetation; high infiltration into groundwater.
  - Groundwater seepage sustained reliable summer streamflow.
  - Infrequent violent flooding; complex, minimally downcut stream channels.
  - Substrates of well-sorted rock, gravel, and sand.
  - Abundant pool habitat near tree roots, boulders, and banks.
  - Riparian vegetation ranging from [[Cottonwood-Willow Forest]] to dense brushland.
  - Marshes formed where groundwater reached the surface.
  - Thermal moderation via groundwater exchange and riparian shading.

- **Current (degraded) conditions:**
  - Rapid runoff from grazed/timbered watersheds → frequent flash floods.
  - Increased flood power, erosion, and sediment transport in narrow, downcut channels.
  - Homogenized sandy substrates; loss of pool habitat.
  - Lowered water tables from channel incision and groundwater pumping.
  - Riparian vegetation loss from flood uprooting and drought-driven root desiccation.
  - Reduced summer flows to thin channels between barren, near-vertical banks.
  - Extreme thermal fluctuation (rapid heating/cooling) due to low water volume and reduced groundwater exchange.
  - Proliferation of [[Non-Native Fish Introductions]] in artificial habitats (reservoirs, canals, ponds), which subsequently invade and displace remnant native populations via predation and competition.

Native fishes are framed ecologically as **bioindicators**: their rarity signals broader degradation in water quality, quantity, or habitat structure with implications beyond fish conservation, including human water supply concerns.

## Key Findings
- **25 of 33** originally present Arizona fish species (kinds) are listed as Endangered, Threatened, or Candidate species under the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **1 species** is already extinct; **5 additional species** were newly recommended as listing candidates at time of writing.
- **29 of 31** non-marine native species are endemic to western American rivers (found nowhere else on Earth).
- Two additional native "fishes" are marine species from the [[Gulf of California]] that entered Arizona via the [[Colorado River]].
- Arizona is described as the **first U.S. state** where nearly the entire native fish fauna faces extinction.
- Primary drivers of decline (compounding since the 1800s):
  1. **Timbering and livestock grazing** (1800s onward) — altered vegetation and drainage-wide hydrology (runoff, erosion, sedimentation).
  2. **Water development** (early 1900s onward) — stream drying and flow alteration.
  3. **Non-native species introductions** (1940s onward) — restructured aquatic communities via predation/competition.
  4. **Groundwater pumping and channel downcutting** — compounding riparian and hydrologic collapse.
- Artificial, stabilized habitats (reservoirs, canals) disproportionately favor [[Non-Native Fish Species]], which then spread into and displace remaining native populations.
- Proposed conservation strategies:
  - Recognize native fishes as natural, low-cost **indicators of environmental/watershed health**.
  - Promote sustainable grazing, mining, and water-use practices to protect remaining natural habitats.
  - Regulate stocking practices and other vectors of non-native fish spread.
  - Increase public awareness to support proactive refuge and management programs for remaining native fish populations.
- The author explicitly acknowledges that large-scale infrastructure (dams, canals) supporting human populations in Arizona and neighboring states (e.g., California) cannot realistically be removed, framing conservation solutions as necessarily working within existing water infrastructure constraints.
