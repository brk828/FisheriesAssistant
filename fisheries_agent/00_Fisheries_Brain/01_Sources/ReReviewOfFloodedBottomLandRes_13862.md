# Flooded Bottom Land Restoration Program Review and Technical Memorandum

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #habitat-restoration #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document comprises correspondence and program proposals (1994) regarding the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] [[Flooded Bottom Land Restoration Program]], focused on recovering the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Dr. C.F. Bryan of the [[Louisiana Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] provides critical technical review of the program's umbrella proposal, emphasizing the need to understand historical pre-dam river conditions and early life history requirements before implementing restoration. The program proposes screening, evaluating, and restoring approximately 20 flood plain sites across 870 miles of the [[Green River]], [[Colorado River]], and [[Gunnison River]] to provide larval rearing habitat. Major concerns include predation by non-native fishes (smallmouth bass, sunfishes) on drifting larvae and the lack of baseline ecological data from pre-dam conditions.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] historically occurred throughout the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] but has experienced severe population declines, with recruitment failure being a primary concern. The species requires flooded bottom land habitats to complete its life cycle, particularly for larval rearing. Construction of dams (including [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]) fundamentally altered river hydrology, eliminating natural flood regimes that historically inundated nursery habitats.

The [[Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes]] initiated a systematic inventory of flood plain habitats throughout 870 river miles, scoring and ranking sites based on: proximity to razorback sucker spawning areas, floodability during 1993 spring runoff, proximity to other flood plain habitats, and property ownership (Federal/State lands preferred). In the [[Green River]] subbasin, priority sites are located downstream from the last known spawning area. In the [[Colorado River]] and [[Gunnison River]], where spawning populations are extirpated, sites are prioritized near historical capture locations.

The program identified two general categories of flood plain habitats: **terraces** (inundated during high flows, then dewatered) and **depressions** (isolated pools/lakes including oxbows, wetlands, gravel pits). Five initial sites were selected for detailed evaluation: [[Old Charley Wash]] (Wood's Bottom), [[Moab Slough]], [[Escalante State Wildlife Area]], Gravel Pit 29 5/8, and [[Escalante Ranch]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dr. Bryan's review raises fundamental ecological questions about the program's approach. He hypothesizes that adult and yearling non-native predators (e.g., [[Smallmouth Bass]], sunfishes) inhabit littoral areas and prey on drifting catostomid and cyprinid larvae transported to [[Lake Mohave]]. The razorback sucker evolved without defenses against such predation pressure. Approximately 43 non-native fish species now occur in the basin versus only 13 native species.

Bryan emphasizes that without understanding pre-dam river characteristics—historical stage hydrographs, temperature profiles, water chemistry, aerial photos of riverine and extrariverine habitats—the program cannot determine what constitutes "sufficient" or "suitable" habitat. He argues that natural positive attributes that historically supported early life survival must be identified before restoration can succeed.

The program acknowledges critical uncertainties: whether restored habitats can support razorback survival given non-native predation; whether intensive management (screens, rotenone, controlled water levels) is necessary versus natural flooding regimes; and whether the scope should focus exclusively on razorback recovery or include other endangered fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]]) and ecosystem values.

Contaminants, particularly selenium, pose additional concerns at several candidate sites including [[Escalante Ranch]], requiring Level I, II, and III assessments before restoration proceeds.

## Key Findings

**Technical Review Recommendations (Bryan):**

- Initiate comprehensive studies of razorback sucker early life history requisites before large-scale restoration
- Obtain and analyze all available historical data (USGS, Corps, Bureau of Reclamation, BLM) on pre-dam river conditions: hydrology, temperature, chemistry, habitat structure
- Consider three potential management approaches: (i) shoreline rotenone applications each fall to reduce juvenile predators; (ii) divert lotic headwaters to specially prepared, predator-excluded floodplain nurseries (January-April flooding); (iii) create extensive organically enriched nursery areas where larvae aggregate, providing "overkill" rearing habitat to exceed predator grazing capacity
- Recognize that 1-2 inch hatchery-raised suckers are not fully developed (incomplete scaling, organ systems) and remain vulnerable to predation
- Acknowledge that logarithmic diminution of recruits during first months of life may be natural, not necessarily indicating specialized habitat deficiencies

**Program Implementation Plans (FY 1995-1997):**

- Screen 10-15 highest priority sites for contaminants, hydrology, geomorphology, biology, and landowner cooperation
- Conduct Level I and II contaminant assessments; design Level III studies where problems identified
- Determine overbank flood flows, frequency, duration, timing (historical vs. existing conditions)
- Assess fish species composition, food abundance, vegetative cover, water quality, predation risk
- Develop site-specific Conceptual Restoration and Management Plans
- Test [[Old Charley Wash]] pilot site during 1994-1995 with controlled water levels, screens, and wild-captured larvae; evaluate with natural overbank flooding during FY 1995 using [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] bypass tube releases
- Restore [[Gravel Pit 29 5/8]] to evaluate gravel pit potential for endangered fish recovery
- Conduct feasibility study for [[Escalante State Wildlife Area]] (requires extensive excavation, terracing, construction)
- Resolve selenium contamination issues at [[Escalante Ranch]] before acquisition
- Restore [[Moab Slough]] and [[Leota Bottom]] if screening tests passed
- Conduct selenium toxicity research on razorback sucker survival, growth, and reproduction

**Budget and Timeline:**

- FY 1995: $1.6M requested ($2.792M proposed); FY 1996: $2.5M; FY 1997: $2.5M
- Total three-year budget: $6.6M
- Activities include general coordination ($90K), site coordination ($50K), property acquisition ($50K), NEPA/permits ($50K), contaminants assessment ($150K), hydrology ($100K), biology ($200K), engineering/design/construction ($700K), technical advisors ($25K)

**Fundamental Program Tensions:**

Two competing restoration philosophies emerged: (1) intensive management approach requiring inlet/outlet control structures, screens, predator removal, and controlled stocking until fish reach predator-resistant size; versus (2) natural flooding approach reconnecting sufficient habitats with abundant vegetation for larval refugia, allowing natural recruitment without intensive intervention. The program planned to test both approaches to determine effectiveness.
