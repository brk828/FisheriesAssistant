# Serological Evidence for Inbreeding of Lahontan Cutthroat Trout in Summit Lake, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #population-genetics #serology

## Executive Summary
This 1966 study used isoimmune sera raised in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) to serologically type red blood cells of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii henshawi*) from two Nevada populations: [[Summit Lake]] and [[Catnip Reservoir]]. The goal was to test a field-based hypothesis that the Summit Lake broodstock—considered by [[Robert J. Behnke]] (1960) to be a genetically "pure" Lahontan cutthroat lineage—had become inbred, based on observations of poor fry survival in intra-population matings versus excellent survival in outbreeding crosses. Blood-typing results showed markedly more uniform (homogeneous) antigenic reactions among Summit Lake fish compared to the more heterogeneous Catnip Reservoir fish (descended from Heenan Lake hatchery stock), statistically supporting the inbreeding hypothesis. The study represents an early application of immunogenetic/blood-group methods to fisheries management and broodstock evaluation.

## Background & Description
This paper does not describe external or meristic morphology directly (morphological/meristic differentiation of Lahontan cutthroat trout populations was addressed separately by [[Behnke 1960]]). Instead, the "phenotype" under study is at the **cellular/antigenic level**:

- **Sample basis:** Red blood cells (erythrocytes) collected via cardiac puncture from anesthetized adult trout.
- **Cell handling:** Cells stored in citrate solution, held on ice for up to 4 days prior to testing.
- **Trait measured:** Presence/absence and reaction strength of erythrocyte surface antigens (blood-group factors) detected using a panel of isoimmune rainbow trout sera.
- **Assay method:** Capillary-tube agglutination technique ([[Chown and Lewis 1946]]), with reaction strength graded on a semi-quantitative scale (e.g., very strong to no reaction).
- Antigen expression patterns are treated as heritable markers under simple genetic (single-locus) control, per prior work by [[George J. Ridgway]] (1962b).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Isolated high-desert lentic systems within the Lahontan Basin (Great Basin), Nevada — landlocked, non-marine trout habitat.
- **Summit Lake population:** Regarded as a genetically pure, self-sustaining relict population of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], of high ichthyological and fish-cultural (broodstock) significance.
- **Catnip Reservoir population:** An introduced/stocked population derived historically from [[Heenan Lake]] hatchery stock (California), itself suspected to carry some introgressed [[Rainbow Trout]] genes (per Behnke 1960).
- **Broodstock role:** Summit Lake serves as a primary genetic reservoir for propagation programs of Lahontan cutthroat trout in California and Nevada, making its genetic integrity a direct conservation and hatchery-management concern.
- **Threats noted:** Historical stocking practices and general anthropogenic pressure ("civilization") had already drastically reduced pure Lahontan cutthroat trout populations range-wide by the time of this study.

## Key Findings

- **Reagent panel:** 40 isoimmune rainbow trout sera reagents were screened against cutthroat trout erythrocytes.
  - 24 reagents: no or negligible reaction.
  - 6 reagents: reacted uniformly with **all** cells tested (non-discriminating).
  - **10 reagents**: showed variable reactivity across individuals — used as the primary discriminating panel (6 absorbed/pooled single-specificity reagents; 4 unabsorbed single-bleeding reagents with possible multiple specificities).

- **Summit Lake results:**
  - Highly uniform reactivity: 7 of 10 reagents reacted with either **all** or **none** of the 19 fish tested.
  - Only 1 of 19 fish deviated qualitatively for each of the remaining 3 reagents.
  - Reaction *strength* (quantitative) was also highly consistent among individuals.
  - Interpreted as reflecting a high degree of homozygosity — consistent with **inbreeding**.

- **Catnip Reservoir results:**
  - Markedly less uniform reactivity both qualitatively and quantitatively across the 20 fish tested for the same reagent panel.
  - Interpreted as reflecting a genetically heterogeneous, mixed-genotype population.

- **Statistical support:** Chi-square tests comparing reaction frequencies between Summit Lake and Catnip Reservoir populations across the 10 discriminating reagents:
  - 7 of 10 reagents significant at **p < 0.01**.
  - Remaining 3 significant at **p < 0.05**.
  - Reagents **297** and **507** were especially discriminating between the two groups.

- **Interpretation:** Uniform antigen reaction frequency/strength within Summit Lake fish suggests fixation (homozygosity) at blood-group loci absent detectable "dosage effects," consistent with inbreeding depression as the likely cause of previously observed poor fry survival in intra-Summit-Lake crosses (versus successful outbreeding with other cutthroat strains).

- **Methodological/Management implications:**
  - Blood-group serology is proposed as a practical, environmentally robust tool (traits resistant to environmental influence, per [[Race and Sanger 1962]]) for detecting inbreeding in hatchery broodstocks before fry losses become severe.
  - Potential application: developing selectively bred "blood lines" in hatcheries analogous to domestic animal blood-typing/registration practices ([[Stormont 1958]]).
  - Cross-reactivity of rainbow trout isoimmune reagents across salmonid species suggests such reagents could be generated in hardy species and applied broadly across the Salmonidae family.
  - Builds on and corroborates prior serological work in salmonids by [[Ridgway 1962a]], [[Ridgway 1962b]], [[Bingham 1963]], [[Calaprice and Cushing 1964]], and [[Sanders and Wright 1962]].

- **Broader context:** Described as part of a "rapidly expanding use of serological methods in fishery research" over the preceding 15 years, with the authors indicating ongoing research into isoimmune serum specificities and their genetic basis.
