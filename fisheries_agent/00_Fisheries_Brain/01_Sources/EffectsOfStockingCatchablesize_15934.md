# Effects of Stocking Catchable-Size Channel Catfish on Three Warmwater Fisheries, Yuma County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AGFD), prepared for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region), evaluates whether supplemental plants of tagged, catchable-size [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) improve angler catch rates, harvest, and wild fish populations in three Yuma County waters: the [[Gila Gravity Main Canal]], [[Mittry Lake]], and [[Redondo Lake]]. Fieldwork ran from April 1983 through September 1985.

The study found that stocking did not produce a lasting angler benefit. Catch rates of wild channel catfish fell after stockings at the canal and at Mittry Lake, and overall catch at Mittry Lake also declined. The authors conclude that catchable-size stocking should be limited to waters without a reproducing population or where angler use exceeds what wild stocks can sustain.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The study addressed six objectives: (1) suitability of commercially reared fish in cost, reliability, and condition; (2) whether stocking improves catch rates; (3) rate and percent of stocked fish returned to the creel; (4) effects of stocking density; (5) effects on length/weight relationships of resident game species; and (6) program cost relative to angler benefit.

**Regional context.** The stocking program was a mitigation measure. The 1944 U.S.–Mexico treaty, Mexico's [[Morelos Dam]] diversions, and rising salinity (about 850 to 1500 ppm by 1961) led to the [[Colorado River Salinity Control Act]] (1974) and a desalting plant west of Yuma. Plant construction dewatered about 34 km of the border reach. The Federal government funded a warmwater hatchery to replace an estimated 14,358 lost annual angler days, with a planned output of up to 16,000 kg per year. Concerns about hatchery costs led AGFD and the Bureau to test purchased catchable fish instead.

**Study waters.**
- *Gila Gravity Main Canal*: a 33.3 km earthen canal (completed 1943), about 50 ha in surface area, with a mean current near 0.5 m/s. Fish community: 15 species recorded, dominated by redear sunfish, bluegill, largemouth bass, carp, and channel catfish.
- *Mittry Lake*: a shallow Colorado River backwater of about 300 ha total, with roughly 130 ha of open water. Fish community: 17 species, with carp, bluegill, black crappie, redear sunfish, largemouth bass, and blue tilapia making up 93% of numbers.
- *Redondo Lake*: a 4 ha former borrow pit, treated with rotenone in 1969 and restocked with largemouth bass, bluegill, and redear sunfish. Those three species made up more than 94% of collected fish. Redondo had no naturally reproducing channel catfish population.

**Fish procurement problems.** Supplier problems were significant. The first shipment (July 1983) arrived without disease certification, with about 6% dead in transit, about 10% more lost during tagging, and 13% dead within two weeks at the canal terminus. Symptoms were consistent with *Aeromonas hydrophila* infection. The second shipment (August 1983) also lacked certification, and about 15% died within 10 days. AGFD cancelled the contract. A five-month gap followed while the Colorado River Wildlife Council's [[Channel Catfish Virus]] rule was revised. Later contracts with Arizona farms produced healthier fish, and tagging at the farm nearly eliminated tagging mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Canal benthos:** dominated by Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*), mean density 2115/m² and wet tissue biomass 2503 g/m² at two sites. Crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) were presumed present.
- **Vegetation:** coontail, pondweeds, Chara, and filamentous algae in the canal; coontail and spiny naiad dominated Mittry Lake and Redondo Lake, with naiad occupying 50% or more of Redondo's volume in summer.
- **Temperatures:** surface water ranged from 11–30°C in canal backwaters, 9.5–33.0°C at Mittry Lake, and 12.5–31.0°C at Redondo Lake. Redondo developed a summer thermocline.
- **Hydrology:** canal discharge ranged from about 10 m³/s (December) to about 45 m³/s (spring/early summer). A roughly 2.5-fold increase in Colorado River flow in June 1983 destroyed Mittry Lake's inlet and outlet structures and forced closure from June 26 through October 1983. This flood also led to Hunter's Hole being dropped from the study because of emigration risk.
- **Climate:** Yuma receives a mean annual rainfall of slightly more than 6.4 cm, and the study area lies in Sonoran desertscrub.
- **Other aquatic vertebrates:** bullfrog, soft-shelled turtle, muskrat, and beaver were common.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview.**
- Roving creel survey (stratified by season, day type, and six-hour period) estimated angler use and catch. Voluntary postcard creel surveys served as a comparison.
- Fish sampling used electrofishing, hoop nets, trammel nets, and trotlines. Electrofishing and hoop nets sampled channel catfish poorly; baited trotlines captured the most.
- Condition factor (k) was tracked for marked and wild fish. Tag retention was checked in a golf course pond.

**Tagging and tag retention.**
- Monel butt-end leg tags were applied at up to 600 fish per hour by a crew of four.
- Tag retention regression: Y = −0.0959605(X) + 94.71738688, R² = 0.962. More than 90% of tags were retained during the roughly 60 post-stocking days when most fish were harvested.
- Tag loss was concentrated in larger fish; only 4% of fish at or below 0.68 kg lost tags over seven months.

**Stocking totals (Table 1).**
- Gila Gravity Main Canal: 30,576 fish across 14 plants (first eight initially; reduced density later).
- Mittry Lake: 9,388 fish across six plants (December 1984–September 1985).
- Redondo Lake: 491 fish across three plants.
- Grand total: 40,455 fish.
- Mean condition factor at planting: 0.849 (range 0.811–0.911).

**Angler use.**
- Use was low to moderate. Weekend days drew 2–3 times the pressure of weekdays. Morning and afternoon were the peak periods.
- Arizona residents made up 83.9–95.5% of anglers. Yuma County residents made up 86.8–99.6% of Arizona anglers.
- Bank fishing dominated the canal (98.1%) and Redondo (64.5%). Boat fishing dominated Mittry Lake (61.2%).
- Channel catfish was the preferred species in the canal (68.0% of anglers). Largemouth bass was preferred at Mittry Lake (38.3%) and Redondo (26.0%).
- Completed trips averaged 3.20 hours versus 1.88 hours for incomplete trips (P < 0.05).
- Angler success overall: Gila Canal 36.5%, Mittry Lake 48.2%, Redondo Lake 51.2%.

**Catch and harvest rates.**
- Gila Gravity Canal (individual summaries, all species): 0.28–0.80 fish per hour (catch); 0.24–0.59 (harvest).
- Mittry Lake: 0.33–1.57 fish per hour (catch); 0.20–0.68 (harvest).
- Redondo Lake: rates were unreliable because of small samples.

**Harvest return of stocked fish.**
- Interval method: 3.5–110.9% return for the canal; 10.5–45.5% for Mittry Lake (Stock 6 excluded).
- Mean return at 90 days: 46.1% (canal) and 25.2% (Mittry Lake).
- Regression method (fifth-order polynomials, where slopes were significant): 24.5% return from the first canal stock (23 July 1983); 5.7% from 30 August 1983; 57.5% from 11 July 1984; 42.9% from 24 August 1984; 62.8% from the 30 January 1985 Mittry Lake stock.
- Stock timing mattered: about 50% of harvest occurred within 10–12 days for several plants.

**Cost per fish harvested.**
- Gila Gravity Canal: mean $3.82 per fish (after excluding anomalous stocks), or $6.63/kg.
- Mittry Lake: $8.61 per fish (excluding Stock 6), or $14.79/kg.
- The authors concluded that no long-term net benefit accrued to anglers.

**Effects on wild fish.**
- Post-stocking catch and harvest rates of wild channel catfish fell significantly at the Gila Gravity Canal (P < 0.05 for individual and daily summaries) and at Mittry Lake.
- Overall catch at Mittry Lake dropped significantly after stocking. No significant changes occurred at Redondo Lake, which lacked wild channel catfish.
- Condition (k) of marked channel catfish declined significantly over time in the canal and Mittry Lake but rose after 90 days at Redondo Lake.
- Wild channel catfish and largemouth bass condition did not show clear stocking effects.
- Largemouth bass length-frequency changes at Mittry Lake were attributed to poor 1984 reproduction, not stocking.

**Mechanisms proposed.**
- Disruption of intraspecific social behavior and competition for forage, leading to energy costs and mortality, following literature on salmonids (e.g., [[Brown Trout]] studies).
- Emigration from the canal and Mittry Lake, which are not closed systems; marked fish were caught as far as Palo Verde Weir, about 145 km upstream in the Colorado River.
- Reduced catchability of wild fish was considered less likely, because wild catch did not recover during a five-month period without stocking.

**Management recommendations.**
- Limit catchable-size stocking to waters with no successfully reproducing population, or where angler use exceeds the productive capacity of wild stocks.
- For supplier contracts: require client references, inspect hatcheries and transport before award, require holding of subcontract shipments, and reject lots with high *Aeromonas hydrophila* incidence.

**Voluntary creel survey.**
- 793 cards were returned; 587 were usable (412 Gila Canal, 90 Mittry Lake, 85 Redondo Lake).
- Estimates were within the range of roving-survey rates overall but showed little seasonal agreement, likely because cards were filled out after unusually good or poor trips.
