# Movements and Habitat Use of Young Colorado Squawfish in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Ptychocheilus-lucius #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This study by Harold M. Tyus (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1991) investigated the seasonal and diel movements and habitat use of young [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the Green River, Utah, using seining and fin-clip marking/recapture methods (1979–1981). Of 1,194 young squawfish captured, 922 were fin-clip marked and 234 (~25%) were later recaptured. The vast majority of fish (84.7%) were captured in shallow shoreline [[Backwater Habitat|backwaters]], with peak abundance occurring in April (67.9% of total captures). Fish exhibited mobility among backwaters, eddies, and shoreline runs, including movements across the river channel, and diel abundance patterns in backwaters were strongly tied to water temperature differentials between backwaters and the main channel rather than to light/dark (dawn/dusk) cycles. The findings suggest that recovery and monitoring programs for this [[Endangered Species|endangered]] cyprinid should account for temperature-driven diel variability in backwater catches and should evaluate habitat diversity beyond backwaters alone.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Ptychocheilus lucius* (Colorado squawfish), family Cyprinidae — a large, piscivorous minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Age-0 fish (autumn, October–November)**:
  - October 1979: 30–60 mm TL (n = 49)
  - October 1981: mean 45 mm TL, SD = 7 (n = 151)
  - November 1981: mean 43 mm TL, SD = 8 (n = 21 new captures)
- **Age-1 (yearling) fish (spring, April)**:
  - April 1980: mean 51 mm TL, SD = 8 (n = 511 marked)
  - April 1981: mean 51 mm TL, SD = 11 (n = 206 new captures)
- Marking method: small clips taken from margins of the caudal fin (upper lobe = backwater origin, lower lobe = shoreline origin) and dorsal fin (eddy origin), enabling habitat-of-origin tracking upon recapture.
- Growth/size classes were consistent between age-0 (~43–60 mm TL) and age-1 (~51 mm TL) cohorts sampled across years, indicating a relatively narrow size range for young fish captured in shoreline habitats during the study period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary habitat**: Shallow shoreline embayments ([[Backwater Habitat|backwaters]]) were overwhelmingly preferred, accounting for 84.7% of all captures across the study; other habitats used included shoreline eddies and main-channel shoreline runs.
- **Seasonal pattern**: Backwater use peaked in **April** (67.9% of total captures), coinciding with age-1 fish emergence into warmer shoreline waters; fewer fish were captured in **autumn** (October–November) when temperatures were lower.
- **Diel pattern**: Backwater abundance was positively correlated with backwater temperature and negatively correlated with main-channel temperature differentials:
  - April 1981: catch strongly correlated with backwater temperature (r = 0.91, p = 0.004); fish were nearly absent at dawn (0600–0700 h, 7°C) and most abundant at midday (1100–1500 h, 17°C).
  - October 1981: weaker, non-significant correlation with temperature (r = 0.64, p = 0.17), consistent with smaller diel temperature swings (±6°C vs. ±9°C in April).
  - November 1981: no correlation with temperature (r = 0.04, p = 0.94), likely due to low overall temperatures (backwater 8.6°C; main channel 7.3°C) and reduced fish activity.
- **Movement and habitat connectivity**: Marked fish were recaptured across multiple habitat types (backwater ↔ eddy ↔ shoreline run) and even on the opposite side of the river channel, demonstrating active habitat-switching and an ability to negotiate river currents. Some fish remained in the same general area for up to a month.
- **Community context**: Ten fish species were sympatric with young Colorado squawfish in backwaters. Of 5,305 total fish captured in 1981 backwaters:
  - [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) — 85.6%
  - Colorado squawfish — 7.8%
  - [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) — 5.2%
  - [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — 1.1%
  - Unlike Colorado squawfish, red shiner and fathead minnow abundance was **not** significantly correlated with water temperature (red shiner r < 0.17, p > 0.38; fathead minnow r < 0.34, p > 0.50), suggesting these non-native/mesic-adapted species tolerate a broader thermal range than the native squawfish.

## Key Findings

- **Backwaters are the dominant but not exclusive habitat**: 85% of all young Colorado squawfish captures occurred in backwaters, but substantial movement into eddies and shoreline runs demonstrates a mixed-habitat strategy rather than strict backwater dependency.
- **Recapture rate**: Overall recapture rate was ~25% (234/922 marked fish), with year-specific rates ranging from 20.4% (1979) to 23.7% (April 1980).
- **Temperature, not photoperiod, drives diel backwater use**: Backwater occupancy tracked the thermal differential between backwaters and the main channel (backwaters warmer in afternoon by 3–9°C in spring), rather than dawn/dusk light transitions — challenging the conventional light-dark "zeitgeber" model of diel fish activity (Muller 1978).
- **Cross-channel movement capability**: Recaptures of marked fish on the opposite riverbank from their original marking site confirm that young squawfish (as small as ~45–51 mm TL) can actively negotiate river currents, contradicting assumptions of passive drift-only dispersal at this life stage.
- **Management implications**: Because age-0 backwater catch rates vary with time of day and water temperature, standardized [[Interagency Recovery Program|recovery program]] monitoring protocols (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1987) should incorporate temperature and time-of-day criteria into seining schedules to avoid biased abundance estimates. Habitat management strategies should prioritize a **diversity of shoreline habitats** (backwaters, eddies, runs) rather than backwaters alone.
- **Life-history interpretation**: The species' reliance on movement among thermally variable habitats may reflect a narrower physiological tolerance range compared to more habitat-generalist cyprinids (e.g., red shiner, fathead minnow), consistent with prior findings that Colorado squawfish incorporate substantial movement into their overall life strategy (Tyus 1986).
