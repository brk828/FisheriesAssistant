# Scientific and Technical Advisory Committee Meeting on the State of America's Rivers

#freshwater-ecology #river-conservation #biodiversity #endangered-species #riparian-habitat #dam-impacts #flow-regime #watershed-management

## Executive Summary

This document is a memorandum and agenda for the American Rivers Scientific and Technical Advisory Committee (STAC) annual meeting and "State of America's Rivers" Forum held June 3–4, 1993, in Washington, DC. The forum brought together leading river scientists and natural resource policy analysts to assess the condition of America's rivers, identify threats, and propose science-based principles for river system protection and restoration. The attached issue paper, "Endangered Rivers of America," argues that despite improvements in point-source pollution control, the overall ecological health of rivers is declining at an alarming rate due to incremental development impacts—dams, diversions, channelization, land clearing, and land development. The paper emphasizes the critical ecological roles of rivers, documents widespread declines in aquatic biodiversity, and proposes a three-part conservation strategy: protecting headwaters, restoring riparian vegetation, and restoring natural flow regimes.

## Background & Description

### Meeting Context and Participants

The STAC meeting and forum were convened by [[American Rivers]] in conjunction with the organization's annual Board of Directors meeting. The forum was co-sponsored by the [[National Park Service]]. Attendees included members of the STAC (a panel of leading river scientists and policy analysts), American Rivers board members and staff, selected press, Congressional staff, and public agency officials. The forum aimed to inform national policy debates on the [[National Biological Survey]], [[Clean Water Act]] reauthorization, [[Endangered Species Act]] reauthorization, and a proposed national "State of the Rivers" study.

### Ecological Roles of Rivers

The issue paper emphasizes that rivers are dynamic natural systems with critical ecological functions:

1. **Biodiversity Corridors**: Rivers serve as highways for migratory birds, fish, and other organisms, connecting diverse physiographic regions. For example, the [[New River]] in West Virginia traverses the Appalachian Plateau, connecting the North Carolina Piedmont with the Ohio Valley, and harbors plant and animal life found nowhere else in the state.

2. **Nutrient Transport**: Rivers transport water, sediment, and nutrients from land to sea, building deltas and beaches, regulating salinity and fertility of estuaries and coastal zones.

3. **Habitat Diversity**: Rivers exhibit unique patterns of biological diversity among taxonomic groups and regions. Ecologist [[Eugene Odum]] notes that rivers increase in diversity as they flow from higher elevations to their mouths, where different communities mix.

4. **Aquatic Invertebrate Richness**: Dr. David Allan points out that the average stream has ten times more aquatic invertebrate species than fish species. Streams thought to be species-poor have yielded hundreds of previously unknown invertebrates upon close examination.

5. **Hyporheic Zone**: All rivers have an underlying ecosystem—the [[hyporheic zone]]—comprised of water and alluvial gravel extending under and out from the water course, sometimes for miles. This zone has unique inhabitants (insect larvae and invertebrates) that form the beginnings of the food chain in some systems.

6. **Riparian Habitat**: The near-stream environment (riparian zones) provides food and shelter for many wildlife species. Fully 80% of western wildlife species rely on riparian areas and rivers for habitat and sustenance.

7. **Life Support**: All assimilation of nutrients by living organisms takes place in a water medium derived from flowing freshwater systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Evidence of Decline

A consensus among scientists interviewed for a New York Times Science Times article ("River Life Through the U.S. Broadly Degraded") concluded that incremental developments and wide-ranging environmental problems are causing serious decline in overall river system health. Specific indicators include:

- **Riparian Forest Loss**: Riparian forests have been reduced by 66% nationwide. In the arid West, Midwest, and lower Mississippi Valley, riparian vegetation has been reduced by more than 88%.

- **Aquatic Species Imperilment**: 
  - 34% of North American fish species are classified as rare to extinct
  - 65% of crayfishes are rare to extinct
  - 73% of unionid mussels are rare to extinct
  - 20% of native fishes of the Western United States are extinct or endangered

- **Regional Declines**:
  - Since 1933, 20% of mollusks (clams and relatives) in the [[Tennessee River]] system have been lost, and 45% of remaining species are endangered or seriously depleted
  - Since 1910, salmon runs on the [[Columbia River]] have declined by 75–85%. Over 200 wild Pacific salmon and steelhead stocks are currently at risk of extinction

Studies by [[The Nature Conservancy]] and the Endangered Species Committee of the [[American Fisheries Society]] concluded that aquatic fauna are much more imperiled than terrestrial fauna.

### Threats to River Systems

#### Dams

Dams are a major cause of river ecosystem decline:

1. **Nutrient Blockage**: Dams stop the natural flow of biological and chemical nutrients to downstream areas. Suspended solids drop out in pooled water, analogous to removing cells from blood plasma.

2. **Oxygen Depletion**: Dams deplete dissolved oxygen in water, reducing its capacity to support life.

3. **Sediment Retention**: Dams halt delta-building, wetland-building, and stream bank-building functions of rivers and prevent downstream soil deposition.

4. **Flow Fluctuations**: Dams hold back water (de-watering downstream stretches) and then release high volumes for peak power production. Such fluctuations cause downstream aquatic habitat to be dried out at times and scoured/eroded at others. Temperature changes from flow fluctuations often exceed the tolerance of aquatic species.

5. **Hydropower Operations**: Most hydropower dams hold back water during certain times of day to maximize power output during "peak" power use times. Dams also divert water from rivers through conduits to increase velocity and power production capacity. When water is removed or flows unnaturally, the entire aquatic and riparian environment is dramatically altered and ecological balance is disrupted.

#### Other Threats

- **Channelization**: Alters natural stream morphology and habitat
- **Streamside Development and Runoff**: Degrades water quality and destroys sensitive habitat
- **Flow Depletion**: Natural flows have been diminished by dams, diversions, and ditching

### Federal Water Management

The federal government, through the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC), [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Army Corps of Engineers]], and [[Soil Conservation Service]], manages 40% of the water resources of the West and manipulates the natural flow of hundreds of rivers in the East. This presents both a challenge and an opportunity for flow regime restoration, particularly through hydropower relicensing.

## Key Findings

### Proposed Three-Part Conservation Strategy

The issue paper proposes a "middle ground" approach to river conservation that could accomplish watershed protection without fully regulating every square inch of a watershed:

1. **Protect Headwater Areas**: Headwater areas (upstream tributaries and springs) must remain intact if downstream rivers are to be saved. A protected headwaters region is like a trust fund that sends proceeds in the form of clean water and nutrients to impoverished, over-developed downstream areas.

2. **Restore Riparian Vegetative Communities**: The "near stream" environment (trees, grasses, soils, wetlands, flood plains) has central importance to sustained river system health. These narrow strips:
   - Filter out runoff pollutants
   - Cool water temperatures
   - Provide wildlife and aquatic species habitat
   - Preserve natural beauty

3. **Restore Appropriate Flow Regimes**: Protecting and restoring flow regimes that will sustain river life over the long term is vitally important. The effect of dams on flow regimes and how changes in flow affect river diversity are not well understood by the public. Natural flows must be sustained at all times for a river to remain healthy.

### River Conservation as Ecosystem Approach

River conservation strategy can help develop ecosystem approaches to natural resource conservation. Because rivers traverse many diverse areas, river conservation can:

- Bridge fragmentation in the natural resource field (separating park policy from refuge policy from forest policy)
- Bring together diverse scientific disciplines and political constituencies
- Provide opportunities for interagency and intergovernmental cooperation

**Example**: The Northwest Ancient Forest "summit" led by President Clinton was initially framed as "Spotted Owl protection" by the forest protection community, allowing the timber industry to cast it as "jobs versus owls." Had a more integrated, watershed-based approach been used emphasizing that timber cutting destroys [[salmon]] habitat, the issue might have been cast as "jobs versus jobs."

### Forum Agenda Topics

The forum addressed five key questions:

1. **What role do rivers play in overall ecosystem and human health?** (As corridors for biological movement, preservers of biological diversity, nourishers of wetlands/estuaries/lakes/coastal regions, sources of water supply)

2. **Are river systems declining?** (Ecosystem function, water quality)

3. **What are the greatest threats to rivers and how do they cause degradation?** (Dams and diversions, channelization, dumping and non-point pollution)

4. **Where are the greatest needs for river science and data?** (Ecosystems, economics, legal reforms, social impacts)

5. **What are the critical elements of river protection and restoration from a scientific standpoint?** (Headwaters and watershed, near-stream environment, flow regimes, water quality)

### Data on American Rivers Conservation Success

When American Rivers correlated its work on protection of national forest and public land rivers with state natural heritage programs, they found a higher than 75% correlation on a per-river basis and several hundred biological elements within this 75%. In 1987, on the 75 river segments American Rivers succeeded in protecting, there were 400 identified biological elements.
