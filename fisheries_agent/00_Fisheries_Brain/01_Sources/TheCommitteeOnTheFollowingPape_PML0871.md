# The Committee on the following papers by Mr. Girard, reported in favor of publication

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of three items from the early *Proceedings* of the [[Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia]], written by [[Charles Girard]] and, in part, [[Spencer F. Baird]]. The first is a taxonomic essay on the North American pikes and pickerels of the genus *Esox*, which proposes a split into two groups based on cheek and opercular scalation. The second is a short natural-history note on a nest built by a catfish and the young it guarded. The third is a set of formal descriptions of new fish species collected during the [[United States and Mexican Boundary Survey]] in Texas and the Gila region, and during expeditions in Arkansas. Committee approval for publication is recorded for each item.

## Background & Description

**Esox taxonomy.** The paper notes that North American *Esox* species were called "pike" or "pickerel" without consistent distinction. Fishermen said the terms referred to size, with large individuals called pike and small ones pickerel. Girard argued that scalation provides a reliable diagnostic character:

- **Naked cheeks and opercles (smooth skin):** *Esox estor* (Lesueur) and related forms, including the maskallonge (*Esox nobilior* Thompson) and the Lake Superior pike.
- **Scaled cheeks and opercles:** *Esox reticulatus* (Lesueur), *Esox americanus* (Lacépède, also cited as *E. niger*), and related species.

He proposed retaining "pike" for the naked-cheeked group and "pickerel" for the scaled-cheeked group. He also observed that *E. reticulatus* is the largest pickerel known, and that *E. nobilior* probably reaches a larger maximum size.

**Catfish nest.** Girard described a nest found in a small pond above Schuylkill Falls, Pennsylvania. It was a subspherical mass of green filamentous algae (conferva), with an opening for entry and exit.

**New species descriptions.** Baird and Girard described new species from specimens collected by [[John H. Clark]] on the Boundary Survey (Texas, Mexico border region) and by [[Randolph B. Marcy]] and [[George B. McClellan]] in Arkansas. Descriptions use meristic formulas (fin ray counts), body proportions, and colour pattern. Names appear in the source in garbled OCR form, and only the corrected genera are given here with confidence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic pattern:** Girard linked the two *Esox* groups to distribution. Pikes were confined to the Great Lakes and western waters, while pickerels occurred in ponds and rivers draining to the Atlantic. He noted that an introduced pike in the [[Connecticut River]] had multiplied without contradicting this pattern. A reported pickerel from the [[Ohio River]] was an unverified possible exception.
- **Nest habitat:** The catfish nest was located about 1.5 feet deep, among shoreline aquatic plants, in water with little violent motion. Girard suggested that nests in flowing water would need more durable materials.
- **Brackish and spring habitats:** Several new species were collected in brackish waters near Indianola, Texas, and in springs and tributaries of the Rio Grande, Rio Nueces, and Gila systems.
- **Parental behaviour:** The catfish young remained congregated around the nest under parental care, and were fed by the parent.

## Key Findings

**1. Two scalation-based groups of *Esox*.** Girard proposed a formal distinction between pikes (naked cheeks and opercles) and pickerels (scaled cheeks and opercles), and recommended that the terms be applied accordingly. Specimens of the Lesueur species that could not be identified were to be checked for this character.

**2. Distribution correlates with the morphological split.** Pikes were associated with the Great Lakes and western waters, and pickerels with Atlantic drainages. The Ohio River record remained unconfirmed.

**3. Catfish nest and brood care.** The nest contained about 300 to 400 young (described as "at least from three to four hundred") of different sizes. Girard inferred that eggs were deposited and fertilised at different times. The nest measured about 8 inches by 6 inches, and the young showed distended abdomens from gorging on food. Girard called for further observation and noted that the material was soft and adapted to still water.

**4. New freshwater species described (Boundary Survey).** Sixteen species were described from Texas and the Gila region, including:
- *Pimephales*-like and *Notropis*-like minnows (names garbled in OCR)
- ***Pomoxis*** species from Eagle Pass, Texas, and the Rio Salado (described as *Pomoxis* spp.)
- ***Cyprinodon*** species, including *Cyprinodon elegans* (Rio Grande del Norte), *C. macularius* (Rio Gila), and *C. nevadensis*-group forms
- ***Fundulus*** species, including *Fundulus grandis* (brackish waters near Indianola, Texas), *F. tenuis* (Prairie Mer Rouge, Louisiana, and Russellville, Kentucky)
- Additional taxa from Indianola, Leona Springs, Medina and Salado rivers, and the Rio Santa Cruz (Gila)

Each description includes a fin formula. Example: *Cyprinodon elegans* was given as D.10, A.11, and P.12 (fin ray counts as printed).

**5. New freshwater species described (Arkansas).** Six species were described from Otter Creek, Arkansas, including two *Pomoxis* forms, two *Lepisosteus*-like forms (garbled OCR), and a *Chrosomus*-like minnow. Descriptions cover body shape, scale counts, and colour pattern. The source notes that colour in preserved specimens may have been altered by alcohol.

**6. Methodological note.** Girard emphasised that detailed morphological features, especially scalation, are more reliable than size or local common names for identifying *Esox* species. He called for further study of the Ohio River pickerel and the unidentified *Esox* species described by Lesueur.
