# Rare and Endangered Vertebrates and Plants of Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #vertebrate-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1975 report, prepared by the [[Rare and Endangered Species of Oklahoma Committee]] with assistance from the [[Soil Conservation Service]] (U.S. Department of Agriculture), is the first state-level inventory of rare and endangered vertebrates and plants in [[Oklahoma]]. It is meant to inform public servants in land and water management and to supplement U.S. federal lists from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. It covers fishes, mammals, reptiles, amphibians, birds, woody plants and herbaceous plants, and assigns each taxon to one of four status categories. The authors treat Oklahoma as an island: any species at risk within the state is listed, regardless of its status in neighboring states.

Of 167 fish species known from Oklahoma, 34 are considered endangered or rare. None of these 34 appear on the federal threatened list. The committee states that the lists are the first of their kind, probably incomplete, and due for revision within a few years.

## Background & Description

The report is organized into six sections: fishes, mammals/reptiles/amphibians, birds, trees/shrubs/vines/other woody plants, and herbaceous plants. Preface maps show Oklahoma counties and the [[Land Resource Areas]] used in soil conservation planning, such as the [[Ozark Highland]], [[Ouachita Mountains]], [[Cross Timbers]] and [[Bluestem Hills]].

**Status definitions used by the committee:**
- **Endangered:** threatened with extinction through habitat loss, overutilization, disease, predation or other factors; survival unlikely without special protective measures.
- **Rare-1:** not presently threatened, but in such small numbers it may become endangered if the environment worsens.
- **Rare-2:** may be quite abundant where it occurs, but known from few localities or a restricted habitat within the state.
- **Status Undetermined:** suspected to be rare or endangered, but more information is needed.

**Scope notes:** Insects were excluded because their distribution and population data are not yet sufficient. Birds known only as migrants, accidental visitors or rare visitors were excluded, so the bird list covers only species that nest or winter in Oklahoma. Scientific names for fishes follow [[Bailey et al. 1970]] and for birds the [[American Ornithologists' Union]] checklist. Woody plants follow [[Williams 1974]], and herbaceous plant nomenclature follows [[Waterfall 1969]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish section cites [[Miller 1972]] for the main threats to freshwater fauna: industrial, agricultural and domestic pollution (including toxic chemicals and pesticides), dam construction, deforestation and overgrazing, channelization, excessive groundwater removal and introduction of exotic species. The report says many of these are active in Oklahoma, "sometimes with disastrous results."

Recurring threats across taxa include:
- **Dams and impoundments:** reduce current velocity, increase siltation, and block spawning migrations (e.g., [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]], [[Mooneye]], [[Blue Sucker]]).
- **Siltation and turbidity:** affect clear-water species such as the [[Bigeye Chub]] and [[Crystal Darter]].
- **Drainage and channelization:** of swamps, oxbow lakes and bottomlands in [[McCurtain County]] threatens several fishes, amphibians and plants.
- **Pesticides:** a large fish kill in Lee's Creek followed spraying, according to the fish section.
- **Human disturbance:** of caves and roosts (bats), and of abandoned buildings used by bats.
- **Poisoning and control campaigns:** against prairie dogs, coyotes and other rodents, which affect predators such as the [[Black-footed Ferret]] and [[Swift Fox]].
- **Habitat clearing:** of woodlands, mesquite and bottomland for agriculture.

Many listed species sit at the edge of their range in Oklahoma, which the authors repeatedly note as a reason for rarity.

## Key Findings

### Fishes (Robison, Moore and Miller)
- **Endangered (6 species):** [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (Arkansas and Red River systems, threatened by dams), [[Ironcolor Shiner]] (only known Oklahoma records from the Mountain Fork River), [[Arkansas Darter]] (spring-fed streams with watercress, Neosho drainage; populations lost to Grand Lake), [[Scaleyhead Darter]] (one of the rarest Oklahoma fishes), [[Leopard Darter]] (Little River system, with a wider known range than previously believed), and [[Chain Pickerel]] (Pushmataha County and Lake Texoma).
- **Cavefishes:** [[Ozark Cavefish]] (23 known specimens, all from northeastern Oklahoma) and [[Southern Cavefish]] (a few specimens; one from Cave Spring, Ottawa County).
- **Rare-1 (examples):** [[Alabama Shad]], [[Mooneye]], [[Bigeye Chub]], [[Pallid Shiner]], [[Blackspot Shiner]], [[Mountain Madtom]], [[Crystal Darter]] (27 new specimens collected in the Little River, but still rare), and others.
- **Rare-2 (examples):** [[Highfin Carpsucker]], [[Blue Sucker]], [[Neosho Madtom]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Plains Topminnow]], and [[Least Darter]].
- **Status Undetermined:** [[Goldeye]] and [[Flathead Chub]].
- The authors suggest further recent collections in the Kiamichi basin could change the status of several species.

### Mammals, Reptiles and Amphibians (Glass)
- **Endangered mammals:** [[Gray Myotis]] (nursery and hibernation colonies very vulnerable), [[Indiana Myotis]] (Oklahoma is at the edge of its range; 90% of the population hibernates in four Kentucky caves), [[Swamp Rabbit]], [[Black-footed Ferret]] (probably extinct in Oklahoma after intensive surveys failed to find it), [[River Otter]] (first record in many years, captured February 1975 near Spiro), and [[Cougar]] (most recent record in 1968 near Lake Eufaula).
- **Endangered reptile:** [[American Alligator]] (threatened in the Red Book, protected by law).
- **Rare-1:** [[Prairie Vole]], [[Desert Shrew]], [[Townsend's Big-eared Bat]], [[Scarlet Snake]], [[Ringed Salamander]], [[Four-toed Salamander]] and [[Oklahoma Dwarf Salamander]].
- **Rare-2:** About 40 taxa, including [[Eastern Harvest Mouse]], [[Golden Mouse]], [[Texas Kangaroo Rat]], [[Mule Deer]], [[Mud Snake]], [[Ozark Blind Salamander]] and [[Green Tree Frog]].
- **Status Undetermined:** the [[Southeastern Shrew]], [[Seminole Bat]], and the regina snake (Regina rigida).

### Birds (Lewis)
- **Endangered (6 species):** [[Prairie Falcon]] (Cimarron County mesas), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (uncommon winter visitor), [[Swallow-tailed Kite]] (last sighted September 1970), [[Red-cockaded Woodpecker]] (nests in shortleaf pines of southeastern Oklahoma), [[Southern Bald Eagle]] (last reported nesting attempt in 1950, Wagoner County), and [[Anhinga]] (nested in McCurtain and Sequoyah Counties).
- **Rare-1:** [[Golden Eagle]] and [[Verdin]].
- **Rare-2:** About 20 species, many of them western Black Mesa residents (Cimarron County). Also [[Sandhill Crane]], which winters in southern Jackson County and sometimes numbers 3,000 to 4,000 birds.
- **Status Undetermined:** the [[Least Tern]], whose recent observations suggest rapid decline.

### Woody Plants (Smola and Teate)
- **Rare-1 trees:** [[Yellowwood]], [[Smoketree]], [[Umbrella Tree]], [[Blue Ash]]; shrub [[Chinquapin]].
- **Rare-2:** roughly 60 trees, shrubs and vines. Notable examples: [[Water Hickory]], [[Nutmeg Hickory]], [[American Beech]], [[Bald Cypress]] (the largest tree in Oklahoma, 7 miles east of Broken Bow), [[Live Oak]], and [[Ponderosa Pine]] (extreme west Cimarron County).
- **Status Undetermined:** [[Shingle Oak]], [[Swamp Privet]] and [[Carolina Holly]], among others.

### Herbaceous Plants (Crockett, Snook and Tyrl)
- The report is described as "a first attempt" at listing plants whose numbers are declining or so few that habitat change could eliminate them.
- **Rare-1:** Grasses, sedges, orchids, and forbs such as [[American Ginseng]], [[Goldenseal]], and [[Ozark Violet]]. Several entries are marked with an asterisk (*) because evidence is lacking; some are "reported to be in Oklahoma, location unknown."
- **Rare-2:** A very long list of ferns, grasses, sedges, lilies, orchids, cacti, legumes and composites, many with only one or two known county records.

### Recommendations and Conclusions
- Recognition of threatened status is "a primary step" toward recovery; neglect may lead to extinction.
- The lists should be revised within a few years, as field investigations clarify status.
- Anyone with data or questions should contact the [[State Resource Conservationist]] at the Soil Conservation Service, Stillwater.
