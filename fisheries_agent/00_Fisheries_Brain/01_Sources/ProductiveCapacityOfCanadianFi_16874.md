# Productive Capacity of Canadian Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries Research Board of Canada Circular, compiled by W. E. Ricker (June 1962), reproduces the full background paper prepared for the [[Resources for Tomorrow]] Conference (Montreal, October 1961). It estimates the future sustainable Canadian landings of commercially important Atlantic, Pacific, Arctic and freshwater fisheries, with most projections set for about 1980. Its central argument is that the yield potential of a stock, not its absolute size, governs what a fishery can deliver, and that many fisheries face a choice between overfishing, underfishing and foreign competition. Catch figures are given in millions of pounds, mostly round fresh weight, and projections are explicitly conditional on assumed demand, climate and international competition.

## Background & Description

The paper begins with general principles of [[Fishery Yield Potential]]. Annual yields of fish stocks range from about 5% to possibly 200% of stock weight, depending mainly on growth rate and age at maturity. Canadian cool-temperate and subarctic stocks usually yield 50% a year or less. For example, commercial-size [[Lake Whitefish]] in cool [[Great Slave Lake]] grow 10–12% a year, versus 30–40% in warmer [[Lake Erie]]. The author stresses that stock size is a misleading guide: heavily fished stocks decline, and early exploitation of accumulated old fish produces catches that cannot be sustained.

The estimates draw on [[Fisheries Statistics of Canada]], provincial annual reports and international commission bulletins from [[ICNAF]] (International Commission for the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries), the [[International Pacific Halibut Commission]] and the [[International Pacific Salmon Fisheries Commission]]. Regional coverage includes Atlantic fisheries (north and south regions), Pacific fisheries ([[British Columbia]]), [[Arctic Fisheries]] and [[Freshwater Fishes]] of the Canadian interior.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several environmental and anthropogenic factors shape the projections:

- **Climate:** Warming permits warm-water species to expand and pushes cold-water species northward. Some Atlantic cooling since the unusually warm 1950s was noted, which would benefit cod and salmon while reducing visits by tuna and swordfish. Pacific temperatures had risen slightly over the previous decade after falling sharply from 1940 to 1950.
- **Migration barriers and habitat change:** Anadromous fishes are most vulnerable to dams and obstructions, such as the [[Hell's Gate]] slides of 1913–14 that devastated [[Fraser River Sockeye]] runs. Logging, pollution and water diversion reduce coho and other salmon habitat.
- **Beneficial interventions:** Fishways, spawning-ground improvement, artificial propagation, predator control and controlled sewage fertilization can increase stocks.
- **Biological interactions:** [[Sea Lamprey]] invasion sharply reduced Great Lakes [[Lake Trout]], and a disease affected oyster stocks in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. Foreign trawling also bycatches halibut.
- **Management tools:** Closures, size limits, quotas, licensing and property leases are used to achieve maximum sustained yield where economics alone would not.

## Key Findings

**Management principles**
- Underfished stocks: more fishing raises sustained yield while lowering stock size.
- Overfished stocks: less fishing raises sustained yield and stock size.
- Economic limits and short-term catch gains can obscure long-term equilibrium effects.

**Atlantic fisheries**
- [[Atlantic Cod]]: Northern region catches fell from 626 million lb (Canada, 1946–50) to 252 million lb (Canada, 1960 preliminary). Foreign vessels take over half the Newfoundland catch. Projected Canadian landings are 600 million lb in the northern region and 250 million lb in the southern region, about 850 million lb total. Southern stocks are fished at about 40% a year, and the 1959 total of over 600 million lb exceeded sustainable levels.
- [[Haddock]]: Projected total of 120 million lb (northern 40, southern 80 million lb), dependent on year-class strength and temperature.
- [[Redfish]]: Northern stocks are in an early-depletion phase. Sustained yield may fall to about 100 million lb, and Canada is unlikely to take much. Southern projected yield is 50 million lb, of which about 25 million lb goes to Canada.
- [[Atlantic Herring]]: Current exploitation is only 5–7% of stock per year. Projected total of 350 million lb, with growth in offshore human-food fisheries.
- [[Atlantic Mackerel]]: Projected 40 million lb, limited by rapid fat oxidation after capture.
- [[Swordfish]] and [[Bluefin Tuna]]: Swordfish landings peaked at 6.7 million lb in 1959; future estimate 10 million lb. Tuna is projected at 0.5–1 million lb.
- [[Atlantic Salmon]]: Recent landings were below average due to natural conditions and DDT forest spraying. Future expectation is 6–7 million lb by net and at least 100,000 fish by angling.
- [[American Lobster]]: Fishery is very intensive, taking about two-thirds of legal-sized stock annually. Projected 48 million lb, with a warning that cooling will limit gains.
- [[Giant Scallop]]: Landings rose from 0.8 million lb of meats (1946–50) to 7.9 million lb (1960). Projected 8 million lb, mainly from [[Georges Bank]], with fluctuations expected.
- [[Harp Seal]]: Combined Canadian and Norwegian kills greatly exceed sustainable yield, and the stock is dwindling. The paper warns of commercial extinction within 10 years without a ban on adult harvest. A 1980 target of 100,000 pups is proposed, with Canada taking 50,000.
- [[Small Whales]] and [[Large Whales]]: Pilot whale future yield is 5,000 a year; minke whale up to 1,000. Sustained large-whale yield of at least 500 a year is considered possible in the northwest Atlantic. Blue and humpback whales are protected.

**Pacific fisheries**
- [[Sockeye Salmon]]: Projected Fraser River yield may reach 40–65 million lb (Canada's share of the system could reach 67 million lb under the largest estimate). Total sustained yield of Fraser sockeye is estimated at 80–134 million lb, with Shuswap Lake as the main production basis. Japanese high-seas netting and treaty expiry in 1992 are noted as threats.
- [[Pink Salmon]]: Projected sustained yield of 100 million lb by 1980, with a possible increase through off-year runs. Earlier Hell's Gate obstruction eliminated upriver runs.
- [[Chum Salmon]]: Projected average of 80 million lb, about 20 million lb above the 1946–54 average.
- [[Coho Salmon]]: Projected 30 million lb commercial and 2 million lb sport catch. Dams and DDT are major threats.
- [[Chinook Salmon]]: Projected 15 million lb commercial and 2 million lb sport catch. Columbia River stocks have declined steeply.
- [[Pacific Herring]]: Largest British Columbia fishery by tonnage. Utilization is roughly 50–55% of mature southern stocks per year. Projected 520 million lb total.
- [[Pacific Halibut]]: Managed by a catch-limit treaty since 1932. Projected Area 2 yield is 25 million lb (Canada 20 million) and Area 3 is 40 million lb (Canada 18 million).
- [[Pacific Cod]] and other round groundfish: Modest increases are projected. Dogfish potential is 50 million lb but awaits demand.
- [[Pacific Crabs and Shrimps]]: Projected crab yield of 6 million lb and shrimp yield of 2.5 million lb.
- [[Pacific Oysters]] and clams: Projected Pacific oyster yield of 300,000 US gallons of meat by 1980.
- [[Whales (Pacific)]]: Projected equilibrium catch of about 800 whales, provided only the existing shore station operates.
- [[Fur Seals]], [[Sea Otters]] and [[Sea Lions]]: Canada receives about 13,000 fur-seal skins a year. Sea lion sustained yield is about 1,000 animals a year.

**Arctic fisheries**
- [[Arctic Char]]: Projected sustainable yield of 500,000 to 1 million lb for the whole Arctic.
- Western Arctic marine fishes may support at least 1 million lb a year locally.
- Subsistence harvests include about 800–1,200 walrus a year (eastern Arctic), ringed seals possibly harvestable at 200,000 a year, and belugas with local takes of 250–700 at Churchill.
- [[Bowhead Whale]] commercial exploitation is prohibited by international agreement and is not expected to resume by 1980.

**Freshwater fisheries**
- [[Lake Whitefish]]: Landings of about 24–25 million lb a year. Projected 36 million lb, mostly from opening northern lakes.
- [[Lake Trout]]: Projected 6.4 million lb. Great Lakes stocks are very depleted by sea lamprey, and recovery by 1980 is unlikely.
- [[Walleye]] (yellow pickerel): Projected 10 million lb from Ontario and 8 million lb from Manitoba under the combined total of 12 million lb projection. Great Lakes catches are intensively fished.
- [[Sauger]] and [[Blue Pickerel]]: Blue pike collapsed from a record 12 million lb (1956) to about 5,000 lb (1960).
- [[Yellow Perch]]: Lake Erie catches rose sharply in the mid-1950s. Projected 11.4 million lb with cautious optimism.
- [[Northern Pike]]: Projected 12 million lb.
- [[Ciscoes]]: Projected 10 million lb a year from the Great Lakes, with possible parasite constraints on prairie lakes.
- [[Lake Sturgeon]]: Projected 415,000 lb, using large-mesh gill nets. Caviar is a valuable by-product.
- Other species: [[Smelt]] and [[White Bass]] are major Lake Erie fisheries, and [[Common Carp]] is expected to remain largely an Ontario fishery.

**Unused or potential resources**
- Dogfish, skates, sharks, rock crabs, shrimp, sand lance and billfish offer possible yields but are currently unutilized.
- Introduction of Pacific salmon (pink, sockeye, chum) into Atlantic waters is recommended. A pink salmon experiment was underway in Newfoundland.
