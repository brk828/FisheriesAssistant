## Executive Summary

This 1993 annual report describes a Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program study (Project #28) investigating the role of **nonnative predatory fish** in the decline of endangered native Colorado River fishes, specifically at the confluence of the [[Green River]] and [[Duchesne River]] in Utah. The site was selected because it contains prime juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically "squawfish") nursery habitat, yet supports very low natural pikeminnow abundance despite high densities of nonnative predators — suggesting a negative correlation between exotic predator abundance and native juvenile recruitment. The study employed novel **flexible cage exclusion/enclosure experiments** in backwater eddies to quantify size- and species-specific predation risk to young [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and to test the feasibility of predator-exclusion structures as a low-cost recovery tool. Preliminary results identify **smallmouth bass** and **channel catfish** as the most significant predatory threats to juvenile pikeminnow.

## Background & Description

Limited direct morphological data are reported; the study is predation-ecology focused. Notable size-related parameters include:

- **Juvenile Colorado Pikeminnow (squawfish)**: experimental subjects ranged from **25–40 mm total length (TL)**, representing young-of-year (YOY) life stage.
- **Predator size classes**: Study explicitly investigates a **body-size gradient among nonnative predators**, ranging from small-bodied [[Green Sunfish]] to large-bodied [[Channel Catfish]].
- **Mesh-size exclusion gradient** (used as a proxy for predator body size classification):
  - 20 mm mesh — excludes all fish, retains only invertebrates (control/baseline).
  - 60 mm mesh — excludes largest smallmouth bass and most channel catfish.
  - 100 mm mesh — excludes only the largest catfish.
- This gradient implies that **gape size and body girth**, not just length, govern predator exclusion effectiveness, since juvenile pikeminnow could freely pass through the two larger mesh sizes and predators of intermediate size could potentially still enter smaller mesh classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study site**: Backwater eddies at the Green–Duchesne confluence, identified as critical **nursery habitat** for juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and other native [[Colorado River Fishes|Colorado River endemic fishes]].
- **Habitat dynamics**: Backwaters become increasingly isolated from main channel flow in late summer/fall, with reduced flow, higher temperatures, and altered structure — raising concerns about both **predation pressure** and **abiotic stress** (e.g., thermal stress) independent of predation.
- **Flow regime context**: Stabilized, lower summer/fall flows from upstream reservoir releases (over the following five years) were expected to create more predictable hydrologic conditions, improving feasibility of long-term cage-based exclusion experiments.
- **Community composition (Ouray nursery habitat section)**: Dominant nonnative species identified via density surveys:
  - [[Red Shiner]] (Cyprinella lutrensis)
  - [[Channel Catfish]] (Ictalurus punctatus)
  - [[Green Sunfish]] (Lepomis cyanellus)
  - [[Smallmouth Bass]] / other black bass
  - Lower-density nonnatives: [[Crappie]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Fathead Minnow]]
- **Predator-prey interaction**: A qualitative **negative correlation** was observed between nonnative predator abundance at the confluence site and juvenile pikeminnow abundance, relative to upstream sites with fewer predators — supporting the hypothesis that nonnative predation, compounded by dam-altered flow/temperature regimes, is a major driver of native fish decline (citing [[Joseph et al. 1977]] and [[Tyus et al. 1982]]).

## Key Findings

- **Highest predation risk**: [[Smallmouth Bass]] (all size classes) posed the greatest threat to juvenile pikeminnow, with individuals consuming **up to 30 squawfish per day** in cage trials.
- **Secondary predation risk**: [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] also showed substantial predation potential, with individuals consuming **up to 16 squawfish per day**.
- **Lower but notable risk**: [[Red Shiner]] exhibited surprisingly low predation rates (**<5 squawfish/day**) but may still be ecologically important at the very smallest pikeminnow life stages (newly hatched/first-feeding larvae).
- **Methodology validated**: Flexible PVC-frame, nylon-mesh cage systems (~5 m dimensions, buoyed with styrofoam, anchored with bungee cord) proved operationally viable under variable flow and sediment conditions, adapted from methods used previously by co-PI Todd Crowl in New Zealand and Australian rivers at flows up to 2,000 cfs.
- **Management implication**: Because catfish and smallmouth bass show the highest predation potential while red shiners primarily target only the smallest size classes, **maintenance of shallow backwater refuge habitats** may provide a viable, cost-effective strategy for protecting young pikeminnow from the largest-bodied, most damaging predators.
- **Study status**: Project on track; FY93 budget of $20,500 fully expended with no discrepancies; study designed to continue through 1994 with expanded objectives on seasonal predation dynamics and size-selective predator removal (via gill nets, trawling, electrofishing, or chemical treatment) in future years.
- **Broader significance**: Findings contribute directly to the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Program]]'s effort to evaluate whether **size-specific predator exclusion structures** are a feasible, low-cost recovery tool for endangered Colorado River Basin fishes, including [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Humpback Chub]].
