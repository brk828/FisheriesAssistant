# The Nutrition of Trout: Cortland Hatchery Report No. 14 (1945)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries Research Bulletin (No. 7) reports the 1945 work of the [[Cortland Hatchery]], operated jointly by the [[New York Conservation Department]], the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[Cornell University]]. The study covers four areas: vitamin requirements (riboflavin, pantothenic acid and thiamin) of [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]; the physiological effects of dietary and bath-applied [[Sodium Chloride]] on brook trout; the fate of ingested [[Glucose]] and its interaction with [[Insulin]]; and practical diet trials plus [[Paprika Coloration]] of trout flesh and fins. Requirements were defined as the dietary level producing maximum vitamin storage in the liver, a method the authors openly describe as imprecise.

## Background & Description

The document is a hatchery research report (originally Cortland Hatchery Report No. 14) by A. M. Phillips, A. V. Tunison, H. B. Shaffer, G. K. White, M. W. Sullivan, Curtis Vincent, D. R. Brockway and C. M. McCay. Dr. McCay was on military leave from Cornell with the US Navy, and Mr. Brockway was serving in the armed forces. Experiments used small troughs and aerated aquaria, with water held at 47°F unless stated otherwise (52°F for one thiamin series and 50°F for salt-bath trials).

Assays followed established methods: the [[Lactobacillus Casei Assay]] for riboflavin and pantothenic acid, and a [[Phycomyces Blakesleeanus]] fungal growth method for thiamin. Results were statistically compared using analysis of variance and least-difference tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report stresses that trout are freshwater salmonids reared under hatchery conditions, not wild populations. Several findings concern the aquatic environment directly:

- Crystalline vitamins added to diets were largely lost by dissolution in the water, so the true intake could not be known.
- Salt-bath concentration (2.5, 3.0 and 5.0 per cent) governed how quickly blood salinity changed, and therefore fish survival.
- The authors note that in a natural habitat trout would not encounter high dietary carbohydrate levels, which may explain their limited carbohydrate handling.
- Normal blood chloride was given as 496 mg per 100 cc, consistent with literature values.

## Key Findings

**Vitamin requirements**
- Riboflavin and pantothenic acid requirements were the same for brook, brown and rainbow trout.
- Pantothenic acid requirement: 0.99 to 1.25 mg per kg body weight per day; maximum liver storage about 19 mg per gram of liver (versus 23–24 mg in 1944).
- Riboflavin requirement: 0.44 to 0.68 mg per kg body weight per day (1944 range 0.36–0.53); maximum liver storage 15–16 mg per gram.
- Riboflavin was utilized well from cottonseed meal but poorly from an animal concentrate mixture (dried skim milk and white fish meal).
- Brook trout thiamin requirement: 0.115 to 0.125 mg per kg per day at 47°F, and 0.150 to 0.168 mg per kg per day at 52°F. The difference was highly significant and was attributed mainly to water temperature, with fish size also a possible factor.
- Fluke-infested beef liver denatured by dying lost considerable thiamin, which reconciled the present results with earlier work.

**Sodium chloride**
- Dietary salt of 10, 15, 20 or 25 mg per fish (0.91–1.82 g/kg) raised blood chlorides; 20 and 25 mg levels produced edema (2.72–3.64 g/kg).
- At 20 mg, edema resolved with no deaths; at 25 mg, 66 per cent of fish died within 24 hours. A 50 mg trial caused nausea and was discontinued.
- Salt baths of 3.0 and 5.0 per cent raised blood salinity; 2.5 per cent had no effect.
- A 3 per cent bath for 60 minutes caused high distress and a 45-hour recovery; a 5 per cent bath for 15 minutes caused about 50 per cent loss.
- Recommended safe limits: 3 per cent for up to 30 minutes, and 2.5 per cent for up to one hour, depending on fish health.
- Absorption curves resembled those for carbohydrates in earlier studies.

**Glucose and insulin**
- Feeding 25 mg glucose produced a blood sugar peak of 110 per cent above baseline, with a curve similar to diabetic humans.
- Injected insulin (0.24 units per fish, average weight 12.0 g) lowered blood sugar faster than in controls; the injected fish were normal by 15 hours, while controls stayed high.
- Sugar removed from the blood was not recovered as liver or muscle glycogen. The authors conclude that insulin accelerated oxidation of glucose.
- The authors conclude that carbohydrates can substitute for fats and proteins in trout diets only to a limited extent.

**Practical diets**
- Supplementing the standard [[Cortland No. 6 Diet]] with beef liver, yeast or Rib-o-fern gave no growth improvement; mortality was very low (0.02–0.19 per cent per two-week period) across 3,915 trout.
- The report recommends adding 15 per cent beef liver to the No. 6 diet, based on past experience rather than fresh vitamin assays.

**Paprika coloration**
- Two per cent paprika in the No. 6 diet colored brook trout fins and spots within about 6 weeks in 2–3 inch fingerlings.
- Brown trout colored the adipose fin in about 8 weeks, but body spots colored only after about 6 months.
- Rainbow trout showed little or no coloration, so paprika is not recommended for rainbow fingerlings, and for brown fingerlings it is considered inadvisable.
