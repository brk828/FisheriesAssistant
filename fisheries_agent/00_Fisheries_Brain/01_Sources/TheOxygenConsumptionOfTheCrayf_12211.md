# Oxygen Consumption of the Crayfishes Orconectes immunis and Orconectes nais in Response to Temperature and Oxygen Saturation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

*Note: The source is a crustacean physiology study, not a fish study; the tag line follows the instruction for non-fish taxa.*

## Executive Summary

Wiens and Armitage (1961) compared the oxygen consumption of two freshwater crayfishes, [[Orconectes Immunis]] and [[Orconectes Nais]], across four temperatures (16°, 24°, 30°, 35°C) and four oxygen saturation levels (full, one-half, one-quarter, one-eighth). The work was motivated by the local separation of the two species: O. immunis lives in temporary roadside ditches, while O. nais inhabits running streams and permanent ponds in Douglas County, Kansas. Temperature and oxygen concentration both strongly affected metabolic rate in both species. The two species did not differ significantly under moderate conditions, but O. nais regulated its metabolism less effectively under combined high temperature and low oxygen, which the authors link to the exclusion of O. immunis from streams.

## Background & Description

The study places physiological differentiation within the broader framework of [[Speciation]] and [[Ecological Distribution]]. Its authors cite earlier work showing that pond-dwelling crayfishes and insect larvae generally tolerate heat and low oxygen better than stream-dwelling relatives (e.g., [[Bovbjerg 1952]], [[Park 1945]], [[Prosser 1950]]). Previous studies had measured either survival tolerance or oxygen consumption, but rarely both across combined temperature and oxygen gradients. This study addresses that gap.

Key morphological and habitat descriptions:
- *O. immunis* is a burrower and chimney-builder. Its Kansas range covers about the northeast one-eighth of the state (around fourteen counties). Its broader range extends from Massachusetts to Wyoming and from Tennessee to Ontario.
- *O. nais* is a burrower in mud and clay banks but not a chimney-builder. It occurs throughout Kansas, with a range covering the Great Plains and Ozark regions.
- Animals were seined from the Wakarusa River valley and grouped into small (mean 1.80 g), mid (mean 3.45 g) and large (mean 8.37 g) size classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat contrasts (summer 1959):**
- *O. immunis* occurred at only one site, a roadside ditch in Haskell Bottoms with abundant vegetation. The ditch dried completely twice for nearly a week. It was six to eight feet wide and never deeper than eighteen inches. Water was mainly runoff from cultivated fields.
- *O. nais* occupied small flowing streams up to ten feet wide with average depth of eighteen inches and holes up to four feet deep.

**Measured conditions (Table 6):**
- Ditch (*O. immunis*) water reached a maximum of 36°C, with 30–35°C common. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 0.3 to 8.0 mg/L, and the single reading of 0.3 mg/L was about 4 per cent saturation.
- Stream (*O. nais*) water reached a maximum of 32°C, with 26–30°C common.
- Burrow temperatures at 12–18 inches depth were 5–7°C cooler than open water.

**Experimental versus natural ranges:** Experimental oxygen levels (0.79–8.76 mg/L) were similar to, but less extreme than, the recorded natural range (0.3–8.12 mg/L). Experimental temperatures (16–35°C) covered the natural range, which extended from 22.5°C in a stream burrow to 36°C in the ditch.

**Competing explanations:** The authors note that spatial barriers are unlikely, since ditch water drains into a creek where O. nais lives. They also suggest that the ditch's periodic lack of open water may limit other species. Finally, the higher daily metabolic rate of O. nais (about 10 per cent above O. immunis) and its larger adult size might give it a competitive edge in streams.

## Key Findings

1. **Temperature effect:** Temperature markedly and significantly affected oxygen consumption in both species. The log of oxygen consumption varied linearly with temperature, and the mean Q10 was 1.42 for O. immunis and similar for O. nais.

2. **Oxygen saturation effect:** Reduced saturation produced a significant decrease in metabolic rate in both species. Q10 values declined slightly with lower saturation (saturated 1.49; one-half 1.43; one-quarter 1.40; one-eighth 1.34 for O. immunis).

3. **Regulation:** Neither species fully regulated or conformed. Both acted as partial regulators under moderate stress. Regulation weakened as temperature rose, and the clockwise rotation of the rate/concentration curves with temperature confirmed this loss of regulatory power.

4. **Body weight:** Metabolic rate per gram decreased with increasing body weight. Large animals were most strongly affected by oxygen stress, partly because they depleted oxygen in the respiratory cylinder faster.

5. **Factorial analysis (Table 2):** Main effects of size, saturation and temperature were significant. None of the interactions reached the 5 per cent significance level, though the saturation-temperature interaction came close. The authors argue that the cumulative effect of heat and hypoxia is stronger than either factor alone.

6. **Interspecific differences:**
   - Under moderate conditions (full and one-half saturation), the species did not differ significantly.
   - At one-quarter saturation, the temperature curves separated significantly, and the difference grew with temperature.
   - At 35°C, O. nais failed to increase metabolic rate at one-eighth saturation, while O. immunis did. O. nais curves showed a steeper slope than the conformer curve, indicating metabolic breakdown.
   - Thirteen of fifteen comparable points showed O. immunis with a higher rate than O. nais in the rate/oxygen plots.

7. **Conclusion:** The authors conclude that O. immunis is better adapted to periodic high temperatures and low oxygen. The inability of O. nais to regulate as well may keep it out of the roadside ditch habitat. The study is acute or semi-acclimated, and natural acclimation may take longer, so the results should be read as stress responses rather than long-term tolerances.

**Methodological notes:** Oxygen consumption was measured by flow respirometry with an acclimation period of 20–30 minutes for oxygen changes and 1.5–2 days for temperature changes. Oxygen was measured by the unmodified Winkler method. Individuals were checked for good condition, and animals in molt were excluded.
