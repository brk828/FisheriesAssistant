# Evaluation of Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction Programs in Central Arizona (1986–1990 Surveys)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report by Dean A. Hendrickson (Arizona Game and Fish Department) evaluates the reintroduction of two federally endangered fishes, the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Colorado Squawfish]], into the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] of central Arizona. More than 10 million hatchery-produced razorback suckers and over 623,000 squawfish were stocked between 1981 and 1990, yet intensive electrofishing and netting surveys captured only 519 razorbacks and 444 squawfish. Most recaptures occurred within weeks of stocking, and no wild reproduction was documented. Predation by exotic [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]], combined with hatcheries' inability to produce large fish for release, are identified as the main impediments. The author recommends continued stocking, a shift toward experimental research, habitat protection, predator control, and a reorganized, better-coordinated program.

## Background & Description

Both species are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and formerly ranged widely through the Gila River system, including the Salt and Verde rivers, supporting protein harvests and commercial fisheries into the mid-20th century. Natural populations disappeared from the Gila River and lower Colorado River by about 1960. Both were listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and a 1980 Memorandum of Understanding between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] initiated razorback stocking in 1981 from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] stock. Squawfish stocking began in 1985, after the 1985 "experimental, nonessential" designation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] allowed reintroduction into designated Salt and Verde reaches.

The study covered the Verde River (286 km long, drainage about 16,900 km²) and the Salt River canyon (Highway 60 bridge to Roosevelt Diversion). The Verde has spring-fed, stable flows (base flow about 24–28 cfs at Verde Ranch) and a diverse native fish fauna, while the Salt is far more variable, surface-runoff driven and almost entirely dominated by exotic fishes in its study reach. Standardized surveys used boat-mounted electrofishing, seining, gill and trammel nets, and a fish-monitoring database designed for statewide use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish decline:** The upper Verde retains seven native species, only 58% of its original native fauna. Lower reaches are dominated by introduced species such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], and bass.
- **Exotic predators:** Flathead catfish, channel catfish, black bullhead, green sunfish and both black bass species are known or suspected predators on stocked fish.
- **Habitat:** Backwaters are rare, small and structurally controlled by geology, but they held a disproportionate share of captures for both species.
- **Water quality and sampling limits:** In the Salt River, conductivity exceeded electrofishing capacity below about 400 cfs, and drifting [[Cladophora]] algae clogged gill and trammel nets.
- **Parasites:** Heavy infestations of the copepod [[Lernaea]] were recorded on razorbacks, with up to 80–100 parasites on a single ~300 mm fish, and were associated with warm-season stress and mortality.
- **Diversions and barriers:** Irrigation diversions and dams in the Verde Valley and below Roosevelt Dam trap or divert downstream-moving fish, many of which are lost to canals or reservoirs.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and capture totals**
- Over 10 million razorback suckers (11,888,900 recorded in appendices) and 623,000+ squawfish (770,210 recorded) were stocked, but only 519 razorbacks and 444 squawfish were captured in surveys.
- The total effort recorded was 2,216 sampling efforts (about 59% Verde, 36% Salt, 5% tributaries).
- Nearly 200,000 fish specimens were processed; over 90% of the catch came from electrofishing and seining.

**Survival and movement**
- Razorbacks moved rapidly downstream after stocking, and most recaptures were close to stocking sites and taken soon after release.
- Squawfish moved markedly nocturnally, often several kilometres per night, and some were recaptured more than 2 km per day from release sites within a month.
- Survival appeared better for razorbacks in the upper Verde and for squawfish in the Salt River.
- Few individuals survived beyond one year; those that did were usually in isolated sites such as the Granite Creek pools and the Canyon Creek cobble-dam pool.

**Growth**
- Razorbacks grew in the wild. One Granite Creek cohort stocked at ~76 mm TL averaged 113.96 mm (n = 26) by 4 December 1987, 187.26 mm (n = 27) by 30 July 1988, and a 1990 upper-Verde pool sample of 34 fish averaged 371.82 mm.
- A Cherry Creek sample of six razorbacks averaged 172.33 mm TL.
- Squawfish length increased linearly with time since stocking, though the author notes size-differential mortality could explain part of this trend.

**Condition**
- Verde razorbacks had lower weight at a given length than razorbacks from other populations (slope 2.944 vs. 3.118; slope difference rejected at F = 228).
- Condition factor (K) declined with time since stocking in Verde razorbacks, a statistically significant trend.

**Reproduction**
- No unequivocal evidence of wild reproduction was found, although ripe male razorbacks were taken in December 1989 from the uppermost Verde.
- Squawfish are not expected to mature until about 5 years of age, so reproduction was not anticipated during the study.

**Causes of mortality**
- Predation by flathead and channel catfish and smallmouth bass is the primary identified cause; nearly all stomachs of sampled smallmouth bass and black bullheads collected 1–48 hours after squawfish stockings contained squawfish.
- Diversion canals are considered a significant cause of mortality for squawfish stocked above Verde Valley.
- Lernaea infestations, transport stress and high water temperatures are secondary contributors.

**Habitat preference**
- Connected backwaters held a disproportionate share of captures: about 45% of razorbacks and 63% of squawfish came from backwater efforts that made up only 21% of total effort.
- Razorbacks were rarely caught in run habitats.

**Recommendations**
- Continue stocking both species, favouring larger fish and closed, peripheral habitats (isolated backwaters, ponds) with low predator densities.
- Shift the program emphasis from production to experimental research, with hatcheries managed as research facilities.
- Establish a broad-based advisory oversight committee and fund at least one full-time coordination position.
- Create a centralized, standardized database; use unique batch marks (e.g., otolith marking) for every stocking; and deposit specimens in museums.
- Pursue predator control, particularly flathead catfish removal from the Salt River, which may be feasible given the upstream barrier at Apache Falls and the blockage at Roosevelt Diversion Dam.
- Protect habitats and minimum flows, and coordinate with diversion operators to reduce losses.
- Conduct annual, standardized monitoring in spring and summer, with more passive netting and angling.
- Research topics include condition factor decline, size and experience effects on survival, transport and temperature stress, parasites, homing behaviour, and post-stocking density.
