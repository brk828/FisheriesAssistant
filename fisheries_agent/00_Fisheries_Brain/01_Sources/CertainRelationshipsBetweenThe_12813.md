## Executive Summary
This 1960s-era study by Jerry B. Denton (Alabama Dept. of Conservation, in cooperation with Auburn University) investigated the elemental/chemical composition of three widespread nuisance aquatic macrophytes — [[Alligatorweed]] (*Alternanthera philoxeroides*), [[Parrotfeather]] (*Myriophyllum brasiliense*), and [[Waterhyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*) — grown in polluted (Flint River arm of Lake Seminole, GA) versus non-polluted (Auburn experimental plastic pools) waters. Plant tissue, surface water, and bottom soil samples were analyzed for ash, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium to determine how [[Water Quality]] and substrate chemistry influence aquatic plant nutrient composition. The study is foundational to understanding [[Nutrient Pollution Abatement]] potential of rooted and floating aquatic vegetation, a topic directly relevant to fisheries management since excessive macrophyte growth affects dissolved oxygen, habitat structure, and water chemistry in fish-bearing systems.

## Background & Description
No fish morphological data present. Plant-relevant structural/growth observations:
- **[[Waterhyacinth]]**: Floating, non-rooted plant; absorbs/adsorbs nutrients directly from water column or suspended clay particles. Grew markedly larger in the Flint River (polluted) than in plastic pools, though populations were maintained at small size in pools.
- **[[Alligatorweed]]**: Rooted emergent plant capable of forming dense mats in riverine conditions; did not form comparable dense mats in plastic pools despite individual plants reaching comparable size.
- **[[Parrotfeather]]**: Rooted/submersed-emergent plant; grew at a comparable rate in both polluted and non-polluted environments and began spring growth ~2 months earlier than the other two species, a phenological trait that influenced its nitrogen assimilation timing.
- Calcium is noted as a relatively immobile element that concentrates in older/mature plant tissue, meaning tissue-age (mature vs. immature ratio) can confound elemental comparisons across samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat contrast**: Polluted riverine habitat (Flint River arm, Lake Seminole, GA) vs. non-polluted controlled plastic pools (Auburn, AL Agricultural Experiment Station).
- **Water hardness gradient**: Flint River averaged 26.74 ppm total hardness vs. 22.37 ppm in plastic pools (minimum 20.50 ppm vs. 7.70 ppm respectively) — consistent with prior findings ([[Juday 1942]]; [[Boyd 1966]]) that harder waters support larger standing crops and greater species richness of macrophytes.
- **Nutrient dynamics**: The study frames macrophytes as potential agents of [[Nutrient Pollution Abatement]] via nitrogen and mineral uptake/storage, positioning aquatic plant biomass as an interface between water chemistry and broader aquatic food-web/water-quality dynamics relevant to fish habitat.
- **Competitive soil dynamics**: Carbon:Nitrogen (C:N) ratios in bottom soil organic matter affect nitrogen release via microbial decomposition, indirectly regulating nutrient availability in the aquatic environment (relevant to eutrophication processes affecting fish habitats).
- **Species-specific nutrient strategy**: Differing uptake strategies (rooted substrate-feeders like alligatorweed vs. floating water-column feeders like waterhyacinth) suggest functional niche differentiation among macrophytes in nutrient cycling.

## Key Findings
- **Ash content** varied directly with water hardness, especially in alligatorweed (61.08 mg/g in pools vs. 183.90 mg/g in the river) — supporting earlier hard-water/soft-water biomass relationships reported by [[Juday 1942]] and [[Boyd 1966]].
- **Carbon content** was relatively stable across environments for all three species (range ~395–433 mg/g), with plastic-pool plants slightly higher, possibly due to tissue maturity/lignin differences rather than environmental chemistry.
- **Nitrogen content** was the most environmentally variable element:
  - Alligatorweed: 8.48 mg/g (pool) vs. 30.45 mg/g (river) — over 3.5x increase, the most habitat-variable of the three species.
  - Waterhyacinth: 13.71 mg/g (pool) vs. 26.10 mg/g (river).
  - Parrotfeather: 13.47 mg/g (pool) vs. 15.80 mg/g (river) — comparatively stable, suggesting efficient early-season nitrogen assimilation.
  - Alligatorweed was identified as the most efficient species for nitrogen assimilation/storage overall (avg. 30.45 mg/g), suggesting strong potential for [[Nutrient Pollution Abatement]] applications.
- **Carbon:Nitrogen ratio** varied primarily due to nitrogen (carbon being stable); e.g., alligatorweed C:N dropped from 96.23 (pool) to 13.94 (river). Soil organic matter C:N ratios (14.49 river vs. 17.39 pool) were discussed as a threshold factor (~10) governing nitrogen release during decomposition.
- **Phosphorus** showed no significant species differences; substrate (bottom soil) phosphorus appeared to dominate uptake in rooted alligatorweed, while water-column phosphorus appeared more influential for floating/water-absorbing parrotfeather and waterhyacinth.
- **Calcium** was consistently slightly higher in river-grown plants across all three species, correlating with higher water/soil calcium in the Flint River (7.60 ppm water, 518.00 ppm soil) vs. pools (3.73 ppm water, 413.00 ppm soil).
- **Magnesium** was significantly higher in pool-grown parrotfeather and waterhyacinth than river-grown counterparts, tracking higher magnesium levels in pool water/soil; alligatorweed magnesium varied little between habitats, possibly due to a suppressive K–Ca–Mg antagonism ([[Broyer 1959]]) from its high potassium uptake in the river.
- **Potassium** was highest in river-grown alligatorweed (45.60 mg/g) and waterhyacinth (41.65 mg/g), indicating possible luxury consumption; parrotfeather potassium was comparatively stable across habitats. Water potassium concentration (not soil) appeared to be the dominant driver of plant uptake.
- **Sodium** was consistently higher in plants grown in plastic pools, mirroring elevated sodium in pool water (6.12 ppm) and soil (53.00 ppm) versus the Flint River (0.98 ppm water, 36.00 ppm soil), consistent with substrate-driven sodium uptake reported for rice by [[Ehrler 1960]].
- Overall, the study establishes species-specific and element-specific patterns of macrophyte responsiveness to [[Water Quality]] and bottom substrate chemistry, with implications for using specific aquatic plants (e.g., alligatorweed) as bioindicators or bioremediation tools in nutrient-polluted freshwater systems that also serve as fish habitat.
