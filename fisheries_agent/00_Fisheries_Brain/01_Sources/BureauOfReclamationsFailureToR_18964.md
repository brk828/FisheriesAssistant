# Letter to Secretary Babbitt Regarding Central Arizona Project Impacts on Native Fish

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This August 24, 1993 letter from Dr. Robin Silver of the [[Maricopa Audubon Society]] to Secretary Bruce Babbitt addresses the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]'s failure to respond to a Section 7 "jeopardy" opinion regarding the [[Central Arizona Project]]'s introduction of exotic species threatening Arizona's federally listed native fish. The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] issued a draft jeopardy opinion on May 30, 1991, but the Bureau had not acted for 26 months while exotic species continued spreading through the [[Gila River]] basin. Seven federally listed species are at risk, including the [[Desert Pupfish]] (endangered), [[Spikedace]] (threatened), [[Loach Minnow]] (threatened), [[Gila Topminnow]] (endangered), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (endangered), [[Razorback Sucker]] (endangered), and [[Desert Nesting Bald Eagle]] (endangered).

## Background & Description

Arizona was historically home to 33 native fish species that evolved over tens of thousands of years to survive extreme environmental conditions including temperature swings, flash floods, and droughts. The [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), once the most common species in the Gila and lower Salt rivers, exemplifies these adaptations by tolerating water conditions ranging from maximally dilute fresh water during floods to maximally concentrated brine during droughts. These physiological adaptations may hold important clues for treating human kidney ailments and potentially freeing patients from dialysis.

The [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]] went extinct when predatory [[Largemouth Bass]] were introduced into their habitat, illustrating the lethal impact of exotic species introductions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Of Arizona's original 33 native fish species, 23 are listed by the state of Arizona, 18 are listed federally as threatened or endangered, and one (Monkey Spring pupfish) has gone extinct. Native fish serve as living barometers of river conditions. The primary causes of decline are habitat loss and introduction of non-native species.

The [[Central Arizona Project]]'s feeder canals and inadequate protective systems provide access routes for exotic species throughout the [[Gila River]] system. Scientists had warned for more than a decade about the disastrous effects of the project on native wildlife and Arizona rivers. Some native species have been federally listed as endangered since 1967.

## Key Findings

The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] initiated formal consultation with the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] on February 12, 1991, regarding the [[Central Arizona Project]]'s introduction and spreading of non-native species throughout the [[Gila River]] basin. The Fish and Wildlife Service issued a draft "jeopardy" opinion on May 30, 1991. As of the letter date (August 24, 1993), the Bureau had refused to act on the jeopardy opinion for nearly 26 months while deadly contamination by exotic species continued.

The letter demands answers regarding: (1) How long the Bureau will continue its procrastination while feeder canals and inadequate protective systems offer access to exotic species throughout the Gila River system, and (2) How long the Bureau will be permitted to continue jeopardizing Arizona's surviving federally protected native fish.

The correspondence invokes Section 7(a)(2) of the [[Endangered Species Act]], which requires federal agencies to ensure their actions do not jeopardize the continued existence of endangered or threatened species or result in destruction or adverse modification of critical habitat.
