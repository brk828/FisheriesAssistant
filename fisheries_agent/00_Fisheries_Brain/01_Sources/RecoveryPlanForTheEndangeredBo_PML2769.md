# Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan addresses the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), a large-river cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]] listed as endangered on April 23, 1980. The species was listed without designated critical habitat because reproducing populations were unknown and extinction appeared imminent. The plan states that the immediate goal is to prevent extinction. Downlisting and delisting can only be considered after that threat is removed. Prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] with federal and state agencies, the plan reviews historical and present distribution, life history, causes of decline, and a prioritized stepdown recovery outline with an implementation schedule.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and identification.** The species was first described by [[Baird And Girard]] (1853) from the Zuni River, New Mexico, a site later considered atypical for a large-river fish. It belongs to a group of close relatives that includes [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Hybridization among the three species has been suggested, and identification problems persist, especially for young fish.

**Morphology.**
- Adults typically reach 30–35 cm total length. Larger specimens have come from [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- The body is elongated and laterally compressed, with a long, thin caudal peduncle, a small terminal oblique mouth, and a predorsal nuchal hump.
- Dorsal and anal fin rays are usually 10-10. Gill rakers on the second arch are usually 18–21 (versus 12–15 in roundtail chub). Pharyngeal teeth are 2,5–4,2.
- Adults have a gray or olivaceous back and silvery sides. Breeding males show red-orange lateral slashes and breeding tubercles.

**Identification.** Bonytail are distinguished from roundtail chub by fin ray counts, gill rakers, and a narrower caudal peduncle. They are distinguished from humpback chub by a less abrupt nuchal hump and a non-overhanging snout. Records from the 1950s and 1960s often lumped all three large-river chubs together, so such records must be treated cautiously.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The species evolved in the swift, torrential mainstem flows of the Colorado River system and is considered a big-river species. It has rarely been caught in smaller tributaries. Adults and roundtail chub showed no difference in habitat preference in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (DNM), both using pools and eddies. Reservoir populations occupy lacustrine habitat, which the plan notes may reflect the cold water of the inflowing [[Colorado River]].

**Historic distribution.** Records once spanned the mainstem and major tributaries, including the [[Green River]], [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], [[Gunnison River]], and the lower [[Yampa River]]. Historically it was apparently abundant in the late 1800s, when reports described it as common to abundant.

**Decline.** The plan identifies several drivers:
- **Lower basin:** Dam construction and loss of natural riverine habitat. Surviving fish in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] are considered remnants of formerly large populations with no evidence of successful reproduction.
- **Gila system:** Flow depletions from overgrazing, groundwater depletion, dams and irrigation, mining, and nonnative species. The species was gone from the Salt River before 1926 and absent from the Gila and lower Colorado by 1950 (Miller 1961).
- **Upper basin:** Closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in November 1962 altered flow and temperature regimes and led to loss of habitat, and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closure in 1963 similarly reduced abundance. Consumptive water use reduced flows, and nonnative competition and predation and hybridization may have contributed.

**Reproduction and thermal limits.** Spawning in DNM was extrapolated to occur from late June to early July at about 18°C. In hatchery work at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], 90% of eggs hatched at 20°C, 55% at 16–17°C, and 4% at 12–13°C, with 96% failing at the coldest temperature. The authors suggest colder temperatures may be a factor in the decline.

## Key Findings

**Status.** The species is extremely rare and close to extirpation. The last confirmed bonytail was taken from the Green River, which may hold the last remnants of the riverine population. Reproduction appears extremely low, with the most recent juvenile reported from [[Desolation Canyon]].

**Population data.**
- Lake Mohave: 19 taken by scientific researchers since 1974, with several more reported by anglers. The total population is unknown.
- Holden and Stalnaker (1975): 35 adults in a 4-year upper-basin study (the text also cites 36 adults for 1968–1971), mostly from DNM.
- No bonytail were collected in Colorado or Utah Colorado River tributaries despite sampling from 1977 to 1983.

**Reintroduction.** The [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] stocked [[Lake Mohave]] with about 4,500 tetracycline-marked fingerlings in 1982 and about 45,000 swim-up fry in 1983, both from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Monitoring in 1983 produced no results, so success is unknown.

**Hybridization.** In 1980, five female bonytail were crossed with eight roundtail and 33 humpback chub males at Willow Beach. Hybrids are a concern because the small remaining population raises the likelihood of hybridizing with more abundant species.

**Life history (limited data).**
- Back-calculated growth in DNM: about 55 mm in year one, 100 mm in year two, and 150 mm in year three. The largest fish was 388 mm and seven years old.
- Juvenile diets included chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs. Adults ate terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae, indicating surface feeding.
- Lake Mohave spawning was observed over a gravel bar in up to 9 m of water, with about 500 fish and a 2:1 male-to-female ratio.

**Recovery objectives.**
- Interim: prevent extinction.
- Downlisting: at least four viable, self-sustaining riverine populations established and maintained.
- Delisting: six naturally self-sustaining populations secured with legally protected habitat.

**Stepdown priorities (Priority 1 is essential to prevent extinction).** Key tasks include:
1. Determine status in the historic riverine range, including intensive sampling of the Green River in Gray and Desolation canyons, the Green and Yampa rivers in DNM, and Lake Mohave.
2. Determine threats and protect populations and habitat, including enforcement of the [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation for federal projects.
3. Augment life history knowledge on spawning, movement, habitat, diet, growth, and self-sustaining population criteria.
4. Restore populations through habitat-based site selection, stocking strategies, broodstock maintenance to protect genetic diversity, monitoring, and a basin-wide management plan.
5. Conduct public information and education programs.
6. Consider downlisting when four populations are established.
7. Consider delisting when six populations are secured.

**Principal conclusion.** The plan states that changes in habitat caused by dams and other alterations have been a major factor in the decline of the species in the upper basin, and mainstem dams have contributed in the lower basin. Hybridization, nonnative species, and flow depletion are considered secondary or possible contributing factors. The plan emphasizes that a status determination is a prerequisite to all other recovery efforts.
