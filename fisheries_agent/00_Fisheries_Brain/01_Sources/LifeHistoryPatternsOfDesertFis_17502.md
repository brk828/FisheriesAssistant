# Life History Patterns of Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter by George D. Constantz (Academy of Natural Sciences, Philadelphia) reviews the life histories of native fishes in [[Desert Springs]] and [[Desert Streams]] across the [[Great Basin]], [[Mohave Desert]], and [[Sonoran Desert]] of North America. It compares 16 well-documented focal species and asks which environmental factors shaped their life history patterns. The central finding is that spring fishes and stream fishes differ consistently: spring forms are chubby, small-bodied, low-fecundity, large-egged, and breed over longer seasons, while stream forms are fusiform, larger-bodied, higher-fecundity, and breed over shorter seasons. The author argues these differences fit predictions of [[R and K Selection]] theory and may also reflect shared ancestry and convergent selection. Desert fishes are not qualitatively different from fishes of surrounding mesic biomes.

## Background & Description

The chapter is one of the contributions to the volume *Fishes in North American Deserts* (edited by R. J. Naiman and D. L. Soltz, 1981). The author limits the scope in three ways: it emphasizes evolutionary (genetically based) traits rather than plastic responses; it covers only fishes native to desert springs and streams, excluding man-made lakes and montane-fed rivers; and it is restricted geographically to three deserts and taxonomically to 16 species.

The focal sample includes 8 Great Basin, 3 Mohave, and 11 Sonoran records (some species occur in more than one desert). By family, the set includes five cyprinodontids, three poeciliids, six cyprinids, and two catostomids. Eight species occur in streams, four in springs, and four in both habitats. Data come mainly from the literature and the author's own field work, notably on [[Gila Topminnow]] at [[Cocio Wash]].

Table 6 is the main data source. It lists range, habitat, morphology, food, breeding season, body size, age at maturity, fecundity, offspring size, behavior, dispersal, population characteristics, and references for each species. Its details are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Springs** are small (typically under 20 m across and under 3 m deep), warm, often oxygen-poor, and physicochemically constant. Least variable are outflows with no surface drainage and a deep, constantly warm source. Springs have few resident species, low interspecific interaction, and high endemism. Seasonal change is driven mainly by the solar cycle, so it is gradual and predictable.

**Streams (arroyos or washes)** hold permanent pools that are closed, stratified, and internally recycling between floods. Flow ranges from modest discharge that imports nutrients to flash floods that reshape pools, scour biota, and displace fishes downstream. Discharge can rise suddenly by five orders of magnitude. Streams are larger, more variable in volume, cooler, more oxygen-rich, and less isolated than springs. Their complexity supports more species and more biotic interactions.

The author treats springs and streams as ends of a continuum. Variable springs, intermittent-subterranean streams, and spring-fed downstream reaches fall between them.

## Key Findings

**Morphology**
- Stream fishes are mostly fusiform with pointed fins, forked caudal fins, and mouths in varied positions. Spring fishes are chubby, with rounded fins, rounded caudal fins, posterior dorsal and anal fins, and superior or terminal mouths. Some spring endemics have degenerate lateral lines or lack pelvic fins.
- Generalist species show intermediate forms. The relict dace (*Relictus solitarius*) is the classic spring morphology.

**Food habits**
- Across 34 desert fishes categorized from state fish manuals, there are 25 omnivores, 7 carnivores, and 2 herbivores.
- All spring species are omnivorous. Streams support a few exclusively carnivorous and herbivorous species. The author suggests that complex, productive streams allow dietary specialization, while short spring food chains enforce omnivory.

**Breeding season**
- Stream species breed over a mean of 4.3 months (range 2–8, n = 9). Spring species breed over a mean of 9.8 months (range 4–12, n = 6).
- Spring forms such as [[White River Killifish]], [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], and spring populations of [[Amargosa Pupfish]] and Gila topminnow spawn year-round.
- The [[Red River Pupfish]] has a long season (February–November) in a stream, which the author attributes to unpredictable optimal conditions.

**Body size at maturity**
- Focal stream species average 172.5 mm TL (range 28–800 mm TL, n = 8). Focal spring species average 31.3 mm SL (range 26–66 mm SL, n = 3).
- Streams contain both small and large species. Springs are occupied only by small fishes (26–76 mm TL). The author attributes large-bodied species to the greater habitat volume of streams.
- Amargosa pupfish females mature at 24.0 mm SL in spring and 25.9 mm SL in stream populations, a difference the author ties to genetic dissimilarity.

**Age at maturity**
- Stream fishes *Agosia*, *Meda*, and *Plagopterus* spawn at one year. *Gila robusta* does not reproduce until about five years.
- Spring fishes mature within a few months to about one year, with more variable generation times. The author proposes that a young spring fish cannot predict its food conditions because environmental cycles outlast its life history.

**Fecundity**
- Five stream species average 2088 eggs per female (range 11–10,000). Two spring forms average 9 (range 4–19). The arithmetic mean is skewed by one very high-egg species, so the author compares fecundity classes instead.
- Within the Amargosa pupfish, females carry about 10 eggs in Big Spring and 40 in the Amargosa River. Gila topminnows carry 5 embryos in a warm spring and 14 in a wash.

**Investment per offspring**
- Six stream fishes average 1.5 mm egg diameter (range 0.9–2.1). Two spring populations average 1.7 mm (range 1.5–1.9).
- Gila topminnow eggs weigh 2.2 mg in a warm spring and 1.6 mg in a wash. Amargosa pupfish egg diameters are 1.39 mm (Big Spring) and 1.34 mm (river), with genetic influence suspected.
- Gila topminnow egg weight varies by 22 percent over an annual cycle, independent of female size.

**Reproductive effort**
- Gila topminnows allocated 4.8 percent of their energy budget to reproduction in a wash, and 2.1 and 3.5 percent in a warm spring. Measurements exist for only one desert fish.
- The author suggests high metabolic costs in warm springs leave little energy for gonads.

**Breeding frequency**
- Spikedace produce one clutch per year as yearlings and two thereafter. Laboratory interbrood intervals for three *Poeciliopsis* species are 10–12 days. Data are too limited for generalization.

**Social behavior**
- All five focal stream taxa with information have external fertilization and aggregative spawning. Springs show no aggregative spawning.
- Spring males are often territorial and brightly colored. Spring populations of generalist species show alternative mating strategies, with large territorial males and small satellite males.
- Streams show little territoriality, and satellite males are not obvious there.

**Dispersal**
- The author distinguishes displacement (involuntary downstream movement, not adaptive) from dispersal (voluntary movement from permanent pools to temporary waters).
- Longfin dace and stream populations of speckled dace disperse over vast reaches. Desert pupfish, Amargosa pupfish, and Gila topminnow disperse modestly, about 0.5 km.

**Dispersal hypothesis (Cocio Wash)**
- In August, the probability of flow 60 days after occupying a temporary pool was 0.55, so the probability of desiccation was 0.45.
- Females in the temporary pool carried 52 percent more embryos and reproductive mass than females in the permanent pool.
- The author concludes the benefits of dispersal (more food, higher fecundity) roughly equal or exceed its costs, and that fish should not disperse when fecundity gains do not offset desiccation odds.
- Only part of the population dispersed, suggesting possible genetic or physiological polymorphism.

**Sex ratio**
- Three of four stream forms had males equal to or exceeding females. Warm-spring relict dace were predominantly female.
- Populations in warm water had fewer males (M/F ratios 0.28, 0.43, 0.66) than cooler-water populations (0.38, 2.56, 0.94, 0.47, 1.0). The author conjectures that warmer temperatures accelerate male aging.

**Population dynamics**
- Five of six stream forms with data show large interannual variation in reproductive success and survival. Some, such as *Poeciliopsis monacha*, undergo periodic local extinctions. The roundtail chub shows no clear link between discharge and year-class strength.
- In springs, the Devils Hole pupfish cycles between about 200 individuals in October and 700 in August, driven by solar input and algal production. Spring populations are generally more stable.

**Causes of the patterns**
- The author proposes three non-exclusive causes: (1) water temperature, which limits energy for growth and reproduction; (2) homology, since cyprinids and catostomids occupy streams while cyprinodontids and poeciliids occupy springs; and (3) analogy, through convergent selection, consistent with r- and K-selection.
- The author declines to rank the three causes.

**Adaptations in other deserts and conclusions**
- Egg diapause and adult estivation, seen in other arid zones, are absent in the focal North American species.
- The author proposes that North American desert fishes lack unique adaptations because colonizing taxa lacked precursor traits and the deserts are only about 12,000 years old.
- The author concludes that desert fish life histories are not qualitatively different from those of surrounding mesic biomes, and that the spring–stream contrasts are consistent and general within the focal sample.
