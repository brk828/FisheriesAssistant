# Resource Inventory of the Gila River Complex, Eastern Arizona (1976–1978)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #baseline-survey

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management–funded baseline inventory, prepared by Arizona State University researchers (led by W. L. Minckley and R. R. Sommerfeld), documents the physical, chemical, biological and biotic character of the Gila River Complex in eastern Arizona between October 1976 and September 1978. The study area lies in a transition zone between the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and the [[Sonoran Desert]], and riparian corridors were identified as the most important habitats, supporting a disproportionate share of the fauna. The inventory covers [[Basin and Range Province]] geology, hydrology and climate, vegetation, birds, mammals, herpetofauna, aquatic invertebrates, fishes, water chemistry, bacteriology and algae. Its stated purpose was to provide pre-development baseline data and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

The Gila River drains the eastern [[Mogollon Mountains]] and [[Black Range]] of New Mexico, passes through the [[Duncan–Virden Valley]], cuts across the north end of the [[Peloncillo Mountains]] and enters the [[Safford Valley]] before flowing into [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. Major tributaries include the [[San Francisco River]], [[Eagle Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]]. The basin lies within the Texas Zone of intense structural deformation, characterised by short parallel fault-block ranges and alluvial basins filled with Tertiary Gila Group sediments.

Hydrology is dominated by flash floods. Mean annual discharge at the head of the Safford Valley was roughly 22,000 ha-m (winter and summer combined in the 1914–64 record), with winter flows about 68% of the annual mean. The peak recorded flow on the Gila near Clifton was about 799 m³/s (1941), and the San Francisco River at Clifton exceeded 3,000 m³/s in 1916. Evaporation rates are 7–10 times annual precipitation.

Historical accounts from [[Pattie]] (1820s trapping), the [[Kearny Expedition]] (1846) and later surveys describe a river lined with cottonwood, willow and mesquite. Post-1904 floods removed most riparian vegetation in the Safford Valley and widened the channel by up to 12 times. Grazing, drought and flooding are identified as the principal drivers of change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Terrestrial vegetation:** The flora comprises 88 families, 277 genera, 389 species and 394 taxa (the authors estimate 65–85% of the flora was recorded). Vegetation includes Semidesert Grassland on slopes, creosote-ocotillo Desertscrub on terraces, pinyon-juniper and Madrean Evergreen woodland at higher elevations, and riparian bosques and broad-leafed riparian stands (cottonwood, sycamore, walnut, willow, seepwillow). Mesquite made up 95–99% of trees on several mainstream transects. Salt cedar (*Tamarix*) was largely absent from canyon reaches, and the authors suggest that canyon erosion prevented its establishment.

**Birds:** A total of 183 bird species was observed during the two-year study; 58 of 98 nesting species were permanent residents. Breeding density in broad-leafed riparian habitat was about 170–260 individuals/40 ha, and the Gila River Complex has an unusually rich raptor fauna (22 kinds, including [[Black Hawk]] and [[Zone-tailed Hawk]]). Bird species diversity correlated positively with foliage height diversity (P>0.01) across 14 transects.

**Mammals:** 58 non-domesticated species were confirmed, plus 13 probable. Trapping success was very low (1.95% overall across ~34,560 trap days), and the authors attribute this partly to prolonged drought. Cactus mouse dominated most habitats. The [[Arizona Gray Squirrel]] record represents a substantial southern range extension.

**Herpetofauna:** About 64 species were recorded (amphibians and reptiles). Fifteen were semi-aquatic or aquatic. Non-native [[Bullfrog]] was found along the mainstream, and the [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]] was abundant in the Gila but absent from Eagle and Bonita creeks. The authors consider the spiny softshell and painted turtle probably introduced.

**Aquatic systems:** Water quality was studied at mainstream, tributary, spring and hot-spring sites, including [[Gillard Hot Springs]] and [[Clifton Hot Springs]]. Physico-chemical, bacteriological (total, fecal coliform and fecal streptococci) and algal (diatom and filamentous) data were collected. Benthic invertebrates were sampled quantitatively. The fish fauna includes native chubs of the genus *Gila* (including the [[Colorado River Chub]]), and the common [[Channel Catfish]] was present. Fishes were scarcely affected by floods, according to the authors.

**Endangered and proposed taxa:** Four plants on the 1975 Federal proposed list were found: *Echeveria* (Mexican), *Echinocereus triglochidiatus* var. *arizonicus*, *Fraxinus anomala* and *Pellaea ternifolia*. Protection under Arizona native plant laws was noted for *Echeveria*.

**Management concerns:** Grazing, off-road vehicles, flooding, groundwater pumping (Eagle Creek diverted ~28% of annual flow), mining (Morenci) and recreation were identified as threats. The report recommends protection of riparian corridors and microhabitats.

## Key Findings

- Riparian corridors are the most important habitats, supporting a disproportionate share of birds, mammals and herpetofauna.
- The Gila River Complex lies in a Chihuahuan–Sonoran transition zone, reflected in mixed plant and bird faunas.
- Mean annual flows are highly variable and flood-driven; a single 1916 San Francisco River flood exceeded 3,000 m³/s.
- Riparian vegetation was largely destroyed in the 1904–1917 floods; the channel has since narrowed, but cottonwood regeneration has failed since the 1930s.
- Eagle Creek water pumping (average ~28% of annual discharge since 1945) reduces flow, causing intermittency below the pump station.
- Black Hawk and other riparian raptors are vulnerable to human disturbance and flood-driven prey loss.
- Small-mammal densities were low, likely reflecting drought and local habitat constraints.
- The authors recommend management that treats small, local microhabitats as critical for maintaining regional diversity.
