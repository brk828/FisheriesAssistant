# Fishing Effort and Harvest by Arizona's Licensed Resident Anglers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #recreational-fishing #angler-survey

## Executive Summary

This report presents results from the first year (1980) of a three-year mail survey program designed to estimate statewide angling effort and fish harvest by Arizona's licensed resident anglers. A random 10% sample of Class A and Class F license holders received questionnaires asking about fishing trips, days fished, locations visited, and fish harvested across 34 designated Fishing Areas. Approximately 35% of delivered questionnaires were returned, yielding 9,571 usable responses representing 3.2% of all resident licensees. The survey documented that resident anglers made approximately 2,108,000 trips and spent 2,999,000 days fishing in Arizona during 1980, harvesting over 10 million fish. [[Trout]] comprised the plurality of harvest (26.1%), followed by [[Sunfish]] (20.5%) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (19.6%). The data were analyzed by License Class, Preference Group (trout only, warm water only, or both), and Residence Zone to characterize geographic patterns and angler behavior statewide.

## Background & Description

The Arizona Game and Fish Department initiated this comprehensive angler mail survey in 1980 to address the lack of statewide information on fishing effort and harvest. While localized creel surveys had provided data on specific fisheries, no systematic statewide assessment existed. The survey targeted resident license holders only; non-resident, one-day, and complimentary pioneer licenses were excluded. Arizona resident license holders accounted for 84% of all anglers licensed to fish in Arizona, meaning the survey likely represents more than 84% of statewide angling effort and harvest.

The state was divided into 34 [[Fishing Areas]] based on similarity of fisheries resources, management approaches, fish species present, access, and angler use patterns. Additionally, 22 [[Residence Zones]] were delineated based on population centers, political boundaries, and zip codes to analyze geographic patterns in angling demand. Three License Classes were examined: Class A (non-trout species only), Class A with Trout Stamp (permits trout harvest), and Class F (combination hunting and fishing license, trout permitted without additional stamp).

Total 1980 license sales included 302,479 Class A licenses (without trout stamp), 106,176 Class A with Trout Stamp, and 108,868 Class F licenses. Almost 50% of resident license purchasers resided in the Phoenix vicinity, with another 14% from Tucson. The questionnaire asked anglers to report trips and days fished by area, distinguishing between lake and stream fishing, and to enumerate fish caught and kept by species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's diverse aquatic habitats support both cold-water trout fisheries and warm-water species assemblages. The survey documented that approximately 65% of anglers fished exclusively in lakes, 9% only in streams, and 26% in both habitat types. Lake anglers accounted for approximately 78% (2.3 million) of all angler days, with lake fishermen averaging 9.6 days compared to 2.8 stream days for stream-only anglers.

Geographic proximity to fisheries strongly influenced angling patterns. Residents of zones with locally abundant trout fisheries (e.g., Zone D - [[Flagstaff]]) spent 1.1 days per trip fishing for trout, indicating primarily local day trips. In contrast, warm-water anglers from these same zones averaged 1.8 days per trip, requiring longer travel to reach warm-water fisheries. Conversely, residents of zones with only warm-water fisheries locally available (Zones J and R) averaged 2.3 days per trip when targeting trout, reflecting the need for extended travel.

The [[Colorado River]] and associated reservoirs ([[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Powell]]) represent important boundary waters where Arizona residents constitute as little as 4% of total angler populations. Residents living along or near the Colorado River (Zones C, I, and R) accounted for 24% of trips and 19% of angler days statewide.

Local fishery composition influenced harvest patterns. Trout comprised over 60% of fish harvested by residents of Zones D and F, where most of the state's trout fishing opportunities exist. Similarly, 31% of the [[Striped Bass]] harvest was caught by the 3.4% of anglers residing in Zone C, local to striped bass fisheries. Phoenix residents, despite having only warm-water fisheries locally available, caught 43.8% of the state's trout harvest, demonstrating willingness to travel for preferred species.

## Key Findings

**Angling Effort:**
- Licensed resident anglers made approximately 2,108,000 trips and spent 2,999,000 days fishing in Arizona in 1980
- Of total effort, approximately 638,000 trips and 1,014,000 days targeted trout; 1,470,000 trips and 1,984,000 days targeted warm-water species
- Anglers fishing for both trout and warm water species averaged the most effort: 10.9 trips and 16.0 days per angler annually
- Trout-only anglers averaged 5.0 trips and 7.7 days; warm-water-only anglers averaged 8.6 trips and 11.4 days
- Class F licensees who fished averaged 9.5 trips and 14.0 days, more than any other license group, though 29.8% of Class F licensees did not fish at all
- Phoenix and Tucson area residents accounted for 48% of trips and 54% of angler days statewide

**Fish Harvest:**
- Over 10 million fish were harvested statewide by resident anglers in 1980
- [[Trout]]: 2,660,400 fish (26.1% of total harvest)
- [[Sunfish]] (primarily [[Bluegill]]): 2,088,500 fish (20.5%)
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 1,990,500 fish (19.6%)
- [[Crappie]]: 1,419,400 fish (13.9%)
- [[Catfish]] (channel and flathead): 893,200 fish (8.8%)
- [[Carp]]: 197,000 fish (1.9%)
- [[Smallmouth Bass]]: 167,700 fish (1.6%)
- [[Striped Bass]]: 144,700 fish (1.4%)
- [[Tilapia]]: 138,800 fish (1.4%)
- [[Bullhead]]: 103,300 fish (1.0%)
- [[Northern Pike]] and [[Walleye]]: 101,500 fish combined (1.0%)
- [[White Bass]]: 95,600 fish (0.9%)
- [[Bullfrogs]]: 64,400 (0.6%)
- [[Soft-shelled Turtles]]: 6,700 (0.1%)

**Trout Harvest Patterns:**
- Approximately 40% of all licensed anglers caught one or more trout during 1980
- Over 27% of anglers who specifically targeted trout caught none
- 50% of trout anglers caught fewer than six trout annually (median harvest)
- 23% caught more than 20 trout; 8% caught more than 50 trout
- Class F licensees accounted for 41% of statewide fish harvest but only 36% of license sales
- Almost 11% of Class A licensees without Trout Stamps voluntarily reported illegally catching approximately 80,000 trout

**License Class Patterns:**
- Class F licensees reported highest average catch rates for trout, all bass species, catfish, bullhead, crappie, sunfish, bullfrogs, and "other" unidentified fish
- Anglers with Trout Stamps accounted for 53% of resident trout harvest
- Class A licensees with Trout Stamp averaged fewer trips but more days fished than those without stamps

**Survey Methodology:**
- Questionnaire return rate: 35% overall (39.9% for Class F, 25.6% for Class A without trout stamp)
- Sample size: 9,571 usable responses from 27,532 delivered questionnaires
- Data analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS)
- Potential biases acknowledged: nonresponse bias, memory inaccuracy, species identification errors, questionnaire confusion, and exaggeration
- Harvest estimates considered less reliable than effort estimates due to tendency of respondents to round numbers and potential overreporting
- No statistical reliability measures calculated; reproducibility in subsequent annual surveys intended as primary validation

**Management Implications:**
- Baseline data suitable for evaluating long-term trends when combined with subsequent survey years
- Geographic patterns in effort and harvest can inform regional fisheries management decisions
- High trout harvest relative to other species justifies continued investment in trout stocking programs
- Data storage and retrieval systems permit additional multiple levels of analysis for specific user needs
