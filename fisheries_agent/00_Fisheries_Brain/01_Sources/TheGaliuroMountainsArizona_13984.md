# The Galiuro Mountains, Arizona

#geology #physiography #basin-range

## Executive Summary

Davis and Brooks (1930), published in the *American Journal of Science*, interpret the [[Galiuro Mountains]] of southeastern Arizona as a tilted, dissected [[Fault-Block Mountains|fault block]] typical of the [[Basin and Range Province]]. The authors, based on field visits in the spring of 1929, argue that the range's southwestern escarpment was produced by up-faulting, that its northeastern side is tilted, and that the adjoining troughs were down-faulted and filled with detritus. The paper does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton, or water chemistry. Its aquatic relevance is limited to the [[San Pedro River]] and to historical interpretations of ancient playa lakes.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The range trends northwest-southeast, is roughly 50 to 60 miles long and 10 to 15 miles wide, and its crest reaches 4,000 to 6,000 feet. It descends 3,000 to 4,000 feet to the [[San Pedro Valley]] on the southwest.
- **Valley floor elevations:** About 3,200 feet opposite the southeastern end of the range and about 2,000 feet near the northwestern end, where the San Pedro joins the [[Gila River]] valley near Winkelman.
- **Nearby settlements:** Mammoth (an abandoned mining district village, 20 miles above Winkelman), Benson (55 miles farther upstream, on the Southern Pacific railroad), and Sombrero Butte.
- **Neighboring ranges:** The [[Little Dragoon Mountains]] and [[Winchester Mountains]] extend the range south and southeast. The Santa Catalina and Rincon mountains lie southwest of the San Pedro trough, and the Pinaleno (Graham) mountains lie northeast.
- **Terminology:** The authors use "valley" only for erosional valleys and call aggraded intermont basins "plains," with marginal slopes termed [[Bajada|bajadas]]. They compare this to the zoological sense of "fish" that includes whales.
- **Drainage:** Copper Creek and Mulberry Wash head within the range and flow southwest to the San Pedro. [[Arivaipa Creek]] cuts a deep canyon through the northwestern third, carrying drainage from the northeastern trough across the range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a physiographic and geological study. Its environmental content concerns landforms, deposits, and paleo-water bodies rather than living aquatic communities.

- **Historical water bodies:** Blake (1902, 1903) proposed that the San Pedro silts were marine or lake deposits. He reported volcanic glass and diatoms, and a specialist consulted by Blake called the diatoms "mostly marine." Albert Mann (Carnegie Institution) noted by letter that "marine" diatoms need not have grown in the sea and may have lived in saline or mineralized playa-lake water. Davis and Brooks reject a large lake or sea arm for the trough.
- **Playa and spring environments:** Thin gypsum beds in the silts suggest shallow, local playas or salt-lake basins. A bone-bearing greenish clay is interpreted as marginal freshwater springs, where animals became "hopelessly mired." The authors infer that saline lakes must have persisted "a few thousand years."
- **Fossil vertebrates:** Gidley reports proboscideans, edentates, rodents, birds, and turtles from silts near Benson. These are non-fish taxa, and the paper gives no fish records.
- **Modern vegetation and flow:** The San Pedro floodplain supports mesquite and cottonwood groves and irrigated alfalfa. The benchlands and uplands are dominated by creosote bush and cholla. The river is wet-weather and braided at Mammoth, and a channel at Benson, 30 feet or more deep and over 150 feet wide, filled to overflowing in the destructive flood of September 1927.
- **Sediment transport:** Erosion of the uplifted range now carries fine detritus through side streams to the San Pedro, the Gila, the Colorado, and the Gulf of California.

## Key Findings

**1. Fault-block interpretation.** The range is regarded as "in essence, a tilted fault-block." Up-faulting occurred along the western base, with northeastward tilting. The adjoining troughs were down-faulted or down-tilted. The authors state that the scarp reflects physiographic evidence, and no independent geological confirmation was found.

**2. Lava cover and sub-lava relief.**
- Andesite cover is thickest through the middle of the range, where it measures 1,200 to 1,500 feet.
- Before the lava was erupted, the basement had hilly relief of 500 to 1,000 feet over 10 to 20 miles.
- Earlier writers (Blake, Campbell, Darton, Ross) had described a syncline or faulting differently, and the authors judge several of Blake's structural claims improbable.

**3. Fault exposure at Copper Creek.** A fault exposure at Copper Creek shows steeply inclined, northwest-striking fault contact. It is marked by 15 to 20 feet of crushed rock ("gouge"). At least 800 feet, and probably 1,000 feet or more, of uplift occurred after the upland gravels were deposited.

**4. Sombrero Butte.** This detached lava slab, about 2 miles long and half a mile or more wide, is interpreted as incompletely up-faulted, with a failure of uplift of 500 feet or more. Its lava section totals perhaps 1,000 feet. A fault plane dips 65° west in a Mulberry Wash road cut.

**5. Limits of physiographic fault determination.** The authors acknowledge that both geological and physiographic methods of fault detection are fallible. They cite Baulig's work on the Central Plateau of France as an example of unrecorded faults in massive crystalline rocks.

**6. Post-faulting deformation of detritus.** The piedmont deposits were uplifted after deposition. Silt beds dip 3° to 5° commonly, with a steepest dip of 23° in a small flexure. Gravels slant up to 7° away from the mountains. Coarse detritus stops well short of the medial silts, which is taken as evidence of post-depositional upheaval. The authors also cite warping at the Rincon and Santa Catalina margins and tentatively link it to warping beneath the Tucson intermont plain.

**7. Sub-surface depth.** Deep wells in Sulphur Spring Valley reached 1,095, 1,050, and 1,200 feet without reaching bedrock, except for granite in the last 10 feet of the 1,200-foot well. The deepest boring in the San Pedro trough is the county well at Benson, at 1,505 feet in sand and gravel.

**8. Geomorphic stage.** The authors argue the range has reached a "fan-dented" stage but has not advanced to the pedimented stage seen in the Santa Teresa and Little Dragoon ranges. Its piedmont gravels were later dissected by renewed uplift.

**9. Drainage evolution.** Arivaipa Creek is judged consequent on the earlier uplift and antecedent to the later uplift, having cut a valley up to 1,500 feet deep where it enters the range. The San Pedro river replaced the earlier centripetal playa drainage.

**10. Conclusion.** The authors accept the Galiuro Mountains as a "Gilbert block": a tilted, eroded fault block with a hilly, pre-faulting "Powell surface" under a lava cover. They concede that many structural and historical details remain unresolved.
