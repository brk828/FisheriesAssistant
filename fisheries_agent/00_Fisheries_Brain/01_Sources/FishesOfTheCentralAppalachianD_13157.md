# Fishes of the Central Appalachian Drainages: Their Distribution and Dispersal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #zoogeography #Cyprinidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Robert E. Jenkins]], [[Ernest A. Lachner]] and [[Frank J. Schwartz]], is Part III (Vertebrates) of a 1970 symposium monograph on the distributional history of the southern Appalachian biota. It treats the native freshwater fish fauna of the central Appalachian drainages, covering 313 native species and subspecies that are mostly stream fishes. The authors analyse distribution, diversity, endemism and faunal resemblance by drainage, relate these patterns to drainage history, [[Stream Capture]] events, [[Pleistocene Glaciation]] effects and physical barriers, and conclude that the Atlantic slope–Ohio basin divide is the most important faunal boundary in the region.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Drainages from the Peedee northward to the Potomac on the Atlantic slope, and from the Monongahela to the Tennessee within the Ohio River basin. The study region was not glaciated and has very few natural lakes; [[Mountain Lake]] in Virginia is the only natural montane lake and has a depauperate fauna.
- **Taxonomy:** Nomenclature follows the third edition of the American Fisheries Society common and scientific names list ([[Bailey et al. 1970]]). Twenty-three undescribed species or subspecies are included.
- **Habitat categories:** Fishes were classed by typical habitat as lowland, upland, montane, or by stream size (creek under about 30 ft wide, stream 30–200 ft, river over 200 ft).
- **Status codes (table 1):** N (native), E (endemic to one drainage), NP and NI/IP (probably native or probably introduced), I (introduced). Exotic introductions include [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).
- **Diadromous and euryhaline species** (e.g., *Petromyzon marinus*, *Morone saxatilis*) are listed separately because they may compete with strictly freshwater fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Gradient:** An Average Gradient Index was calculated for each family and drainage. Upland forms dominate: 146 forms (47%) are entirely upland, and 252 (81%) are partly or entirely upland. Only 48 (15%) are strictly lowland and 13 (4%) strictly montane.
- **Montane fauna:** Depauperate, attributed to the rigors of swift-water adaptation and the scarcity of large streams in montane districts.
- **Lowlands:** Non-tidal Coastal Plain waters are thought to have offered the most constant conditions, favouring relict groups.
- **Water chemistry:** Knowledge of chemical preferences is insufficient for detailed analysis; aquatic vegetation was judged to have little direct effect on drainage-level distribution.
- **Impoundments and pollution:** Many large channels were impounded over the past four decades. Several upper Roanoke species may soon be endangered by impoundments, pollution and introductions.
- **Waterfalls:** [[Kanawha Falls]] (about 24 ft) and [[Cumberland Falls]] (about 85 ft) are major barriers that divide faunas.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size:** 313 native forms (species and subspecies) among 22 families; about 277 species at minimum. This is roughly half of the approximately 500 North American freshwater species.
- **Endemism:** 51 forms (16%) are endemic to one drainage; 31 (10%) are exclusively shared by two drainages.
- **Dominant families:** Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae and Percidae together contain 271 forms (87%), and 47 of the 51 endemics.
- **Atlantic–Ohio divide:** 179 forms (57%) occur only in the Ohio basin, 77 (25%) only on the Atlantic slope, and 57 (18%) on both sides. Of the endemics, 39 (76%) and of exclusively shared forms 22 (71%) are Ohio basin species.
- **Richest drainages:** Tennessee (191 forms; 27 endemics; 19 exclusively shared) and Cumberland (150 forms; 6 endemics; 15 exclusively shared). The Tennessee has more endemics than any other North American drainage.
- **Roanoke:** The richest Atlantic slope fauna (83 forms; 6 endemics), with a Distinction Index of 31, the highest on the Atlantic slope. It was probably a major centre of evolution and dispersal, receiving Ohio basin (Teays) derivatives via capture from the New drainage.
- **Depauperate faunas:** New drainage above Kanawha Falls (47 forms; 5 endemics) and upper Cumberland above Cumberland Falls (38 forms; no endemics); attributed to high gradient, lack of lowland habitat, falls as barriers and Pleistocene cooling.
- **Cape Fear–Neuse break:** One of the sharpest faunal breaks on the Atlantic slope, separated by 28 forms with range terminations.
- **James–Roanoke break:** Sharp, especially for catostomids (only 8 of 14 Roanoke suckers occur in the James).
- **Centrarchids:** Lowest Average Gradient Index among the five largest families (2.0), reflecting lowland restriction northward.
- **Stream capture:** Geological evidence exists for captures between nearly all adjacent drainage pairs (e.g., Roanoke–New, James–Potomac, Cumberland–Kentucky). Captures of large rivers were rare, so small-stream species were most likely to cross divides.
- **Historical drivers:** Relict distributions and Pleistocene glaciation (cool climates, Greater Susquehanna and Teays routes) strongly shaped faunas, with historical factors judged more important than drainage size alone.
- **Summary conclusion:** Faunal richness is linked to habitat diversity, drainage size, proximity to rich faunas and historical connections; the Tennessee and Roanoke are the principal centres of diversity and endemism.
