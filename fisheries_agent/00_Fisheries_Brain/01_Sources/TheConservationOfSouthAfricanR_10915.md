# The Conservation of South African Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is the proceedings report of a three-day workshop held at [[Midmar Dam]], Natal, in August 1985, convened by the Nature Conservation Research Division of the Foundation for Research Development, CSIR, and edited by J H O'Keeffe. The workshop aimed to build a consensus on [[River Conservation]] and to give conservationists, managers and planners guidelines on river ecology, monitoring, management options, legislation and research priorities. The core argument is that South African rivers, the country's only large-scale surface water resource, are being degraded by water abstraction, regulation, pollution and catchment land use, and that conservation must be managed holistically at the whole-[[Catchment]] scale through mitigation rather than strict preservation.

## Background & Description

The report is a multi-chapter synthesis (eight chapters plus two appendices) prepared with a companion [[Conservation Status Map]] of South Africa's major rivers at 1:2.5 million scale, which classified rivers into four conservation categories. The workshop was financed through the Ecosystem Programmes section of the FRD and produced a bilingual (English and Afrikaans) summary. The scope is river ecosystems in general, covering invertebrates, algae, plankton, water chemistry, flow and habitat. Fish are mentioned mainly in relation to endemic species (for example endemic redfin *Barbus* species), introduced predators and fish passage, rather than as a focal taxon.

Chapter overview:

- **Chapter 1, Riverine Ecosystems:** Describes river zonation from headwaters through middle reaches, lower reaches and estuary, and presents key hypotheses including the [[River Continuum Concept]], [[Nutrient Spiralling]], the [[Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis]], [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] and river resilience.
- **Chapter 2, Conservation Aims:** Adopts an holistic, IUCN-based definition of conservation and lists criteria (naturalness, representativeness, diversity, rarity, fragility, geographical position, size) and goals such as maintaining diversity of function.
- **Chapter 3, Uses and Human Impact:** Reviews agriculture (about 73% of water abstraction), urban areas (about 8%), rural settlement, industry (about 4 to 6%), power generation, mining and recreation.
- **Chapter 4, Management Options:** Proposes protective, manipulative and legislative management, with conservation importance and conservation status graded on two scales.
- **Chapter 5, Catchment Response:** Covers geology, soils, topography, climate and the biosphere, including riparian zones, and their control of flow, stormflow and water quality.
- **Chapter 6, Monitoring and Surveillance:** Distinguishes survey, surveillance and monitoring, and recommends benthic macro-invertebrates (stones-in-current) as the minimum biological indicator.
- **Chapter 7, Legislation:** Reviews about 15 principal and partially relevant Acts and concludes that catchment-level legislation and authorities are needed.
- **Chapter 8, Research and Information Needs:** Argues for long-term, interdisciplinary, holistic research and lists priorities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Longitudinal zonation.** Headwater mountain streams are well oxygenated, low in nutrients and light, and depend on allochthonous leaf litter; invertebrate communities are dominated by insects (mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, midges) and are shredder-rich. Middle reaches are based on autochthonous production, with grazers and collectors and more abundant invertebrates and insectivorous fish and birds. Mature lower reaches are muddier, carry phytoplankton and deposit feeders, and are prone to oxygen depletion. Estuaries can be among the most productive ecosystems and are strongly affected by upstream flow reduction.

**Catchment linkage.** The river mirrors its catchment. Geology controls water transmission, flow pattern and water chemistry; soils control infiltration and runoff; topography controls gradient, drainage density and sediment deposition; and riparian vegetation supplies food and stabilises banks. Acid rain, fertiliser and biocide runoff, and sewage, including septic-tank drainage, are identified as major non-point pollution sources.

**Major human impacts.** The report identifies salinisation under irrigation, eutrophication from phosphates, sediment-driven silting (for example in Natal estuaries, where silt was identified as a major problem in 30 estuaries), pollution by heavy metals and biocides, flow regulation by impoundments, interbasin transfers that spread pest and alien species, and bank erosion from riparian clearing.

**Other documented concerns.** Acid mine drainage in the Olifants River reached pH 2 to 3 in places. Blackfly outbreaks followed stabilised flows on the Orange River, and bilharzia transmission increased in the Transvaal Lowveld after irrigation development.

## Key Findings

- **Water scarcity dominates.** Rivers provide almost all exploitable surface water in South Africa; there is no national policy for river conservation and no legislation specifically protecting rivers as functioning ecosystems.
- **Holistic, catchment-based management is required.** Passive preservation is not possible because rivers reflect upstream catchment events; the management unit should be the whole catchment.
- **Diversity of function is the central goal.** The workshop frames conservation as maintaining essential functions (water supply, nutrient recycling, self-purification, recreation) within sustainable exploitation.
- **Flow regulation is the most serious effect of impoundment.** It reduces natural annual flow variation, degrades channels downstream, and shifts biota. The [[Reset Distance]] below a dam should be minimised.
- **Sustainable water allocation.** Roberts (1983) proposed an allocation for river maintenance; a global figure of 11% of flow was discussed as a starting point, to be replaced by scientifically quantified requirements for individual rivers.
- **Management categories.** Options include formal reserves (rarely practical for downstream reaches), participatory conservation through Natural Heritage Sites and conservancies, and generalised compromise with development that minimises harm.
- **Legislation is fragmented.** Acts including the Water Act No 54 of 1956, the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act No 43 of 1983 and the Forest Act No 122 of 1984 contain relevant clauses, but are poorly enforced, under-staffed and often lack regulations. Gaps include no control over river systems in totality, no limit on aggregate storage capacity in a catchment, and no control over rivers in self-governing or national states.
- **Surveillance design.** Distinguishes natural variability from permanent change. The authors note that no South African river has been surveyed long enough (twenty years or more of data are needed) to understand the long-term drought cycle. Recommended minimum surveillance uses benthic macro-invertebrates from stones in current, sampled in early to mid-winter in the summer rainfall area, using a 300 micron net; a biotic index value consistently above 6 indicates considerable deterioration.
- **Research gaps are severe.** Taxonomic knowledge is inadequate: only 14% of known adult Afrotropical [[Trichoptera]] are described at the larval stage, and 41 adult Baetidae species were described versus 29 known as nymphs. Of 422 registered scientific projects in 1984, 69 were river-related, with fish the most-studied group.
- **Long-term funding is needed.** Long-term studies (5 to 10 years for clear-felling effects) are poorly served by typical two- to three-year funding cycles; the authors recommend sustained, interdisciplinary programmes and a Red Data Book for endangered river systems.
- **Research priorities** (Appendix 1, 16 items) were ranked by 23 respondents. The top-ranked were the development of an expert-system-based conservation status method, a consensus policy of conservation aims, and early-stage protocols for river development projects.
- **Conservation classification.** The Appendix 2 national system grades rivers from Class I (both biotic and physical elements pristine) to Class IV (severely degraded), with an unknown class where data are insufficient. Gaps remain in Swaziland, Transkei and the northern Cape.
