# Avian Use of Quitobaquito Springs Oasis, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona

#ornithology #avian-ecology #riparian-birds

## Executive Summary

This Technical Report (No. 13) from the [[Cooperative National Park Resources Studies Unit]] at the University of Arizona, published December 1983, examines how birds use the two management areas of [[Quitobaquito Springs]], a desert oasis within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. Breeding and migrant birds were censused in spring 1983 on two paired riparian plots using the [[Spot-Map Method]]. The study had two aims: to test whether the two [[National Park Service]] management areas suit birds, and to produce a baseline description of the oasis avifauna.

The [[Wildlife Resting Plot]] supported a slightly higher density and diversity of breeding birds, but the difference was only marginally significant. The [[Visitor Use Plot]] held significantly more migrants, which the authors attribute mainly to its mature [[Cottonwood Trees]]. The authors conclude there is "no basis" for the current placement of the two management areas with respect to avian use.

## Background & Description

Quitobaquito is a spring-fed oasis on the U.S.-Mexico boundary near the center of the [[Sonoran Desert]], at an elevation of 332 m. Two perennial springs feed a man-made diked pond of roughly 60 x 70 m. A mesquite woodland (*Prosopis velutina* and *P. pubescens*) with scattered cottonwoods surrounds the pond. The Foreword describes the oasis as a [[Man and the Biosphere Reserve]] attraction and notes that the nearby [[Sonoyta River]] is intermittent and largely choked by [[Saltcedar]], so Quitobaquito is the main perennial surface-water source in the area.

The oasis is known for its only native population of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), which may be a distinct subspecies and was then being proposed for endangered listing. Uncommon [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] and [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] populations (*Kinosternon sonoriense*, *K. flavescens*) also occur in the pond. One aster, *Machaeranthera arizonica*, is endemic to Quitobaquito and one or two other nearby border localities.

Human use has changed the site considerably. Papago (Tohono O'odham) people used the spring seasonally, and later settlers channeled and diked the outflow, cut wood, grazed livestock and planted orchards. After the monument was established in 1937, residents farmed there until the 1950s, when the [[National Park Service]] acquired the site. Shortly afterward the open water was drained, bulldozed and diked into a discrete pond. This removed the shallow, gravelly shoreline that once attracted shorebirds and wading birds, while dense water-loving vegetation spread around the pond. Livestock were removed in the 1960s and 1970s.

In the early 1970s the Park Service designated two management areas: a closed wildlife resting area south of the pond and a visitor-use area north of it, with trails, picnic tables and interpretive signs. Both were unchanged as of 1983.

Earlier bird work at the site includes Mearns (1894 visit, published 1907), [[Laurence Huey]] (1942, vertebrate survey of the monument), and [[Max Hensley]] (1954), who recorded 59 species, 15 of them breeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study plots.** The two plots are the Wildlife Resting Plot (0.88 ha) and the Visitor Use Plot (0.82 ha). Both are restricted to woody riparian vegetation above 1.8 m. The resting plot is mostly undisturbed apart from a dirt road on its southern edge. The visitor plot holds trails, picnic tables and signs.

**Vegetation (Table 1).**

| Parameter | Wildlife Resting Plot | Visitor Use Plot |
|---|---|---|
| Woody species | 18 | 15 |
| Woody plants/ha | 787 | 710 |
| Mesquite composition | 81% | 54% |
| Total canopy volume | 4727 m³ | 4550 m³ |
| Mesquite share of canopy | 82% | 67% |
| Cottonwood share of canopy | 1% | 11% |

The resting plot is mesquite-dominated, while the visitor plot has more cottonwood canopy.

**Setting.** Traffic noise from Mexican Highway 2, just south of the boundary, often hinders hearing bird calls. Non-riparian desert species such as [[Gambel's Quail]], [[Cactus Wren]] and [[Gila Woodpecker]] were treated as visitants because they nest in adjacent desertscrub.

**Island biogeography.** The authors describe Quitobaquito as a riparian "island" in a desertscrub "sea." Its small size and low habitat diversity help explain the low breeding diversity, applying [[Island Biogeography]] theory. The authors note they could not fully separate the effects of island size from habitat homogeneity.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** The two plots were censused for breeding birds 14 times and for migrants nine times between 28 March and 26 May 1983, at 0600–0700 and 1745–1845 hours, with up to two censuses per day. Breeding densities used spot-map data, except for White-winged and Mourning doves, which were counted by nests. Diversity was measured with the [[Shannon Diversity Index]] (H'), and plots were compared by t-test at the 5% level.

**Breeding birds (Table 2).**
- Combined breeding density was 470.1 pairs/40 ha (20 pairs in the plots). The resting plot held 12.0 pairs and the visitor plot 8.0 pairs.
- Breeding species numbered 10 in the resting plot, 7 in the visitor plot and 11 combined.
- H' values were 0.9587 (resting plot), 0.7943 (visitor plot) and 0.9799 (combined). The resting plot's diversity was significantly higher (p < .05).
- If the [[Yellow-Breasted Chat]] is excluded, the difference is not significant (p > .05), so the result is only marginally significant. Chat activity was confined to under one week, which suggests migrants.
- Verdin, Phainopepla and Hooded Oriole were among the regular breeders. The [[House Finch]] nested in cholla west of the pond but defends only its nest sites, so it was not counted as an on-site breeder.
- Brown-headed and Bronzed cowbirds were not found breeding in the plots.

**Comparison with other riparian sites.**
- Szaro and Jakle (1982), a less structurally diverse tributary of the Gila River: 388 pairs/40 ha.
- Gavin and Sowls (1975), San Pedro River: 476 pairs/40 ha, with much higher species diversity.
- Carothers et al. (1974), Verde River cottonwood forests: 847 pairs/40 ha.
- Breeding species at Quitobaquito (11) are fewer than at larger Arizona riparian sites (13 and 28 species).

**Historical change.** Hensley (1954) recorded 15 breeding species, including [[Killdeer]] and [[Crissal Thrasher]], which were absent in 1983. Killdeer declined after the open gravel shoreline was lost. Several species that are present now, including Cactus Wren, Brown Crested Flycatcher and Black-tailed Gnatcatcher, were not recorded by Hensley.

**Migratory birds (Table 3).**
- Resting plot: 132 individuals (a flock of 60 [[Brewer's Blackbird]] accounts for much of this), 18 species, H' 0.8603, J' 0.6853.
- Visitor plot: 123 individuals, 32 species, H' 1.2119, J' 0.8052.
- Without the blackbird flock, the resting plot had about half as many migrants as the visitor plot.
- The visitor plot's higher migrant diversity (highly significant) is linked to its more mature cottonwood canopy. Stevens et al. (1977) found riparian cottonwood areas hold more than 10 times as many migrants as adjacent non-riparian habitat.
- The monument-wide Foreword cites 193 migrating and wintering species and 220 total avian species recorded at Quitobaquito, from roughly 275 species for the monument (from the erratum).

**Shorebirds.** [[Spotted Sandpiper]] and [[Black-Necked Stilt]] used the pond but could not find open sandy or gravelly shoreline to land on, so they were excluded from Table 3. Vegetation spread around the pond removed the shoreline habitat that shorebirds needed.

**Conclusions and management implications.**
- Breeding birds were marginally more diverse in the resting plot, and migrants were significantly more diverse in the visitor plot.
- Migrant differences are explained by cottonwood canopy. Breeding differences are harder to explain.
- The authors state there is "no basis" for the current placement of the two management areas with respect to bird use.
- The Foreword states that the studies aim to supply scientific information for a management plan that protects this "unique desert oasis" while allowing public use.
