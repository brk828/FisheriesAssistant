## Executive Summary

This 1976 technical report documents a multi-decade investigation into naturally occurring **salt seeps and springs** along the Upper Salt River in Arizona, a tributary system feeding the Gila River. The study, conducted by the Arizona Department of Health Services' Bureau of Water Quality Control, identifies and quantifies the sources of high total dissolved solids (TDS) — dominated by sodium and chloride — entering the river in a rugged, largely inaccessible ~20-mile reach above U.S. Highway 60. While the paper is not fish-centric, it explicitly frames water quality within the context of the river's multi-use value, including **fish and wildlife habitat**, and raises concerns about salinity, boron, and phosphate loading that have direct implications for aquatic ecosystem health and irrigation-based land productivity downstream.

## Background & Description

The Salt River originates from the confluence of the White River and Black River in the White Mountains of Arizona and flows southwest to join the [[Gila River]] near Phoenix. The watershed drains the south-facing **Mogollon Rim** escarpment and is regulated by a series of four dams and reservoirs (including [[Roosevelt Lake]]) that serve irrigation, domestic water supply, hydroelectric power, recreation, and fish and wildlife purposes.

Historical investigations date back to 1904 (Sheldon K. Baker) and 1928 (R. A. MacDonald), with subsequent unpublished Salt River Project reports by J. A. West (1939) and T. A. Hayden (1940). A 1973 intensive water quality survey by the Bureau of Water Quality Control, combined with a September 1973 Arizona State University survey (Sommerfeld, Olsen, and Love), pinpointed and quantified the major salt-contributing seeps and springs in the canyon reach between the White/Black River confluence and Roosevelt Lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity and habitat quality**: TDS concentrations in the White and Black Rivers were measured at 254 mg/l and 96 mg/l respectively, but increased dramatically to over 2,700 mg/l roughly 32 miles downstream at the Salt River Canyon gage — approximately 80% attributable to sodium and chloride ions. Such shifts in ionic composition and salinity represent a significant environmental gradient for any aquatic organisms, including fish, inhabiting this reach.
- **Temperature**: Seep and spring temperatures were observed at or above 20°C, with some as high as 29°C (Soda Spring, White River Springs), suggesting geothermal influence that could create localized thermal refugia or barriers within the river's fish habitat.
- **Boron and phosphate loading**: Elevated boron concentrations were observed in several seeps, and total phosphate loads in the Salt River exceed National Water Quality Criteria — both factors with potential downstream implications for aquatic productivity, primary producers, and consequently the food web supporting fish populations.
- **Iron precipitates**: Still pools formed by salt seeps exhibited orange precipitates indicative of iron salts, a visual marker of geochemical seep activity that may also affect local benthic habitat conditions.
- **Dilution dynamics**: Salt concentrations were observed to decrease as river flow increases, with the reservoir system (e.g., Roosevelt Lake) buffering annual variation — an important consideration for aquatic species relying on more stable downstream conditions.
- **Land and water use interactions**: The report notes that irrigation return flows are the primary source of dissolved salt replenishment in local groundwater, tying agricultural practices directly to long-term salinity trends affecting river and reservoir habitat.
- **Jurisdiction**: The upper Salt River and its major tributaries lie within the **Fort Apache Indian Reservation**, and all surface waters are appropriated under Arizona water law by the Salt River Water Users Association, framing habitat and water quality management within a complex legal and cultural context.

## Key Findings

- **Source confirmation**: The bulk of the Salt River's high TDS load originates from a ~20-mile inaccessible reach above U.S. Highway 60, not primarily from the well-known [[Salt Banks]] springs, which were found to contribute comparatively little TDS relative to total ungaged inflow.
- **Maximum observed salt concentrations** (mg/l TDS) across four major seep areas:
  - Soda Spring: up to 20,000 mg/l (29°C)
  - White River Springs (near mouth): up to 11,000 mg/l (29°C)
  - Abdoula Canyon Springs: up to 15,000 mg/l (20°C)
  - Salt Bank Springs: up to 24,000 mg/l
- **TDS load estimates** (Tons/Day) by seep/source, compiled across multiple historical surveys (MacDonald 1928, West 1939, Feth & Hem 1963, Bureau of Water Quality Control 1973):
  - Soda Spring: 162 (1973); 180 (Feth & Hem); 58 (MacDonald, computed); West data noted separately
  - Black River Seep(s): 63 (1973); 242 (computed, West-derived)
  - White River Springs: 291 (1973); 168 (Feth & Hem)
  - Salt River Springs (upper reach): 104 (1973); 12.25 (Feth & Hem); 426 (West, computed)
  - Abdoula Canyon Springs: 200–600 (1973); less than 57 (Feth & Hem); 213 (West)
  - Salt Bank Springs: 78.5 (1973); 140 (West, 1954 data)
- **Average system-wide TDS load**: 1,213 Tons/Day entering Roosevelt Lake, averaged over 1962–1972.
- **Ungaged inflow estimates** at the Salt River Canyon USGS gage ranged from 211–418 CFS across historical surveys, with associated TDS loads of 838–1,060 Tons/Day, and ungaged TDS contributions from Carrizo Creek, White River, and Black River gages estimated at 500–970 Tons/Day.
- **Two newly hypothesized seep locations** were identified through data analysis: one near the mouth of the Black River, and another on the upper Salt River reach above the mouth of Carrizo Creek — both determined by computation rather than direct sampling.
- **Management implications**: The report calls for continued interdisciplinary research, more precise stream gaging at spring/seep locations (acknowledged as logistically difficult given the terrain), and recognizes potential future economic mineral recovery opportunities from the salt seeps, alongside risks that upstream/downstream water development (e.g., additional hydroelectric projects or diversions) could exacerbate salinity impacts on the watershed's [[Fish and Wildlife|fish and wildlife]] resources.
