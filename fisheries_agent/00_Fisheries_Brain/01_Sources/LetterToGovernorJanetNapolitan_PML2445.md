# Letter to Governor Janet Napolitano on Native Fish Conservation Policy in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is a letter dated April 1, 2004, from [[Sally Stefferud]], a retired [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] employee and independent biologist with 25 years of experience in Arizona native fish conservation, to Governor [[Janet Napolitano]]. It urges the Governor to appoint [[Arizona Game And Fish Commission]] members with a stronger conservation ethic and knowledge of native fishes. It also criticizes the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] for favoring nonnative sport fish management, restricting scientific collecting permits, dismantling the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], and rejecting an independent successor group. The letter supports a resolution from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (December 2003) and calls for restored communication and cooperation among biologists.

## Background & Description

- **Author:** [[Sally Stefferud]], a biologist with 25 years of experience in the conservation of Arizona's native fishes. She is a former [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] employee (retired) who now works as an independent biologist and has worked closely with the Department.
- **Trigger:** Recent controversy over mountain lion management in the [[Santa Catalina Mountains]] prompted concerns about Commission and Department policies, which the letter extends to native fish issues.
- **Council resolution:** The [[Desert Fishes Council]], a 30-year-old professional society devoted to desert fish conservation, sent a resolution to the Governor in December 2003 (enclosed). It asked for Commissioners with stronger conservation ethics and knowledge of native fishes, and for policies that enhance and restore native fishes and aquatic ecosystems.
- **Position on governance:** The author opposes making the Department Director directly answerable to the Governor's office. She views the Commission system as sound if its members are knowledgeable and ethical, providing balance between science and politics.
- **Scope:** The author confines her comments to native fish issues, though she notes that communication problems may extend beyond this arena.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conservation status:** Native fishes are described as highly imperiled. The letter states that certain Commission policies and directives have been "actively destructive" of native fish and have stymied recovery efforts.
- **Core management conflict:** The Department faces internal conflict between mandates to enhance nonnative sport fish and to conserve native fish, whose welfare is threatened by nonnative species. The letter says the Department too often favors traditional sport fish management.
- **Habitat concerns:** The letter cites habitat impacts as the stated reason for disallowing student labor on the [[Aravaipa Creek]] monitoring project, though the author states that none could be identified over 35 years of prior sampling.
- **Basin-scale status:** The [[Desert Fishes Council]]-linked independent team's October 2003 report addressed the status of native and federally listed fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Study sites:** Fish sampling on the [[Coronado National Forest]] was conducted under a contract between the author's group, Arizona State University, and the Forest Service.

## Key Findings

1. **Scientific collecting permits have become restrictive.** Historically, permits encouraged information gathering on fish populations and ecosystems. The letter reports that the Department has recently shifted toward extensive, repetitious, and sometimes irrelevant questioning of permit applicants. The author describes many questions as ill-informed, aggressive, and unprofessional. Examples include:
   - A request that the author justify why the Forest Service did not perform the Coronado sampling with its own staff biologists.
   - A University of Arizona biologist's long-running Aravaipa Creek monitoring project, which was intensely questioned and issued with substantial restrictions.
   - A 35-year student-labor practice on the Aravaipa project that has now been disallowed.
   - The author knows of one permit denied outright, but several restrictions or partial denials.

2. **Dissolution of the Desert Fishes Recovery Team.** The Service disbanded this team, which was composed of leading native fish biologists from multiple agencies and universities and served for 19 years (the OCR text reads "lg years"), in November 2002, citing vague bureaucratic reasons. In March 2003, Department representatives publicly stated the disbanding was at the Department's urging, due to dissatisfaction with the team's activities and recommendations.

3. **Formation of a Department-controlled replacement.** The Department then formed the Native Fish Conservation Team. The author describes it as authoritarian in nature despite being billed as collaborative. Members reportedly cannot set the mission statement, meeting dates, agenda, minutes, or internal structure. Objections from attendees were omitted from minutes, and critics were publicly vilified.

4. **Rejection of the Independent Desert Fishes Team.** Former Recovery Team participants formed this unaffiliated forum to exchange information on native fish issues in Arizona and adjacent areas.
   - Its first meeting in March 2003 was convened by the Department to discuss objections to the group.
   - Department representatives opposed any group not led by the agency and considered key members unfit for significant roles.
   - Department participation was made contingent on the group accepting Department control.
   - The group continued independently, met three more times, and issued a well-received report in October 2003.
   - The Department has since refused further participation, and the U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service reportedly directed its biologists not to participate, apparently following a request from Director [[Duane Shroufe]] to Regional Director [[Dale Hall]].
   - Letters to Director Shroufe requesting participation and open communication went unanswered.

5. **Breakdown of communication and cooperation.** The author concludes that Commission and Department attitudes discourage native fish conservation by sidelining biologists whose viewpoints diverge from Commission or Department policies. This practice, she argues, shuts down dissenting voices and undermines the broad-based, innovative efforts needed to stem native fish declines.

6. **Recommendations.** The author urges the Governor to:
   - Replace Commission members with designees more sensitive to fish and wildlife issues and less focused on resource extraction and development.
   - Retain the Commission structure rather than making the Director answerable to the Governor's office.
   - Support cooperative Department work with other biologists and entities on native fish recovery.

7. **Enclosures.** The letter includes four enclosures, among them the Desert Fishes Council resolution and a letter sent to Director Shroufe by [[Sally Stefferud]], [[Paul Marsh]] of Arizona State University, and a colleague, which did not receive a reply.
