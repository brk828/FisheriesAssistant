# Three New Pupfish Species (*Cyprinodon*) from Chihuahua, México, and Arizona, USA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This formal species description, by [[Minckley]], [[Ralph R. Miller]], and [[Stephen M. Norris]], names three new pupfishes of the genus *Cyprinodon* that were long recognized as distinct. The species are [[Cyprinodon Pisteri]] (Guzmán Pupfish), [[Cyprinodon Albivelis]] (Whitefin Pupfish), and [[Cyprinodon Arcuatus]] (Monkey Spring Pupfish). Two occur in the closed-basin [[Guzmán Basin]] and [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] of northern Chihuahua, México. The third is restricted to the upper [[Santa Cruz River]] basin of southeastern Arizona and northern Sonora. *C. arcuatus* is now extinct in the wild. Its last natural population, at [[Monkey Spring]], was destroyed by introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] around 1971. The authors link the regional decline of these fishes to groundwater mining, stream diversion, channel incision, and nonnative fish introductions, and they discuss the biogeography of the group.

## Background & Description

**Context and methods.** [[Carl L. Hubbs]] recognized two of the three species in the 1920s and deposited preserved material from the Lago de Guzmán and Rio Yaqui basins. A third species from the Gila River drainage had been referred by Hubbs to *Cyprinodon nevadensis* (later *C. macularius*). The authors collected these fishes for many years. Morphometric measurements and meristic counts follow [[Miller]] (1943, 1948). Vertebral counts came from radiographs, with the urostyle counted as one. Cleared-and-stained specimens are noted as "CS." Hubbs' unpublished notes and data were made available to Miller in the 1950s.

**Diagnostic characters.** The species share the general pupfish form: a compressed, deep body, a nearly terminal mouth, and sexual dichromatism with brilliant breeding males. They differ in these ways:

- ***C. pisteri*** has a dusky to black dorsal fin in breeding males, a narrow caudal band, a thickened spike-like first dorsal ray, and usually 10 dorsal and anal rays. It has 7 pelvic rays, 26–28 lateral-line scales (mode 26), and a modal count of 26 vertebrae. The maximum observed size is 53 mm SL. The holotype is UMMZ 162629, a 41.5 mm male collected by Miller and H. E. Winn on 9 March 1950 at Ojo de Palomas Viejo, Chihuahua. Male fins are often enlarged, especially in the Rio del Carmen basin.
- ***C. albivelis*** is named for its breeding males, whose dorsal and anal fins are milky to pearly white with a blackened blotch. It has modally 9 dorsal and anal rays, 7 pelvic rays, 26–29 lateral-line scales (mode 27), and modally 28 total vertebrae. The maximum observed size is 54 mm SL. Its breast is fully scaled, and the cleithral scale is 2–4 times as wide as those following. Its holotype is a 36.8 mm male from Rio Papigóchic at Yepómera, Chihuahua, collected on 19 June 1978.
- ***C. arcuatus*** is identified by a distinctly convex predorsal profile that drops abruptly into a deep postdorsal concavity, most developed in breeding males. Breeding males lack yellow or orange pigment on the caudal fin or peduncle. The species has weak lepidonts and modally six preopercular pores. Its maximum size is 46 mm SL. The holotype is UMMZ 162700, a 39.3 mm male from Monkey Spring, Santa Cruz County, Arizona. The name *arcuatus* comes from the Latin *arcus* (arch or bow), referring to the convex dorsal profile.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Guzmán Basin hydrography.** The [[Lago de Guzmán Complex]] of northern Chihuahua once formed a pluvial system of lakes, streams, and springs. It drained four major subbasins: the Rio Casas Grandes, Rio Santa María, Rio del Carmen (Santa Clara), and Laguna Bustillos. At least eleven native fish species persist in remnant aquatic habitats. [[Edwin Brand]] (1937) recorded that none of the three main rivers had flowed continuously from source to terminal lake since 1924, and that many springs that fed the system about 80 years earlier had failed. Groundwater and surface-water changes in this region are described in detail below.

**Habitat of *C. pisteri*.** The Guzmán Pupfish occupies springs, ciénegas, shorelines, cutoff channels, and even roadside ditches. Its type locality was a partially impounded pool of about 1.5 ha, fed by springs and bordered by ciénegas with sedges (*Scirpus* spp.), while deeper open water (>1.0 m) was shared with other native and nonnative fishes. The pool dried by about 1975. The species was once established in artificial ponds near Columbus, New Mexico, where it was apparently introduced for mosquito control. That population, 38 specimens collected in 1951, is no longer extant.

**Habitat of *C. albivelis*.** This species is distributed mostly in the upper Rio Papigóchic, where it occurs consistently at high elevations (about 1800–2100 m). It is also known from a single lower-elevation spring complex at Ojo de Arrey (1430 m), in the Rio Santa María system, where its origin is uncertain. Mitochondrial DNA and allozyme data place the Ojo de Arrey fish with Rio Papigóchic populations, which suggests that the population may be nonnative or introduced.

**Habitat of *C. arcuatus*.** Historically the species occurred throughout the upper Santa Cruz River, which is a flood tributary to the Gila River. The river loses much of its flow to infiltration across alluvial basins. In a 1965 flood, a peak of 9.7 m³/s fell to 5.4 m³/s after about 80 km and to 1.2 m³/s 70 km farther downstream. Monkey Spring is a spring perched on a terrace about 10 m above an ephemeral arroyo. Tufa deposits created a natural dam and marsh that isolated the fishes. A chub (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and the pupfish both became genetically distinct there. The spring was altered about a century ago, when the marsh was deepened into a pond and spring outflow was diverted into a canal.

**Human impacts.** The authors identify three main drivers of habitat loss:

1. **Channel incision (arroyo cutting).** Starting after about 1640, watershed damage from timbering, agriculture, and livestock grazing caused incision. Since the 1920s, several streams, including the lower Rios Casas Grandes, Santa María, and del Carmen, have incised 6 m or more below their original floodplains.
2. **Diversions and dams.** Structures for irrigation and domestic use dried some reaches and altered flow in others.
3. **[[Groundwater Mining]].** Water extraction beyond natural recharge, enabled by electric pumps, dried springs and small channels and reduced baseflow in essentially all systems.

Nonnative fishes, such as ictalurids and centrarchids, and the translocation of native fishes between drainages, such as [[Gambusia Affinis]], added predation and competition pressure.

**Monkey Spring extinction.** The pupfish persisted at Monkey Spring until the late 1960s or early 1970s. Attempts to maintain and repatriate it failed. The population was lost in 1971 when [[Largemouth Bass]] became abundant. Downstream, the system now supports bass, nonnative [[Bluegill]], and a few native topminnows. The authors also note that a population may still exist in private aquaria, and they urge any keeper to return it to the wild.

**Nonnative populations.** [[Marsh]] and [[Sada]] (1994) reported questionable, illicitly stocked *macularius*-like pupfish at Bog Hole Tank near the Santa Cruz headwaters and at a cattle tank on the Research Ranch near Elgin, Arizona. The authors conclude these stocked fish do not represent *C. arcuatus*.

## Key Findings

- **Three new species are described:** *C. pisteri* (Guzmán Pupfish), *C. albivelis* (Whitefin Pupfish), and *C. arcuatus* (Monkey Spring Pupfish).
- ***C. pisteri*** is restricted to the [[Lago de Guzmán Basin]] of northern Chihuahua, and it may have occurred in southwestern New Mexico. It is named for [[Edwin Philip Pister]], who worked for almost four decades to preserve desert aquatic habitats.
- ***C. albivelis*** is mostly confined to the upper Rio Papigóchic (Rio Yaqui basin) at relatively high elevations. Its Ojo de Arrey population is genetically similar to Rio Papigóchic fish, and the authors suggest it may be introduced.
- ***C. arcuatus*** was historically limited to the upper Santa Cruz River basin in Arizona and Sonora. It was first collected in the late 1840s and was described about 150 years later. It is extinct in the wild.
- **Meristic differences:** Gill rakers in *C. albivelis* from Ojo de Arrey (mean 17.2, n = 20) differ from those of Rio Yaqui fish (mean 14.6, n = 80). The authors argue this is too large a difference to arise quickly without a severe bottleneck.
- **Phylogenetic position:** The Guzmán and Rio Yaqui forms belong to a western pupfish clade. Relationships among the southern forms are unresolved, and additional undescribed species may exist in the Guzmán Basin.
- **Conservation status:** *C. pisteri* has declined sharply, with its lower-elevation range shrinking as springs disappear. *C. albivelis* is thought to have lost local stocks but is not considered in jeopardy. *C. arcuatus* is extinct in the wild.
- **Drivers of decline:** Groundwater mining, diversions, channel incision, and nonnative predators have caused the regional losses. The authors link these to human activity since the 1600s and especially since the 1920s.
