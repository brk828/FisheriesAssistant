# Grass Carp Spread and Risks to Texas and Louisiana Waterways

#ichthyology #marine-biology #grass-carp

## Executive Summary

This newspaper article reports concern that [[Grass Carp]] are spreading and reproducing successfully in Southern Louisiana rivers and are becoming more widespread in Texas. It cites a [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] study, *Grass Carp in the Lower Mississippi*, which found a rising share of grass carp among larval fish in the [[Lower Mississippi River]] system. The article also cites a [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Department]] biologist, [[Bill Provine]], who identifies irrigation canals on the Upper Texas Coast as a possible hatchery for the species. The article warns that population growth could damage habitat for native fish and shellfish, waterfowl, and marsh vegetation.

## Background & Description

- **Species of focus:** [[Grass Carp]], a fish whose spread in Texas and Louisiana is the subject of the article.
- **Research source:** A report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] titled "Grass Carp in the Lower Mississippi," described as a three-year study conducted in Southern Louisiana.
- **Sampling method:** Larval fish were sampled from the [[Red River]] and [[Atchafalaya River]] during the May–July spawning period.
- **Expert sources:**
  - [[Bill Provine]], grass carp expert, [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Department]].
  - [[Alan Allen]], executive director, [[Sportsmen's Clubs of Texas]].
- **Size:** Provine stated the world record grass carp weighs "somewhere around 400 pounds."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning requirements:** Grass carp need flowing water over a long distance to keep their eggs suspended until they hatch. Provine said the state has numerous waterways that meet these basic spawning requirements.
- **Spawning distance:** Provine estimated that a spawning river needs 30 to 50 miles of flowing water. In a rice irrigation canal with stilling basins, the eggs might need only about 20 feet.
- **Habitat threat:** The article says the study concluded "the potential for dispersal (of grass carp) is high." It warns that grass carp could reduce or eliminate vegetation, as they have in [[Lake Conroe]], which is choked with [[Hydrilla]].
- **Native species:** The report warns that a reduction in river species that depend on vegetation could follow.
- **Connected waters:** Two bayous flowing into the [[Galveston Bay]] system give grass carp access to a large system of irrigation canals.
- **Marsh vegetation:** A population explosion in the irrigation system could jeopardize marsh vegetation that supports finfish, shellfish, and waterfowl.

## Key Findings

- **Larval fish increase (1981–84 study):** The percentage of grass carp in the total catch of larval fishes during the spawning period increased from 2 percent to 23 percent, according to the report.
- **Dispersal risk:** The report concluded that "the potential for dispersal (of grass carp) is high" and that grass carp could soon expand their range.
- **Texas expansion:** Grass carp are becoming more widespread in Texas, and the article argues that what is happening in Southern Louisiana rivers "could easily happen in Texas rivers."
- **Spawning suitability:** Provine said that numerous Texas waterways meet the basic spawning requirements of grass carp.
- **Irrigation canal hazard:** Provine identified rice irrigation canals with stilling basins as a potential "hatchery," since they need only about 20 feet of flowing water for eggs to stay suspended, compared with 30 to 50 miles in rivers.
- **Galveston Bay risk:** Two bayous flowing into the Galveston Bay system on the Upper Texas Coast allow grass carp access to a large irrigation canal system.
- **Stakeholder concern:** Alan Allen of the [[Sportsmen's Clubs of Texas]] reacted strongly to the discovery. The article notes that shrimpers, waterfowl hunters, fishermen, and bass fishermen fought the introduction of grass carp and would regret its spread.
- **Potential impacts:** Population growth could damage native river fisheries, marsh vegetation supporting finfish, shellfish, and waterfowl, and sport and commercial fishing.
- **Illegal introduction:** The article cites irresponsible persons stocking or accidentally releasing grass carp across the state, along with the placement of grass carp in [[Lake Conroe]] and [[Texas A&M University]] waters, as contributors to spread.
