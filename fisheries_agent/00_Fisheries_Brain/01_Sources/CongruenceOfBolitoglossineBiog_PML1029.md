# Vicariance Biogeography of New World Bolitoglossine Salamanders and Suspect Terranes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #plethodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Dean A. Hendrickson of the Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, proposes a vicariance hypothesis for New World biogeography. The hypothesis holds that a lineage of lungless salamanders ([[Bolitoglossine Salamanders]], Plethodontidae) was carried on crustal fragments (tectonostratigraphic or "suspect" terranes) from a single ancestral landmass, and that these fragments were later accreted to positions ranging from southern Alaska to northern South America. The author argues that the salamander distributions, geology and phylogenetic patterns together support this model, and that active biotic dispersal across the Panamanian isthmus before the Pliocene may have been less prevalent than previously believed.

Note: the document is a scientific article (with figures, a reference list and figure legends), not a fish study. Its focus is amphibian biogeography and terrane tectonics.

## Background & Description

- **Study group:** The family [[Plethodontidae]] (terrestrial, lungless salamanders) is thought to have originated from an [[Ambystomatid Salamanders]] lineage in the Appalachian region of North America in the Mid-Mesozoic or earlier. The tribe [[Bolitoglossini]] is believed to have diverged by the Late Cretaceous or the beginning of the Tertiary.
- **Genera discussed:** The supergenera [[Batrachoseps]] and [[Bolitoglossa]] form a sister group to [[Hydromantes]]. Batrachoseps is known from California and southern Europe.
- **Competing models:** Earlier explanations invoked repeated overseas or land-bridge dispersal, such as the Arcto-Tertiary geoflora route and southward spread across the Panamanian isthmus. The author contrasts these with [[Vicariance Biogeography]], which relies on fragmentation of an ancestral range followed by terrane transport.
- **Terranes:** The [[Western North American Terranes]] include the Franciscan Complex, Salinia, Alexander Terrane, Baja California terranes and the [[Chortis Block]]. The [[Costa Rica-Panama Terrane]] was sutured onto the Chortis Block and attached to the Caribbean plate by the Eocene.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution patterns:** Distributions of bolitoglossine taxa show a high degree of congruence with terrane mapping. Batrachoseps species in California are widely panpatric or allopatric, and many ranges are not correlated with ecological gradients such as vegetation or topography.
- **Habitat range:** Batrachoseps occupies habitats from Alaskan forests to Mohave Desert canyons, and tropical Bolitoglossa occupies cloud forests and tropical forests. Interior regions between suitable habitats remain unoccupied.
- **Tropical diversity:** The high endemism and diversity in tropical bolitoglossines are attributed to Tertiary dispersal followed by isolation on arid-island highlands, with terrane tectonics also potentially shaping regional geomorphology.
- **Mobility:** Individual Batrachoseps attenuatus were recorded moving 1.5 m during a 2-year study; the tropical Bolitoglossa subpalmata has an average home range radius of 1.5 m. The author describes these as very sedentary, supporting high terrane fidelity.
- **Paleogeography:** Paleomagnetic data indicate rapid northward displacement of many terranes from low paleolatitudes, while [[Central American Land Bridge]] closure occurred in the Late Miocene to Pliocene.

## Key Findings

1. **Core hypothesis:** Bolitoglossine distributions are explained by vicariance of an ancestral landmass, with lineages carried on terranes accreted from the Alaska to northern South America corridor.
2. **Congruence:** Area cladograms built from geology match plethodontid phylogenies. Incongruences are presented as exceptions and require only limited inter-terrane dispersal.
3. **Panama isthmus:** The Costa Rica-Panama composite terrane was carried eastward relative to North and South America and closed the isthmus in the Late Miocene to Pliocene. Salamander arrival in South America may have occurred via accretion of Costa Rica-Panama-derived terranes rather than direct dispersal.
4. **Timing:** The hypothesis implies greater ages for cladogenic events than earlier models. Biochemical age estimates (for example, [[Molecular Clock Dating]] with 48 Ma and 66 Ma estimates) are considered unreliable, and the author notes the broad confidence intervals of those estimates.
5. **Terrane ridersl concept:** Many South American faunas that appear suddenly in fossil records before the isthmus formed are proposed to have arrived on accreted terranes, reframing "island hopping" as "[[Terrane Riders]]."
6. **Testability:** The author states the hypothesis would be falsified by incongruent phylogenies or by geological studies invalidating the paleogeographic model. Its acceptance would depend on congruent phylogenies from additional groups, such as Central American xenodontine snakes, which the author places in a different biogeographic unit.
7. **Conclusion:** The hypothesis is considered testable and parsimonious, and is presented as the best candidate at present for further development, although it is acknowledged as somewhat speculative due to geological uncertainty and limited phylogenetic data.
