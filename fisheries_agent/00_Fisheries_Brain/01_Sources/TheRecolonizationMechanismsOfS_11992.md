# The Recolonization Mechanisms of Stream Benthos

#stream-benthos #freshwater-ecology #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study investigates how denuded (disturbed/bare) areas of stream substrate are recolonized by benthic invertebrate fauna, identifying and quantifying four discrete recolonization pathways: (1) downstream drift, (2) upstream migration within the water column, (3) vertical migration upward from within the substrate (hyporheic zone), and (4) aerial colonization (primarily oviposition by adult insects). Using specially designed exclusion traps deployed in a small agricultural stream tributary of the [[Nith River]] in Ontario, Canada, the authors found that **drift was the dominant recolonization mechanism**, contributing 41.4% of total colonizers, followed by aerial sources (28.2%), vertical substrate migration (19.1%), and upstream migration (18.2%). The study demonstrates that different taxonomic groups show distinct directional preferences for recolonization, suggesting that isolating any single pathway (e.g., focusing solely on drift, as is common in the literature) would produce a biased and incomplete picture of natural recolonization dynamics and could lead to divergent faunal assemblages.

This work is frequently cited in fisheries and [[Stream Ecology]] contexts because benthic invertebrate recolonization dynamics directly underpin prey availability for stream fish such as [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), whose feeding ecology in comparable Dartmoor streams was documented by Elliott (1967b).

## Background & Description

Not a morphology-focused paper; no anatomical descriptions of fish are provided. However, the experimental design implicitly characterizes functional/behavioral traits of the invertebrate taxa studied via their mesh-size selectivity and dispersal mode:

- **Trap mesh specifications**: 135 µm and 250 µm nitex netting were used to differentially exclude organisms by body size, implying most colonizing taxa (nematodes, cladocerans, ostracods, copepods, dipteran larvae) fall within this micro-to-mesoscale size range.
- **Taxonomic size/behavior groupings** discussed:
  - **Chironomidae** (non-biting midge larvae) — dominant taxon by numbers across nearly all trap types; capable of aerial oviposition and drift dispersal.
  - **Oligochaeta** — surface-crawling dispersal (not true drift), high substrate association.
  - **Nematoda** — small enough to be entrained readily in drift and interstitial movement.
  - **Hirudinea** (leeches) — larger-bodied, substrate-associated, strong vertical migrators.
  - **Pelecypoda** (bivalves) — restricted almost entirely to slow upstream crawling behavior.
  - **Trichoptera** (e.g., *Hydropsyche* sp.) — represented by small larvae in aerial traps, indicating slow growth relative to other insects like *Microtendipes* sp. (Chironomidae), which showed both early and late instars.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: A small (~4 m wide, ~30 cm deep in riffles, up to 8 m wide/50 cm deep in pools), slow-flowing (10–20 cm s⁻¹), meandering, silty, agriculturally-influenced stream with a substrate of ~30 cm mixed gravel over sand and clay. Water temperature during the study (June) was ~20°C.
- **Niche partitioning by recolonization pathway**:
  - **[[Drift (Stream Ecology)]]**: Dominant pathway overall (41.4%); primary route for Chironomidae, Ephemeroptera nymphs, Trichoptera larvae, Nematoda, and Diptera.
  - **Aerial colonization**: Second most important (28.2%); dominated by Chironomidae oviposition, Collembola, aquatic Coleoptera, and taxa presumed to crawl in from outside the trap (Gastropoda, Hirudinea).
  - **Vertical hyporheic migration**: 19.1% contribution; dominant for Hirudinea, Cyclopoida, and generally important for Oligochaeta and Nematoda.
  - **Upstream migration**: 18.2% contribution; especially important for Cladocera, Ostracoda, Cyclopoida, and the sole pathway observed for Pelecypoda.
- **Taxon-specific directional preferences** (see Key Findings) suggest a form of **niche differentiation in dispersal strategy**, potentially reflecting differences in life-history traits, body size, motility, and reliance on the hyporheic zone versus the water column or aerial adult stage.
- **Trophic linkage**: The recolonizing invertebrate assemblage constitutes the prey base for stream fish (e.g., *Salmo trutta*, per Elliott 1967b), linking benthic recolonization dynamics to [[Fish Feeding Ecology]] and secondary production in lotic systems.
- **Comparable/related systems**: [[Speed River]] (Ontario) upstream movement study (Bishop & Hynes 1969); Dartmoor stream drift studies (Elliott 1967a).

## Key Findings

- **Relative contribution of the four recolonization sources** (average animals per trap, % of theoretical total):
  | Source | Avg. no. animals/trap | % Contribution |
  |---|---|---|
  | Drift | 14,074 | 41.4% |
  | Aerial | 9,557 | 28.2% |
  | Vertical (hyporheic) | 6,505 | 19.1% |
  | Upstream migration | 6,195 | 18.2% |
  | **Control (all 4 combined)** | 34,016 (actual) / 36,351 (theoretical sum) | 100% |

- **Chironomidae dominance**: Chironomid larvae vastly outnumbered all other taxa combined in nearly every trap type (e.g., chironomid:non-chironomid ratio = 4.1:1 in drift traps, 113:1 in aerial traps, 9.3:1 in controls), except upstream traps (0.64:1) and vertical traps (1.7:1), where non-chironomid taxa were relatively more important.

- **Drift trap composition**: Nematoda (37.2%) and Oligochaeta (43.8%) were the most numerous non-chironomid colonizers, followed by Cladocera (3.6%), Cyclopoida (3.5%), Ephemeroptera (2.6%), Ostracoda (2.1%), and Coleoptera (1.7%).

- **Upstream migration traps**: No significant rheotactic accumulation was detected between upstream vs. downstream halves of the trap tray, suggesting upstream-moving organisms do not preferentially cluster near the current-facing screen. Dominant taxa: Nematoda (30.9%), Oligochaeta (29.5%), Cyclopoida (22.4%).

- **Vertical migration traps**: Dominated by Cyclopoida (29.4%), Hirudinea (27.8%), Oligochaeta (19.0%), and Nematoda (16.1%) — indicating strong hyporheic zone association for leeches and copepods.

- **Aerial traps**: Overwhelmingly dominated by larval Chironomidae (from oviposition), followed by Collembola (29.4% of non-chironomids), aquatic Coleoptera (12.5%), Gastropoda (12.5%), and Hirudinea (12.5%) — the latter two likely arriving by crawling over the trap rim rather than true aerial dispersal.

- **Taxon-specific directional preferences**:
  - **Pelecypoda (bivalves)**: 100% restricted to upstream migration — a striking and unique finding.
  - **Collembola**: 100% aerial.
  - **Cladocera & Ostracoda**: predominantly upstream migrators (51.1% and 70.9% respectively).
  - **Hirudinea (leeches)**: predominantly vertical/hyporheic migrants (82.1%).
  - **Ephemeroptera & Trichoptera**: predominantly drift-derived (67.9% and 53.9% respectively).
  - **Cyclopoida**: split roughly evenly between upstream migration (50.5%) and vertical migration (43.1%).
  - **Nematoda & Oligochaeta**: split roughly evenly between drift and upstream migration, each contributing similarly.

- **Control trap validation**: The sum of the four isolated trap types (36,351 animals) closely approximated the actual control trap average (34,016 animals) open to all pathways simultaneously, validating the additive framework used to calculate percentage contributions — though the authors note qualitative (compositional) differences likely exist between assemblages recolonizing via different pathways, even when total quantities are comparable.

- **Ecological/methodological implication**: All four recolonization directions are ecologically significant, and studies attributing total benthic recolonization to a single mechanism (e.g., drift alone, as is common in prior literature) risk substantial bias. Isolating a single pathway may also slow recovery of pre-disturbance taxonomic diversity and density, since preferred colonization directions vary by taxon — a finding with implications for restoration and recovery timelines following stream disturbances such as spates, erosion, chemical spills, or insecticide application.

- **Seasonal caveat**: The experiment was conducted only in June 1972; the authors explicitly note that contribution values (especially the aerial pathway, tied to insect oviposition phenology) would likely vary seasonally, with zero aerial contribution expected during winter months in temperate systems.
