# Sportsman's Guide to the Fresh-Water Fishes of New Hampshire

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1947 handbook, published by the [[New Hampshire Fish And Game Commission]], is a field identification guide for anglers and laymen. It covers **62 known species and subspecies** of fresh-water fishes recorded in New Hampshire. The guide uses a dichotomous [[Key To Families]], family-by-family accounts, and sketched identification marks, with common and scientific names, colour, size, and state range for each species. It also includes a distribution table by watershed and a spot-colour chart for trout and salmon. It is a practical taxonomic and distributional reference rather than a research study.

## Background & Description

- **Authorship and publication:** Prepared by Carpenter (Director) and Siecier (Biologist) for the New Hampshire Fish & Game Department. Technical assistance was provided by Dr. Reeve M. Bailey of the University of Michigan. The handbook was published in 1947 and sold for 30 cents per copy.
- **Sources drawn on:** Biological surveys of the New Hampshire Fish & Game Department, Hubbs & Lagler's *Guide to the Fishes of the Great Lakes*, Jordan & Evermann's *Fishes of North and Middle America*, Forbes and Richardson's *The Fishes of Illinois*, and Kendall's *The Fishes of New England*.
- **Structure:** The guide has an introduction and glossary of fin and body terms (dorsal, adipose, anal, pelvic and pectoral fins; rays; opercle; lateral line; barbels). It then gives a Key to Families, a list and distribution table, and detailed species accounts. The index runs to pages 85–87.
- **Identification method:** Scale counts along the [[Lateral Line]] are central. Other key characters are fin ray counts, mouth position, barbels, and gill raker counts for whitefishes. Colour patterns are described for each species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Anadromous and migratory species:** The Sea Lamprey, American Sea Sturgeon, Alewife and American Shad all formerly ran New Hampshire rivers, notably the [[Merrimack River]] and [[Connecticut River]]. The guide attributes the loss of these runs to dams and, for the sturgeon, also to pollution.
- **Landlocked and relict populations:** Landlocked Alewives persisted in Cobbetts Pond and Golden Pond. Two sizes of smelt occur in deep, cold lakes of all major watersheds, and the guide calls the cause of the size difference "still a biological mystery."
- **Cold-water specialists:** Charrs and salmonids depend on cold lakes and streams. The Slimy Muddler is restricted to cold streams of all watersheds except the coastal one.
- **Tidewater and brackish habitats:** Mummichog, Banded Killifish, Ninespine, Mailed Threespine and Fourspine Sticklebacks all occur in tidewater or coastal-watershed areas.
- **Introduced species:** Several species were introduced, including Brown Trout (all major watersheds), Coast Rainbow Trout (most of the state), Largemouth and Smallmouth Bass, Carp, Goldfish, Northern Pike in the Connecticut River, Yellow Walleye in the Merrimack and lower Connecticut watersheds, and Black Crappie and Bluegill in limited ponds.
- **Watersheds:** Five watershed units are used for distribution: Androscoggin, Saco, Coastal, Merrimack and Connecticut.
- **Lake hosts:** Lakes Winnipesaukee, Newfound, Squam, Winnisquam and Umbagog are named repeatedly as key habitats for the salmonids and whitefishes.

## Key Findings

### Identification and Taxonomy

- The guide recognises 62 species and subspecies of fresh-water fishes in New Hampshire, with a family key that uses fin position, spines, rays, mouth shape, scale counts and barbels.
- **Whitefishes:** The Round Whitefish has fewer than 20 gill rakers on the first gill arch, while the [[Inland Lakes Whitefish]] has more than 23. The Inland Lakes Whitefish also has two nostril flaps per side against one in the Round Whitefish.
- **Herrings:** Alewife and [[American Shad]] are separated by jaw shape and by the cheek's silvery area. The alewife's cheek area is longer than deep; the shad's is deeper than long.
- **Suckers:** The Northern Sturgeon Sucker has more than 95 lateral line scales, and the Dwarf Sturgeon Sucker has 80–100. Watershed origin decides the subspecies: Androscoggin specimens are northern and Merrimack or Connecticut specimens are dwarf.
- **Catfishes:** The horned pouts have 19 or more anal fin rays and an adipose fin that is not fused. The madtoms have about 14–18 anal rays and a fused adipose fin.
- **Trout and salmon:** The [[Spot Color Chart]] separates species by black and red spots. Lake Trout have about 190–200 lateral scales, Common Brook Trout about 230, and [[Sunapee Trout]] about 210. Sunapee Trout are found only in Sunapee Lake, Third Connecticut Lake and Dan Hole Pond.
- **Pickerels and pike:** Bulldog Pickerel has opercles fully scaled and a short snout. Chain Pickerel has a long snout and 114–131 lateral scales. Northern Pike has a half-scaled opercle and about 123 lateral scales.

### Notable Species Accounts

- **Sea Lamprey:** Formerly abundant in the Merrimack River, but dams block entry. Two adults were collected at Exeter in May 1938 by R. V. Witter.
- **American Sea Sturgeon:** Once occurred in the Merrimack and Connecticut Rivers. Dams and pollution now prevent entry. Length is given as "large fish" with four barbels.
- **Alewife:** Formerly ran New Hampshire rivers in large numbers to spawn. Length about 15 inches.
- **American Shad:** Formerly abundant in the Merrimack and Connecticut Rivers. Length up to 2½ feet.
- **[[American Smelt]]:** Mouth large, strong teeth, fewer than 75 lateral scales, and a dorsal fin with about 10 rays. Large variety averages about 7 inches (maximum 14 inches). Small variety averages about 4 inches.
- **[[Atlantic Salmon]] (landlocked):** Maximum weight 40 or more pounds. Found in several large lakes and in the Androscoggin River.
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]:** Not native and does not reproduce in New Hampshire. It has not been stocked for years, so the guide considers it doubtful any remain in the state.
- **[[Brown Trout]]:** Maximum weight about 30 pounds. The only black-spotted trout with red or orange spots on its sides.
- **[[Coast Rainbow Trout]]:** Maximum weight about 40 pounds. Introduced over most of the state.
- **[[Lake Trout]]:** Maximum weight up to 100 pounds but rarely over 25 pounds. Found in several Connecticut Lakes and in Winnipesaukee.
- **Dublin trout:** Formerly found in Dublin Pond and Christine Lake. Neither has been seen in late years.
- **Carp:** Found in the lower Connecticut River, Mascoma Lake and the lower Merrimack River. Length 3 feet or more.
- **[[Northern Pike]]:** Introduced to the Connecticut River. Length to 3 feet or more.
- **[[American Eel]]:** Recorded throughout the state. Maximum length 4–5 feet.
- **[[Eastern Burbot]]:** Found in deep lakes and occasionally in streams throughout the state. Length 2 feet.
- **Northern Redbelly Dace:** Found in the Sugar, Israel, Mohawk and upper Connecticut Rivers and in Lime Pond in Columbia.

### Distribution Highlights

- **Most widespread:** The [[Eastern White Sucker]] is described as the commonest fish in New Hampshire and occurs statewide. Yellow Perch, Pumpkinseed and American Eel also occur across the whole state.
- **Restricted to the south:** The Eastern Creek Chubsucker is mainly in the southern half of the state. The Banded Sunfish is mostly in the southeastern part.
- **Restricted to the north:** The Northern Blacknose Shiner is recorded only from the Androscoggin Watershed.
- **Largemouth Bass:** Introduced to several ponds in the southern state, as far north as Hanover.
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]:** Introduced in most of the state, with few if any in Coos County.
- **Sculpin and stickleback:** The Slimy Muddler occurs in cold streams of all watersheds except the coastal. Ninespine, Mailed Threespine and Fourspine Sticklebacks are all tidewater or coastal species.

### Management and Conservation Notes

- The handbook records that the dams and pollution of the Merrimack and Connecticut Rivers cut off sturgeon, lamprey, alewife and shad runs.
- Introductions of Brown Trout, Rainbow Trout, Largemouth Bass and others are documented by watershed.
- The guide notes that the Chinook Salmon is unlikely to remain in the state, and the Dublin trout has not been reported in late years.
- Permission is needed from the Fish & Game Department to reproduce the sketches.
