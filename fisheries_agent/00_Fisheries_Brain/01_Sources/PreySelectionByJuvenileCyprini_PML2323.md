# Prey Selection by Juvenile Cyprinids from Running Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Alasdair Scott and Paul C. Marsh (Freshwater Biology, 1987) examines how juvenile [[Dace]], [[Roach]] and yearling [[Minnow]] select prey in flowing and still water. The three [[Cyprinidae]] species showed broadly similar feeding behaviour and prey preferences. Prey selection depended on predator gape, prey size, prey activity and water movement. Prey motion was essential to trigger attack, and prey were usually taken in proportion to their activity. Specialization on one prey type occurred in still water, directed at the most active prey, but was absent in flowing water, where the juvenile fish also lost their size selectivity. The authors frame these results against [[Optimal Foraging Theory]] and earlier work on [[Prey Selection]] in standing waters.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** juvenile dace (*Leuciscus leuciscus*), which is normally restricted to rivers; juvenile roach (*Rutilus rutilus*); and yearling minnows (*Phoxinus phoxinus*), which occur in both rivers and lakes.
- **Rationale:** Earlier detailed studies of cyprinid foraging had been confined to still-water populations. The authors argued these results may not apply to riverine fish, which pay an extra energetic cost to hold position in flowing water.
- **Collection:** Fish were taken in late June and early July from the [[River Frome]], a small, fast-flowing Dorset river, using a 10 mm mesh seine net. They were held in a large aquarium at a high density (>500 dm⁻³) of natural prey, which kept them feeding mid-water rather than on benthos.
- **Principal prey:** Mainly [[Daphnia Pulex]], with [[Simocephalus Vetulus]], [[Eurycercus Lamellatus]], [[Cyclops Fuscus]], [[Baetis]] nymphs and Tanypodinae chironomid larvae. The ostracod [[Notodromas Monacha]] was used in the cover experiments.
- **Experimental design:** Size-selection trials used five graded Daphnia size classes (I–V, widths 0.3–1.6 mm ±0.1 mm). Single- and two-prey trials were run in still and flowing arenas (flowing water at a mean current of 0.1 m s⁻¹). A separate cover experiment used five [[Macrophyte Cover]] levels of [[Elodea]] (0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 g).
- **Replication:** Size-selection trials used eight individuals per species (fork lengths 25.0 ± 0.1 mm, and 30.0 ± 0.1 mm for dace). Fish were starved for 18 h before trials, and each fish was given seven days of prior exposure to the arena.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Juveniles of all three species occupy backwaters for roughly 2 months before moving to faster currents (Scott, unpublished, as cited). The dace and roach in the trials therefore had little experience of foraging in flowing water compared with the yearling minnows.
- **Social behaviour:** The species are often found in mixed shoals of similar-sized individuals in nature. In flowing water, shoal members tended to hold specific feeding stations, which partly reduced competition through spatial separation.
- **Habitat structure:** Cyprinids forage poorly in dense weedbeds. This may explain their preference for open water or sparsely vegetated habitats.
- **Learning:** Experience shaped foraging in both prey size and attack behaviour, which the authors link to the "adaptive flexibility" of other fish groups in lotic systems (see [[Learning]]).
- **Comparison with other taxa:** Earlier work on [[Salmonids]] (rainbow trout, Atlantic salmon) and [[Sunfishes]] showed similar size selection and prey-motion dependence. Competitive exclusion among the three cyprinids was considered unlikely, because they feed with equal efficiency at a given body length.

## Key Findings

1. **Common feeding pattern:** Dace, roach and minnows showed generally similar feeding behaviour and prey preferences. They captured a given prey type with equal efficiency, with the exception of the ostracod *Notodromas monacha*, which dace did not eat.

2. **Size selection tied to gape:** All three species preferred prey about 0.6× their maximum gape. At 25 mm fork length, the three species had very similar gapes (1.4–1.7 mm), so these fish could not capture size-class V prey. The 30 mm dace (gape 2.0 mm) could ingest all size classes and preferentially took larger prey. Preferred prey size was about 0.6× maximum gape at both 25 and 30 mm.

3. **Prey motion is essential:** Movement was needed to induce attack. In still water, fixation efficiency was closely tied to prey locomotor activity. Only *Daphnia* was fixed at more than 60% efficiency. In flowing water, fixation efficiencies of 98–100% were recorded for all prey types, and dead prey were fixed as readily as live prey.

4. **Still-water specialization:** In still water, fish specialized on the most active prey type. After about three fixations (dace, *Daphnia*/*Simocephalus*) or seven fixations (*Simocephalus*/*Eurycercus*), the other prey were ignored. The authors suggest this reflects a "specific searching image." Fish ignored alternative prey for four to fourteen encounters after the preferred prey were depleted.

5. **Flowing-water behaviour:** Specialization was not observed in flowing water, where the model was an excellent predictor of prey decline. Dace and roach were significantly less size selective in flowing water (P<0.01), although 25 mm fish still did not take size-class V prey. Yearling minnows remained size selective in both still and flowing water.

6. **Escape and retention:** Capture efficiency differences between prey were small, apart from the copepod *Cyclops fuscus*, which evaded capture at the moment of attack. Retention efficiencies were above 90% for all prey except *Eurycercus* (37% for minnows, 43% for roach), which resisted mastication.

7. **Learning in evasive prey:** Dace increased strike efficiency on *Cyclops* from about 5% on the first day to 20% after five days. Fish fed only copepods for 48 h raised capture efficiency to roughly 50%, changing from repeated attacks to following and attacking at close range (<10 mm). The authors report capture efficiency gains of about 300% and a further 150% under these conditions.

8. **Predictive model:** A stochastic model (10,000 simulations of the first twenty encounters) using encounter index, capture efficiency and prey numbers accurately predicted prey decline in the *Simocephalus*+*Baetis* and flowing-water trials. It departed from observed patterns in still-water specialization trials.

9. **Prey activity over capture efficiency:** In the *Daphnia*+*Cyclops* trials, using equal capture efficiencies changed predicted maximal *Daphnia* decline only slightly (27% versus 26%). The authors conclude that prey activity, not escape ability, dominated selection in this case. This challenges the common assumption that cladocerans are preferred over copepods because of evasion.

10. **Effect of macrophytes:** Cover did not change the pattern of prey selection, but it significantly reduced predation rates. Numbers of *Daphnia* and *Simocephalus* declined markedly less at high weed density (significant after only a few hours). A LOGIT model with a weed-by-time interaction showed foraging was proportionately less efficient in dense weed (most evident at 100 g), with dace not eating *Notodromas*.

11. **Optimal foraging:** The preferred prey size (about 60% of gape) closely matched the optimal size reported for several sunfishes (0.59× gape). Optimal foraging theory predicts that equally profitable moving prey should all be eaten, which held in flowing water but not in still water. The authors attribute the still-water deviation to the absence of shoal-based spatial separation.

12. **Methodological caution:** The authors argue that focusing on a single determinant of prey selection, such as encounter probability or escape ability, risks false conclusions. Models that omit prey motion are unsuitable for fish that require movement to attack.
