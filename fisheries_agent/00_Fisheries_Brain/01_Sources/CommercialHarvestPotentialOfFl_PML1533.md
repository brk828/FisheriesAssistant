# Commercial Harvest Potential of Flathead Catfish in the Colorado, Gila, Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Contract 002), assesses whether introduced [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) populations in Arizona's rivers could support commercial harvest. Riverine populations are abundant enough for commercial use only in the lower [[Colorado River]] near Yuma. Harvest there would be limited to periods of stock abundance and could not be sustained. In a 3.5-km reach sampled in 1987, the estimated maximum annual potential yield was 5,956 kg, of which anglers harvested an estimated 3,277 kg, leaving about 1,669 kg for commercial or other harvest. Gross value of a commercial fishery was estimated at roughly $5,900 to $18,000 per year, with perhaps $3,000 to $8,000 realized by fishermen. Contaminant residues were below [[Food and Drug Administration]] (FDA) tolerances except for heptachlor in two Colorado River samples.

## Background & Description

The flathead catfish is a large, piscivorous ictalurid native to the Great Lakes region and the Mississippi, Missouri and Ohio basins, the Gulf coastal plain, and the Rio Grande drainage. It was introduced into the Gila River system before 1951 and stocked into the lower Colorado River in 1963, with further introductions in the Salt, Verde and San Francisco rivers from 1968 to 1975. The species is unscaled, has a depressed head, a large adipose fin and a broad, projecting lower jaw, with 18 barbels in total.

Life history varies by size. Juveniles feed mainly on aquatic insect larvae and shift to fish as they grow past about 350 mm; fish over 450 mm are almost exclusively piscivorous. Males mature at 3–5 years and females at 3–6 years in rivers, with delayed maturity in reservoirs. Spawning occurs in June–August in secluded cover, and males guard the nest. The world angling record is 41 kg (Lake Lewisville, Texas, 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study focused on flowing reaches of the lower Colorado (Yuma Division, Laguna to Morelos dams), the Gila, Salt and Verde rivers. Target reaches were selected for flowing water, flathead presence and access by both anglers and commercial fishers. Angler pressure on the Gila and Verde was light, and the lower Salt River had moderate use. Creel surveys in 1985 recorded 847 anglers over 44 survey days, with flathead comprising 56% of fish caught.

Native fishes in the Salt and Verde rivers, including Sonoran sucker, desert sucker and roundtail chub, were highly vulnerable to entanglement gear and were captured at 2–10 times the rate of flathead in some devices. Incidental capture of a river otter in a trammel net was documented. Lower Colorado flow declined after April 1987, reducing suitable habitat and concentrating fish, which contributed to a population collapse by 1988.

## Key Findings

- **Harvest survey:** Twenty-one state agencies responded. Reported commercial harvest ranged from a minimum of 136 kg (Kansas) to more than 450,000 kg (Arkansas, valued at $749,165 in 1981). Hoop, trammel and gill nets were the most common gears, and electrofishing was legal only in North Carolina.
- **Contaminants:** Of 72 marketable-size flathead samples, residues were within FDA limits in the Gila, Salt and Verde rivers. Two of nine Colorado River samples exceeded the 0.3 ppm heptachlor tolerance (0.32 and 0.49 ppm). Other residues (DDT metabolites, lindane, delta-BHC, toxaphene) were below tolerance in all samples.
- **Age and growth:** Lower Colorado fish ranged from 0 to 9 years, the largest being 9 years old, over 1,000 mm and 14.5 kg. Gila fish were 1–5 years old, Salt fish 1–6 years, and Verde fish 0–8 years. Growth in the Salt and Gila was faster during early years; lower Colorado fish reached larger sizes than those in other Arizona rivers.
- **Population estimate (1987, Yuma):** Four mark-recapture methods gave estimates of 1,733 to 11,508 fish over 300 mm in the study reach (Table 13). A Yuma Division extrapolation gave 6,890 to 15,960 fish, though the authors considered this extrapolation uncertain.
- **Gear efficiency:** Electrofishing was clearly the most effective method, yielding 33.3 fish/hr on the lower Colorado and 34.7 fish/hr on the Verde, versus 1.3 fish/hr on the Salt. Entanglement nets, hoop nets and set lines had poor catches and high incidental mortality.
- **Yield model:** Instantaneous total mortality Z = 1.0017, fishing mortality F = 0.6017 and natural mortality M = 0.4053. Maximum potential yield was about 5,956 kg. Anglers took an estimated 3,277 kg, leaving about 1,669 kg for commercial harvest. Sensitivity analysis showed yield declined about 5% for each 0.05 increase in natural mortality.
- **Economic value:** Retail value in Phoenix was $4.40 to $9.90 per kg of dressed fish. A commercial fisher could expect about $3,000 to $8,000 gross per year under the study conditions. Extrapolated to the 300 km upstream reach, gross value could reach $60,000 to $150,000 annually, but the authors considered this unlikely.
- **Population collapse:** Flows below 50 m³/s after 1987 reduced habitat, and population decline followed. May 1988 sampling yielded one young-of-year and one small adult. September 1988 sampling yielded 49 fish with a catch rate of 40.13 fish/hr, but only 3 fish fell within the marketable range of 400–500 mm.
- **Conclusion:** Only the lower Colorado River population is suitable for commercial exploitation, and any harvest would be intermittent. Sustained commercial harvest is unlikely because it would deplete large fish and reduce mean individual weight. Reservoir populations (San Carlos, Roosevelt, Bartlett and Horseshoe) were not assessed and may have greater commercial potential.
