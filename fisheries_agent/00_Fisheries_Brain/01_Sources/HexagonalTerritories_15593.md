# Hexagonal Territories in Mouthbreeding Cichlids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 paper by [[George W. Barlow]] tests the long-standing prediction that animal territories should become hexagonal (the most efficient polygonal packing) at high density in a uniform environment. Using the male breeding pits of the African mouthbreeding cichlid [[Tilapia Mossambica]] in an outdoor pool at Berkeley, the author shows that as breeding density rose, the proportion of hexagonal pits nearly doubled, pits with four or fewer sides declined, and mean pit size did not change. The study concludes that polygonal territories may be largely restricted to animals whose territories are small enough to be continuously tended.

## Background & Description

- **Theoretical basis:** Maximum packing of cells in a homogeneous medium is achieved by hexagons (Thompson 1917). [[Grant]] (1968) argued that territories should be hexagonal at maximum density and circular at low density.
- **Prior difficulty:** Maps of bird territories in grasslands rarely showed clear hexagons (Wiens 1969). Grant's tundra analysis of red-backed sandpipers found only two complete pentagons, and about two-thirds of corner angles were of hexagonal size in incomplete maps.
- **Study species:** Territorial males of *Tilapia mossambica* are jet-black maternal mouthbreeders that excavate breeding pits on open, undifferentiated sand. Reciprocal spitting of sand between closely spaced males forms conspicuous parapets that mark boundaries.
- **Study system:** Breeding occurs year-round in a large outdoor pool with a uniform sand bottom (4–6 × 9–10 m, 0.61 m deep, 27–30 °C), with male density highest in spring and summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Boundary formation:** Pit edges reflect the aggressive behaviour of male occupants. Digging is interpreted as redirected aggression, citing [[Heiligenberg]] (1965) on *Pelmatochromis subocellatus kribensis*.
- **Widespread behaviour:** Pit building as territorial expression is reported in other cichlids, including *Etroplus maculatus* (Asia) and *Cichlasoma nicaraguense* (Central America), where rival males build sand parapets and the dominant male pushes the wall toward the subordinate.
- **Unfavourable sites:** Acute corners are costly, since a defender there faces two side rivals plus one ahead. Such pits (especially triangular ones) were usually unoccupied and were absorbed by neighbours or used by newly territorial males.
- **Dominance signals:** Straight boundaries indicate equal rivals; outward-bowed boundaries suggest the owner is dominant over that neighbour.
- **Natural context:** Low-density Illinois pond populations had circular, non-contacting pits, but overpopulated waters are expected to produce contact. Related species such as *Tilapia grahami* and *Tilapia alcalica* also show straight contacting edges.
- **Scope limits:** Polygonal shapes are probably valid where territories are small enough to be continuously tended (e.g., colonial oceanic birds such as royal terns, which Buckley found to nest in hexagonal territories, personal communication). Larger, more heterogeneous territories should be less polygonal.

## Key Findings

- **Sample sizes:** The low-density sample contained 46 pits and the high-density sample 73 pits, almost twice as many. Statistical tests were not performed because the same males likely contributed to both samples.
- **Shape shift:** With increasing density, the proportion of hexagonal territories nearly doubled, and territories with four or fewer sides declined markedly. Pentagons remained the most frequent shape.
- **Size:** There was no significant change in mean pit size across densities. Maximum pit width was about three times male body length at the crowded end, and an isolated pit was about four body lengths in diameter.
- **Packing efficiency:** The shift to hexagons produced more efficient packing without loss of space, and small unoccupied gaps disappeared at high density.
- **Conclusion:** The hexagonal-territory prediction was supported for this species in a uniform environment, though variation existed, possibly from differences in male size and motivation.
- **Predicted neighbour number:** Given the inefficient packing of territories in nature, the modal number of neighbours for fully surrounded territories is predicted to be five.
