# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Phase II: Research Program and Proposals for Testing Dam Operation Effects

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This is a compiled review packet. It opens with a 4 April 1990 cover letter from GCES Senior Scientist Duncan T. Patten asking W. L. Minckley for a two-week review of research proposals. The letter asks reviewers to judge whether each proposal addresses its hypothesis and whether its design tests the hypothesis logically and statistically. The packet contains:
- the GCES Research Program (20 questions with null hypotheses);
- a justification for controlled "research discharges";
- a Bald Eagle / spawning trout proposal for Nankoweap Creek (Brown, Leibfried and Stevens, 29 December 1989), with logistics and budget;
- an Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Phase II proposal on ecosystem-level processes, lower trophic levels, and trout (April 1990).

Together they plan to measure how daily fluctuation magnitude, minimum discharge, and ramping rate from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] affect beaches, sediment, fish, eagles, productivity, recreation, cultural sites and economics. The results are meant to inform the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) and the Section 7 consultation.

## Background & Description
- **Program logic:** The GCES uses null hypotheses (H0), with response curves relating a resource response (y) to a perturbation (x). Each curve needs at least three points on the x axis. Controls are constant discharges of equal daily volume, or paired no-use sites.
- **Research discharges:** A dendrogram splits flows into constant and fluctuating types. There are five constant regimes (including 1,000, 5,000, 8,000, 11,000 and 15,000 cfs) and seven fluctuating regimes. Fluctuating flows are low/medium (<12,000 cfs) or high (>20,000 cfs), with low, medium or high minima and high or low up and down ramping rates. Examples are 3,000–13,000, 3,000–26,000, 10,000–33,200 and 10,000–20,000 cfs. Each episode lasts 10–11 days and is followed by 3–4 days of low constant flow (about 3,000 cfs).
- **Question areas:**
  - beach and sediment dynamics;
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] stranding and behavior;
  - wintering [[Bald Eagle]] foraging at [[Nankoweap Creek]];
  - native fish, especially [[Humpback Chub]];
  - nutrients and productivity;
  - recreation, cultural resources and economics (power, recreation, non-use);
  - mitigation alternatives (variable intake, reregulation dam, beach protection, sediment augmentation).
- **Nankoweap proposal:** Eagles began wintering at Nankoweap in the early 1980s, drawn by the rainbow trout spawn (January–March). Up to 18 eagles were seen at once by February 1988. The authors compare the situation to kokanee salmon and eagles at McDonald Creek in Glacier National Park.
- **AGFD proposal:** It covers six lower-trophic and ecosystem objectives (1.1–1.4 and related methods) and trout objectives 2.1–2.4. A separate native fish proposal was still in preparation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Dam effects:** Deep hypolimnetic release from Lake Powell makes the tailwater perennially cold and sediment-poor. Lake Powell traps more than 90% of incoming sediment and associated phosphorus. Phosphorus is the likely limiting nutrient, and the reservoir is considered oligotrophic.
- **Food web:** Clearer water supports [[Cladophora Glomerata]] and epiphytic diatoms, which feed chironomids, oligochaetes and the amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]]. These in turn feed a cold-water trout fishery that was formerly restricted to a few spring-fed tributaries. Warm-water sport fishes and native fishes have declined.
- **Fluctuation effects:** Fluctuating flows increase organic drift and cause stranding and desiccation of algae, invertebrates and fish. They also create an alternately wetted "tidal zone."
- **Trout reproduction:** Phase I found that about 28% of the Lee's Ferry creel catch came from natural reproduction. Natural reproduction, in the mainstem and tributaries, supplies a higher proportion of trout in Grand Canyon National Park. Few stocked trout migrate downstream, so Grand Canyon trout depend largely on natural reproduction and on tributaries such as Nankoweap.
- **Beach and vegetation issues:** The packet lists camping beach loss, motorboat wake erosion, and invasive [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix ramosissima*) and *Alhagi camelorum* in the riparian zone.

## Key Findings
- **Hypotheses:** The program poses about 20 questions with null hypotheses and secondary sub-hypotheses. These cover:
  - beach degradation;
  - recreational disturbance;
  - vegetation stabilization;
  - sediment transport (suspended sediment at six gaging stations; bed load at fewer);
  - trout stranding and behavior;
  - eagle foraging;
  - native/introduced fish dynamics (LCR, Kanab, Havasu and Paria tributaries);
  - nutrients and productivity;
  - recreation, camping-beach availability at 31,500 or 33,200 cfs, cultural sites, and economics.
- **Mitigation questions:** The "No Change" alternative, variable intake structures, a reregulation dam, beach protection devices and sediment augmentation are treated as questions or comments, not testable hypotheses.
- **Phase I trout stranding (Oct 1984):** Discharge dropped from 24,000 to 5,000 cfs. More than 800 stranded trout were seen; 639 were examined and 85 (13%) were alive. Only 9% of stranded trout lacked the stocking dye mark, compared with 27.5% naturally reproduced in electrofishing and creel samples. Sluiceway experiments showed much higher embryo and alevin mortality under fluctuating flows (10 hours of daily dewatering).
- **Spawning bars:** Many bars begin to be exposed at 8,000–10,000 cfs. Wetted perimeters are to be measured at steady flows of 3,000, 5,000, 8,000 and 11,000 cfs.
- **Nankoweap design:**
  - Daylight observation post about 500 m west of the creek mouth, with eagle-minutes of five behaviors recorded.
  - Rim and fixed-wing censuses, with at least 30 simultaneous rim versus post census periods.
  - Weekly Lake Powell eagle censuses as a relative control.
  - Trout netting at least three nights per week.
  - Floy-tagged river trout; three 200-m creek reaches with 50 fin-clipped fish each.
  - An augmentation experiment if fewer than 100 trout are in the creek (as in January 1989).
  - Two field seasons (Feb–Mar 1990; winter 1990–91), needing very low steady, fluctuating and higher steady flows for at least two weeks each.
  - Interim report 31 Oct 1990; final 31 Oct 1991.
  - Direct costs of $33,222 plus 20% indirect ($6,644) (the grand total itself is not shown).
- **AGFD methods:**
  - Diel oxygen metabolism along subsets of 20 transects.
  - Organic matter split into CPOM, FPOM and DOM.
  - Nutrients: nitrogen, phosphorus and silica.
  - Size-frequency secondary production for *Gammarus*, with stranding trays.
  - Colonization on substrates at different elevations.
  - Cladophora nutritive quality after 6 h to 7 days of desiccation.
  - Trout stranding surveys at pools since December 1989.
  - About 2,000 fingerlings in two dam sluiceways, with counts every two weeks for six months or more.
  - Double marking and otolith aging.
  - Radio telemetry.
- **Gear and regulation notes:** Electrofishing injury was reduced from about 40% to 3–9%. About 75% of Lee's Ferry anglers' trout are released, under a new 16–22 inch slot limit and two-fish bag limit.
- **Schedule and budget:** AGFD field work runs June 1990–June 1991, keyed to a flow schedule (5,000 cfs interludes, fluctuating tests such as 3,000–26,200 and 10,000–33,200 cfs). Draft report 30 Sep 1991, final 30 Nov 1991. The AGFD budget is deferred to the native fish proposal.
- **Reviewer caveat:** Patten acknowledges the short-term studies carry large uncertainty. Proposals were written by agency scientists without an RFP process, and he considers this adequate but not ideal.
