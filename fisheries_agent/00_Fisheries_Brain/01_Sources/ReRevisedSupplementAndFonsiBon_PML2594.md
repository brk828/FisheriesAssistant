# Bonita Creek Native Fish Restoration: Fill Placement and Rotenone Reapplication

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This package concerns a proposed [[Bureau of Reclamation]] action in lower [[Bonita Creek]], within the [[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]]. The action would (1) place about 1,800 cubic yards of sand and gravel fill on a 0.64-acre ponded area upstream of a [[Fish Barrier]], and (2) reapply the piscicide [[Rotenone]] (CFT Legumine) to a 1.7-mile reach to eradicate three nonnative fishes. The package includes a Revised Supplement to the 2007 Environmental Assessment (EA), a draft [[Finding of No Significant Impact]] (FONSI), a public notice, and a supporting public comment letter from fisheries scientist Paul C. Marsh, Ph.D. Reclamation's preliminary determination is that the action will not significantly affect the human environment, and no environmental impact statement is required.

## Background & Description

Bonita Creek is a Gila River basin stream that supports resident native fishes, including the endangered [[Gila Chub]]. The 2007 EA authorized a fish barrier, a one-time rotenone renovation, salvage of native fishes, and repatriation of federally listed species, including [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Gila Topminnow]].

Key chronology:

- **July 2007:** The [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) and Reclamation signed decisions on the 2007 EA.
- **September 2008:** The fish barrier was completed.
- **October 2008:** Native fishes were salvaged and the 1.7-mile reach was renovated with rotenone. Native and listed fishes were then restocked.
- **2009–2010:** Nonnative fishes reappeared. [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were detected in 2009, and [[Fathead Minnow]] in 2010. Mechanical removal (nets, traps, electrofishing) failed.
- **April 2010:** A supplement analyzed effects of future renovations, but did not address fill placement.
- **September 2010:** A Biological Assessment was submitted to the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS).
- **November 17, 2010:** FWS issued a Biological Opinion.
- **December 2010:** The Revised Supplement and draft FONSI were released for comment, with comments due January 10, 2011.

The proposed fill site is a former backwater channel cut off by the barrier, plus a City of Safford access road west of the active thalweg. Beaver dam breaching would partly dewater the pool before fill placement. Fill material would be sourced from a commercial sand and gravel pit about three miles south of Safford, operating since 1929.

Proposed renovation activities include:
- Salvage, holding, and propagation of native fishes (with [[Gila Topminnow]] excluded from salvage to avoid mixing with mosquitofish)
- Breaching beaver dams days before treatment
- Possible salvage of [[Sonora Mud Turtle]], aquatic invertebrates, and leopard frog eggs and tadpoles
- Repatriation and [[Augmentation]] of native species upstream of the barrier
- Possible propagation via hormone injection (Ovaprim) for fish held through winter 2011–2012

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Reclamation originally expected sediment deposition to fill the impounded area within 3–5 years. Sediment mobilization begins at a 2-year flow (about 2,750 cfs). However, 97.4 percent of mean daily flows after barrier construction were within 10 cfs of base flow, and peak flow has not exceeded 350 cfs. Mean daily base flow between October 2008 and October 2010 was 3.8 cfs at the USGS gauge. As a result, little sediment has accumulated behind the barrier.

**Nonnative threat.** Mosquitofish, green sunfish, and fathead minnow are the three nonnative species now established in the renovated reach. Commenters and the agencies consider green sunfish the most damaging to native fish assemblages in the Gila River basin. The agencies note that fathead minnow is less harmful and its reinvasion would not trigger another renovation.

**Listed species and critical habitat.** Proposed critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow was published October 28, 2010 (75 FR 66482), covering 14.8 miles upstream from the Gila River confluence, including the treatment reach. The project is expected to benefit listed fishes by removing nonnative competitors and predators, although some native mortality is unavoidable.

**Southwestern willow flycatcher (SWWF).** Surveys from 2004 to 2007 documented no SWWF along Bonita Creek. The nearest nesting record was one bird about three miles south of the project in 2003. Habitat suitability declined from 2004 to 2007, mainly due to beaver activity, and was judged marginal by 2007. Surveys will be repeated before any renovation during the nesting season.

**Water quality and regulation.** Piscicides will not be applied during precipitation or storm runoff. The [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] issued a Section 401 certification waiver, and the fill qualifies for Nationwide Permit 27 under the [[Clean Water Act]]. The EPA's 2006 exemption of pesticide discharges from permitting was vacated in January 2009. Arizona will issue AZPDES coverage for piscicide applications after March 2011, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) will obtain that coverage.

**Recreation.** AGFD will close Bonita Creek downstream of the San Carlos Apache Reservation boundary to angling beginning in 2011 to reduce bait-bucket transfers of nonnative fishes.

## Key Findings

**Agency findings (draft FONSI):**
1. No significant effects on federally listed fishes are expected. Mortality of native fishes from future renovations is expected to be offset by long-term conservation benefits.
2. Critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow would not be significantly affected, and no destruction or adverse modification is likely.
3. SWWF surveys and avoidance measures will ensure no significant effect.
4. Compliance with Clean Water Act requirements is assured.

**Scientific and management points from the documents:**
- Piscicide treatment is presented as the only practicable option to eradicate nonnative fishes from Bonita Creek. Mechanical removal has failed.
- Filling the pool reduces the treatment area, lowers piscicide volume and cost, and removes a public attraction where humans could transfer nonnative fishes past the barrier.
- Salvage is unlikely to capture all fishes, so an unquantified level of native mortality is expected.
- Reclamation plans only one more treatment, but keeps additional treatments open if nonnatives re-establish. Continued failure could lead to abandoning restoration, but the timing of such a decision cannot be predicted.
- Upstream reaches (over 80 percent of the stream by Reclamation's estimate) and downstream source populations are expected to allow recolonization by macroinvertebrates, which the documents describe as resilient to rotenone.
- Sources of stocking fish include Bubbling Ponds Hatchery, the Lower San Pedro River Preserve ponds, Arizona State University, Blue River (loach minnow), Gila River in New Mexico (spikedace), Bylas Springs (Gila topminnow), and El Doctor Marsh, Mexico (desert pupfish).

**Public comment (Marsh letter):**
- Dr. Marsh, a fisheries scientist with more than three decades of experience, supports filling the pond and reapplying piscicide.
- He argues that nonnative fishes are detrimental to native communities, that the pond is a persistent liability, and that chemical renovation is the only practicable way to restore a viable native fish community in Bonita Creek.
- He notes that the project could still be undermined by intentional or accidental human release of nonnative fishes, but that filling the pond reduces the risk of establishment and spread.

**Public comment summary (Reclamation response table):**
- Commenters questioned the value of repeated poisoning and asked how nonnatives re-established. Reclamation's responses cite flood-driven fathead minnow transport, possible inadequate treatment in complex habitat, and possible human release.
