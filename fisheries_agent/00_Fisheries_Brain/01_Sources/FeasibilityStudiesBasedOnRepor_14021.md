# ERTS Remote Sensing Feasibility for Aquatic and Arid-Region Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This multi-report document, produced by the [[University of Nevada System]] for the U.S. Geological Survey under Grant No. 14-08-0001-G-28, assesses whether imagery from the [[Earth Resources Technology Satellite]] (ERTS, later Landsat) and small-scale aircraft multiband photography can support natural resource management in arid regions. It includes project memoranda, two feasibility progress reports (1971), a 1972 progress report, a [[Aquatic Systems in Deserts]] study on the Salt River lakes, a bibliography, and imagery interpretation sections.

The team concluded that interdisciplinary use of ERTS data is feasible and offers synoptic views unavailable from other methods, but that it needs large-scale supplementary imagery and extensive [[Ground Truth]] data before its utility can be fully tested. Most of the evidence came from degraded aircraft imagery, because no actual ERTS data were available during the study.

## Background & Description

**Program and team.** The work was part of the [[EROS Program]] (Earth Resources Observation Systems). The principal investigators were Paul T. Tueller (co-coordinator), G. William Fiero Jr., James E. Deacon, Lawrence Lattman, Harold L. Klieforth, Charles Saladino, W. L. Minckley, and Garwin Lorain. Their disciplines included geology, hydrology, biology-limnology, forestry, range and wildlife management, urbanology, and meteorology. Institutions were the University of Nevada (Reno and Las Vegas), the [[Desert Research Institute]], the University of Cincinnati, and Arizona State University.

**Study areas.**
- **Reno/Lake Tahoe/Pyramid Lake site:** about 4,000 square miles in western Nevada and eastern California. [[Lake Tahoe]] (elevation 6,230 ft, about 195 square miles, maximum depth over 1,600 ft) drains through the [[Truckee River]] to [[Pyramid Lake]], a terminal lake that has dropped about 100 ft in the last century.
- **Las Vegas/Lake Mead/Spring Mountains site:** about 1,200 square miles in southern Nevada and Mohave County, Arizona. It includes [[Lake Mead]], the [[Spring Mountains]], and the [[Las Vegas Wash]], the only through-flowing drainage.

**ERTS specifications (as described in the report).** The satellite was to carry three return-beam vidicon cameras (red, green, blue) and a multispectral scanner. It was planned in a sun-synchronous orbit at about 500 nautical miles, with an 18-day revisit interval, frames about 100 nautical miles on a side, nominal scale 1:1,000,000, and estimated ground resolution of 200 to 300 feet.

**Imagery available.** No ERTS data were available. Analyses used:
- Color infrared at 1:250,000 (July 1, 1969) and at scales of 1:113,000 to 1:56,500 (NASA RB-57F, July 27, 1970)
- 70 mm multiband frames (red, green, blue, black-and-white IR) at about 1:432,000 to 1:450,000
- Optically degraded frames at about 1:800,000, used to simulate ERTS scale and resolution (reported as poor in quality)
- Later October 1971 flights, including thermal IR

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salt River lakes, central Arizona.** This study, integrated with a Bureau of Commercial Fisheries project (P.L. 88-309), used four reservoirs as ground-truth analogs for Tahoe-Pyramid:
- [[Roosevelt Lake]]: 17,000 to 18,000 acres at full pool, depth just over 50 m
- [[Apache Lake]]: about 2,500 acres, depth near 100 m
- [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Saguaro Lake]]: about 1,500 acres each, depths 30 to 40 m

The preliminary analysis found a high correlation (r ≥ 0.80) between fish abundance, food abundance, and nutrient input, with the highest densities near the upstream ends. [[Threadfin Shad]] (Dorosoma petenense) schools and entrained plankton created color artifacts on imagery, especially in Roosevelt Lake.

**Water quality and nutrients.** Tahoe and Truckee River chlorophyll estimates were high near sewage sources (Upper Truckee River, Tahoe Keys, Reno sewage plant). Chlorophyll declined with distance downstream from Reno, consistent with nutrient uptake. Pyramid Lake showed strong color heterogeneity, with high chlorophyll near the Truckee input and plankton swirls, presumably from wind-driven currents or Coriolis effects.

**Phreatophytes and groundwater.** Phreatophyte distribution (cottonwood, willow, tamarisk, mesquite, cattails) was used as an indicator of groundwater discharge, salinity, and water quality. Cattails near the Las Vegas sewage discharge showed brighter IR reflectance, possibly due to nutrient enrichment.

**Hazards.** The report identifies flash floods, debris flows, landslides, avalanches, wildfire, urban air pollution and thermal inversions (Warm Springs Valley), and fog and smog as hazards that imagery could help map.

## Key Findings

### 1. Feasibility by scale and repetition

- **Broad overview** is well suited to regional vegetation mapping, regional tectonics, synchronous hydrologic events, and locating key areas for multistage subsampling.
- **Time repetition:** The 18-day interval was judged appropriate for some biology and seasonal work but too coarse for air pollution, rapid monitoring of volcanic or shoreline change, and some storm-related studies. Long-interval (10-year) applications included erosion in bajada areas, plant succession, fire scars, and mining.

### 2. Film and band performance

| Discipline | Preferred imagery (reported) |
|---|---|
| Renewable natural resources | Color infrared (most useful for nearly all vegetation work) |
| Geology | Color (equal or superior for most uses) |
| Biology-limnology | Color film for algae and sediment; color IR for vegetation |
| Hydrology | Color IR for recharge/discharge; color for lake sediments and floods |
| Meteorology | Black-and-white for cloud types; thermal IR for cloud-top heights |
| Urban | Color IR for relationships between adjacent urban areas |

Among the bands, red was the most useful, black-and-white IR was moderate, and blue was least useful. Color enhancements were found to make some features easier to detect, but no composite revealed a feature not already visible on color IR at the same scale.

### 3. Statistical results from the Salt River ground-truth study

| Parameter (Salt River lakes) | Film and scale | Correlation (r) |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll A (n = 12) | Ektachrome 1:45,000 | -0.89 |
| Chlorophyll A (n = 12) | Ektachrome 1:86,100 | -0.86 |
| Chlorophyll A (n = 12) | Infrared 1:45,000 | -0.72 |
| Dissolved oxygen (n = 12) | Ektachrome 1:45,000 | -0.08 |
| Chlorophyll A vs Centroid chip numbers (n = 21) | Ektachrome 1:45,000 | -0.83 |
| Chlorophyll A vs Centroid chip numbers (n = 21) | Infrared 1:45,000 | -0.66 |

- Ektachrome gave more reliable surface color information than infrared.
- 95% confidence limits (about ±4.3 mg/m³) were comparable to the mean chlorophyll A (4.3 mg/m³ of water), so the estimates indicated orders of magnitude only.
- The best distance-from-input correlation was for Canyon Lake (r = 0.83 at 1:86,100, and the highest infrared value at 1:45,000), while the Tonto arm of Roosevelt Lake showed a negative correlation (r = -0.92 at 1:86,100).

### 4. Ground-truth needs

- Simultaneous ground truth during overflights is essential, especially for phenology, soil moisture, and unique hydrologic events.
- Remote weather stations, precipitation gauges, and a time-lapse camera were installed or built in the Tahoe area, including an automatic station on Peavine Mountain (8,400 ft).
- Thin cirrus clouds were noted to attenuate both visual and IR imagery.

### 5. User identification

- Two formal seminars: Reno (24 attendees from 12 agencies or institutions, including the U.S. Forest Service and Desert Research Institute) and Las Vegas (41 attendees, including the Bureau of Reclamation and Clark County agencies).
- The project reports at least 32 potential user groups.
- Follow-up discussions with city planners, flood control officials, and resource agencies indicated strong interest and readiness to accept data.

### 6. Interdisciplinary operational system

The proposed system is built around an Interdisciplinary Review Panel (IRP) that selects problems, assigns a project coordinator, approves disciplines, reviews ground-truth needs, estimates cost and time, supervises analysis, and publishes results. The authors stressed that without constant administrative effort, interdisciplinary studies tend to become merely multidisciplinary (parallel, not interacting).

### 7. Specific applications proposed

- **Warm Springs Valley:** a proposed city of 64,000 people. Imagery could help rank flood hazard, groundwater zones, and inversion-related air pollution, though large-scale stereo imagery would be needed for detailed siting.
- **Las Vegas:** mapping of flood-prone washes and waterlogging, phreatophyte density as a water-quality indicator, and density currents in Lake Mead affecting nutrient distribution.
- **Pyramid Lake:** monitoring shoreline changes, delta and plume size (which affect fish spawning access), algal blooms, and wind-related hazards (winds above 25 knots producing visible whitecaps).

### 8. Conclusions

1. An interdisciplinary team of natural resource scientists can work together successfully.
2. ERTS imagery provides sequential information that cannot be obtained any other way.
3. Larger-scale supplementary imagery is required for planning.
4. Strong ground-truth data are needed to test the utility of tones, textures, and spectral signatures.

The authors also note that the study remained an overview, and that later work would establish parameters for interpreting actual ERTS imagery.
