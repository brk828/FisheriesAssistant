# Fishes and Aquatic Habitats of the Upper San Pedro River System, Arizona and Sonora

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Land Management]] by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) in March 1987, documents the fish fauna of the upper [[San Pedro River]] basin, traces its decline, and evaluates how stream flow, water quality, and upstream mining affect native fishes. It also recommends ways to restore aquatic habitat and reintroduce native species within the proposed San Pedro Riparian National [[Conservation Area]].

The river supported at least 13 native fish species historically, and eight persist as remnant populations. Downcutting (arroyo cutting) beginning in August 1890 converted a marshy, perennial stream into an incised, intermittent channel, and non-native fishes have increasingly replaced natives. Only two native species, [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]], now persist in the mainstream. The author argues that restoring the stream's historic stability, through [[Cienega]] reconstruction and off-river habitats, is the most realistic route to recovery.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The San Pedro River rises in northern Sonora, Mexico, flows about 240 km north, and joins the [[Gila River Basin]] near Winkleman, Arizona. Its watershed covers 11,635 km². Elevations range from forests above 2300 m to Sonoran desertscrub at 588 m near the mouth. The average channel gradient is about 4.0 m/km. Flow is strongly bimodal, with winter and summer floods separated by spring and autumn droughts. Substrate is mostly shifting sand, which offers little pool, cover, or invertebrate production.

**Historic context.** Before 1890 the mainstream was a sequence of marshy [[Cienega]] reaches interspersed with short incised sections. Early travelers, including the Mormon Battalion (1846) and Emory (1857), described boggy bottoms, sacaton grasslands, dense willow and cottonwood, and beaver-dammed pools "full of fishes." Human influences included Indigenous irrigation and fire use, Sobaipuri agriculture (about 2000 people in 14 villages, per Kino), 19th-century cattle ranching, and mining. Cattle numbers peaked at perhaps 400,000 head in the late 1880s, followed by a drought that killed more than half of the region's livestock by 1895.

**Study species.** Table 1 lists 13 native species (four families) and 14 introduced species. The native group includes Cyprinidae minnows, Catostomidae suckers, the Cyprinodontidae [[Desert Pupfish]], and the Poeciliidae [[Sonoran Topminnow]]. Introduced taxa include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channel incision and habitat loss.** Downcutting exceeded 3 to 4 m where floodplains were narrow. Dense riparian vegetation, marsh, channel, and streambed areas declined sharply. Table 2 shows dense riparian cover rising from 22% of the 31,590 ha study area in 1935 to 39% in 1966 and 45% in 1978, while the "other" category (marsh, channel, and streambed) fell from 66% to 18%. Agricultural land rose from 11% to 28% over the same period. Irrigated area reached 5000 ha in 1946, declined to 3900 ha by 1970, and was estimated at 5950 ha in 1979.

**Hydrology.** Mean discharge near the mouth was 1.62 m/sec over 1966 to 1979 (median 1.08), ranging from no flow to 476 m/sec as reported. Historic cienegas, beaver ponds, and alluvial floodplains buffered floods and sustained base flow. Their loss made the river flashy and intermittent.

**Fish community change.** Large fishes disappeared first, followed by cienega and pool-riffle species. Species of permanent gravel-bottomed creeks persisted for about 50 years after 1890. Only tolerant species remain. Native fishes did better in erosive tributaries such as [[Aravaipa Creek]], where seven of the original 13 species remain.

**Threats.** Introduced fishes, especially Mosquitofish and Largemouth Bass, displace natives through predation and competition. Copper mining in the Sonoran headwaters (Cananea area) poses acute and chronic risks. A 1979 spill turned the river brick-red, with pH as low as 3.1 and dissolved oxygen as low as 2.0 mg/l, and killed aquatic life for at least 100 km north of the border. Invertebrates and longfin dace were back four months later.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish richness:** The Gila River system historically held 18 native fish kinds, of which one is extinct and 10 are listed or of special concern. The San Pedro supported at least 13 of these, and eight remain as remnant populations.
- **Current mainstream fauna:** Only [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] persist in the mainstream. Longfin dace made up 70 to 100% of mainstream catches since 1944, and desert sucker made up less than 30% since 1964. [[Sonoran Sucker]] comprised only a few percent of catches since 1964.
- **Introduced species:** Of 14 introduced species, most were first recorded between 1880s and 1960s. Introduced fishes (common carp, rainbow trout, mosquitofish, and others) are now the dominant threat to natives.
- **Mechanism of decline:** Arroyo cutting from August 1890 caused the loss of cienegas and the shift to intermittent flow. Extirpation of native species followed habitat change and introductions.
- **Species ecology:** Species were grouped into four categories by habitat need. Category I ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]) needed large rivers. Category II ([[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Roundtail Chub]]) needed perennial, moderate-sized streams. Category III ([[Gila Chub]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Sonoran Topminnow]]) needed spring-fed or margin habitats. Category IV ([[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]]) tolerated variable or intermittent conditions.
- **Flood effects:** Native fishes are generally flood-resistant, and floods selectively remove non-native fishes. Minckley and Meffe (1987) found that native diversity was inversely related to the number of introduced species. Drought, low oxygen, and high temperatures pose greater dangers.
- **Mining toxicity:** In Lewis (1977), a study of Pinto Creek, Arizona, zinc was the most lethal single ion to longfin dace (LC50 = 0.79 mg/l), and a copper-zinc mixture was the most lethal combination (LC50 = 0.21 mg/l copper and 0.28 mg/l zinc). Primary production fell 36% in silted areas.
- **Flow recommendations:** The author recommends maintaining at least present minimum and median discharges in permanent reaches. Doubling median discharge and raising minimum flow to at least 0.28 m³/sec would maintain the present fauna and allow reintroduction of most indigenous species.
- **Off-river habitat:** Excavated, oxbow-like channels a few meters wide and at least 100 m long, dug to the water table, could provide habitat for essentially all native fishes. These channels can be isolated from non-natives and used to contain pollution.
- **Priority ordering:** Priority 1 is protecting existing populations in [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Redfield Canyon]], and O'Donnell Creek. Priority 2 is renovating the [[Babocomari River]] and its cienega, which could host Desert Pupfish, Sonoran Topminnow, and Razorback Sucker. Priority 3 is establishing species in semi-natural floodplain habitats.
- **Reintroduction and legal status:** Eight native species have legal or quasi-legal protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] or state listings. The author opposes classifying reintroduced endangered fishes as "experimental, nonessential," arguing that any biologically endangered population should be treated as essential.
- **Control of non-natives:** Recommended measures include curtailing stocking of non-native fishes, prohibiting live bait (to avoid red shiner introductions), discouraging mosquitofish stocking, and encouraging sport fishing for predatory species.
- **Mining management:** The author recommends monitoring systems for early warning of pollution, diversion and holding facilities for toxic inputs, and negotiated agreements to abate chronic pollution from Sonoran mining.
