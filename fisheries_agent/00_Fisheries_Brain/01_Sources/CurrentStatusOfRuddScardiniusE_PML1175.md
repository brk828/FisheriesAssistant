# Current Status of Rudd (*Scardinius erythrophthalmus*) in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This status report by Dawn P. Jennings, James D. Williams, and Noel M. Burkhead of the [[National Fisheries Research Center]] (Gainesville, Florida) reviews the [[Rudd]], an exotic [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] native to Europe and central Asia. Rudd has been introduced to several US states mainly through the bait trade and ornamental pond fish trade. It is now documented in bait dealer inventories in 16 states and in open-water populations in eight states. Most states regulate it as generally restricted or prohibited, and five states specifically ban its rearing or sale. The report also covers ongoing [[Hybridization Research]] into whether rudd can hybridize with the native [[Golden Shiner]]. Studies to date indicate that hybridization is possible.

## Background & Description

- **Origin and spread:** Rudd is native to Europe and central Asia. It is established outside its native range in Ireland, Australia, the Netherlands, Africa, the North Island of New Zealand, and in Maine and New York in the United States.
- **Introduction history:** The first US introductions were believed to be for ornamental purposes in the early 1900s. Rudd was reintroduced in the late 1960s and early 1970s as a new bait minnow and outdoor ornamental pond fish. In the early 1980s its popularity as bait for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Striped Bass]] increased, and interstate shipment promoted its dispersal.
- **Similarity to native species:** Rudd closely resembles the native [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) and appears to be closely related to it phylogenetically. Rudd also interbreeds with other European minnows. Concern centers on its potential to hybridize with the golden shiner.

**Diet:** Rudd is omnivorous. It consumes [[Zooplankton]], aquatic insects, crustaceans, snails, and macrophytes, and occasionally fish eggs. Adults primarily consume macrophytes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use:** Rudd occurs predominantly in freshwater but also in brackish water. It is generally found in lentic systems, including pools, backwaters, and littoral areas of lakes and ponds, usually associated with [[Aquatic Macrophytes]].

**Environmental tolerances:**
- Thermal tolerance is wide, with reported tolerance up to 38 °C.
- Reported salinity tolerance extends up to 10 ppt.

**Distribution in the United States:**
- **Bait dealers:** Rudd was recorded at bait dealers in 16 states: Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Massachusetts, Missouri, New York, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and Wisconsin.
- **Open-water introductions:** Documented in eight states, with the most important county-level records as follows:
  - Arkansas: Prairie County
  - Illinois: Cook, Lake, DeKalb, Kankakee, Kendall, and LaSalle Counties
  - Maine: Kennebec County
  - Missouri: Ralls, Monroe, Shelby, and Audrain Counties
  - New York: Columbia County
  - Oklahoma: Bryan County
  - Texas: Cooke and Grayson Counties
  - Virginia: Fairfax County
  - Wisconsin: Fond du Lac County

**Regulatory context:**
- A majority of US states consider rudd a "generally" restricted or prohibited species under existing exotic fish regulations.
- Five states (Alabama, Connecticut, Louisiana, Michigan, and Virginia) specifically prohibit the rearing or sale of rudd.
- Eight states (California, Kansas, Nebraska, New York, North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, and Wisconsin) were considering or developing rudd-specific regulations.

## Key Findings

1. **Multiple introduction pathways:** Rudd spread through ornamental trade, bait-minnow use, and interstate shipment of bait, with bait-trade demand from anglers driving dispersal.
2. **Broad US distribution:** Rudd is present in bait dealer inventories in 16 states and in open-water populations in eight states, with the county records listed above.
3. **Regulatory response:** Most states restrict or prohibit rudd, and several others are developing new rules.
4. **Hybridization concern:** Rudd's close resemblance and relatedness to the golden shiner raise concern about hybridization with this native species.
5. **Hybridization results to date:**
   - Studies indicate that natural hybridization of rudd × golden shiner is possible.
   - Successful crosses were obtained for female rudd × male golden shiner and for the reciprocal cross.
   - F1 hybrids are being reared for further study.
6. **Ongoing research objectives:**
   - Determine whether hybridization is possible.
   - Document courtship and reproductive behavior. Videotapes of reproductive behavior are currently being analyzed.
   - Characterize hybrid progeny using morphometric and meristic techniques. These data are presently being analyzed.
   - Assess the reproductive potential of F1 hybrids.
