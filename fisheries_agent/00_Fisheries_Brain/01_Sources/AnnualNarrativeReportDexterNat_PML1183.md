# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Narrative Report, Calendar Year 1983

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This annual narrative report, submitted by Hatchery Manager Buddy Jensen on November 14, 1983, documents the operations of the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the Pecos River Valley of southeastern New Mexico. The hatchery's entire program in 1983 focused on holding, spawning, rearing, and distributing imperiled southwestern fishes. Major achievements included a substantial increase in [[Razorback Sucker]] egg viability and fry production over 1982, the first documented [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawning by artificial (hormone-induced) methods, successful [[Bonytail Chub]] spawning on two-year-old broodstock, and continued maintenance of populations of at least a dozen threatened or endangered species. Distribution went primarily to the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers in Arizona and the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado.

## Background & Description

The hatchery was initiated in 1931, with fish stocking beginning in 1932. It was originally built to supply warmwater game fish, but its mission shifted under new laws and Service policies toward native southwestern species conservation. In 1983 the staff comprised seven permanent employees and eight seasonal or temporary workers, including a hatchery manager, assistant manager, two fishery biologists, a maintenance worker, an office assistant, YCC enrollees, and fish tagners. Two staff changes were noted: [[Joe Owens]] transferred from Jones Hole NFH (Vernal, Utah) on March 21, and [[Connie Foster]] joined on June 13 after graduating from New Mexico State University.

Planned work for 1983 included maintaining a refuge for 12 imperiled species, producing 500,000+ razorback sucker fry and 100,000 fingerlings, 100,000+ Colorado squawfish fry and 100,000 fingerlings, studying bonytail chub spawning on two-year-old brood, supplying preserved fish specimens, and administering the Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team. Table 1 lists the species held in 1983, which fall into four families: Cyprinidae (six species), Cyprinodontidae (three pupfishes), Poeciliidae (three live-bearers), and Catostomidae (two suckers).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most held species are imperiled southwestern natives, and the hatchery's work is directed at recovery and reintroduction. Stocking sites included the Salt River, Coon Creek, Cherry Creek, Bonita Creek, Eagle Creek, West Clear Creek, Oak Creek, the Verde and Gila rivers in Arizona, the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado, and Lake Mohave. Water supply is a critical constraint. A 1973 water rights adjudication reduced the station's allotment, and in 1983 the state court granted an additional 527 acre-feet per year, raising the total to 1,880 acre-feet. The report also notes water quality studies at Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge and the need for careful management of larval fish in holding tanks, where crowding caused losses.

Cooperating agencies included state wildlife departments in New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, and California, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service regional offices, the Bureau of Reclamation, the National Park Service, universities, and the Mexican government. Federal and academic collaborators included [[Dr. Jim Sublette]] (Eastern New Mexico University) and [[Dr. Paul Turner]] (New Mexico State University).

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker (RBS)**
- 55 wild females were spawned using Ovaprim; 46 (83.6%) spawned successfully, producing 5,728,025 eggs (average fecundity 124,522).
- Egg viability at 72 hours averaged 75.1%. Swim-up fry numbered 2,915,761 (68.1% of expected fry; 50.9% of eggs), a major improvement over 1982 (30.5% viability, 22.5% swim-up).
- Of the swim-up fry, 376,213 were stocked in production ponds, 2,617,548 were hauled to Arizona, and 158,367 advanced fry were produced; 136,416 three-inch fingerlings (86.1%) were coded-wire tagged and distributed to the Gila and Verde rivers.
- Feed conversion was 2.03.
- About 350 two-year-old future broodfish were held for 1984 production.

**Colorado Squawfish (CSF)**
- 26 females were spawned by hormone induction, with mean body weight of 3.3 lbs. Domestic broodfish fecundity ranged from 57,766 to 113,341 (mean 87,436); a wild female weighing 2.6 lbs produced 142,891 eggs.
- A total of 2,178,883 eggs yielded an overall mean fecundity of 83,803.
- Egg viability averaged 59% in Heath incubators and 66% in jars. Jar hatching was shown to be feasible and faster.
- Of 2,100,587 eggs (60.1% mean viability for both methods), 1,274,753 fry were expected and 354,000 swim-up fry were produced; crowding losses at tank corners were severe.
- 234,000 fry were stocked in rearing ponds, yielding 150,000 advanced fry (68.9%); 87,153 fingerlings were returned (72% return), with 86,638 finally recovered after one month (short of the 100,000 requested).
- Feed conversion was 3.90. Fish were nose-tagged with coded-wire tags and planted in the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- 215 mature adults and 450 two-year-old future broodfish were held for 1987 spawning.

**Bonytail Chub (BTC)**
- Two adult females spawned using carp pituitary hormone produced 50,096 eggs (38% viable) and 35,376 eggs (12% viable), averaging 26.8% viability.
- 24 two-year-old females (mean weight 0.28 lbs) spawned over four weeks in May. Mean fecundity was 4,677 (range 1,015 to 10,384), with 119,764 eggs taken at 67.5% mean viability.
- 10,000 fry went to California Fish and Game, and 28,7xx fry to Arizona Game and Fish, which stocked Lake Mohave backwaters.

**Other Species**
- Chihuahua chub: successful induced spawning in 1983, with eventual mass production possible; preserved specimens provided to researchers.
- Woundfin: natural spawning experiments continued in ponds.
- Yaqui chub and beautiful shiner: maintained through natural reproduction.
- Big Bend gambusia and Gila topminnow: large populations maintained. The Yaqui topminnow did not overwinter outdoors and was removed from the station under Regional directives after stock was preserved.
- Comanche Springs and Leon Springs pupfish: maintained at the station, with young produced through the summer.
- Yaqui sucker: one fish was maintained with the RBS broodstock, and 460 wild fish were collected from the Yaqui River basin in Mexico to bolster stock.

**Preserved Specimens and Cooperation**
- Preserved specimens went to numerous state, federal, and academic institutions, including the Mexican government and the British Museum of Natural History.
- Staff assisted Kansas with fish culture for smallmouth bass, spotted bass, and striped bass, and provided water quality sampling support.

**Facilities and Expenditures**
- A $365,000 Jobs Bill funded the subdivision of four ponds into 13 smaller ponds, with work starting in November.
- Expenditures included about $15,000 for an oil and paint storage building, $8,000 for hatching battery equipment, $3,877 for well maintenance, $2,500 for water line valve maintenance, about $11,000 for pond maintenance, $4,500 for grounds maintenance, and about $8,700 for quarters and appliances.
- About 1,200 visitors toured the hatchery in 1983.
