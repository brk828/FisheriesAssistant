# The Odonata of a Moorland Fishpond

#odonata #freshwater-ecology #limnology #zygoptera #anisoptera

## Executive Summary

This paper by T. T. Macan (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside) reports the Odonata (dragonflies and damselflies) of [[Hodson's Tarn]], a small artificial moorland fishpond in the [[Lake District]]. The study ran from 1955 to autumn 1960, when 500 specimens of *Salmo trutta* were introduced, ending the fishless baseline. The aim was to establish species present, life histories, and population sizes before predation by fish could be assessed. Seven Odonata species were recorded; the three zygopterans were abundant, while the anisopterans were scarce. Most detailed results concern [[Pyrrhosoma Nymphula]], [[Enallagma Cyathigerum]] and [[Lestes Sponsa]].

## Background & Description

**Study objective.** The wider investigation aimed to document the invertebrate fauna of a fishless pond, to determine the life history and abundance of common species across the pond and through the year, and so to estimate total populations and year-class decline. Only Odonata data are presented here. The author notes that no two ponds are similar enough for one to serve as a control, so long-term observation was needed to gauge natural year-to-year variation.

**Site.** Hodson's Tarn is a typical artificial moorland fishpond, similar to [[Three Dubs Tarn]], which was surveyed earlier by Macan (1949). Key characteristics:
- Surface area 4427 m² (somewhat under half a hectare); greatest depth about 3 m; altitude 190 m.
- Hinterland of rock, mostly covered by thin peat or boulder clay, with some *Picea* and *Larix* plantations.
- Soft, dilute water, poor in dissolved salts (Table 1, July 1954).
- One small inflow, compared with six streams feeding Three Dubs Tarn.

**Vegetation zones** (areas as estimated):

| Zone | Dominant taxa | Area (m²) |
|---|---|---|
| Emergent | [[Carex Rostrata]] (some *Eleocharis palustris*, *Glyceria fluitans*) | 595 |
| Floating-leaved | [[Potamogeton Natans]] | 568 |
| Sward | [[Littorella Uniflora]] | 474 |
| Milfoil | [[Myriophyllum Alterniflorum]], *Juncus bulbosus* | 2316 |
| Bare | none | 474 |

The author stresses these figures are approximate, particularly for *Myriophyllum*.

**Methods.**
- **[[Pond Net]]:** a round net about 26 cm in diameter (10 meshes per cm), swept three times along a 2 m course. It collected many animals, yielded life-history material, and gave relative (not per-area) numbers.
- **[[Quantitative Sampler]] (Scs):** two rotating cylinders with teeth that cut vegetation as the device is lowered and sealed into the mud. It gave numbers per unit area but caught fewer individuals and species.
- **[[Emergence Trap]]:** wooden frames of 1 or 1 m² area, with curtains against waves, counting cast skins. It was used from 1954 and in 1956–57 was checked almost daily in May and June; a floating trap replaced it from 1959.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling bias.** Macan (1963) showed the net is selective. Compared with the sampler, the sampler caught 4.5 leeches for every 1 caught by the net, while other animals were more abundant in net catches. Net-to-sampler ratios were 1:2.4 for *Pyrrhosoma* and 1:8 for *Enallagma*, interpreted as *Pyrrhosoma* clinging to vegetation when disturbed and *Enallagma* swimming away. Net-based distribution and abundance estimates therefore needed correction.

**Hydrology and weather.** The dam leaked, so water level fell about 25 cm in dry weather. Levels in 1957 were thought to have strongly affected *Littorella* exposure during egg-laying. Meteorological records from Ambleside were used, since water-level measurements were not made. Wet-spell rainfall in early June ranged from 21.84 mm (1957, 6 days) to 96.77 mm (1959, 6 days).

**Vegetation change.** *Carex* at the inflow became sparser, *Littorella* spread and densified, and *Myriophyllum* declined from 1958, leaving much of the bottom bare by 1959–60.

**Prey.** The dominant food of the nymphs was [[Chironomid Larvae]] and [[Entomostraca]] (small crustaceans), with *Enallagma* feeding similarly.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory.** Seven species were recorded:
- Zygoptera (abundant): *Pyrrhosoma nymphula*, *Enallagma cyathigerum*, *Lestes sponsa*.
- Anisoptera (scarce): *Aeshna juncea*, [[Libellula Quadrimaculata]], [[Sympetrum Danae]], [[Cordulia Aenea]].

**Pyrrhosoma nymphula** ([[Pyrrhosoma Nymphula]])
- Identifiable food remains in 80 of 192 specimens examined; only one small nymph was found in the examined sample, so cannibalism was not confirmed.
- Emergence in mid May to early June; oviposition on floating *Potamogeton* leaves was watched on 22 June 1956. Eggs were not confirmed in *Carex*.
- Life history was two years in *Littorella* and three in *Carex*. The 1957 year-class was exceptionally large; some members took three years to develop, and the 1959 year-class was poor.
- A captive egg hatched after 18 days, and 347 days elapsed from egg-laying to adult male emergence.
- Net and sampler estimates suggest the population falls to about one quarter each summer. Total second- and third-summer nymphs were calculated at about 600,000 in an average summer, roughly 95% in *Carex* and *Potamogeton natans*.
- The 1957 *Littorella* success was attributed to low water, which exposed leaves for egg-laying. This is a hypothesis, not a tested result.

**Enallagma cyathigerum** ([[Enallagma Cyathigerum]])
- Identifiable food in 35 of 142 specimens opened; feeding habits were considered identical to *Pyrrhosoma*.
- Emergence from mid May to mid July, mostly in June; adults disappear in September.
- Oviposition: females entered the water and walked down plant stems, submerging for up to about 15 minutes in the 8 July 1960 observation, and laid 49 eggs on one *Littorella* leaf. Macan cites Robert (1958), who recorded nearly 100 minutes of submersion.
- Development is usually one year, though some eggs hatched in July or August give fast-growing nymphs that emerge the following summer.
- Estimated totals: about 400,000 newly emerged nymphs, 200,000 in second or third summer, and 100,000 about to emerge. Mortality appeared "unexpectedly low", and the author notes calculation uncertainty.

**Lestes sponsa** ([[Lestes Sponsa]])
- Overwinters in the egg stage; tiny nymphs appear in early April, and growth is rapid.
- Emergence from late June to early September, with 18 specimens in 1960 and one in 1959 from the floating trap.
- In late April 1958, 331 specimens were netted; 96% were in emergent vegetation. Nymphs spread to *Potamogeton* and *Myriophyllum* about a month later.
- Sampler estimates for April 1959 were 560/m² (about 300,000 in emergent vegetation), against 80/m² in *Littorella* (about 38,000).

**Aeshna juncea** ([[Aeshna Juncea]])
- Three large specimens (October 1957) contained a mite, a caddis larva and an *Enallagma* nymph.
- Eggs overwinter and hatch in the second summer; nymphs reach about a quarter of full size before winter, and some complete development in two years and others in three.
- Adults were seen hawking on only three occasions, all in September. Netted maxima were 5, 9 and 9 in 1958, 1959 and 1960.
- Mostly collected in *Carex* around the inflow; none in *Myriophyllum*.

**Libellula quadrimaculata, Sympetrum danae and Cordulia aenea**
- Only 14, 13, 18, 23 and 31 midsummer nymphs were recorded from 1956 onward; most were small and unidentified, so data are sparse.
- *Cordulia* nymphs were most common in *Littorella*, *Sympetrum* in emergent vegetation.
- Earliest *Cordulia* on the wing was 13 May; *Libellula* peaks a week or two later; *Sympetrum* is abundant in September, earliest flier 25 June.
- Oviposition was observed on 26 September 1957 and 21 September 1960 (*Sympetrum*, dipping into *Sphagnum* and *Apium*). *Libellula* laid eggs by dipping its abdomen in flight over *Carex*.

**Overall conclusions**
- The number of nymphs reaching the final stage varies little from year to year and seems governed by the tarn's resources rather than egg numbers. When young nymphs are numerous, more die and more take three years to develop.
- Total population estimates for the tarn were about 400,000, 200,000 and 100,000 nymphs in the three stages for *Enallagma*, and about 4, 2 and 1 hundred thousand for the species as a whole, with *Enallagma* showing much lower mortality than *Pyrrhosoma*.
- The author states the calculations have wide error margins and that the relationship between weather, egg-laying and year-class success still needs field testing.
