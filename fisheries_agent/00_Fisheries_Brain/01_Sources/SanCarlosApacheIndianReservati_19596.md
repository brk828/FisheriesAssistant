# Fishery Management on the San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation (1963)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 Fishery Management Report, prepared by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (Branch of Fishery Management Services) for the San Carlos Apache Tribe, evaluates the sport fishery of the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]]. The Bureau's 1963 program had four phases: a records search, a resource survey, active management, and a recreational potential assessment. Most of the Reservation's [[Stock-Water Tanks]] were found to suit only warm-water species, and few held fish populations capable of sustaining sport fishing. The report recommends extensive stocking, selective fish removal, and construction of [[Trout Lake]] developments, supported by new roads, to build a recreational fishery that could generate income and jobs for tribal members.

## Background & Description

The Reservation covers 1,643,939 acres in southeastern Arizona, with elevations from 2,400 feet at [[Coolidge Dam]] to 8,000 feet at Malay Gap. The terrain is mostly mountainous and rough. In 1958 the Tribe and the Bureau agreed that the Bureau would provide technical assistance in managing Reservation fisheries and supply fish for stocking. Until 1963, lack of personnel and funds prevented the Bureau from delivering that assistance.

### Phase I: Fishery Management History

- Before 1943, 13 tanks were stocked. A 1943 stocking table lists 11 waters, including Bloody Basin, Salt, Phillips, Deer Creek, and Sycamore, with largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, and crappie.
- In 1944, applications were submitted for about 18 additional waters. No records show these were stocked.
- The last pre-1963 stocking was in 1958, when channel catfish, largemouth bass, and bluegill were planted in 23 listed waters, including Warm Springs on the San Carlos River.
- No follow-up studies were conducted on these plantings before 1963.

### Phase II: Surveys

In summer 1963, 70 of the most permanent stock-water tanks were surveyed. They totaled 300 surface acres, and only a few held satisfactory fish populations. Many tanks stocked in 1958 had dried and refilled without fish. The Black and Salt Rivers were also surveyed, but [[San Carlos Reservoir]] was not.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stock-water tanks:** Most were suitable only for warm-water fish. Their viability depends on water supply, and several are shallow and prone to drying. Warm-water populations in some tanks (Bobcat, Elwood, East Deer, Big Boni) must be eradicated before trout can be stocked.
- **Black and Salt Rivers:** The Salt River forms at the confluence of the Black and White Rivers and marks the northern boundary of the Reservation, shared with the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. Along the Reservation, about 32 miles of the Salt River and 76 miles of the Black River form boundary lines. Fishing pressure is light. Green sunfish, [[Gila Chub]], and suckers occur along nearly the full length.
  - The lower 40 to 50 miles support an excellent catfish population.
  - The upper 16 miles hold a poor-to-fair trout population that could be developed if access were provided.
  - The 42 to 52 miles between these zones contain no important game fish. [[Smallmouth Bass]] may suit this reach.
- **San Carlos River:** Anglers reportedly catch 4 to 5 pound catfish a few miles above the diversion dam, though no survey was completed.
- **Temperature limits:** Threadfin shad suffer complete population mortality when water temperatures fall below 45 degrees Fahrenheit, so they must be restocked each spring.

## Key Findings

### Trout Suitability

- Fifteen tanks were judged suitable for trout, including Upper Highway, Gimme, Deer, East Deer, Elwood, Bobcat, Drylake, Cienega, East Salt Shed, Freezout, B.S., Loafer, Dry Prong, Sawbuck, and Big Boni.
- Nine of these 15 were devoid of fish at the time of survey.
- Bobcat, Elwood, and East Deer hold warm-water populations that must be eradicated first.
- Upper Highway and Deer each had a single fish species and were stocked experimentally with catchable trout.
- Big Boni has year-round suitable conditions, though trout would be confined to a small area in hot summers. Armstrong Tank was stocked as an indicator for Big Boni.
- Trout were chosen as the priority because [[Rainbow Trout]] are in the greatest public demand locally and offer the most angler-days of any sport fish.

### Management Actions (1963 and Planned)

- **Rainbow trout stocking, 1963:** 13 entries, including 1,000 to 5,850 fish per tank. Drylake received the most (5,850 fish, 6–8 inches).
- **Channel catfish:** Stocked in 44 stock tanks in 1963. Forty-four tanks were judged suitable for [[Channel Catfish]] management.
- **Largemouth bass:** A sizable number of tanks can support [[Largemouth Bass]], with plants planned for 1964.
- **Smallmouth bass:** Recommended for the middle Black River section.
- **Rotenone eradication costs:** Powdered cube or derris root (5% rotenone) at $0.40 per pound.
  - Bobcat Tank: 164 lb, $65.60
  - Elwood Tank: 300 lb, $120.00
  - East Deer Tank: 110 lb, $44.00
- **Forage fish:** Golden shiners for persistently turbid waters. [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] for relatively clear ponds with dense phytoplankton blooms. Apart from channel catfish, no other species should be stocked with rainbow trout.
- **Balancing populations:** Some tanks are overpopulated with [[Bluegill]] and others with largemouth bass. Fish must be transferred between tanks, and seining low tanks during dry years is recommended.

### Recreational Potential and Economics

- Permit sales indicate stable fishing pressure. About 1,000 man-days of fishing per year occur on the Reservation, with about 20 percent by enrolled Apaches.
- The report projects a 1,000 percent increase in fishing pressure in 1964, with a slight further rise in 1965.
- Arizona trout fishermen average $13.68 per fishing day, according to a survey cited in the report.
- A better local benchmark is the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] recreation enterprise. In 1962 it recorded about 150,000 man-days of fishing and roughly $850,000 in revenue, of which at least 75 percent ($637,500) was attributed to trout anglers. This yields about $4.25 per man-day.

### Proposed Dam Sites

| Site | Key figures | Estimated use / value |
|---|---|---|
| Willow Creek, site 1 | 55 ft dam, 300 ft crest, ~300 surface acres | 50,000 man-days; $212,500/yr |
| Willow Creek, site 2 (~500 yd downstream) | 45 ft dam, 300 ft crest, 80-acre lake | 15,000 man-days; $63,750/yr |
| Point of Pines Creek (below Drylake) | Up to 60 ft dam; at least 75 surface acres | Value depends on engineering survey; lots at ~$100/unit/yr |
| Cienega Creek | 30 ft dam, 10–15 acre lake | ~3,000 man-days within 3 years |
| Tanks Canyon Creek | 40 ft dam, 300 ft crest, 25-acre lake | 10,000 man-days; $42,500/yr |

- Willow Creek outlets must draw water from both the top and bottom of the reservoir so warm surface water can be "skimmed" off in summer.
- Drylake Tank is too shallow and should be abandoned in favor of a smaller downstream site.
- [[Seneca Dam]] is complete. Seneca Lake is projected at 10,000 man-days per year and about $42,500 in income, with the Bureau stocking 10,000 catchable rainbow trout in fall 1963.

### Access and Infrastructure

- All Point of Pines values depend on completing the all-weather road under construction and on access to new lakes.
- A road across the Black River above the Black River Pump Station would connect to the Ten of Diamonds road on Fort Apache. The upper Black River section could be worth about $150,000 per year to both reservations.
- A road from Point of Pines would link Globe, Arizona, to eastern Fort Apache waters, [[Big Lake]] (about 400,000 visitors annually), and the proposed reservoirs. The report estimates such a route would need capacity for about 1,000,000 persons annually.

### Conclusions

- Extensive new trout lakes, roads, and public service facilities are necessary to build a recreational fishery capable of substantial tribal income and employment.
- Fees should remain competitive, since high use fees would discourage the angling demand the program depends on.
