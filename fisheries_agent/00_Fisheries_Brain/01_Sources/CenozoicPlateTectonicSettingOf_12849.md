# Cenozoic Plate Tectonic Setting of the Cordilleran Region in the United States

#geology #plate-tectonics #cordillera

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[William R. Dickinson]] (Stanford University) sketches the plate tectonic framework of the western United States Cordillera from the Late Cretaceous to the present. It is a geological and tectonic synthesis, not an aquatic or ichthyological study. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or water-chemistry data. Its main content is a sequence of paleotectonic and paleogeographic maps, and the author describes them as personal reconstructions meant as "points of departure for further discussions" rather than final conclusions.

The core argument is that the evolution of the Cordillera was controlled by interactions among the [[Pacific Plate]], [[Farallon Plate]], and [[American Plate]]. Changes in the dip of the subducting [[Farallon Plate]] drove arc magmatism and [[Laramide Orogeny]] deformation inland. Later, the [[San Andreas Transform]] system replaced subduction along the coast.

## Background & Description

The paper covers roughly the last 80 million years, with reconstructions at selected stages:

- **~80 Ma (Late Cretaceous):** Subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath North America along a continuous Farallon-American trench from Mesoamerica to Alaska.
- **80–40 Ma:** Rapid, oblique subduction, coeval with the Paleogene subduction complexes such as the [[Coastal Belt Franciscan]] and the [[Laramide Orogeny]].
- **40–20 Ma:** Subduction convergence returned to normal values as the [[Pacific-Farallon Ridge]] approached the trench. The [[San Andreas Transform]] began to form around 20 Ma.
- **20 Ma to present:** Lateral transform motion dominated. The Farallon Plate had split into the modern [[Juan de Fuca Plate]] and [[Cocos Plate]].

Plate motions were reconstructed from [[Magnetic Anomalies]] on the seafloor and from [[Hotspot Reference Frame]] tracks. The author notes these reconstructions are most reliable for the past 75 million years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address ecological or biological conditions. Its environmental context is geological and tectonic:

- **Subduction geometry:** Slab dip controls where arc magmas form. Dips were about 50° in the Late Cretaceous, fell to no more than about 10° in the Paleogene, and were about 30° beneath the [[Cascades Subduction Zone]] today.
- **Arc positioning:** In the Late Cretaceous, arc magmatism lay 150–200 km from the trench, and the slab was 125–175 km deep beneath the arc.
- **Accretionary prisms:** Surficial sediments scraped off the subducting plate form wedge-shaped accretionary prisms near the trench.
- **Slab-free region:** Beneath the [[Basin and Range Province]], the absence of a subducted slab since the Neogene is linked to extension and to the lack of arc volcanism.
- **Volcanic provinces:** The [[Snake River Plain]], [[Columbia River Plateau]], and [[Yellowstone Hotspot]] are discussed as enigmatic backarc features.

## Key Findings

1. **Plate reorganization:** The Pacific-Farallon ridge contacted the Farallon-American trench in the Oligocene, and the San Andreas transform was initiated by Pacific-American interaction. By about 20 Ma the Farallon Plate had been divided into derivative plates.
2. **Anomalous subduction rates:** Farallon subduction was approximately normal (less than 10 cm/yr) before 80 Ma. From 80 to 40 Ma it was abnormally rapid, nearly 15 cm/yr, and oblique with a dextral component. Juan de Fuca subduction slowed to less than 5 cm/yr after about 5–7.5 Ma.
3. **Paleogene shallow subduction:** Slab dip decreased to about 10° in the Paleogene. This coincided with a prominent magmatic null in the western Cordillera and with Laramide deformation inland.
4. **Mid-Cenozoic steepening:** Slab dip increased again through the Oligocene. Arc magmatism recovered, and the author suggests this may have promoted [[Cordilleran Core Complexes]].
5. **Slowing of continental motion:** The net motion of the continental interior relative to the asthenosphere fell from about 5 cm/yr (80–40 Ma) to about 2.5 cm/yr over the past 40 Ma. The author links this slowdown to the end of orogeny in the eastern Cordillera.
6. **Transform-driven displacement:** The San Andreas system transferred crustal slices to the Pacific Plate and opened the [[Gulf of California]] as [[Baja California]] moved away from mainland Mexico.
7. **Restoration assumptions:** For the map sequence, the author restored 240 km of post-Miocene dextral slip along the San Andreas, an extra 65 km of intra-Miocene(?) slip, and a nominal 100 km of proto-San Andreas slip. Neogene extension in the Basin and Range was assumed to be about 30 percent of the original width. The author states these restorations are "probably the minimal ones required."
8. **Acknowledged uncertainty:** The author notes that the Transverse Ranges, the California Continental Borderland, and Paleogene paleomagnetic data are not fully resolved.
