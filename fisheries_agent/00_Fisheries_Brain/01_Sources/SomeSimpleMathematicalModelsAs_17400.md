# Some Simple Mathematical Models as Aids in Interpreting the Effect of Fishing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

Carlander (1958) argues that the effects of fishing on fish populations and catch can be explored with models that use no more than simple algebra. The study uses [[Walleye]] biology from [[Clear Lake Iowa]] as its base. [[Model A]], modeled on [[Baranoff Model|Baranoff]] (1918) and Thompson (1937), lets populations accumulate to unrealistic sizes at low mortality. [[Model C]] adds density-dependent compensation in growth, recruitment and natural mortality around a [[Carrying Capacity]] of 2000 pounds. Under Model C, both numbers and poundage in the catch rise with fishing pressure. Models E and F test limits on compensation and the effects of [[Recruitment Failure]]. The author concludes that fishing pressure up to about 50 percent is unlikely to be harmful under the modeled conditions, while stressing that the models are heuristic and need field data to validate them.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a central but poorly understood question in fisheries: how fishing affects fish populations and the catch that follows. Earlier marine fisheries work using mathematical models (Baranoff, 1918; Beverton, 1954; Ricker, 1948; Schaefer, 1954; Thompson, 1937, among others) had been questioned for its fit to real populations (Burkenroad, 1946; Watt, 1955). The author uses the models to show what fishing might do and to identify which field data would best discriminate between them.

Key methodological choices:

- **Cohort approach:** Each model starts with 1000 fish at age I and subtracts annual mortality year by year until the year class disappears.
- **Annual rates:** Total annual mortality is calculated as n + m − nm, where n is [[Natural Mortality]] and m is [[Fishing Mortality]]. The author acknowledges that [[Instantaneous Mortality Rate]] values, as described by [[Ricker Method]], would be more accurate but keeps annual rates for simplicity.
- **Growth data:** Average weights for the first seven years were 0.3006, 0.3938, 0.6493, 0.6126, 0.8625, 0.50 and 0.7433 pounds. Later growth is set at 0.625 pounds per year.
- **Length-weight relation:** Log W = −2.370 + 3.0907 Log L, with W in ounces and L in inches.
- **Mortality estimates:** Whitney (1955) suggested adult annual mortality of 35 to 50 percent. Tag returns gave fishing mortality of 15.7 percent in 1952 and 6.3 percent in 1953.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The walleye biology in the models comes from a [[Clear Lake Iowa]] study. The author notes that the curves would look similar for any species with a comparable life span. Recent age-class composition at Clear Lake matched the output of Model A at about 49 percent total annual mortality. That match was the basis for setting the carrying capacity at 2000 pounds.

Anglers in the models catch only walleyes over 9 inches total length. Age group I therefore experiences only 0.7 of the fishing mortality of older groups, since only 70 percent of age I fish exceed that length. Brood stock dynamics, egg production (drawing on Eschmeyer, 1950) and the survival of eggs to age I (1 to 3 percent, based on salmon studies) are also considered.

## Key Findings

**Model A (no compensation)**
- With 25 percent natural and 20 percent fishing mortality, one 1000-fish year class yields about 414 fish caught, totaling 528.4 pounds (subtotals 413.1 fish and 520.9 pounds through age XII).
- Maximum poundage occurs at fishing rates that roughly equal the natural rate: 20% at 15% natural mortality, 22% at 20%, 25% at 25%, 30% at 30%, and 40% at 40%.
- Accumulated populations at low mortality are judged unrealistic, and the model ignores compensation.

**Model C (carrying capacity with compensation)**
- The population is held near 2000 pounds. Below capacity, growth and recruitment rise and natural mortality falls. Above capacity, these trends reverse.
- Catch in both numbers and poundage increases steadily with fishing mortality, with no decline at high rates.
- Growth factors required to hold the standing crop range from 1.67 at 90 percent total mortality to 0.23 at 15 percent (Table 3, with recruitment deviation halved).
- Model C is considered more realistic than Model A, but the degree of compensation was set arbitrarily and needs field study.

**Model E (constrained compensation)**
- Growth is capped at 1.4 times the present average, natural mortality has a floor of 15 percent, and egg-to-age-I survival is capped at 99 percent.
- These limits have little effect on the computed catch unless they are made much tighter.

**Model F (recruitment failure)**
- One missed recruitment year at 90 percent total mortality reduces the standing crop and changes the catch but is partly offset by faster growth and lower natural mortality.
- Two consecutive failures at 25 percent total mortality were fully compensated by growth adjustment (Table 7).
- With a failure one year in ten, the population stands up well. With 90 percent total mortality, five consecutive failures lead to loss of brood stock. At 80 percent, seven consecutive failures do the same.
- Failure once in three years is absorbed up to about 50 percent fishing pressure. Failure in half of all years lowers catches, but the catch still increases until fishing mortality reaches about 50 percent.

**Large fish (over 20 inches, 2.75 pounds)**
- Model A produces the most large fish at 15 to 20 percent fishing mortality. Models C through F produce the most at 40 to 50 percent.
- The maximum proportion of large fish under Model C is about 72 per thousand. It is reached at fishing mortality of 28 to 30 percent (natural 15%), 23 to 25 percent (20%), 18 to 20 percent (25%), 11 to 13 percent (30%), and 4 percent (50%).

**Average size**
- In Model A, average size falls quickly as fishing pressure rises when natural mortality is low.
- In Model C, the maximum average size occurs at fishing mortality of about 41 percent (natural 15%), 38 percent (20%), 34 percent (25%), 31 percent (30%), 19 percent (40%) and 5 percent (50%).
- Real fishers select for middle and large fish, so actual average sizes are probably larger than modeled.

**Brood stock and catch per effort**
- Brood stock becomes a limiting factor only at the highest annual mortality rates. Egg survival may decline sharply as stock becomes sparse.
- The paper sets up catch per effort as a measure of angler success. If catch per unit effort is proportional to density, it reaches a minimum in Model C at fishing mortality of 30 to 40 percent and is highest at high fishing mortality under Model A.

**Conclusions**
- Model C suggests that fishing pressure up to about 50 percent carries little risk under the conditions modeled.
- The author stresses that these are simple, heuristic models. Field studies are needed to measure compensation in growth, recruitment and mortality, and to test the predicted catch responses against real fisheries data.
