# Special Status Fishes Habitat Management Strategy (BLM, 1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 strategy plan from the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) Special Status Fishes Team sets out a 10-year program (fiscal years 1991–2000) to maintain, protect, and enhance rare fishes and their habitats on public lands in eleven western states. BLM manages 155,000 miles of fishable streams and more than 4 million acres of lakes and reservoirs. Among its resources are 39 fishes listed as threatened or endangered by the Department of the Interior and 73 species that are candidate, BLM-sensitive, or State-listed.

The plan rests on the [[Endangered Species Act]] and Bureau Manual Section 6840, and it implements the objectives of [[Fish and Wildlife 2000]], approved in 1987. Its four main tools are improved activity planning, habitat restoration, protection and acquisition, water rights and water quality protection, and reestablishment of fishes in historic habitats.

Full implementation is projected to produce these results:

- Federal species: complete recovery of 11 species, reclassification of 8 from endangered to threatened, and prevention of extinction of 11 more.
- Other species: complete recovery of 6 State-listed species, prevention of the need to list 18 candidate species, and prevention of extinction of 33 others.

The estimated cost is about $53.5 million over ten years, with about 320 work years of staff time.

## Background & Description

**Scope and diversity.** The Director's preface notes that 112 endangered, threatened, candidate, sensitive, and State-listed fishes occur on BLM lands. These range from desert pupfish and springfish in the Southwest and Great Basin to salmon and steelhead in California and Idaho, rare trout across the West, and the [[Colorado River]] big-river fishes, including the razorback sucker, which the preface describes as reaching 80 pounds.

**Historical drivers.** The plan explains that the present fauna reflects geologic and hydrologic history: glaciation, uplift, drainage reconfiguration, and the desiccation that isolated [[Great Basin]] and desert populations after the last ice age. The region has a high degree of endemism. Habitat loss from water removal, reservoir construction, and nonnative introductions have since displaced many native fishes.

**Threats documented in the text:**

- Water diversions and dams that reduce instream flow and block fish movement, including Warner Valley, Oregon, where irrigation diversion dams block Warner sucker spawning runs.
- Livestock grazing that causes soil compaction, increased turbidity, and toxic ammonia and nitrite levels in heavily used areas.
- Groundwater pumping that dried springs, such as Jackrabbit Spring at [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada, where populations of Ash Meadows pupfish and Ash Meadows speckled dace were destroyed.
- Mining, which increases siltation and occasionally releases toxic heavy metals.
- Nonnative species. Of 20 native California desert fishes, 7 are extinct, 6 are endangered, and 7 are not presently listed. Those surviving must compete with more than 50 introduced species.

**Lahontan cutthroat trout.** This subspecies once occupied about 3,790 miles of streams and 333,585 acres of lakes in Nevada, California, and Oregon. Surveys in the late 1970s and early 1980s found it in only 304 miles of stream and 1,295 acres of lakes. On BLM lands, 233 miles of stream remain occupied out of about 1,713 miles of historic habitat. The text estimates that 75 to 85 percent of occupied and historic habitat on public lands needs improved management for recovery.

**Management history.** The [[Taylor Grazing Act]] of 1934 and the creation of BLM in 1946 established the legal and institutional base. BLM built the School Springs Refugium in 1970 for Warm Springs pupfish. The [[Desert Fishes Council]] was formed in 1968. The Supreme Court upheld the Endangered Species Act in the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] case. FLPMA (1976) provided a legal mandate for land use plans that address aquatic resources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biodiversity.** The plan adopts the Office of Technology Assessment definition of biodiversity at three levels: community or landscape, species, and genetic diversity within species. It warns that introductions of nonnative fishes usually reduce long-term community stability. Intact watersheds on public lands supply most of the drinking water in western states, buffer floods and fire, shade streams to keep them cool for trout, and filter silt-laden runoff.

**Scientific value.** Pupfish in highly saline springs (up to nine times seawater salinity) have informed studies of vertebrate kidney function. Isolated populations help explain zoogeography and speciation. Genetic studies by Oklahoma State University scientists found that protecting many populations across a species' range is needed to preserve most of its genetic information.

**Recreational and viewing value.** Redband trout populations in isolated basins are genetically and ecologically distinct, and some thrive in streams too warm for typical trout. Bonneville cutthroat trout once ranged across Idaho, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming, but most of their streams are now barren of sportfish. Springfish and pupfish are visible in small habitats, which suits nonconsumptive wildlife viewing.

**Key habitats cited:**

- [[Fish Slough]], California: a 35,926-acre [[Areas of Critical Environmental Concern]] (ACEC) that is the type locality for Owens pupfish and supports Owens tui chub.
- Foskett Spring, Oregon: the only native habitat for Foskett speckled dace, acquired by land exchange.
- Cienega Creek, Arizona: habitat for Gila topminnow and candidate Gila chub.
- Warner Valley, Oregon: critical habitat for Warner sucker.
- Mahogany Creek, Nevada: a genetically pure population of Lahontan cutthroat trout.

## Key Findings

**Inventory and planning needs (FY1991–2000):**

- Inventories are needed for 5,749 stream miles, 4,694 lake and reservoir acres, and 299 springs and ponds. Information is lacking for most of these.
- 305 activity plans are needed, with plans favored by habitat or basin rather than by individual species.
- 153 research and study needs were identified, because the life histories of many special status fishes are poorly known.

**Habitat protection and acquisition:**

- Acquisition of 361 stream miles and 78,637 acres is needed to consolidate mixed ownership and protect habitat.
- BLM identified 196 sites where wells for springs and ponds, or protection of instream flows, are needed.
- Recent examples include the 1986 Foskett Spring exchange (160 acres), the 1986 San Pedro River exchange, the 1988 Empire, Cienega, and Rose Tree ranch exchange in Arizona, and the 1989 acquisition of 5,089 acres in Warner Valley, which also created the Warner Lakes ACEC.
- Warner Valley livestock exclusion covered 17.7 stream miles of critical habitat.

**Water rights and quality:** Instream flow studies on Burro and Aravaipa Creeks (Arizona) led to instream flow water rights granted in 1989. Burro Creek and Peoples Canyon Creek were protected under the State Unique Waters Program in 1985. The plan notes that water rights may not guarantee survival if aquifers are not managed sustainably.

**Translocation:** Relocation is treated as a last resort. Examples include Foskett speckled dace stocked in 1979–1980, desert pupfish and Gila topminnow stocked by BLM and the Arizona Game and Fish Department under a 1982 Memorandum of Understanding, and refuges such as Chimney Hot Springs and Blue Link Spring in Nevada. [[Fish Translocation]] must follow established guidelines.

**Habitat improvements (Table 5 summary):**

- 456 improvements are needed. These include fencing, riparian vegetation management, instream structures, exotic species removal, stocking and reintroduction, barrier construction and removal, grazing management, streambank rehabilitation, spring development, and diversion control.
- 42 nonnative species removal or control projects have been identified.
- Estimated cost is about $11.0 million.

**Monitoring:** Monitoring is planned for the effectiveness of habitat actions and the condition of target species, with schedules that permit rapid response to problems.

**Personnel and funding:**

- Approximately 320 work years are needed.
- As of December 1989, BLM employed 40 fisheries biologists, about 25 percent fewer than 10 years earlier. Oregon has 13, Nevada has 3 biologists responsible for 28 special status fishes, and Arizona and New Mexico have none.
- The plan estimates 142 fisheries biologists are needed to implement Fish and Wildlife 2000. The text also states that the Bureau lost about two-thirds of its fisheries positions over the past decade.
- Total estimated cost is about $53.5 million. The text gives $34.3 million for habitat and water rights acquisition, $11.0 million for habitat improvements, about $2.2 million each for inventory, monitoring, and research, and $1.4 million for new activity plans. Table 17's column totals do not match this breakdown exactly; the table lists $1.4 million for inventory and $11.0 million for new plans.
- Cost savings are expected from challenge cost-sharing, volunteer programs, MOUs, and cooperative agreements.

**Strategies (eight):**

1. Inventory occurrence, distribution, and condition of special status fishes.
2. Incorporate protection into [[Resource Management Plan]]s and [[Habitat Management Plan]]s.
3. Support research on genetic uniqueness, reintroduction, and habitat requirements.
4. Use land exchanges, Land and Water Conservation Fund acquisitions, and cooperative agreements to protect habitat, and retain essential habitats in Federal stewardship.
5. Encourage coordination among all parties.
6. Maintain biodiversity and genetic integrity through habitat improvements and control of undesirable species.
7. Monitor management actions and fish status.
8. Increase public awareness.

**Expected outcomes:** The plan states that the strategy depends on acquisition of key private parcels adjacent to public lands for species such as Borax Lake chub, Hiko springfish, Railroad Valley springfish, Pahrump poolfish, Big Spring spinedace, and desert dace. The text also notes that BLM has insufficient ownership to significantly improve recovery of 15 other species, and that the number of species that could benefit from translocations on public lands exceeds those listed in the plan.
