# Hoover Powerplant Modification: Endangered Species Impact Assessment for Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a federal consultation package evaluating whether the proposed [[Hoover Dam]] powerplant modification (a new 500 MW powerhouse plus uprating of existing generators) would affect the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and the non-listed but anticipated-to-be-listed [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) Regional Office concurred with the [[Water and Power Resources Service]] (WAPRS) conclusion that the project would not affect either species. The FWS letter asks that consultation be reinitiated if the project changes in a way that could affect a listed species, or if a species affected by the project is listed later.

The assessment concludes that the project will not jeopardize either species or adversely modify critical habitat. This conclusion rests on three points: the bonytail is no longer present in [[Black Canyon]], the canyon cannot support bonytail reproduction under present cold-water releases, and the razorback population in [[Lake Mohave]] does not depend on Black Canyon habitat.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** The assessment was prepared under Section 7(c) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] Amendments of 1978. The FWS supplied a list of threatened and endangered species that may occur in the project area. The action agency must then determine whether those species may be affected. If no impact is evident, the Act's requirements are considered fulfilled. The bonytail is the listed species of concern. The razorback is discussed because it may be listed during project development.

**Project description.** Adding the 500 MW powerhouse and uprating the existing generators would raise total plant capacity at Hoover Dam to 2,300 MW. The plant would reach nameplate capacity on about 4-7 percent of days. Operations would follow the existing pattern. In spring and summer, peak releases would increase to meet demand above 1,340 MW. Fall and winter releases would stay near historical levels, since demand rarely exceeds 1,340 MW in winter. Total water volume is unchanged, so higher peak releases mean lower releases during low-demand periods. A minimum release of 2,000 ft³/s (equivalent to 70 MW) would continue whenever Lake Mohave is below elevation 637 ft.

**Study basis.** Information came from published literature, contracted research, state, federal and private sources. Cited work includes [[Paulson and Miller Studies]] on Black Canyon ecology (1980), the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] Environmental and Water Quality Operational Studies (1980), and FWS aquatic surveys of the lower Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical changes.**
- **Construction cofferdam (5-year period):** Mean cross-section velocity was projected to rise from 1.9 mph to 9.2 mph over a 500-800 ft reach immediately below the dam. Hydrologic analysis confined the effect to this reach. The cofferdam would be removed afterward, and fill material would be hauled to a dump site rather than released to the river.
- **Operational fluctuations:** At 62,000 ft³/s with Lake Mohave at elevation 647 ft, daily water-level fluctuation at the dam base would be about 16 ft (currently 10 ft), falling to about 1 ft at Willow Beach and under 0.5 ft at river mile 14. At Lake Mohave elevation 630 ft, fluctuation would be about 31 ft (currently 24 ft), falling to about 6 ft at Willow Beach.
- **Velocities (Table 1):** Under peak release at Mohave elevation 647 ft, mean velocity (dam to mile 18) would be 2.8 mph, compared with 1.9 mph under current operations. At elevation 630 ft, mean velocity would be 3.6 mph, compared with 2.5 mph currently. Minimum-release velocities would not change.

**Substrate and primary productivity.** The cofferdam and high releases would shift loose sand, gravel, cobbles and boulders. This would tear loose filamentous algae and disturb [[Periphyton]], reducing food production by an unknown percentage. Scouring would be limited to the first 3 miles of the canyon. The assessment describes three possible long-term outcomes, ranked by probability:
1. **Most probable:** After about 6 months of intermittent bottom movement, the bed would re-armor. Scouring to particles of 5 inches or larger would expose rocky substrate, enhancing algal attachment and invertebrate growth. The [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reach, which has comparable velocities, is cited as evidence of luxuriant periphyton growth.
2. **Probable:** Biomass would remain near present levels, with losses in high-velocity areas offset by gains in formerly sandy areas.
3. **Least probable:** Scouring and reduced primary productivity. The periphyton community would not be eliminated, though it could be significantly reduced in the first 3 miles, and substrate instability could persist.

**Temperature.** Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery requires cold water, so releases would continue through the lower penstock gates on unmodified penstocks. Under a 62,000 ft³/s release at Lake Mead elevation 1120 ft (the lowest level predicted for 60 years), with four lower and one upper gate open, maximum temperature would be 57°F, and the minimum would stay at the current 54°F. The worst case (modified penstock alone) would produce a 54-61°F range, a 7°F daily fluctuation. The assessment states that even this would have little effect on the fishery.

**Bonytail habitat.** Bonytail have declined sharply across their range and now persist mainly in Lake Mohave and the Upper Colorado River (Green River, Utah). The species is considered at risk of extinction in Lake Mohave, apparently because it is not reproducing. Eddy and backwater habitat, where the species and its young are reported to occur, is very limited in Black Canyon. Water temperature there has changed markedly from predam conditions (see [[Colorado River Temperature and Discharge Cycles]], Figure 2).

**Razorback habitat.** Razorbacks were once widespread and abundant in the Lower Colorado River. They are now rarely taken below [[Davis Dam]] but are reported as fairly abundant in Lakes Havasu, Mohave and Mead. Spawning typically occurs from late winter to early summer at 54-68°F, over gravel in 1-20 ft of water. Spawning is observed annually in Arizona Cove and Cottonwood Cove in Lake Mohave, with aggregations of 200-300 fish.

## Key Findings

**Conclusion of the assessment**
- The project will not jeopardize the bonytail or razorback, and will not destroy or adversely modify critical habitat.
- Aquatic impacts are confined to the reach between Hoover Dam and the cold-warm water interface in Lake Mohave.

**Bonytail chub**
- The species has not been observed or collected in Black Canyon for nearly 20 years. The last canyon collection was in 1961 at Willow Beach. The last documented young were collected at Cottonwood Landing, Nevada, on June 15, 1950.
- Extensive scuba surveys by FWS and the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, found no bonytail in Black Canyon. The agency therefore concludes the species is no longer present there.
- Cold releases make egg development unlikely. Fertilized eggs of both bonytail and razorback incubated in 54°F river water showed 100 percent mortality, while eggs in 60-70°F hatchery water hatched successfully (Toney, 1974).
- Bonytail feed mainly near the surface (terrestrial insects, plant debris, and in Lake Mohave, algae and midge larvae). Scouring of benthic organisms therefore should not affect the species and may increase food availability downstream.
- The only recorded spawning was an aggregate of about 500 bonytail reported by Jones and Sumner (1954) over a gravel shelf about 10 miles below Eldorado Canyon. Suspected spawning (May-July) coincides with historical flood flows.

**Razorback sucker**
- Adults are present in large numbers in the impact area. The species is a substrate grazer, with a diet of insect larvae, algae and plant debris. [[Cladophora]] was historically the main diet item in the canyon (Moffett, 1942).
- Velocities under the modification remain below pre-dam velocities (3.7 mph and 9.5 mph, as cited).
- All sampled fish were adults, indicating poor recruitment. A post-larval fish from below the Hoover Dam tailrace was tentatively identified as a razorback, suggesting limited reproduction in Black Canyon. Taxonomic confirmation is still needed.
- Divers observed one female and two to three males attempting to spawn near Hoover Dam. Milt was released, but no eggs were found.
- Lake Mohave supports a self-sustaining adult population. Its razorbacks appear healthy and do not depend on Black Canyon food. Senator Wash Reservoir, California, also has a healthy adult population.
- Under an unlikely long-term food reduction, fish in the upper canyon would move downstream to better habitat rather than be lost.

**Causes of decline**
- Dams, reservoirs and inundation of natural habitat, together with the loss of the predam regime of fluctuating flow, velocity and temperature, are the major causes of decline.
- Introduced exotic fish (catfish, carp, sunfish) feed on eggs and fry and can effectively suppress reproduction (Minckley, 1979; Behnke, 1980).

**Monitoring and reinitiation**
- The FWS requires reinitiating consultation if the project changes so that a listed species may be affected, or if a potentially affected species is listed later.
