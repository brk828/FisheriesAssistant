#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Rinne and Kroeger quantifies the physical habitat requirements of the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small, threatened/endangered cyprinid endemic to the Gila River basin, within [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona. Using electrofishing and a standardized transect-based habitat sampler across six sampling periods (winter, spring, summer 1982-85), the authors measured depth, velocity, and substrate at sites with and without spikedace. Results indicate that spikedace generally occupy shallow, moderate-velocity waters over gravel-pebble substrate, largely tracking habitat availability rather than showing a fixed, narrow preference. However, when analyzed by *abundance* (schooling behavior) rather than mere presence/absence, larger schools (≥10 individuals) were strongly associated with deeper, slower, eddying waters near shear zones, boulders, and canyon walls — suggesting these features represent preferred or optimal habitat, potentially linked to energetically efficient drift-feeding and to ontogenetic (juvenile vs. adult) differences in habitat use.

## Background & Description

- **Body size**: Small cyprinid, adult total length < 100 mm.
- **Life stage size classes noted in this study**: Juveniles observed at 28–37 mm total length, comprising 60% of individuals at 5 of 10 large-aggregation (>10 fish) transects — indicating a distinct juvenile schooling/habitat pattern.
- No detailed external morphological description (e.g., fin ray counts, coloration) is provided in this habitat-focused paper; taxonomic morphology is referenced to [[Miller And Hubbs 1960]] as the primary source of descriptive taxonomy for the [[Plagopterini]] group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Taxonomic context**: Endemic cyprinid of the Gila River basin (Arizona and New Mexico), part of the spiny-rayed minnow tribe [[Plagopterini]].
- **Stream type**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] is a low-gradient, small (3–5 m wide), often-braided, hydrologically stable desert stream with primarily gravel-pebble substrate; modal discharge 0.5–1.0 m³/sec, highest flow in winter, subject to violent late-summer flash floods.
- **Habitat use (overall, this study)**:
  - Mean depth used: **20 cm**
  - Mean velocity used: **35 cm/sec**
  - Substrate: gravel-pebble (3–64 mm)
- **Habitat use by aggregation size**:
  - Large schools (≥10 fish): mean depth **22.8–27.3 cm**, mean velocity **22.7–25.7 cm/sec**
  - Single fish: mean depth **15.7–16 cm**, mean velocity **36.6–37 cm/sec**
- **Microhabitat features associated with large aggregations**: eddying currents, shear zones (confluence of stream braids), areas near instream boulders, and canyon walls.
- **Seasonal variation**: Depths used were shallower (14–16 cm) in December, February, and August, and deeper (23–27 cm) in April, May, and September — no clear consistent seasonal pattern, but significant differences among sampling dates (Welch's test, P<0.001 for depth; ANOVA P<0.001 for velocity).
- **Comparative habitat descriptions from prior literature**:
  - [[Miller And Hubbs 1960]]: deep (61–122 cm) swift pools/riffle mouths over sand-gravel in the Gila River; shallow algae-covered riffles for adults in some reaches; young in backwaters with silt-sand in the Verde River.
  - [[Barber Et Al 1970]]: spawning females in deeper pools/eddies; males in riffles (8–15 cm) over sand-gravel.
  - [[Anderson 1978]]: common in moderate-to-swift riffles <30 cm deep, gravel-rubble (15–120 mm) substrate; larvae in slow currents near riffles.
  - [[Turner And Tafanelli 1983]] (also Aravaipa Creek): most common at 18–37 cm depth, 39–82 cm/sec velocity; rare below 18 cm/sec or above 90 cm/sec.
  - [[Propst Et Al 1986]] (upper Gila River): average depth <32 cm, velocity <15 cm/sec.
- **Potential competitor**: introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), noted as needing comparative habitat-use study.
- **Feeding ecology note**: Possible drift-feeding strategy in low-energy eddying zones (referencing [[Schreiber And Minckley 1981]]), allowing reduced energy expenditure.

## Key Findings

- Spikedace habitat use in Aravaipa Creek generally **paralleled habitat availability** rather than reflecting a narrow, fixed preference (based on presence/absence comparisons).
- Significant differences between habitat where spikedace were present vs. absent were **inconsistent across sampling dates**: depth differed significantly only in December 1982 and April 1983; velocity differed significantly only in August 1983; substrate size differed significantly only in December 1982 (Table 1, t-tests/Chi-square).
- When habitat preference was assessed via **relative abundance** (schooling) rather than presence/absence, a clearer pattern emerged: larger aggregations (≥10, ≥12 fish) preferred **deeper, slower, eddying waters**, while solitary fish occupied shallower, faster water.
- These preferred aggregation sites were structurally linked to **shear zones** (confluence of braided channels), **instream boulders**, and **canyon walls** — features that generate eddying currents and localized pool formation.
- **Ontogenetic habitat differentiation** is suggested: large aggregations were often dominated (60%) by juveniles (28–37 mm TL), implying juveniles may preferentially use these slower, deeper eddy habitats.
- Comparison with other studies shows **wide variability** in reported spikedace habitat (depth and especially velocity), which the authors attribute to differences in point of velocity measurement within the water column, ontogenetic differences, and differing habitat availability among study streams/rivers.
- The authors caution that results from [[Aravaipa Creek]] (a small stream) **should not be extrapolated** to larger streams or rivers without comparable methodology.
- Management implications: further research needed on life-stage-specific habitat use, spawning habitat, and competitive interactions with introduced species (e.g., [[Red Shiner]]) to support conservation and recovery planning for this threatened/endangered cyprinid, the [[Spikedace]].
