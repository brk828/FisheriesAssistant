# Spawning Season of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the full spawning season of the [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]], the largest remaining population of the species in the [[Colorado River]] system. Using monthly visual surveys, [[Trammel Netting]] of ripe adults, and [[Larval Sampling]] during 1982 and 1983, the authors found that spawning extended from November to May, but larvae were produced only from spawning in December through March. Peak spawning occurred from January through March at water temperatures of roughly 10 to 15°C. The authors conclude that spawning conditions in the reservoir are adequate for reproduction, but that recruitment of larvae into the juvenile and adult population appears to be limited, and that the cause remains unresolved.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once widespread and abundant in the Colorado River system but has declined over the past 50 years ([[Minckley 1983]]; [[Johnson and Rinne 1982]]). Its upper-basin distribution is now scattered, and the largest known upper-basin population is perhaps fewer than 1,000 individuals ([[Lanigan and Tyus 1989]]). In the lower basin, the species is abundant only in Lake Mohave and rare elsewhere. Historical spawning reports came from [[Lake Mead]], Lake Mohave, [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], where fish used shallow, flat gravel areas.

[[Lake Mohave]] formed after [[Davis Dam]] was completed in 1951. The document reports that the impoundment inundated 73,815 km² of desert (figure as printed). The upper 32 km is a riverine canyon, and the lower 58 km is lentic. The reservoir has a long north-south fetch and is exposed to strong seasonal winds. The widest section, the [[Six Mile Area]], is unprotected and experiences severe wave action. Sheltered sites such as [[Arizona Bay]] (Yuma Cove, Gold Cove) have northerly exposure and cobble, gravel, and sand substrates supplied by desert washes.

The study's stated aim was to describe the entire spawning season within a single year, which earlier work had not done. Earlier reports had placed the season only at January through March.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Water temperature was 13°C in November 1982 when spawning began. It fell to a minimum of 9.5°C in December and January, then rose to over 20°C (22°C in early May) as spawning ended. Lake turnover in November coincided with decreasing temperature and photoperiod and rising reservoir levels. [[Wave Action]] and reservoir elevation fluctuations, together with inputs from [[Flash Floods]], regenerate [[Gravel Spawning Substrate]] in the reservoir.

**Spawning behavior.** Groups of one to eight males chased females. One or two males then pressed against the female and vibrated, flushing fine sediments and forming depressions on the lake bottom. No prior substrate preparation was observed. By late February, spawning was so intense that individual depressions were no longer recognizable. Wave action and fish activity carried silt and sand offshore, which appeared to increase interstitial space for eggs.

**Associated species.** [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] were seen at night in shallow spawning areas (<2 m). [[Common Carp]] were present day and night, with abundance rising through the season. [[Green Sunfish]] appeared offshore near *Myriophyllum* beds after March. Other species netted included [[Largemouth Bass]], rainbow trout, Lahontan cutthroat trout, striped bass, and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Hybrid crosses with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were also recorded.

**Recruitment and predation.** Larvae were produced, but few juveniles have been found, and recruitment appears low ([[McCarthy and Minckley 1987]]). Food limitation does not appear to restrict larval survival ([[Papoulias 1987]]; [[Marsh and Langhorst 1988]]). The authors consider introduced-species predation the most plausible explanation for recruitment failure, but they say direct evidence of predation is still needed.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- **Visual surveys:** Spawning areas were observed monthly from December 1981 through August 1983, using shore, boat, and SCUBA observations by day and night.
- **Trammel netting:** Nets measured 91.4 × 1.8 m, with 3.8 cm inner mesh and 25.4 cm outer mesh. Nets were set for 12 to 24 hours, mostly from January through May. Catch rates were standardized as fish per 100 m² per 24 hours.
- **Larval collection:** Larvae were taken at night with dive lights and dip nets. Stage was identified with [[Snyder 1981]]. Spawning dates were back-calculated from laboratory development rates ([[Bozek et al. 1990]]) and monthly reservoir temperatures measured at 1 m depth.
- **Ripeness:** Fish were ripe if milt or eggs were extruded under abdominal pressure.

**Spawning season**

- Ripe fish (n=546) and larvae (n=1,205) were collected. Spawning activity ran from November through May.
- Larvae were collected only from spawning in December through March, so about four to five of the seven spawning months produced swim-up larvae.
- Peak spawning (ripeness, activity, and larval collections) occurred from January through March, at 10 to 15°C. Fish were observed at 9.5 to 15°C.
- Spawning in November had not previously been reported. Tuberculate razorback suckers netted in November 1974 support a fall maturation pattern.
- Ripe eggs collected in November were fertilized and developed normally in the laboratory.

**Larvae**

- 48 larvae were collected in 1982 (February, Arizona Bay and Six Mile Area).
- 1,157 larvae were collected in 1983. The largest single sample, 973 larvae on 19 February at the Six Mile Area, was 5.1 larvae per minute.
- No larvae were collected in March 1983 at the Six Mile Area after storms, but larvae were still collected at [[Tequila Cove]] in March. No larvae were collected after March.
- Back-calculation indicated that larvae collected in January 1983 were spawned between late November and mid-December.

**Adults**

- Trammel netting caught 1,049 razorback suckers (including unmeasured fish). The species was the most abundant netted fish at Arizona Bay from January through April (84.3% of catch) and in the Six Mile Coves from February through April (71.3%).
- Twelve hybrids were also netted.
- All measured adults were mature. Mean total length was 554.1 mm for males and 626.3 mm for females in 1982, and 554.7 mm and 625.4 mm in 1983. Females were significantly larger (Mann-Whitney U-test, p=0.001).
- Males usually made up more than 60% of netted fish. The highest male share (79.2%) occurred in November and May 1982.
- Condition factor was generally higher in females. Male condition declined in March 1982 and stayed low until the next season.
- In November 1982, 89.5% of males and 20% of females were ripe. From January through March, more than 50% of both sexes were ripe. In May, only males were ripe.

**Conclusions and future work**

- Spawning conditions in Lake Mohave (gravel substrates, suitable temperatures, wave hydrodynamics) appear adequate for reproduction.
- Reproduction is successful, but recruitment into the adult population is low, and the cause is unresolved.
- The authors call for direct evidence of predation and further research on why larvae do not complete the transition to the juvenile stage. This work is needed to improve the long-term survival of this rare species.
