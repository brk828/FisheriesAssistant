# Chubs of the Genus Gila in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife species profile, drawn from the booklet *Colorado's Little Fish*, covers the [[Genus Gila]] chubs and the Creek Chub (*Semotilus atromaculatus*). It addresses taxonomic uncertainty within *Gila*, provides identification keys, and describes the range, habitat, reproduction, and decline of five species in Colorado. Three *Gila* species of the [[Upper Colorado River]] mainstem, the [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]], are federally and state-listed or at risk, and the document links their decline to reservoir-related cold-water releases and hybridization. The [[Rio Grande Chub]] is restricted to the Rio Grande Basin in Colorado, and the Creek Chub is a widespread native minnow.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The taxonomic history of *Gila* is described as "long, clouded and uncertain." The main obstacle has been limited knowledge of geographic and individual variation (Rinne 1976). Early workers treated the bonytail as a variant of the roundtail chub (Ellis 1914) or as a subspecies of the *Gila robusta* complex (Miller 1946). Smith et al. (1979) analyzed 34 morphometric and meristic characters and concluded that three separate species occur in the upper Colorado River mainstem: *G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, and *G. elegans*. A fourth species, *G. pandora*, occurs in the Rio Grande Basin.

**Identification Key.** The document presents a dichotomous key based on Smith et al. (1979):

1. Caudal peduncle deep, anal rays 8, Rio Grande Basin only: *G. pandora*.
2. Caudal peduncle slender, anal rays usually more than 8: go to 2. No nuchal hump, frontals nearly straight, dorsal and anal rays usually nine: *G. robusta*.
3. Nuchal hump present in specimens over 5.9 inches. Nuchal hump abrupt, dorsal rays usually 9, anal rays usually 10: *G. cypha*. Nuchal hump not abrupt, dorsal rays usually 10, anal rays 10 or 11: *G. elegans*.

**Species Profiles.**

- **[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)**: A moderately streamlined minnow. Adults reach about 18 inches and two pounds. Historically the most common *Gila* in the Colorado Basin.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: A streamlined minnow with a prominent nuchal hump. Adults are usually 12 to 16 inches long and weigh 3/4 to 2 pounds. Federally and state endangered.
- **[[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*)**: A highly streamlined fish with a pencil-like caudal peduncle. Seven-year-old adults can reach 14 inches and weigh more than one pound. Federally and state endangered.
- **[[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*)**: An elongated, robust chub with a deep caudal peduncle. Reaches about 12 inches in larger streams.
- **[[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*)**: A large, robust minnow, sometimes exceeding 10 inches, and one of the most common minnows in North America.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybridization.** Where natural habitats and flow regimes have been altered by impoundments or diversions, barriers between the three upper-basin *Gila* species have broken down and hybridization has occurred. Colorado specimens show a complete gradation of characters from humpback to bonytail to roundtail chub. The authors advise that identification should combine physical characters with geographic location. Hybrid individuals reduce the number of genetically pure fish and dilute the gene pool.

**Cold-Water Releases.** Reservoir releases often come from deep, cold layers of the impoundment, lowering downstream temperatures during the growing season. This is considered a leading factor in the decline of *Gila* species.

**Roundtail Chub Habitat.** Adults occupy slow water adjacent to faster currents and move into fast water to feed. Young-of-the-year prefer shallow river runs, juveniles use river eddies and irrigation ditches, and adults take both terrestrial and aquatic insects and filamentous algae. Large roundtails prey on small fish.

**Humpback Chub Habitat.** The species occurs in deep, fast water with a sand, gravel, and boulder bed, notably the Black Rocks area of the Colorado River downstream of Grand Junction. It prefers slower eddies and pools and avoids swift current. Exotic and other native species are normally absent from its habitat.

**Bonytail Habitat.** Little is known about the species, but it prefers eddies and pools rather than swift current.

**Rio Grande Chub Habitat.** The species lives in pools of small to moderate streams near areas of current, associated with undercut banks, overhanging vegetation, and aquatic plants. It has also been collected in small impoundments in the San Luis Valley.

**Creek Chub Range.** Native to much of eastern and central North America. In Colorado it occurs in the mainstem North and South Platte rivers and many permanent western tributaries.

## Key Findings

**Roundtail Chub**
- Declining in the [[Gunnison River]]. Common in the 1970s in the North Fork of the Gunnison, but not found there in 1981 or 1982 (S. Nehring, personal communication).
- The species also declined downstream of the North Fork to the town of Delta over the same period.
- Spawning occurs over gravel in early summer in warmer water than the humpback chub, as spring runoff subsides. Eggs are scattered with no parental care. Young-of-the-year (0.4 inches) were captured from early June in the Colorado River and in late July (Haynes and Muth 1982).
- The document suggests this timing may explain decline below the [[Curecanti Project]], where coldwater releases keep temperatures low all summer.

**Humpback Chub**
- The greatest numbers in Colorado occur at Black Rocks. The species has been found in the Yampa, Gunnison, Green, and Colorado rivers.
- Tagging studies show Black Rocks fish rarely move more than 0.5 miles from their capture and marking site (Miller et al. 1982).
- Spawning occurs at water temperatures of 58 to 62°F (Valdez et al. 1982).
- Hamman (1982) found 27% of eggs hatched at 54 to 56°F, compared with 79 to 84% at 66 to 68°F.
- Valdez et al. (1982) suggest that reduced flows allow the roundtail chub to occupy deepwater areas once used only by humpbacks. A tentatively identified humpback x roundtail hybrid was reported near DeBeque.

**Bonytail**
- Historically found throughout the Colorado River Drainage. In recent years taken only from the Green River in Utah and from Lakes Havasu and Mohave (Miller et al. 1982).
- Jordan (1891) reported one bonytail from the Gunnison River near Delta. No bonytails were collected in Colorado for several years until one was taken at Black Rock, west of Grand Junction, in 1984.
- Hamman (1982b) found that 96% of bonytail eggs incubated in cold water (54 to 56°F) failed to hatch, while optimal hatching and larval survival occurred at 68 to 70°F.
- Juvenile bonytail (3 to 5 inches) had the greatest swimming stamina at 80°F and the least at 58°F (Bulkley et al. 1982). Lower downstream temperatures from impoundments may harm the species.

**Rio Grande Chub**
- Native to Colorado, but restricted to scattered sites in the Rio Grande Basin.
- In 1984, fish were collected from upper Dome Lake in the Cochetopa Creek basin, which lies in the Colorado River basin. The population probably originated from stocking or a bait bucket transfer.
- Hybridizes with the longnose dace, showing intermediate characters (Suttkus and Cashner 1981).

**Creek Chub**
- One of the most common minnows in North America.
- Its biology in Colorado is poorly known.

**Research Status**
- The document notes that much remains to be learned about *Gila* taxonomy, and that research must consider both morphology and location. Its literature list (about 100 references) includes major works on *Gila* taxonomy, Colorado River fish monitoring, and endangered fish recovery.
