# Native Arizona Fish in Decline

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 *Arizona Daily Star* feature (with a companion article on [[Gila Chub]] and green sunfish in Sabino Creek) reports that native Arizona fish are among the most threatened vertebrate groups in the Southwest. Of 33 native species, 29 of the remaining 32 are in serious trouble, according to scientists quoted in the piece. The main drivers cited are damming, groundwater pumping and, above all, [[Non-Native Fish Introductions]] that prey on or compete with natives. The article also reports one extinction, the [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]] (1971), and describes ongoing recovery efforts for the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] and a removal study of green sunfish in Sabino Creek.

## Background & Description

- **Species count and status:** 33 native Arizona fish species were identified. Per [[W.L. Minckley]] (ASU zoology professor, studying native Arizona fish since 1963), one species is extinct, two are confined to the Pacific Ocean drainage, and 29 of the other 30 are endangered, threatened, or under study for listing. The article's own sentence gives "29 of the remaining 32" in trouble; the two figures are reported as stated.
- **Notable species:**
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]]: the largest native fish and the Colorado system's top predator; historically up to six feet long and 75 to 100 pounds. Minnow family (Cyprinidae). Can live 75 years.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: can live 50 years.
  - [[Bonytail Chub]]: can exceed 40 years; described as the most endangered vertebrate in the United States (per Chuck Minckley); now found only in Lake Mohave.
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] and Gila and Yaqui topminnows: endangered; their skin pigmentation is being studied for possible cancer-preventing properties.
  - [[Gila Chub]]: rare; status under study.
  - [[Desert Pupfish]]: endangered.
- **Non-native species:** [[Green Sunfish]] (native to the Great Lakes and Mississippi River system) and largemouth bass, introduced for sport, food, or mosquito control.
- **Expert sources:** W.L. "Mink" Minckley (ASU); Tom Gatz (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Phoenix); Kirk Young (Arizona Game and Fish Department); Chuck Minckley (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Parker office); Rob Dudley (UA graduate student); William Matter (UA wildlife and fisheries professor).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation and drainage:** More than 90 percent of Arizona's waterways drain into the [[Colorado River]]. Only small creeks in the far southeast corner, east of Douglas, flow south into Mexico. Minckley notes the Colorado River Basin is millions of years old and that "as little as three feet of dry land would prevent them from moving."
- **Hydrologic change:** Desert rivers fluctuated greatly in depth, speed and course, trapping fish in pools until the next high-flow period. Native fish tolerate high temperatures and very high salinity and are strong swimmers that disperse rapidly when given the chance.
- **Reproduction and life history:** Long lifespans make reproduction and growth to a size able to defeat predators slow.
- **Ecosystem role:** Fish eat insects, algae, organic material and microscopic animals, helping keep streams and rivers clean. Birds, amphibians and mammals also depend on fish as food.
- **Lower Colorado:** Below Parker Dam there are no native fishes left. Only nine fish kinds existed in the lower Colorado (south of Lee's Ferry), with five abundant. Transplanted fish from the Mississippi system, which hosts hundreds of species, had "vast experience with competition for food and many predators."
- **Historical distribution:** The San Pedro and Gila rivers supported the full native assemblage. The Santa Cruz River, though flowing year-round decades ago, was too small for larger species and held desert pupfish, Gila topminnow, Gila chub, longfin and speckled dace, and two sucker types.
- **Sabino Creek:** Native longfin dace and probably Gila topminnows lived there before non-native fish were introduced. A dam built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in the late 1930s, stocked with smallmouth bass and green sunfish, is the apparent origin of the sunfish. Chubs are 30 times more plentiful beyond a boulder barrier between bridges eight and nine, which blocks the sunfish. Only adult chubs (7 to 8 inches) share space with sunfish. The bass were caught out after the dam's completion, but the sunfish persisted.
- **Monkey Spring:** Ranch hands stocked largemouth bass in Monkey Lake in 1968 (later removed by nets) and again in late 1970 or early 1971. Scientists did not revisit until summer 1971; the pupfish was gone.

## Key Findings

1. **Severe decline:** 29 of 32 remaining native species are in serious trouble; one is extinct (Monkey Spring pupfish, 1971).
2. **Main threats:** Dams, groundwater pumping, and above all non-native predators and competitors. Minckley identifies these as the greatest cause of native losses.
3. **Shared responsibility:** Gatz attributes the release of non-native fish to local, state and federal agencies and individuals.
4. **Predation in Sabino Creek:** Green sunfish, which can eat prey up to three-quarters of their own length, and some 9-inch individuals swallowing whole crayfish, threaten Gila chubs. Sunfish may also outcompete chubs for insects and are aggressive during spawning.
5. **Recovery program:** Chuck Minckley's crews build predator-free coves on Lakes Mohave and Havasu so bonytails and razorbacks can reach about 12 inches before release. The bonytail effort took 25 years to catch 30 wild adults.
6. **Squawfish reintroduction:** Hatchery-bred Colorado squawfish have been planted in the Salt and Verde rivers.
7. **Management proposals:** Minckley says regulations must change to manage for natives and non-natives, requiring compromise and education. Dudley's report would recommend electrofishing to remove green sunfish, selective chemical treatment, or both. Matter warns that people often move fish, including aquarium fish, creating the "Johnny Applefish effect."
8. **Medical value:** Gila and Yaqui topminnow skin pigmentation may prevent cancer, so these fish are subjects of medical research.
