# Multivariate Discrimination of Colorado Plateau Gila (Cyprinidae): The "Art of Seeing Well" Revisited

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #morphometrics #endangered-species #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This study applies multivariate discriminant analysis to meristic and morphometric data to resolve long-standing confusion in identifying three endangered [[Gila]] species from the [[Colorado Plateau]]: [[Gila robusta]] (roundtail chub), [[Gila cypha]] (humpback chub), and [[Gila elegans]] (bonytail). Using museum specimens (81 adult G. robusta, 58 G. cypha, 28 G. elegans, plus 142 juveniles and 17 putative hybrids), researchers tested whether these species are morphologically distinguishable, whether juveniles can be identified using adult characters, and whether putative hybrids show intermediate morphology. Results demonstrate that adults can be discriminated with >95% accuracy using either meristic or morphometric characters, with morphometric data providing slightly superior discrimination (97% vs 95%). A five-character discriminant function was developed for field identification. The study addresses historical confusion among fishery biologists in identifying these species and provides practical tools for resource managers working with these endangered fishes.

## Background & Description

The cyprinid genus [[Gila]] exhibits complex morphological variation that has troubled taxonomists and fishery biologists since initial species descriptions. Three "big river" chubs inhabit [[Colorado River]] tributaries: [[Gila robusta]], [[Gila cypha]], and [[Gila elegans]]. Historical confusion stemmed from multiple factors including ecotypy, hybridization, ontogenetic variation, inadequate character selection, and weak analytical methods. The situation worsened in recent decades as dam construction eliminated or compromised species' niches, forcing previously allopatric forms into sympatry and increasing hybridization potential.

All three species are endangered. Historical records document widespread confusion among state and federal biologists, with many unable or unwilling to identify specimens to species level. Pre-impoundment studies from the 1950s-1960s often lumped all forms as "bonytails" despite clear morphological differences. This confusion reflected both inadequate training in ichthyology and an institutional bias against native fish research in western North America.

The study examined 25 morphometric and 10 meristic variables from museum specimens collected through 1978. Data were partitioned into three subsets: 10 meristic counts, 25 morphometric measurements, and a field subset (3 meristic + 19 morphometric characters measurable in the field). Missing values (<2% of data) were estimated via linear regression. Morphometric characters were size-adjusted using log₁₀ ratios (each measurement subtracted from log₁₀ standard length).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

These species occupy different ecological niches within [[Colorado River Basin]] ecosystems. [[Gila robusta]] is an opportunistic, sporadic feeder consuming fish, aquatic and terrestrial insects, with 23% of examined specimens (n=49) containing tapeworms. In contrast, [[Gila elegans]] (n=67) feeds on terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae, with no tapeworm infections observed. These dietary differences, along with distinct growth rates and length-weight relationships, support species-level rather than subspecies distinctions.

Spawning behavior differs between [[Gila robusta]] and [[Gila cypha]]. At Black Rocks, Colorado, spawning overlaps temporally but differs spatially. Radiotelemetry showed G. robusta (n=17) moved throughout the upper Colorado River during spawning (mean maximum displacement = 33.9 river-km), while G. cypha (n=33) remained restricted to a 1.8 river-km reach (mean maximum displacement = 1.4 river-km). In the [[Yampa River]], both species in reproductive condition were sympatric in eddy habitats over 5-6 weeks following peak spring runoff.

All three species are omnivorous carnivores with specializations related to habitat and food processing. Trophic characters appeared in discriminating character sets across all analyses, suggesting resource partitioning occurs between species.

## Key Findings

**Species Discrimination - Adults:**

Non-parametric discriminant analysis successfully segregated adult specimens with high accuracy across all species pairs:

- [[Gila robusta]] vs [[Gila elegans]]: 100% discrimination using either two meristic characters (Dorsal Rays, Gill Rakers) or single morphometric character (Depth of Caudal Peduncle). This was the easiest species-pair to separate.

- [[Gila cypha]] vs [[Gila elegans]]: Most difficult pair to discriminate. Meristic characters provided 98.8% separation (85/86 specimens), morphometric characters 96.5% (83/86), and field characters 95.3% (82/86). Key discriminating characters included Gill Rakers, Dorsal Rays, Caudal Peduncle Depth, and Length of Upper Jaw.

- [[Gila robusta]] vs [[Gila cypha]]: Meristic characters separated 95.7% (133/139), morphometric 99.3% (138/139), and field characters 99.3%. Depth of Frontal Depression (measuring nuchal concavity) was important, along with Anal Fin Base and Head Depth at Eye.

**Three-Species Discrimination:**

When all three species were analyzed together, discrimination remained >95% for all data types:
- Meristic: 92.2% correct (154/167)
- Morphometric: 95.6% correct (131/137)  
- Field: 96.3% correct (132/137)

[[Gila robusta]] was correctly assigned 100% using morphometric or field data. [[Gila elegans]] proved most difficult to classify, accounting for 3 of 4 misidentified individuals in field data analysis.

**Discriminant Function:**

A parametric discriminant function using five field-measurable morphometric characters was developed:
1. Snout Length
2. Snout to Occiput
3. Dorsal Base
4. Caudal Peduncle Depth
5. Anal Origin to Caudal Base

This function classified specimens 94% better than chance (Kappa = 0.9417, P < 0.00001). Jackknife procedures failed to improve original classification, indicating robustness. The function provides field workers a practical tool using only calipers and calculator.

**Juvenile Classification:**

Overall juvenile assignment was 88% (126/142), but results were misleading:
- [[Gila robusta]] juveniles: 97.2% correct (103/106)
- [[Gila cypha]] juveniles: 60.0% correct (18/30)
- [[Gila elegans]] juveniles: 66.7% correct (4/6)

Misclassified G. cypha juveniles (n=12) were assigned to G. robusta; misclassified G. elegans (n=2) were also assigned to G. robusta. Results suggest adult characters (particularly occiput and nuchal hump) have not developed sufficiently in juvenile G. cypha and G. elegans to serve as diagnostic tools. Different, ontogenetically-specific characters are needed for juvenile identification.

**Putative Hybrids:**

Seventeen putative hybrids were examined:
- G. robusta × G. cypha (n=10): 60% assigned to G. robusta, 40% to G. cypha
- G. cypha × G. elegans (n=4): 50% to each parent
- [[Gila seminuda]] (n=3, a hybrid species of G. robusta × G. elegans ancestry): 66.7% to G. robusta, 33.3% to G. cypha

Hybrids were generally assigned to one or other parental form rather than showing clear intermediacy. However, small sample sizes weakened this test. The hypothesis of hybrid intermediacy could not be definitively rejected.

**Character Comparisons:**

Morphometric characters provided greater discriminatory power than meristic characters, possibly because they better describe shape and shape-change, which are paramount in discriminating these species. Dorsal and anal ray counts appeared diagnostic in most species comparisons but were less useful in field applications. Vertebral counts and gill-raker numbers carried equal discriminating ability.

Misclassified individuals were rarely shared among the three data subsets (meristic, morphometric, field), suggesting multiple character combinations adequately discriminate species.

**Data Quality:**

Few values were missing from original data (G. robusta: 1.76%, G. cypha: 0.98%, G. elegans: 1.83%). Most missing data were morphometric; only four individuals lacked pharyngeal bones or teeth. Normality tests showed most meristic counts were non-normally distributed (only 1 of 10 per species was normal). Morphometric data showed 5-10 non-normal characters per species before transformation, improving after log₁₀ transformation. Within-species covariance matrices were heterogeneous, necessitating pooled variance-covariance matrices for discriminant functions.

**Management Implications:**

The study conclusively rejects the hypothesis that these three [[Gila]] species are morphologically indistinguishable. Historical confusion resulted from inadequate character selection, weak analytical methods, and lack of training in systematic ichthyology among resource managers—not from inherent morphological similarity. The authors emphasize that variation within and between populations must be recognized as natural phenomena amenable to statistical analysis rather than dismissed or redefined. Success in species identification hinges on proper character selection, adequate analyses, and "the art of seeing well"—the ability to recognize diagnostic features through careful observation and training.
