# Asian Tapeworm Control and Spread in Hatchery Fish: Internal Memorandum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service memorandum, written by Ronald D. Major, Director of the [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]] (FHC), answers a request from the Albuquerque regional office about [[Asian Tapeworm]] infection at Hassayampa (Palm Lake). The author reports that a single bath treatment with [[Praziquantel]] eliminated the parasite in [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Beautiful Shiner]] at two facilities, and that complete control is possible if infected water is not reintroduced. The drug is not readily available for fish use, so the memo outlines legal routes for future use. It also gives recommendations for future fish transfers.

## Background & Description

- **Parasite:** The Asian tapeworm is described as [[Panzootic]], spread mainly through [[Grass Carp]] and [[Bait Minnows]]. The source suggests it probably originated in Arkansas.
- **Host species:** The memo addresses [[Bonytail Chub]] (a Gila-group cyprinid being stocked into [[Lake Mohave]]) and [[Beautiful Shiner]].
- **Parasite effects:** The author does not think the parasite harms large fish such as bonytail chub. In heavy numbers, however, it can overwhelm small fish by rupturing the intestine, sometimes causing death.
- **Facilities:** The treatment work took place at a California SFH and at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NFH). Copies went to the Parker FRO and Dexter NFH.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dilution and risk:** The author judges that infection rates would be more concerning in small rivers such as the [[Virgin River]] than in [[Lake Mohave]] or [[Lake Havasu]], because dilution in the larger reservoirs reduces the risk.
- **Transfer history:** Infected bonytails were moved from [[Palm Lake]] to Lake Havasu. The author believes the tapeworm was already present in the receiving waters, so the transfer was not the original source.
- **Reinfection:** The memo notes reinfection problems at Dexter after treatment, which points to ongoing exposure risk in hatchery water.

## Key Findings

1. **Effective treatment:** A single water bath of Praziquantel at 0.75 ppm for 24 hours eliminated Asian tapeworms in 100% of bonytail chub at a California SFH, before stocking Lake Mohave. The same treatment was used on beautiful shiners at Dexter. The author confirmed prophylaxis by necropsy of the fish.
2. **Reinfection at Dexter:** The facility had repeated reinfections, which the author attributes to the importance of not reintroducing infected water.
3. **Legal barriers:** Praziquantel is not readily usable now. It carries a label for cestode treatment in companion animals if a veterinarian administers it. Use under "extra-label" classification may be possible if a local veterinarian agrees. Future use may be allowed through an FDA exemption rule for threatened and endangered (T&E) species.
4. **Risk assessment:** Large fish are not seriously harmed, while small fish can die under heavy infection. Small rivers are a higher concern than large reservoirs.
5. **Recommendation:** Any future cyprinid transfers from the Palm Lake source should be treated first.
