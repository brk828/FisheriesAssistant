# Ecology of the Trematode *Clinostomum marginatum* and Its Hosts in Eastern Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clinostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by Erwin E. Klaas, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*, examines infections of farm-pond fishes by the yellow grub, the metacercarial stage of the trematode [[Clinostomum Marginatum]]. Anglers in eastern Kansas often reported "wormy" fish from farm ponds. The study combined pond seining, snail sampling, and observations of the [[Great Blue Heron]], the definitive host, at a nesting colony in Douglas County. The aim was to determine the extent of infection, the ecological factors linked to it, and whether infection rates depended on distance from the heron colony. Infection was frequent across the sampled ponds, but no environmental factor was clearly correlated with it, apart from the unusual case of one pond.

## Background & Description

The yellow grub is a trematode (flatworm) whose life cycle requires several hosts. Its larval stages develop in [[Helisoma Trivolvis]] snails (first intermediate host). Cercariae then penetrate fishes (second intermediate host), where they encyst as metacercariae. Herons that eat infected fish become the definitive host, in which the worms mature in the upper digestive tract and respiratory passages and release eggs into the water. The parasite is harmless to humans, and there is little evidence that it seriously harms fish health, although many anglers discard infected fish because they are unattractive for food.

The study area comprised farm ponds in Douglas County, Kansas. Hastings and Cross (1962) had reported 1,316 farm ponds in the county in 1954, of which 89.5 per cent were one-half acre or less. Most sampled ponds measured between one-tenth and one-quarter acre, averaged about four feet deep, had soft mud bottoms 6 to 8 inches deep, and were drained mainly by permanent pasture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Heron behavior.** Great Blue Herons began returning to a nesting colony in early March 1961, with 45 to 50 birds present by March 15. The colony contained 46 old nests in nine sycamore trees. Incubation started around March 20, young were first heard on April 23, and herons were no longer seen at the colony after July 15. Early in the season, breeding herons foraged close to the colony; after the young hatched, flights became more frequent and foraging spread in all directions. Herons apparently forage by random wandering, and a successful locality may be revisited for several days. Heron diet in the nest was 92 per cent fish (Kirkpatrick, 1940), and regurgitated remains from young herons were mostly black bullheads and green sunfish.

**Snail hosts.** *Helisoma trivolvis* was the most common *Helisoma* in Kansas and was found in 11 of 32 study ponds, usually with *Physa*. Snail abundance did not correlate with pond condition. Snails were abundant in turbid, livestock-used ponds and also in clear, vegetated, fenced ponds.

**Pond physical conditions.** All ponds were warm and turbid. Turbidity was lower where cattle were excluded and watersheds were well vegetated. Temperature and turbidity were not measured.

**Pond N-4.** This pond, about one-half acre, was an outlier. Orange-spotted sunfish were stocked there in 1956 along with other species. Those fish were infected at a rate of 84.44 per cent in 1960 and 76.54 per cent in 1961, although they were less than three years old, so the infection was acquired in the pond. No *Helisoma* were found there.

## Key Findings

**Study design and sampling**
- 32 ponds were seined in August 1960 and/or 1961, across two transects (Series E east and Series N north of the colony), each about one mile wide and ten miles long.
- 28 ponds contained fish, and 11 of those had infected fish.
- Eleven fish species were recorded from the 28 ponds. Black bullhead (present in 21 ponds) and green sunfish (18 ponds) were most common.
- Ten heron carcasses were examined, and all ten were infected with *C. marginatum*.

**Snail infection**
- Only 5 of 735 *Helisoma trivolvis* (0.68 per cent) were infected, four from pond N-12 and one from pond E-9.
- *H. trivolvis* was found in 8 of 11 (73 per cent) ponds with infected fish, compared with 3 of 17 (18 per cent) ponds where fish appeared free of infection.
- Infected fish occurred in ponds E-6, E-15 and N-4 without any living *H. trivolvis* found there, which suggests that snails are not necessary for infection.

**Fish infection**
- In ponds with large samples, infection rates ranged from 1.31 to 84.44 per cent but usually stayed below 30 per cent.
- [[Black Bullhead]] had the highest infection rate where infections occurred, with a range of 13.63 to 60.71 per cent (average 25.49 per cent).
- Among centrarchids (bluegill, green sunfish, white crappie, largemouth bass), excluding the orange-spotted sunfish in N-4, infection rates ranged from 1.49 to 44.00 per cent (average 13.66 per cent).
- Minnows were seldom infected.
- In pond E-3, 60.71 per cent of 56 bullheads were infected in 1960, but only eight bullheads were taken in 1961, after rotenone treatment.
- Five ponds (E-2, E-3, E-4, E-9, N-4) had infected fish in both years.

**Cyst distribution**
- Cysts in catfish were mostly at the fin bases and in the head and gills. Scaled fish, especially [[Orange-Spotted Sunfish]], often had cysts in the trunk and tail musculature.
- Most cysts were just beneath the skin, so fishermen can remove them during cleaning.
- The mean number of cysts per fish ranged from one to four, but was about 25 in pond E-11. One bullhead from E-9 carried 173 grubs. No correlation was found between fish length and cyst number.

**Heron proximity**
- In Series E, more infections occurred in ponds less than five miles from the colony than in those beyond five miles. No such correlation appeared in Series N.
- Pond density seemed more important than distance. East of the colony, the first five miles averaged about 5.4 ponds per square mile, versus 1.8 for the 6 to 10 mile zone. North of the colony the average was 2.1 ponds per square mile.

**Control options**
- Eliminating herons was judged impractical because of their wide distribution and popular appeal.
- Eradicating snails was considered difficult. Molluscicides such as dinitro-o-cyclohexylphenol and anhydrous cupric sulfate were effective in laboratory tests, but they had not been tested in Kansas ponds.
- The preferred approach was reducing submergent vegetation to confine snails to the shallow shoreline, although this remained a hypothesis.
