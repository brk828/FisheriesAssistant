# Desert Aquatic Ecosystems: Past, Present, and Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This publication, a [[The Nature Conservancy]] newsletter feature and president's letter, argues that desert aquatic ecosystems (springs, playas, cienegas, and desert rivers) are among North America's most endangered ecosystem types. Desert fishes serve as indicators of ecosystem health: of 31 fishes listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1981, 23 were desert species. The Conservancy's [[National Wetlands Conservation Project]], launched in early 1983 with a $25-million challenge grant from the [[Richard King Mellon Foundation]] within a five-year effort of $50 million-plus, has protected key desert sites at [[Ash Meadows]] and the [[Coachella Valley Preserve]]. The feature by Dean A. Hendrickson and Dennis M. Kubly traces the geologic and human history of these habitats and documents how water extraction, arroyo cutting, dams and introduced species have degraded them.

## Background & Description

The president's letter describes desert survival adaptations (for example, the [[Desert Tortoise]] storing water in bladders and [[Kangaroo Rat]] recycling body fluids) and notes that many species depend on oases, the scattered sites where groundwater reaches the surface. The feature by Hendrickson and Kubly describes how western North American species have evolved in isolation since about 100 million years ago, when a seaway covered the western continent. Basin-and-range topography began forming 30 to 15 million years ago, and uplift of the Sierra Nevada and coastal ranges, starting ten to less than five million years ago, blocked Pacific moisture and initiated regional deserts. Pleistocene pluvial periods filled hundreds of interior valleys, which later dried into playas. [[Lake Manly]], in Death Valley, was about 90 miles long and over 600 feet deep during wetter periods.

The desert is described through four major deserts: the [[Mojave Desert]], [[Sonoran Desert]], [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and [[Great Basin Desert]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Springs:** Fed by large groundwater reservoirs, springs are relatively stable and physically isolated, which promotes endemism. The springs of Death Valley and [[Ash Meadows]] and the [[Bolson de Cuatro Cienegas]] are noted for endemic pupfishes and snails. More than half of the basin's 20 native fishes at Cuatro Cienegas occur nowhere else.
- **Playas:** Thunderstorm runoff fills shallow playa lakes, hatching drought-resistant eggs of fairy shrimp, clam shrimp, tadpole shrimp and water fleas. Playas also support migrating waterfowl, waders and shorebirds; on the Llano Estacado, waterfowl numbers reach the hundreds of thousands. The Llano Estacado has roughly 20,000 to 30,000 dry lakes.
- **Saline lakes:** Great Salt Lake and Mono Lake are two to five times as saline as seawater and have no aquatic vertebrates. [[Brine Shrimp]] (*Artemia*) and brine flies (*Ephydra*) provide food for gulls.
- **Fish history:** Fossils show fish diversity declined as aridity increased over the past few million years. [[Pyramid Lake]] in the Lahontan Basin is among the few remaining hospitable desert lakes, and the cui-ui sucker survives only there.
- **Historical streams:** Sonoran Desert rivers once flowed through riparian forests and marshes, with beaver dams and abundant large fish. Hohokam canal irrigation (300 B.C. to 1450 A.D.) and later Pima and Spanish irrigation altered flows locally. After 1860, watershed alteration accelerated, and by the 1890s arroyo cutting had eroded valley streams into deep gashes up to 30 feet deep.
- **Colorado River:** Dams above and below the Grand Canyon, plus channelization of the last 400 miles, have nearly extirpated the native fishes, and introduced predators and competitors dominate the reservoirs.
- **Gila River:** Of 18 known native fishes, one is extinct, five are federally endangered, three are threatened, two more have been proposed for protection, and five are extirpated from the Gila drainage.

## Key Findings

- Approximately 74 percent of federally listed threatened or endangered fish species survive in desert springs and pools (president's letter).
- Desert aquatic habitats are among North America's most endangered ecosystem types; many have been destroyed or altered by grazing, toxic pollution, exotic species, and falling water tables.
- Conservancy preserves protect desert wetlands in Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico and Oregon. Examples include the 232-acre [[Canelo Hills Cienega]] (Gila chub habitat), the 312-acre [[Patagonia-Sonoita Creek Sanctuary]] (Gila topminnow), and the 12,613-acre Ash Meadows, which shelters more than 20 endemic plants, fish and invertebrates.
- Playa and spring habitats are threatened by off-road vehicles, mineral collection, military uses, and proposals for missile installations and nuclear waste sites.
- Economic pressure and reluctance of eastern taxpayers to fund western water projects may do more to protect desert aquatic ecosystems than water shortages alone. The authors conclude that water will ultimately be the limiting factor.
- The Conservancy aims to use the Mellon grant over five to ten years to preserve wetlands worth at least $50 million through fundraising, gifts of land, matching gifts, bargain sales, and resales to government agencies.
- Priority future sites include Lake Erie's south shore marshes, the upper Colorado River Basin headwaters, the White River bottomlands in Arkansas, New Jersey's Pine Barrens, and the Chesapeake Bay area.
