# The Ionic Composition of Some Lowland Lake Waters from Cheshire, England

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #water-chemistry #zooplankton #phytoplankton #[[Cheshire Meres]]

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by Eville Gorham presents detailed ionic composition analyses of nine lowland lakes ("meres") in Cheshire, England, comparing them to the world average freshwater and to waters of the [[English Lake District]]. While the paper is fundamentally a **water chemistry (limnochemistry)** study rather than an ichthyological one, it provides critical **environmental context** for understanding the trophic and faunal characteristics of these lake ecosystems, including notes on [[Phytoplankton]], [[Zooplankton]], and benthic macroinvertebrate fauna (e.g., [[Gammarus]], [[Asellus]], molluscs). No fish species are explicitly discussed in the source text; the ecological focus is on ionic geochemistry and its influence on primary producers and invertebrate communities. Calcium and bicarbonate dominate the ionic budget in most meres, except in two hydrologically isolated lakes (Sandiway and [[Oak Mere]]) whose chemistry is instead governed by rainwater input, resulting in distinct biological communities.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: Nine bodies of water near Northwich, Cheshire, ranging in size from 1–2 acres (Sandiway) to 100 acres ([[Rostherne Mere]]), and in altitude from ~240 ft (Sandiway, Oak, and Hatch Meres) to 60 ft ([[Budworth Mere]]).
- **Geology**: All lakes sit on Triassic substrata (Keuper marl and Rhaetic beds), with surface drift ranging from glacial sands/gravels (Sandiway, Oak, Hatch) to boulder clay and alluvium (Pick, Budworth).
- **Hydrology**: Most meres have inflow/outflow streams; **Sandiway** and **Oak Mere** are unique in lacking both, making them dependent primarily on rainwater and surface runoff rather than groundwater.
- **Anthropogenic influence**: [[Budworth Pool]] has a strongly polluted inflow, reflected in elevated nitrate (2.5 ppm N) and silica levels.
- **Methods**: Surface water samples were collected between August 1954 and October 1955, filtered through Whatman 541 filter papers, and analyzed for major cations (Na, K, Ca, Mg), anions (HCO₃⁻, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻), pH, nitrate, phosphate, and silica.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Primary Producers and Zooplankton
- The **phytoplankton** of Oak, Hatch, Rostherne, and Budworth Meres has been previously studied (Lind 1944). Most meres (excluding Oak Mere) are characterized by abundant but low-diversity blue-green algae and diatoms, with desmids (typical of oligotrophic waters) being scarce.
- **[[Oak Mere]]** is ecologically distinctive, dominated almost entirely by the green alga *[[Botryococcus braunii]]*.
- **Zooplankton** in Oak Mere is similarly abundant but low-diversity; the dominant species, *[[Bosmina obtusirostris]]*, is a northern, oligotrophic-associated form not recorded in any other Cheshire mere.

### Benthic Invertebrate Fauna
- Dr. H.B.N. Hynes described the shore fauna of Budworth Pool, Hatch, Rostherne, Pick, and Budworth Meres as rich, with plentiful **[[Gammarus]]**, **[[Asellus]]**, and (except at Pick Mere) molluscs.
- In contrast, **Oak Mere's fauna is sparse**, with Gammarus, Asellus, and molluscs notably absent during survey visits — a pattern consistent with its distinct rain-fed chemistry and low calcium bicarbonate content.

### Avifauna Context
- Boyd (1951) noted that duck and wader abundance on Oak Mere is far lower than on other Cheshire meres, though Common Gulls frequently visit — an indirect indicator of the lake's altered productivity and prey base.

### Water Chemistry Drivers of Habitat Quality
- **Normal meres** (7 of 9 sampled) are dominated by calcium and bicarbonate ions, with substantial magnesium, sodium, sulphate, and chloride — chemistry reflecting weathering of Keuper marl deposited under arid Triassic conditions.
- **Sandiway** and **Oak Mere** are chemically aberrant: acidic, bicarbonate-free, and compositionally similar to Lake District rainwater and Moor House blanket bog water — indicating minimal groundwater contact.
- Sodium/chloride ratios suggest a marine-influenced ionic signature in the richer meres, distinguishing them from purely limestone-fed lakes such as Sunbiggin Tarn.
- Phosphate is notably higher in Cheshire meres than in Lake District waters (reflecting greater agricultural influence), with Budworth Mere showing the highest phosphate concentration (0.29 ppm P) and Sandiway the lowest (0.001 ppm P).

## Key Findings

1. **Ionic Dominance**: Calcium and bicarbonate predominate in seven of the nine meres; sodium and chloride dominate in the two rain-fed lakes (Sandiway, Oak Mere).
2. **Concentration Gradient**: Total salt concentration ranges from 0.55 meq/L (Sandiway, most dilute) to 6.99 meq/L (Budworth Mere, most concentrated), with Pick and Budworth Meres — located in the Weaver Valley near salt mines — being the richest, though showing no evidence of brine infiltration.
3. **Rain-Dependent Systems**: Sandiway's ionic profile closely matches Lake District rainwater (Na/Cl ratio 0.82 vs. seawater's 0.85), supporting the hypothesis that it is essentially an impermeable rain-fed basin, sealed by a subsurface sand layer ("Foxes' Bench").
4. **Oak Mere as Intermediate**: Oak Mere's chemistry falls between Sandiway and the "normal" meres — low acidity, elevated calcium relative to Sandiway, but still lacking bicarbonate — consistent with surface runoff input rather than groundwater seepage, likely explaining its unique flora and fauna.
5. **Biogeochemical Legacy**: The high sulphate, chloride, and magnesium content of normal Cheshire meres (relative to world average freshwater) reflects the arid depositional environment of the underlying Keuper marls and subsequent partial leaching under modern humid climate conditions.
6. **Low Silica**: Despite high overall salinity, Cheshire meres show low silica concentrations (~4 ppm SiO₂), comparable to dilute Lake District waters, suggesting aluminosilicate weathering has not progressed as far as leaching of more mobile ions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻).
7. **Ecological Implication**: The marked chemical divergence of Oak Mere and Sandiway from the other meres is proposed as the primary driver of their unusual planktonic and invertebrate communities, illustrating a direct link between basin hydrology/geochemistry and lake biology — a foundational consideration for any subsequent fish or higher trophic-level studies in this system.
