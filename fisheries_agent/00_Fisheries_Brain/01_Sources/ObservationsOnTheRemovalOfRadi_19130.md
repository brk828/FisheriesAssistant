# Removal of Radio-Strontium During Treatment of Domestic Water Supplies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-treatment

## Executive Summary

This 1953 laboratory study from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]], published in the *Journal of the Institution of Water Engineers*, examines how radioactive strontium isotopes ([[Strontium 89]] and [[Strontium 90]]) behave during conventional drinking-water treatment. Only 2 to 5 per cent of activity was removed by normal [[Chemical Coagulation]]. Higher removal occurred with very large coagulant doses and with [[Lime Softening]] and [[Ion-Exchange Softening]]. Ion-exchange removed over 97 per cent of activity, the highest of any process tested. In [[Slow Sand Filtration]], filtered water activity rose over a few days to match the influent. The document is a technical research paper, not a fish or ecological study, so the species, family and marine fields are N/A.

## Background & Description

Strontium isotopes are fission products of [[Uranium 235]] and [[Plutonium 239]]. Sr 89 has a half-life of 54 days and Sr 90 has a half-life of 19.9 years. Sr 90 decays to [[Yttrium 90]] (half-life 61 hours), which complicates radio-assay. Strontium shares a group with calcium and radium, is absorbed from the gut and retained in bone, and has high fission yield, which makes these isotopes among the most hazardous fission products.

The International Committee on Radiological Protection (1951) suggested a maximum permissible concentration for Sr 90 in drinking water of 8 × 10⁻⁷ µc per cm³, equivalent to 1.8 disintegrations per minute per cm³. This is the lowest value recommended for any β-emitter and applies to radiation workers. A lower value may suit the general public. Tolerances are for lifetime continuous exposure and are much higher for short contamination events.

The study was requested by the Ministry of Housing and Local Government. Prior work by [[Straub, Morton and Placak]] (1951) and [[Eliassen et al.]] (1951) had used beaker and continuous-flow tests with clay, alum, ferric salts and lime-soda softening.

Methods included:
- Isolation of a mixed Sr 89/Sr 90 preparation (about 20 µg carrier per µc) and a carrier-free Sr 90 solution from 3-year-old fission products.
- Two radio-assay methods: aluminium-absorber counting using differing β energies, and chemical separation of strontium counted within 1.8 hours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Stable strontium is chemically indistinguishable from its radioisotopes, so removing radio-strontium requires removing a similar proportion of inactive strontium. Measured natural levels were 0.55 p.p.m. in a Watford public-supply well and 0.28 p.p.m. in a Thames valley reservoir. [[Braidech and Emery]] (1935) found 0.1 to 10.0 p.p.m. in 24 United States samples.

Synthetic tap water (200 p.p.m. CaCO₃ temporary hardness, pH 8.0) served as the base medium. Coagulation used a flash mix at 130 r.p.m. for 3 minutes and flocculation at 30 r.p.m. for 30 minutes. Ferric chloride was preferred over alum because it coagulates across a wider pH range.

Slow sand filters ran at 4 in. per hour, following an earlier design by [[Eden, Downing and Wheatland]] (1952), which dealt with radio-iodine.

## Key Findings

- **Chemical coagulation:** Normal coagulation with alum or ferric salts removed only 2 to 5 per cent of the initial strontium activity. Table II results ranged from 1.6 to 4.7 per cent across 11 coagulant conditions, including aluminium sulphate with activated silica (2.6 per cent) and ferric sulphate at pH 6.8 (1.6 per cent). Adding 256 p.p.m. clay gave 3.6 per cent.
- **Dose and pH effects:** With ferric chloride at pH 7.0, removal was about 2 per cent at 10 p.p.m. Fe and rose to 73 per cent at 10,000 p.p.m. Fe. Removal increased with pH, reaching 97 per cent at pH 11.0 with 500 p.p.m. Fe, possibly because of co-precipitation with calcium carbonate.
- **Slow sand filtration:** During a 27-day addition of 1 µc Sr 90 per litre with 25 µg carrier, filtrate activity rose to about equal the influent after roughly 14 days. Within 3 days of stopping addition, effluent activity fell to one-tenth of its maximum. About 60 per cent of activity retained in the first 14 days was later released. Over 99 per cent of retained activity was in the Schmutzdecke, which separates readily on washing.
- **Lime softening:** Solubility products suggested no strontium carbonate precipitation below 3.1 p.p.m. Experiments showed 67.6 to 73.0 per cent removal of 0.04 to 5.04 p.p.m. strontium during removal of temporary hardness, with results nearly independent of carrier concentration. This suggests strontium and calcium co-precipitate. Complete softening removed about 80 per cent, with removal linearly related to hardness removed (Fig. 4).
- **Sodium carbonate softening:** With a dose over three times the equivalent quantity, removal was 91.2 per cent (0.88 p.p.m. Sr) and 89.5 per cent (8.8 p.p.m. Sr). Mixed hardness waters with lime plus sodium carbonate gave totals of 60.0 to 71.6 per cent.
- **Ion-exchange softening:** Three materials were tested: synthetic aluminosilicate, sulphonated coal and a synthetic resin. Strontium continued to be removed after calcium breakthrough, most strongly with aluminosilicate, which the authors attributed to differing affinities for Sr versus Ca. Over 97 per cent of strontium activity was removed across several regeneration cycles.
- **Domestic softener trial:** In a commercial softener (about 3 litres aluminosilicate, 9 in. bed) at 1.0 g.p.m., effluent activity stayed at or below 3.3 per cent of initial. At 0.1 g.p.m. (6 g.p.h.), the maximum never exceeded 1.6 per cent, and the weighted mean was 0.78 per cent. Lower flow gave better removal.
- **Overall conclusion:** Ion-exchange softening gave the highest removal, while conventional coagulation was ineffective unless very large coagulant doses were used. The authors noted this confirmed the laboratory findings despite the very shallow bed in the domestic unit.
