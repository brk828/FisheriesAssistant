# Distant Recaptures of Tagged American Shad from Quebec (1945–1950)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #alosa-sapidissima

## Executive Summary

This 1956 Quebec Department of Fisheries paper by Vladykov reports the distant recaptures of [[American Shad]] (*[[Alosa Sapidissima]]*) tagged in the [[St. Lawrence River]] between 1945 and 1950. Of 2,223 fish tagged, 13 were recaptured outside the Province of [[Quebec]], and the paper analyses their timing, distribution and possible links to water temperature. Most recaptures came from the warm years 1949 and 1950, and the farthest, about 2,000 miles, was made in the spring of 1949. The paper is a follow-up to the author's earlier summary of the same tagging programme.

## Background & Description

- **Tagging programme:** Shad were tagged from 1945 to 1950, with 2,223 fish released in total (Table 1).
- **Main station:** [[Isle Verte]] is a small island in the St. Lawrence about 150 miles east of Quebec City. It lies on an important migration path, and 1,886 fish (84.8%, reported as 85%) were released there, mostly from [[Péche Herring Trap]] fishing weirs.
- **Annual totals:** 157 (1945), 526 (1946), 478 (1947), 688 (1948), 222 (1949) and 152 (1950). Non-Isle Verte releases totalled 337 (15.2%).
- **Tag types:** Most fish carried a metallic [[Strap Tag]] (monel or nickel) bent into a ring. A smaller number received a [[Split Ring Tag]] made of stainless steel with a vinylite plate. The author found no major difference in return rates between the two types, so they were analysed together.
- **Recovery rate:** In 1945–1948, 105 fish (5.7%) were recovered. Four of these were caught outside Quebec. Nine more distant recaptures followed in 1949–1951, bringing the total to 13.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water temperature.** The author links the number of distant recaptures to spring water temperatures and possible salinities along the Atlantic coast. Earlier work by [[Hachey and McLean]] (1948) and [[Lauzier]] (1954), based on St. Andrews, New Brunswick data in the [[Bay of Fundy]], described a cold period in 1940–1945 and a warm period in 1949–1953. The Gulf of Maine was unusually warm from June to October 1949, and warm-water species normally found in semi-tropical or tropical areas were caught there.

**Migration pattern.** Recovery dates show a consistent seasonal cycle:
- **Spring ascent:** Sexually mature shad move from the Atlantic into the St. Lawrence and pass [[Strait of Canso]] late in spring. Two recaptures were made there, on May 31 and June 7.
- **Summer departure:** After spawning, Quebec shad leave the St. Lawrence in late summer.
- **Winter range:** From October to mid-April, shad were recaptured along the Atlantic seaboard, from Brown's Bank to the entrance of [[Chesapeake Bay]].

The southern limit reached varied greatly from year to year. The author notes that one or two weeks' difference in migration timing between years did not change the overall pattern.

## Key Findings

- **Total distant recaptures:** 13 fish were recovered outside Quebec, out of 2,223 tagged (about 0.6%).
- **Geographic range:** Recaptures extended from the Strait of Canso to the entrance of Chesapeake Bay (near Old Point Comfort, Virginia). In 1949 alone, Quebec shad were taken south of [[Cape Cod]], between the Delaware River and Virginia.
- **Maximum distance:** About 2,000 miles, for a female tagged at Isle Verte on June 5, 1948 (tag no. 5590) and recaptured at Old Point Comfort, Virginia, on April 13, 1949, after 312 days at liberty.
- **Spring 1949 cluster:** The three most distant recaptures were all made in spring 1949:
  - Tag 5590: Old Point Comfort, Virginia, April 13, 1949 (312 days).
  - Tag 5778: Delaware River off Bowers Beach, March 1, 1949 (267 days; tagged June 7, 1948).
  - Tag 5706: Sandy Hook Bay, New Jersey, March 31, 1949 (298 days; tagged June 6, 1948).
  The author states that no Quebec shad had been taken farther than Cape Cod before or since. He attributes this to changes in hydrographic conditions along the western Atlantic in spring 1949.
- **Temperature link:** More distant recaptures occurred in warm years (1949 and 1950 gave seven; 1947 and 1951 gave four). Normal (1946) or cold (1948) years gave only one each. The author notes that the relationship is not simple, and that tagged-fish mortality, which varied from year to year, may also affect the counts.
- **Non-spawning "lost" shad:** Three recaptures at the head of the Bay of Fundy, made between May 17 and July 12 after 1–2 years at liberty, involved fish with poorly developed gonads recovering from spawning. Fishermen could not determine their sex.
- **Longest time at liberty:** Tag 5021 was released at Isle Verte on June 4, 1947 and recaptured at St. Vallier on June 15, 1951, about 100 miles west of the release site. It was out 1,472 days (four years and twelve days). It was a male that had grown only 3 cm in fork length, with no major injury from the strap tag.
- **Site fidelity:** Two shad tagged with split ring tags at an Isle Verte péche on May 29, 1950 were recaptured in the same péche in the second week of June 1954, after roughly four years and ten days at liberty.
- **Fishermen's reports:** Table 5 contains excerpts from letters by fishermen, fisheries inspectors and US Fish and Wildlife Service staff. These include the Georges Bank and Browns Bank trawler captures, the Gloucester gill net capture, and the Cumberland Basin (Bay of Fundy) capture of June 27, 1946 (21 inches, 3¼–3¾ lb). The author considers these reports valuable because of their scientific importance.
- **Health of tagged fish:** Most recaptured fish showed little or no tissue damage around the tag. Some showed slight redness or indentation of the jaw or tongue, as in the case of the fish in Figure 2, which was recaptured 371 days after tagging.
- **Conclusion:** Distant recaptures of Quebec shad vary with prevailing spring water temperatures on the Atlantic coast, and the most extensive southward movements occurred in the warm spring of 1949. Because the sample is small (13 fish), the author presents these patterns as suggestive rather than conclusive.
