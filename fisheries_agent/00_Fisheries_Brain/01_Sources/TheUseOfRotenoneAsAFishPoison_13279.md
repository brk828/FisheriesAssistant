# The Use of Rotenone as a Fish Poison

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #leguminosae

## Executive Summary

This 1949 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet, prepared by the Branches of Fishery Biology and Game-fish and Hatcheries, gives practical guidance on using [[Rotenone]] (from [[Derris]] and [[Cube]] powders) to eradicate unwanted fish from lakes, ponds, and streams. It stresses that poisoning should be used only after a qualified survey, with state authorization, and it provides dosage, calculation, application, and safety procedures. The leaflet states that the Service itself lacks personnel for this work and that the states hold authority over state waters.

## Background & Description

**Rotenone and its sources.** Rotenone is a plant-derived compound found only in members of the legume family, [[Leguminosae]]. The commonly used source is [[Derris]], a native of Australia, Oceania, and Southern Asia. [[Cube]], another form, comes from South America. Commercial powders are ground roots of these plants. The leaflet notes that rotenone has long been used by peoples of tropical and subtropical regions as a fish poison, and that it is more commonly used in the U.S. as an insecticide, with toxicity comparing favorably to [[Pyrethrum]] and [[Nicotine Sulfate]].

**History of use in fishery management.** Rotenone has been used to renovate lakes and streams since 1934. Since 1940, fishery biologists have recommended its use on more lakes to improve sport fishing.

**Formulations.** Powders were the traditional form. Liquid [[Emulsifiable Rotenone]] and paste forms are now sold; they cost more but are easier to handle. Powders are typically guaranteed at 5 percent rotenone; emulsions are also 5 percent; pastes are 2.5 to 3.0 percent.

**Undesirable fish.** The leaflet groups unwanted fish into three types:
- Predators, such as gars and dogfish.
- Coarse fishes with little angling value that compete with game fish, such as bullheads, buffaloes, drum, carp, goldfish, suckers, and chubs.
- Game species out of place ecologically, such as bluegill, perch, rock bass, largemouth bass, and white bass, in northern trout lakes or some warm-water waters.

These problems often arise from well-meant but mistaken introductions, improper stocking, or selective removal of predators (for example, bass), which leads to overpopulation and stunted fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Selectivity and control.** Spearing, netting, and destruction of young have been tried with little success. Partial or selective poisoning has sometimes worked but often gave only temporary relief. Because surviving fish can rapidly repopulate, the entire population generally must be removed when the underlying cause remains.

**Non-target effects.** Aquatic insects, crustaceans, and other invertebrates are generally less sensitive to rotenone than fish. People, livestock, pets, snakes, and frogs are not affected by swimming in or drinking treated water. Fish killed by poisoning remain fit for human consumption if collected while fresh.

**Environmental conditions.** Best results occur when surface water temperatures are above 70 to 75°F, though lakes have been successfully treated at 44°F. Poisoned water should not drain into other waters for at least 10 days, or two to three weeks in cold water.

**Disposal and residues.** Burial near the lake is generally recommended. Dead fish may alternatively be fed to poultry or hogs or used as fertilizer. [[Acetone]] is used to remove rotenone residues from containers.

## Key Findings

**Authorization and planning**
- Destruction of the existing population must first be justified by a qualified person, and state authorization obtained.
- A preliminary biological and chemical survey by a qualified person is recommended, ideally followed by the same person conducting the poisoning and restocking.

**Dosage**
- Maximum concentration generally required: 0.5 part rotenone per million parts water.
- Where doubt exists about a complete kill, 1 part per million is recommended.
- Stated rate: 1.4 lb of 5 percent powder or emulsion per acre-foot to reach 0.5 ppm. (This figure appears low relative to standard rotenone-to-water arithmetic and should be verified against current guidance before use.)

**Worked example (pond calculation)**
- Average width 140 ft and length 255 ft, giving surface area of 35,700 sq ft, or 0.82 acres.
- Mean depth from 25 soundings totaling 800 inches: 32 inches (2.67 ft).
- Volume: 0.82 acres × 2.67 ft = 2.19 acre-feet.
- Derris required: 3.1 lb for 0.5 ppm, or 6.2 lb for 1 ppm.

**Application methods**
- Pour liquid from the wake of a moving outboard motor, or anchor a boat and drive water toward the shoreline.
- Scatter solution from a pan or dipper where outboards are unavailable.
- Treat shallow edges, muskrat runs, and bank holes separately.
- Towed open-mesh burlap sacks of powder or paste.
- Apply in a criss-cross pattern over the whole surface.
- Aerial application of emulsions has been used on large waters.

**Deep-water treatment**
- Derris does not readily penetrate below 20 to 25 feet when applied at the surface.
- Options include a centrifugal pump (most efficient), an inverted funnel on weighted garden hose (suction reaching about 3 ft depth from a moving boat), and towed weighted burlap bags of paste in water 50 to 60 ft deep.

**Costs**
- Derris and Cube powders: 30 to 60 cents per pound.
- Emulsifiable rotenone: about 75 cents per pound.

**Effects and timing**
- Fish surface, dart erratically, and gasp within minutes.
- Most dead fish sink, then rise through bloating.
- Complete kill typically occurs within 24 to 36 hours.
- Mechanism: gill capillaries constrict, blocking oxygen-carrying blood cells, causing suffocation.
- Fish rescued at the first signs of distress and moved to untreated water can recover.

**Safety**
- Inhaling dust may irritate the nose and throat; masks or damp cloths are advised during mixing.

**Restocking and toxicity testing**
- Before restocking, place a minnow bucket with a few fish in the deep end of the treated water, where temperature is lowest. If fish survive 24 hours, restocking may proceed.
- Oxygen analyses should be conducted first, since oxygen may be deficient near the bottom.
- Failure to test may cause all newly stocked fish to die.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Obtain expert advice before poisoning, since partial kills lead to repopulation and biologists may suggest alternative management.
- Poisoning requires an accurate area estimate because rotenone is expensive.
- Incomplete application may leave survivors.
- The Service does not conduct surveys, poisonings, or restocking itself; these are performed or supervised by [[State Fish and Game Departments]] or permitted qualified biologists.
- States hold complete authority over state waters.
- Legal caution: state laws prohibit use of Derris or other fish-toxic poisons in public waters without authorization.
