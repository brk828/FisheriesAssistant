# Status of Endangered and Threatened Fishes in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Technical Note, written by James E. Johnson of the [[Colorado State Office]], reviews the status of endangered and threatened fishes in Colorado. It covers four [[Cutthroat Trout]] subspecies of the genus *Salmo* and four native minnows and suckers of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The note summarizes each taxon's original distribution, current status, existing management plans and BLM interests. Its central message is that most of these fishes have declined sharply, spawning success is poor, and recovery depends on inventories, habitat protection, hatchery propagation and cooperation among federal and state agencies.

## Background & Description

The note's purpose is to explain the status of species on the federal and Colorado endangered and threatened lists. It draws on the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]], under which the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) listed species, and on the Colorado Division of Wildlife (DOW) state list. The author notes some confusion over the names and status of several listed fishes, and says some listed darters of the Arkansas River drainage were under study. Those names are too garbled in the scan to report reliably.

**Cutthroat trouts (*Salmo clarki* complex)**

- **[[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. stomias*)**: Listed as endangered. Pure strains were identified in several Colorado drainages, including Cow Creek, Black Hollow Creek and Island Lake.
- **[[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. virginalis*)**: Listed as threatened. Its range covers the Rio Grande and Arkansas drainages of Colorado and New Mexico. It has a distinctive colour pattern, with a red-orange throat and yellow belly.
- **[[Colorado Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. pleuriticus*)**: Native to the western slope, including the Colorado, Green and San Juan drainages. Its colour is often described as crimson, and some populations contain hybridized fish.
- **[[Yellowfin Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. macdonaldi*)**: Native to the Twin Lakes area of the Arkansas drainage. Its fins are yellowish.

**Minnows and suckers**

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: Endangered. The largest North American minnow, it once reached lengths of nearly 6 feet. Modern fish rarely exceed about 10 pounds and 3 feet.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Endangered. It has a distinctive keeled hump behind the head and is found mainly in the lower Colorado River system.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: Endangered. It is known from the Grand Canyon and Colorado River, and is rare.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)**: Endangered. It is rare in Colorado, and its taxonomy is considered unclear because of possible hybridization with *G. cypha*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fishes occupy large, warm-water and cold-water river systems, including the [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[Gunnison River]], [[White River]], [[Dolores River]] and [[Arkansas River]]. Many of these rivers have been altered by dams, reservoirs and other man-made changes. The author writes that these changes have broken down natural barriers and allowed the introduction of non-native species, which in turn promote hybridization with native fishes.

Key threats in the text include:

- **Hybridization** with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]], which has affected cutthroat populations.
- **Reduced spawning success** reported for squawfish, humpback chub and other native fishes across most surveyed reaches.
- **Habitat loss** from dams and reservoirs, which the author says limit suitable habitat and fragment remaining populations.
- **Overexploitation** through angling and collection, which is why several states closed sites to fishing.
- **Limited basic knowledge**. The author notes that little is known about the environmental requirements of Colorado squawfish, which hampers recovery planning.

## Key Findings

**Status and distribution**

- Greenback cutthroat trout were thought to be extinct at one point. Pure strains were later found in several Colorado streams.
- A Rio Grande cutthroat trout population at Abion Creek was stocked into Black Hollow Creek, and this transplant had limited success.
- Colorado cutthroat trout in Beaver Creek (Moffat County) were tested in 1971. Seven of the 15 specimens lacked basibranchial teeth, which indicated rainbow trout hybridization.
- Colorado squawfish populations are declining. Recent collections show reduced spawning success in all areas, including the Colorado River between Lake Powell and Grand Junction and the Green River below Ouray.
- The humpback sucker population in Saguaro Lake (Arizona) fell from a catch of about six tons in 1949 to no fish in 1973. Colorado populations have been found in the Colorado River above Grand Junction, the Yampa River below Craig and the White River below Piceance Creek. None have been recently recorded in the Dolores and Green rivers.
- Humpback chub are concentrated in the Colorado River near Grand Junction. DOW personnel collected 32 specimens there on August 10, 1974, which is the largest known concentration of both young and adult humpback chub.

**Management actions reported**

- **Greenback cutthroat**: Around 30,000 eggs were taken in summer 1974 for artificial hatching, but hatching success was relatively poor. The DOW requested about $12,500 to collect 50 brood fish and rear their progeny under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. DOW also closed existing populations to fishing.
- **Colorado squawfish**: Adult fish captured in the Yampa River in spring 1974 were transported to Willow Beach Federal Fish Hatchery. Their offspring were reared successfully, and stocking into Arizona or Colorado tributaries was expected in late 1975. A Colorado squawfish recovery team was formed by FWS and BLM, with participants from Colorado, Utah, Arizona and California.
- **Humpback chub**: Colorado prohibited the capture of humpback chub in 1975 and planned further distribution and ecological studies.
- **Rio Grande cutthroat**: New Mexico and Colorado have prohibited harvest of this subspecies, which is sensitive to overexploitation.

**BLM priorities**

- Inventory all high-quality streams in the Colorado River highlands above about 8,000 feet, including Parachute, Roan, Piceance and Little Snake creeks, to find native cutthroat populations and sites suitable for reintroduction.
- Inventory the most promising squawfish habitat on BLM lands, including the Yampa, Gunnison and Colorado rivers, and give high priority to these areas.
- Manage isolated streams in the diverse portions of the drainage solely for the perpetuation of endangered subspecies.
- Develop habitat management plans to maintain stock purity and increase population size, and begin extensive management as soon as suitable habitat is identified.

**Conclusion**

The note concludes that these fishes need immediate habitat protection, because conflicting land uses threaten their remaining habitat. It recommends setting precedent for establishing habitat requirements quickly, and stresses that inventory and life-history information are needed before recovery plans can be fully developed. Cooperation among BLM, FWS, the Colorado DOW and neighbouring states is presented as essential.
