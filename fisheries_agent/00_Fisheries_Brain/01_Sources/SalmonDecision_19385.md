# Idaho Department of Fish and Game v. National Marine Fisheries Service (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #endangered-species #legal-case #hydropower

## Executive Summary

This 1994 U.S. District Court opinion addresses a legal challenge by the [[Idaho Department of Fish and Game]] (IDFG) against the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) regarding the operation of the [[Federal Columbia River Power System]] (FCRPS) and its impacts on three listed [[Snake River]] salmon species: sockeye (endangered), and spring/summer and fall chinook (threatened). Judge Malcolm F. Marsh found that NMFS' 1993 Biological Opinion employed an arbitrary and capricious jeopardy standard, particularly in its selection of a 1986-1990 baseline period (record low years during sustained drought) and its dismissal of low-range population model assumptions. The court granted IDFG's motion for summary judgment and ordered defendants to re-initiate consultation within 60 days, emphasizing that the ESA requires agencies to focus on species needs rather than system capabilities or status quo operations.

## Background & Description

The case consolidated three civil actions (92-973-MA, 93-1420-MA, 93-1603-MA) challenging NMFS' compliance with the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) Section 7 consultation requirements. [[Snake River sockeye salmon]] were listed as endangered on December 20, 1991; [[Snake River spring/summer chinook]] and [[Snake River fall chinook]] were listed as threatened on May 22, 1992. The litigation arose from NMFS' May 26, 1993 Biological Opinion covering FCRPS operations from April 1993 through January 1994.

The court's opinion provides extensive context on the "4 H's" framework of human-induced mortality factors: hydropower, habitat management, harvests, and hatcheries. Natural uncontrollable factors include drought, fire, and El Niño ocean food depletion. The case specifically addressed hydropower operations through eight mainstem [[Columbia River]] and [[Snake River]] hydroelectric projects, where juvenile salmon navigate via: (1) spill over dams (0-3% mortality), (2) turbine passage (6-32% mortality), (3) bypass to transportation facilities (1-3% mortality), or (4) bypass back to river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population Status (1993):**
- Snake River sockeye: 5 individuals expected
- Snake River fall chinook: 242-246 individuals expected
- Spring/summer chinook: populations at record lows

**Mortality Estimates from 1993 Biological Opinion:**
- Sockeye juveniles: 55-77% mortality (adult losses not quantifiable)
- Spring/summer chinook juveniles: 34.5-76.7% mortality; adults: 33.41% mortality (representing 3-11% reduction from baseline)
- Fall chinook juveniles: 81-93% mortality; adults: 41% mortality (representing 5-9% reduction from baseline)

**Life Cycle Models Used:**
Three models analyzed population trajectories to 2008 (four 4-year life cycles):
1. BPA's Stochastic Life-Cycle Model (SLCM)
2. Northwest Power Planning Council's System Planning Model (SPM) - spring chinook only
3. State and Tribal Fisheries Agencies' Empirical Life-Cycle Model (ELCM)

Models differed significantly in assumptions about transport-to-control ratios (BPA: 2.1:1 fall, 1.6:1 spring/summer vs. STFA: 1:1) and flow-survival relationships.

**Habitat Context:**
The [[Columbia River Fish Management Plan]] (CRFMP), adopted in 1988, provided framework for protecting, rebuilding and enhancing salmon runs and allocating in-river harvest activities. The case built upon decades of state-tribal fishing allocation disputes dating to the late 1960s (United States v. Oregon, Sohappy v. Smith).

## Key Findings

**Court's Legal Determinations:**

1. **Baseline Period Arbitrary and Capricious:** NMFS' selection of 1986-1990 as baseline was flawed because: (a) this period represented record low years during sustained drought; (b) low run numbers during this period influenced the original listing decisions; (c) NMFS failed to consider alternative baselines; (d) the baseline was significantly shorter than prior years (1992 used 1964-1990 for juveniles, 1975-1990 for adults); (e) focus was on system capabilities rather than species needs.

2. **Improper Dismissal of Low-Range Assumptions:** NMFS arbitrarily discounted low-range model assumptions without adequate explanation. Had low-range assumptions been retained, confidence levels for spring/summer chinook stabilization would have been approximately 50% instead of the 60-70% cited by NMFS. The court found no rational explanation for rejecting only pessimistic scenarios while accepting optimistic ones given high model uncertainty.

3. **Failure to Consider Enhanced Risks:** NMFS failed to adequately consider special risks associated with extremely low population levels, including inbreeding depression and "extinction vortex" effects (random environmental catastrophes affecting small populations). This was particularly critical given expected 1993 populations of 5 sockeye and 242-246 fall chinook.

4. **Stabilization Goal Analysis:** NMFS concluded 60-70% probability of achieving 1990 population levels by 2008 represented "reasonable certainty" of stabilization. Model outputs showed wide ranges: low assumptions 41-46%, mid-range 46-56%, high-range 61-82% for spring/summer chinook. Fall chinook models produced "counter-intuitive" results (70-99% probability) that NMFS itself questioned.

5. **Scope of Consultation:** The court rejected bright-line distinctions between dam "existence" vs. "operations" or "survival" vs. "recovery," emphasizing that ESA Section 7 analysis must focus on species needs rather than activity categories. NMFS should have discretion to determine appropriate scope of reasonable and prudent alternatives, providing they are economically and technically feasible.

6. **Failure to Re-initiate Consultation:** NMFS violated 50 C.F.R. 402.15 by failing to re-initiate consultation upon receipt of revised State and Tribal Fisheries Agencies' life cycle model results in March/April 1993, which showed further reductions in probability of achieving stability goals. NMFS biologist Chris Toole identified this as "significant new information" but it was never incorporated into the Biological Opinion.

7. **Process Critique:** The court found the jeopardy analysis "too heavily geared towards a status quo that has allowed all forms of river activity to proceed in a deficit situation" with "relatively small steps, minor improvements and adjustments" when circumstances required "a major overhaul." NMFS and action agencies "narrowly focussed their attention on what the establishment is capable of handling with minimal disruption" rather than "what can be done to protect the species from jeopardy."

**Relief Ordered:**
- IDFG's motion for summary judgment granted
- Federal defendants' cross-motion for summary judgment denied
- Defendants ordered to re-initiate consultation within 60 days
- Request for injunctive relief against FCRPS operations deemed moot (court declined to direct interim river operations)
- Status conference scheduled for April 8, 1994

**Legal Standards Applied:**
The court applied the Administrative Procedure Act's "arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise not in accordance with law" standard, requiring agencies to conduct "reasoned evaluation of relevant information" and articulate "rational connection between facts found and choices made." The ESA Section 7(a)(2) mandate requires agencies to "insure" actions are "not likely to jeopardize the continued existence" of listed species, defined as actions that would "reduce appreciably the likelihood of both the survival and recovery" of species (50 C.F.R. 402.02).
