# Fishes of El Dorado City Lake, Butler County, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1956 survey, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*, documents the fish assemblage of [[El Dorado City Lake]], a 3,000 acre-foot impoundment in [[Butler County]], [[Kansas]]. Following a long decline in angling success, the lake was partly drained in 1954, its remaining game fish were salvaged for [[Lake Bluestem]], and the rest of the population was killed with [[Rotenone Poisoning]]. Random samples taken after the kill were used to estimate relative abundance, size and age structure, and growth. The authors concluded that the fish community was dominated by rough fish and small individuals, and that the ratio of catchable sport fish to other fish was unfavorable for angling.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The impoundment covers 265 surface acres and sits on Satchel Creek. The dam was built in 1927 and the basin filled in 1928.
- **Fishery history:** After stocking, the lake produced a few years of excellent fishing, then sporadic good fishing and a gradual decline in catch. Fishing was described as unsatisfactory in recent years.
- **Drawdown:** The lake overflowed in 1951 and receded from 1952 because of prolonged drought. City officials and the [[Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Department]] chose to remove all fish and restock with desirable species.
- **Commercial removal:** In summer 1954, commercial fishermen netted 60,000 pounds of buffalo and carp (per Kansas Fish and Game, Jan. 1955).
- **Salvage:** In September 1954, most remaining water was pumped into the newly built Lake Bluestem. The Department seined the lake for game fish, moving about 6,000 channel catfish and 50 largemouth bass.
- **Poisoning:** On October 22, 1954, biologists applied 5% rotenone at 3 pounds per acre-foot (slightly more than 1 ppm). Water temperature was 60°F and air temperature 55°F. The lake volume was estimated at 40 acre-feet at that time.
- **Methods:** Fish were collected randomly from a boat for 24 hours after poisoning. Unusual fish were kept separate. Lengths and some weights were recorded. Ages were determined from scales (using a [[Scale Analysis]] method after Van Oosten, Deason, and Jobes, 1934) or from transverse sections of pectoral fin spines ([[Fin-Spine Aging]], after Sneed, 1951).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Kill response and extent:** Freshwater drum, white crappie, buffalo, and channel catfish were affected within 30 minutes. Drum and large catfish circled erratically at the surface. The next morning, many live buffalo were found near shore and live minnows in shallow areas. By the second day, no live fish were seen from shore. The authors judged the kill good and perhaps complete.

**Use of the resource:** The authors stress that Kansas fisheries are used only for recreation. Fishing-method restrictions and angler selectivity limit use to larger sport fish. High total production therefore has little value unless sport fish make up a large share, and high sport-fish production has little value unless fish are large enough to be harvested by anglers.

**Standing crop:** Pre-poisoning standing crop could not be fully measured. Based on commercial and Department removals plus observations at poisoning, the authors estimated it "probably exceeded 400 pounds per acre" of surface area at capacity.

**Possible influences on the sample:** The removals of 6,050 channel catfish and largemouth bass and 60,000 pounds of buffalo and carp, reduced spawning success and heavy predation on small fish during the 1954 drawdown, and possible under-sampling of small fish may all have skewed the ratios.

## Key Findings

### Species Composition and Abundance

- **Assemblage:** 17 species were recorded, including game, rough, and forage fishes. Forage minnows were scarce, so they were not a large food reservoir for the many game fish.
- **Dominant species by random sample:** [[White Crappie]] about 27%, [[Freshwater Drum]] about 27%, [[Carp]] about 22%, [[Channel Catfish]] about 16%, and [[Black Buffalo]] plus [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] together about 7.8%.
- **Rare species:** [[Golden Redhorse]] (8 specimens, under 1%), [[Black Bullhead]] (14 specimens, under 1%), [[Largemouth Bass]] (4 specimens, under 1%), a single [[Bluegill]], and a single young-of-the-year [[Orangespotted Sunfish]].
- **Flathead catfish:** One 9-year-old specimen (34.8 in., 17.34 lb) was taken. In spring 1952, 103 fish averaging six inches were stocked by anglers, per the report.
- **Minnows:** [[Red Shiner]] made up about 98% of minnows found after poisoning. Brook silversides (2), golden shiner (1), plains shiner (rare), and sand shiner (rare) were also found.

### Size, Growth, and Condition

- **Buffalo:** The 44 specimens ranged from 9.7 to 28.2 inches. Most were in their third year and weighed 3 to 5 pounds. An 8-year-old bigmouth buffalo measured 28.2 inches. Commercial fishermen said buffalo made up 99% of the 60,000 pounds netted.
- **Channel catfish:** Of 285 fish (4.8 to 25.6 in.), 51% exceeded 10 inches, 8% exceeded 12 inches, and 5% exceeded 14 inches. Growth seemed slow, but condition was satisfactory. Length-weight data from 60 fish matched the Oklahoma average (Jenkins, 1954).
- **White crappie:** Of 155 fish (3.0 to 9.7 in.), 66% exceeded six inches, 9% exceeded seven inches, and 2% exceeded eight inches. The dominant age group was age 2 (1952 year-class). Fish seemed slightly underweight compared with Carlander (1953).
- **Freshwater drum:** Of 154 fish (3.6 to 31.8 in.), 31% exceeded 10 inches, 3% exceeded 12 inches, and under 1% exceeded 14 inches. 87% were under four years old. Drum were the most poorly conditioned species, described as "razor-backed and hollow-bellied."
- **Carp:** 125 fish ranged from 5.6 to 24.9 inches. Ages were not determined, but all size groups seemed to be in good condition.

### Sport-Fish Ratios (Discussion)

- **Overall ratio:** About 2 sport fish to 1 other fish after poisoning.
- **Acceptable-size ratio:** Fish of angler-acceptable size included channel catfish (12 in.), black bullhead (8 in.), flathead catfish (12 in.), largemouth bass (10 in.), bluegill (6 in.), white crappie (7 in.), and drum (12 in.). These fish made up under 5% of the population, or about 1 acceptable sport fish per 19 others.
- **Conclusion:** The authors judged the ratio unfavorable for satisfactory sport fishing.

### Reference Note

The paper's [[Age and Growth Analysis]] of [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Freshwater Drum]] supports the report's main management point: the lake's fish community was dominated by small, poorly conditioned individuals and rough fish, so the sport-fish yield was low despite a high total biomass.
