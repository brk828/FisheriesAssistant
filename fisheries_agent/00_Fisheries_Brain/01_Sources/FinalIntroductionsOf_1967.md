## Executive Summary
This 1967 short communication reports the final phase (1964–1966) of a multi-year fish introduction program transplanting the [[Bonneville Cisco]] (*Prosopium gemmiferum*) from its native [[Bear Lake]] (Utah-Idaho) to [[Lake Tahoe]] (California-Nevada). Over three winters, 21,506 spawning adults were collected and transported, with 15,888 surviving to be released alive. Concurrently, roughly 250,000 eggs were artificially stripped and fertilized, with the majority released directly into Tahoe's shallow rocky habitat and the remainder reared at a hatchery before release as eyed eggs or alevins. The paper documents a substantial improvement in transportation survival in 1966, attributed to refined handling protocols, indicating an applied fisheries management focus on optimizing translocation techniques for a cold-water salmonid species.

## Background & Description
- Species is a member of the genus *Prosopium* (whitefishes/ciscoes), family [[Salmonidae]] (subfamily Coregoninae).
- Described taxonomically by Snyder; detailed morphological description not repeated in this note (referenced as covered in the prior 1965 paper by Frantz and Cordone).
- Life stages referenced in the transplant program: ripe/spawning adults, fertilized eggs, eyed eggs, and alevins — indicating the full early life-history sequence was utilized in the stocking effort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range:** [[Bear Lake]], Utah-Idaho — a cold, deep, oligotrophic lake; the species is endemic to this system.
- **Introduced range:** [[Lake Tahoe]], California-Nevada — a large, deep, cold oligotrophic alpine lake considered ecologically analogous, motivating the introduction.
- **Spawning behavior:** Collected in January during the spawning run, indicating a winter spawning period, likely in shallow or nearshore/rocky habitat (eggs were released into "shallow rocky areas" of Lake Tahoe, suggesting this mimics natural spawning substrate preference).
- **Trophic role:** As a pelagic cisco, likely intended to serve as a forage fish species within the Lake Tahoe food web (implied by broader context of similar historical [[Coregonid]] introductions into western US lakes).
- Program reflects a fisheries management strategy of interbasin transfer to establish a self-sustaining population in a novel lake ecosystem.

## Key Findings
- **Total effort (1964–1966):** 21,506 adult cisco collected and transported from Bear Lake; 15,888 released alive into Lake Tahoe (Table 1).
- **Year-by-year adult transport:**
  - 1964: 8,500 collected → 3,000 lost → 5,500 released alive
  - 1965: 7,906 collected → 2,614 lost → 5,292 released alive
  - 1966: 5,100 collected → 4 lost → 5,096 released alive
- **Major improvement in 1966:** Transportation mortality dropped to a negligible level (4 of 5,100), a dramatic improvement over the substantial losses in 1964 and 1965 (~35–33%).
- **Causes of improved survival:**
  - More careful fish handling procedures.
  - Lowering transport tank water temperature to 34–40°F (down from 42–50°F).
  - Reducing fish density in tanks to ~3.5 fish/gallon (down from 4.7–7.1 fish/gallon).
- **Egg-stage supplementation:** ~250,000 eggs artificially stripped and fertilized at Bear Lake across the three years.
  - ~205,000 eggs released directly into shallow rocky habitat in Lake Tahoe.
  - Remaining eggs held at [[Verdi Fish Hatchery]] (Nevada); survivors later released as 25,000 eyed eggs and 3,000 alevins.
- **Institutional collaboration:** Program involved joint effort between the Nevada Fish and Game Commission, California Department of Fish and Game, Utah State Department of Fish and Game, and Idaho Fish and Game Department.
- **Context:** This paper serves as a follow-up/completion report to [[Frantz and Cordone 1965]], which detailed the rationale, species description, and first-year (1964) results of the introduction program.
