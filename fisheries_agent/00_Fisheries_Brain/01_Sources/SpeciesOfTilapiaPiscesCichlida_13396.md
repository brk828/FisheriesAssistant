# Species of Tilapia (Pisces, Cichlidae) in Lake Kitangiri, Tanzania, East Africa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study, reprinted from the *Journal of Zoology* (1965), examines a small collection of [[Tilapia]] from [[Lake Kitangiri]] in the eastern Rift Valley of Tanzania. Of 11 specimens, seven belong to [[Tilapia Amphimelas]] (four specimens) and [[Tilapia Esculenta]] (three specimens). The remaining four are intermediate in structure and are interpreted as products of [[Hybridization]]. The authors conclude that at least one parent species reached the lake recently, probably through human introduction. The paper also includes a postscript identifying Tilapia samples from [[Lake Eyasi]] and [[Lake Singida]] and reports a third species, [[Tilapia Melanopleura]], first noticed in the lake after a stocked dam broke.

## Background & Description

**Study material.** The specimens were sent in 1958 to the East African Freshwater Fisheries Research Organization by G. J. Lockley, Chief Fisheries Officer, Tanganyika. They were collected by A. W. White and M. Hammond, Field Officers of the Tanganyika Fishery service. The authors were Ethelwynn Trewavas (British Museum, Natural History) and Geoffrey Fryer (East African Freshwater Fisheries Research Organization).

**Setting.** Lake Kitangiri lies south-west of [[Lake Eyasi]] and belongs to a chain of eastern Rift Valley lakes. It drains into Lake Eyasi through the Sibiti River in the wet season. [[Lake Manyara]] lies in a fork of the rift east of Lake Eyasi. Lakes Eyasi and Manyara are described as highly alkaline "salt" lakes, whereas Lake Kitangiri is considerably less saline, according to chemical analyses by Talling & Talling (in press).

**Fishery.** Tilapia support a fishery. A 1960 catch of 60 Tilapia (average weight 1½ pounds) was recorded from 100 yards of 4½-inch mesh gill-net fished for four hours by daylight. Catches in 1964 were much smaller (M. J. Mann, unpublished report).

**Species accounts.**
- *T. amphimelas* was described by Hilgendorf (1905) from [[Lake Manyara]]. A synonym, *T. manyarae*, was also described from the same lake. Hilgendorf suggested the two names might represent males and females of one species. The authors accept this idea, since darker fins and a deeper body could be breeding colours. A related species, *T. alcalica*, from a volcanic cavity near Lake Nguruman, is not yet regarded as a synonym.
- *T. esculenta* was described by Graham (1928) from [[Lake Victoria]]. It has been studied intensively for growth, fecundity, mortality and juvenile identification.

**Diagnostic characters.** Full-grown specimens of the two species are recognizable at sight by the following contrasts:
- **Body shape:** *T. esculenta* is deep-bodied with a steep, even dorsal profile. *T. amphimelas* is slender with a concave head profile over the eye, giving the names "blunt-nosed" and "narrow-nosed."
- **Eye size:** *T. amphimelas* has the smaller eye.
- **Squamation:** In *T. amphimelas*, scales abruptly become smaller on the lower flanks and belly, and scales extend onto the preorbital bone. In *T. esculenta*, scale rows continue evenly onto the belly.
- **Pectoral fin:** It reaches above the middle of the anal fin in *T. esculenta* (39–44% of standard length, S.L.), but only to the vent or slightly beyond in *T. amphimelas* (22–32% S.L.).
- **Teeth:** Teeth in *T. esculenta* remain bi- or tricuspid at all stages. Larger *T. amphimelas* develop unicuspid outer teeth.
- **Gill-rakers:** On the lower anterior arch, 12–15 in *T. amphimelas* and 15–21 in *T. esculenta*.
- **Dorsal fin:** Spines number XII–XIV in *T. amphimelas* versus XVI–XVIII in *T. esculenta*. Total dorsal rays are 24–26 versus 27–28.
- **Caudal peduncle:** Longer than deep in *T. amphimelas* (16.5–19.5% S.L.), deeper than long in *T. esculenta* (12–15% S.L.).
- **Interorbital width:** 25–35% of head length in *T. amphimelas* versus 41–47.5% in *T. esculenta*.
- **Lower pharyngeal bones:** Their shape and dentigerous areas differ between the species.

**Colour.** Colour is similar in both species: silvery-blue with pinkish areas on the underparts, flanks and paired fins. Males may be darker. Colour descriptions from living Kitangiri fish (by Mr White) fit reasonably well with *T. amphimelas* as described by Neumann at Lake Manyara and with *T. esculenta* from Lake Victoria, though pink areas are reddish in Lake Victoria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introduction history.** No introduction record exists for either species in Lake Kitangiri. The authors consider it probable that *T. esculenta* arrived through human agency. Some Tanzanian waters were stocked from ponds at Korogwe, where *T. esculenta* was bred (Lowe, 1955a).
- **Recent co-occurrence.** The presence of four intermediate fish suggests the two species have only recently lived together. Hybridization is expected to be rare between species with long-established coexistence.
- **Dams and stocking.** Dams at Williamson's Diamond Mines, on a river entering Lake Kitangiri, were stocked before 1958. Although no precise records exist, *T. esculenta* and *T. melanopleura* were among species being placed in East African dams at that time. One dam broke in December 1961. In January 1962, Mr Mkonolah (Fishery Officer) recorded *T. melanopleura* in Lake Kitangiri for the first time. Specimens collected there in 1964 by M. J. Mann confirm this third species.
- **Reproduction.** *T. esculenta* is a maternal mouth-brooder. Tilapia that use oral incubation hybridize readily. Species that naturally share a body of water generally remain separate. Hybrid sex ratios in other tilapia have been reported as male-biased (e.g., Hickling, 1960; Fishelson, 1962).
- **Habitat preferences.** Welcomme (1964b) studied habitats of juvenile *T. esculenta* in Lake Victoria.

## Key Findings

**1. Species identification.** Seven of 11 Lake Kitangiri specimens are identified as *T. amphimelas* (4) and *T. esculenta* (3). These two species are considered well-characterized, with *T. amphimelas* previously known only from Lake Manyara and *T. esculenta* only from the Lake Victoria basin.

**2. Hybrid origin of intermediates.** Four specimens are intermediate in morphology, interpreted as hybrids and possibly including back-crosses. Three resemble *T. esculenta* in body shape, preorbital scalation and dentition. The fourth (235 mm male) resembles *T. amphimelas* in profile but differs in several features.

**3. Sex and maturity of intermediates.**
- The 150 mm and 163 mm intermediates have female and male genital papillae, respectively, with immature gonads.
- The 184.5 mm fish is a female with swollen ovaries containing loose eggs (long axis 2.5 mm).
- The 235 mm fish is a male with testes beginning to ripen.

No gross evidence of reduced fertility was found, but the normality of the F1 sex ratio remains unknown.

**4. Size observations.** The largest Kitangiri specimen is a 269 mm female. Sex is readily visible at 240–269 mm but not at 135 mm, which may indicate a larger size at maturity in Lake Kitangiri than in Lake Manyara. The authors suggest faster growth and larger size at maturity than at Lake Manyara, where populations may be stunted.

**5. Dentition change.** Outer teeth of large *T. amphimelas* become unicuspid in both sexes. This change may accompany sexual maturity and occur at a larger size in Lake Kitangiri, though the samples are too small to prove this.

**6. Taxonomic decisions.** *T. amphimelas* and *T. manyarae* are treated as one species, with the male-female interpretation accepted. *T. alcalica* is not yet placed as a synonym.

**7. Postscript: Lake Eyasi sample (4 specimens).** All resemble *T. amphimelas* in scale characters, dorsal formula (24–25 rays), small eye, concave head profile and longer-than-deep caudal peduncle. One specimen has 17 gill-rakers, and one emaciated female has a large head suggesting a "hunger form." Variation suggests possible cross-breeding or a second species in the lake.

**8. Postscript: Lake Singida sample.**
- One specimen agrees with *T. pangani* in proportions, colour and counts.
- Five specimens (119–177 mm S.L.) resemble *T. amphimelas*, with variation suggesting cross-breeding.
- One male (103 mm) and seven females (112.5–175 mm) resemble *T. esculenta*, though with a slightly smaller eye and shorter pectoral fin than typical Lake Victoria specimens. These divergences lean toward *T. amphimelas*, and cross-breeding may also be involved.

**9. Management implications.** The authors recommend that stocking policy consider the risk of hybridization when species that did not originally coexist are introduced into dams or ponds. Results for hybrid fertility and offspring viability are unknown. Continued observation of Lakes Singida and Kitangiri is suggested to inform future stocking decisions.
