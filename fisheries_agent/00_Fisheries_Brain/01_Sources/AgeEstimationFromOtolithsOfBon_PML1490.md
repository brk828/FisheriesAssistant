# Age Estimation from Otoliths of Bonytail Chub in Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Parker Fishery Resources Office) tests whether [[Otolith Analysis]] can separate naturally produced ("wild") [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*) from [[Hatchery Stocking|hatchery-produced]] fish in [[Lake Mohave]]. Seventeen frozen adults were aged from otoliths by three readers. Estimated ages were 7 to 13 years, corresponding to 1980 to 1988 year classes. The author concludes that wild fish persist in Lake Mohave, that between about 10% and perhaps two-thirds of recently collected bonytail may be wild, and that otolith ageing is useful only as a rough "ballpark" tool. Its reliability has not been validated.

## Background & Description

The bonytail is a federally listed endangered minnow of the [[Colorado River]] system. Historically it was common to abundant in mainstems and major tributaries from Mexico north into Wyoming. It now survives only as sporadic captures in the upper basin and in Lake Mohave, the only place where it can reliably be captured, and then only in spring and early summer. The population is likely in the hundreds to low thousands, though data are insufficient for accurate estimation. Despite its listing, a revised recovery plan, and a large recovery implementation program, its status has continued to decline.

Captive propagation began when bonytail were taken from Lake Mohave into the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the late 1970s. Repatriation to Lake Mohave started in October 1981 with tetracycline-marked juveniles (10.2 cm total length). Later stockings included larvae, juveniles and adults, some marked and some unmarked. A total of 202,622 fish were stocked through May 1996 (see [[Stocking Records]] in Appendix A, Marsh 1996).

Wild and stocked fish share habitat and, after a few years, similar sizes. Unmarked stocked fish therefore cannot be told apart from wild fish by appearance. Only fish marked before release are identifiable as hatchery products. Fish from the 1981 year class or earlier must be wild, since the first stocking was in 1981. Later unmarked fish could be either.

Otolith (ear bone) removal is the only practicable method to age bonytail. However, it requires sacrificing the fish and damages the head, which can make specimens unsuitable for museum accession. The method is therefore limited to fresh specimens from accidental or other mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Adult size does not reliably separate wild from hatchery fish. Aged fish overlapped the sizes of known wild adults collected in 1979 to 1981. Those wild females averaged 51.8 ± 2.8 cm TL (n = 6, range 48.7–56.4 cm), and the five wild males measured 45.4–48.0 cm. The study found no relationship between total length and age, either for all fish or for each sex. The author suggests that hatchery fish, which are fed and protected, may grow faster than wild fish of the same age, which would explain this.

Stocking history and fish origin made interpretation difficult. Records were confused and poorly kept, and the author identifies this as a major source of uncertainty. Some fish carried [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]], mostly applied at the Dexter hatchery. Others came from domestic hatchery stocks or from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona). One fish was of hatchery origin with known age.

## Key Findings

- **Study material:** 17 frozen adult bonytail (9 males, 8 females) died in 1992, 1993 or 1995. Fish were from Lake Mohave or from domestic hatchery stocks. Dexter hatchery provenance was recorded for many. One fish of hatchery origin had known age.
- **Method:** Otoliths were removed, dried, mounted in thermoplastic cement, polished with 1500-grit wet/dry paper, and examined at 10–450× magnification. Three readers each gave a "best estimate" of annuli. Age ranges, mean ages and estimated year classes were derived from their combined readings. See [[Age Estimation]].
- **Size:** All fish averaged 48.4 cm TL (range 44.5–54.5 cm).
- **Males:** 47.7 ± 1.8 cm TL (SD; n = 9, range 45.9–51.0 cm). Ages 7 to 13 years. Year classes 1980 to 1985.
- **Females:** Averaged about 49 cm TL (SD 3.8 cm; n = 8, range 44.5–54.5 cm; the exact mean is partly illegible in the source). Ages 7 to 13 years. Year classes 1980 to 1988.
- **Known-age check:** The one fish of known age (ID 4) was 11 years old, from the 1981 year class. Its otolith gave an estimate of 9 years, so the author regards the other age estimates as conservative.
- **Wild fish identified:** Three fish from the 1980 or 1981 year classes (IDs 7, 8 and 11) are considered wild. Because readers did not always agree, as many as eight more fish from the 1982–1983 year classes could also be wild.
- **Population conclusion:** Wild bonytail persist in Lake Mohave. Between about 10% and perhaps two-thirds of recently collected bonytail were estimated to be wild.
- **Reliability:** Some otoliths were unreadable. Validation is virtually absent, except for one known-age fish that was aged at 9 years against a true age of 11. Estimates are considered useful for ages of about 10 years or more, but absolute age becomes less reliable as fish grow older. Discrimination among year classes also declines as more years pass after the first stocking.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Maintain complete, accurate individual records at capture, transfer, periodic checks, and release.
  - Distribute records to interested parties and to a permanent archive.
  - Explore and apply [[Genetic Parentage Analysis]] to determine parentage and likely origin.
  - Validate the otolith method using otoliths from a series of known-age hatchery fish across a range of ages and year classes.
  - Use otolith ageing as an adjunct to genetic evaluation, and apply it to incidental mortalities from field-caught material.
