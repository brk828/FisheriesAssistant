# Lake Mohave Fisheries and Razorback Sucker Hybridization Correspondence (1983–1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of three linked items concerning [[Lake Mohave]] on the [[Colorado River]] and its native fishes. The main item is a 1988 letter from Professor H. L. Minckley to George Dinuine of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) providing a background review of the reservoir's fisheries and recommending a long-term evaluation of the impact of introduced [[Striped Bass]]. The second item is a 1983 memo from Linda Ulmer (Arizona Game and Fish) on an [[Electrophoresis]] study of [[Razorback Sucker]] hybridization. The third is a 1985 letter from [[Don Buth]] (UCLA) on a manuscript about native versus hatchery razorback suckers and hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Together they argue that striped bass are likely to reshape the Lake Mohave fish community, that razorback sucker recruitment is poorly understood, and that hybridization with catostomids is a serious risk for reintroductions.

## Background & Description

**Lake Mohave** was impounded by [[Davis Dam]] during construction from 1942, with final filling around 1954. Early impacts were linked to World War II, the Korean conflict, and later problems with flow patterns of the Colorado River and the filling of [[Lake Mead]]. Several ecological studies were made before and during construction, and a comparative study of Lake Mead (closed 1935) and Lake Mohave in early impoundment years appeared in the 1950s. Interest revived in the 1970s and 1980s, when about ten research reports covering Lake Mohave were produced. [[Arizona State University]] also maintained a near-continuous fisheries database from 1974 through 1985.

**Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River system. Lake Mohave holds the largest native population in the Colorado River mainstem. Adults in the reservoir are more than 15 years old, and the species is listed as endangered. **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*) is also endangered; Lake Mohave may be the last place it survives in any number, with 23 adults caught over the previous decade (mostly transferred to the USFWS for use as brood stock).

**Native and introduced fishes** present in the reservoir include [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Striped Bass]]. Sports fisheries have relied on warmwater species, plus a put-and-take [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery supported by [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Attempts with [[Coho Salmon]], [[Kokanee]], and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] did not develop into major fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Productivity.** Primary productivity is relatively low. Phytoplankton are nutrient-limited in some areas, and productivity is expected to rise as upstream reservoirs mature. Zooplankton remain sparse. Non-native invertebrates include crayfish (abundance unknown) and the [[Asiatic Clam]], which is locally abundant.

**Forage base.** Piscivores depend mainly on threadfin shad, with smaller centrarchids and red shiners as minor components. Growth of fishes appears relatively rapid, and productivity of both forage and game species seems reasonably high.

**Striped bass.** Anglers reported striped bass in Lake Mohave after 1980, and juveniles appeared in samples in 1981. Ten netting samples from a February–May sampling period included the species, with sizes reported from roughly 15 to 178 cm total length. Minckley noted increasing average size but no decline in the proportion of young fish, which suggests ongoing recruitment without a clear increase in population size. Possible sources include immigration through [[Hoover Dam]] turbines, upstream reservoirs, or spill from [[Lake Mead]].

**Predation and recruitment.** Razorback suckers still spawn and produce larvae each year, but no recruitment into the adult population has been detected since 1974. Minckley attributed this to predation by non-native species, a hypothesis that remained under study. Reintroduced larval and fingerling razorback suckers have been observed being preyed upon by non-native fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Striped bass threat.** Minckley concluded that striped bass can over-exploit a single forage species such as threadfin shad, to the detriment of their own populations and other warmwater fishes. This pattern was documented in Lake Mead, and Utah and Nevada were considering the introduction of alternative forage fishes to maintain striped bass fisheries. Abundant striped bass would preclude a coldwater (hypolimnetic) trout fishery, making stocking at Willow Beach futile.

2. **Expected change.** Striped bass abundance in Lake Mohave was predicted to increase, through immigration or in-lake reproduction, which would change the fish community structure. Concerns included the likely extirpation of benthic populations and further unrecognized losses of razorback sucker recruitment.

3. **Recommendations to the USFWS (1988):**
   - **Long-term evaluation:** Begin an in-depth review and centralization of data on the impact of striped bass introduction and sport fishery management on the Colorado River system, including biological and socioeconomic analyses.
   - **Continued monitoring:** Continue and possibly expand Lake Mohave fish monitoring, adding summer-active species (threadfin shad, carp, and related fish) and basic data on growth, condition, and feeding.
   - **Targeted studies deferred:** Extensive field studies focused on striped bass were not yet justified, as the population was too small to sample except incidentally. Preliminary data on food, feeding, growth, and reproduction should be collected as specimens become available. Sonar monitoring of threadfin shad and permanent transects were recommended.
   - **Committee:** Form a small committee of no more than six members from the USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service ([[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]]), the states of Nevada and Arizona, and a qualified academic familiar with Lake Mohave and fisheries.
   - **Funding:** Monitoring and research should be funded through a single group to avoid divided responsibilities.

4. **Low likelihood of reversal.** Minckley judged it unlikely that striped bass establishment could be prevented or reversed, but documenting the phenomenon has broad implications for basic ecology and reservoir management.

5. **Electrophoretic study (1982–1983).** In September 1982 the USFWS (Albuquerque) funded Don Buth at UCLA to conduct an electrophoretic study of razorback suckers and [[Catostomus]] hybridization, to determine the degree of hybridization and introgression in the Lake Mohave population before reintroductions into the Colorado River. Brood stock at the USFWS Dexter Hatchery was being used for reintroductions in Arizona.
   - In October 1982, 15 razorback suckers (1981 cohort) were transferred from Senator Wash Reservoir to UCLA.
   - On 10 January 1983, 20 fish from the 1982 cohort (1–3 inches) were sent to UCLA; four went to the Living Desert Reserve.
   - In January 1983, about 11 males and 7 females were sampled from Lake Mohave (16 inches to 1.75 feet); 17 looked morphologically like razorback suckers, while some males were suspected to be hybrids.
   - Ageing discrepancies led to fish being returned to Arizona Game and Fish for ageing.

6. **Hybridization and introgression (1985).** Buth's manuscript, prepared for the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists symposium, reviewed hybridization and introgression in catostomids. Earlier authors (Anderson 1949; Hubbs 1955) identified habitat modification and uneven numbers of parent species as the main drivers. Hybridization between razorback and flannelmouth suckers was reported at about 10% in two studies, and introgressive hybridization is possible throughout the middle and upper Colorado River. Reintroductions should be carefully sited, especially where catostomids such as [[Sonoran Sucker]] or flannelmouth sucker are present. The paper was cut from 45 to 25 pages, and submission was delayed; its outcome was pending when the letter was written.

7. **Reintroduction risk.** Buth noted that reintroduced razorback suckers are unlikely to survive to reproductive age in the presence of non-native fishes, which can predate them.
