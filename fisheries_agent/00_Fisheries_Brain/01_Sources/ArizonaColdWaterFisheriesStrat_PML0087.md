# Arizona Cold Water Fisheries Strategic Plan 1985-1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department strategic plan (Federal Aid Project FW-11-R, 1985-1990) quantifies the supply of cold water (trout) angling habitat and fish, compares it with angler demand measured in 1981, and sets management goals, objectives, concepts, standards and problem-solving strategies. Arizona's trout fishing depends almost entirely on [[Hatchery Production]] and stocking, because natural trout reproduction is marginal and trout do not reproduce in lakes. Demand is projected to outpace supply by 1990, and the plan concludes that the hatchery supply, not the water supply, is the binding constraint.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and authority.** The plan functions as a dynamic management document under [[Arizona Revised Statutes]] Title 17, which vests wildlife management in the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and its commission. It serves to evaluate management, communicate with the public and legislature, guide staff, and provide a public review opportunity. A comprehensive review is scheduled for year three, with a new plan every five years.

**Funding.** Development was financed by Arizona fishing and hunting license revenue and federal apportionments under the [[Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act]] (Dingell-Johnson Act) and the [[Federal Aid in Wildlife Restoration Act]] (Pittman-Robertson Act).

**Species history.** Only two native trouts, the [[Arizona Trout]] and the [[Gila Trout]], were originally present, in the White Mountains, Blue River, Eagle Creek and upper Verde River tributaries. Exotic species were introduced from the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries from about 1900 onward: [[Rainbow Trout]] (shortly before 1900, first in Oak Creek), [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] (1917), [[Brown Trout]] (1933) and [[Arctic Grayling]] (1940). Sockeye and coho salmon were added in the 1950s and 1960s. Early stockings were hampered by long transport, small fry, and August timing. The first state hatchery opened on the South Fork of the Little Colorado River near Greer in 1922. The Department or its predecessor later operated fifteen culture stations; six remain.

**Angler scale.** Over 220,000 licensed trout anglers harvest over 3 million trout annually, make almost 1 million trips, and contribute over $64 million to the state economy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat supply.**
- Primary trout streams: 170 streams (excluding the Colorado River), 1,552 miles and 14,471 surface acres, divided into 202 Stream Management Reaches. About 54% of acreage is on [[U.S. Forest Service]] land, 21.6% on Indian Reservations (likely an underestimate) and 13.3% on private land.
- Primary trout lakes: 168 identified, with about 31,915 surface acres of lakes managed primarily for trout. About 27% of lakes are on Forest Service land, 21% are private (under 4% of acreage), and about 35% are on Indian Reservations.
- Warm-water-primary waters with secondary trout management add 399 miles (13,679 acres of streams) and 11,866 lake acres.
- Numerous additional waters have potential for trout management but were not evaluated.

**Natural reproduction.** Natural reproduction is dependable in some streams but marginal in most. Catastrophic events can eliminate populations. Trout do not reproduce in Arizona lakes.

**Non-Department stocking.** The Department stocks only about one-third of trout placed in in-state and bordering waters. Commercial fee-fishing ponds and private stocking supply much of the rest.

**Habitat threats.** The plan identifies excessive nutrients causing algae and vegetation blooms, livestock grazing, silviculture reducing shade and raising temperatures, off-road vehicle channelization, road construction causing siltation, mining discharge, dewatering of regulated flows, and deteriorated instream structures.

**Demand and use.** In 1981, licensed anglers logged about 1,489,000 angler-days on Arizona and bordering waters, with 64.7% on [[U.S. Forest Service]] lands, 11.7% on Indian Reservations and 19.1% on National Park Service lands. Unlicensed anglers under 14 added an estimated 196,000 to 372,000 days.

## Key Findings

**1. Stocking trends are declining per capita.** Arizona's population grew 183% from 1955 to 1981. Catchable stocking rose only about 23% (0.8% per year) and fingerling stocking about 12%. Stocking per resident peaked in the mid-1960s and then declined sharply. By 1981, relative supply per resident was about half the late-1960s level. Trout stamp participation peaked in 1970 and then fell.

**2. Current use is supply-limited.** Licensed non-urban use was estimated at about 1.326 million angler-days in 1981, while demand was about 1.760 million (33% greater). Urban demand was about 0.468 million angler-days, with a potential urban use of 0.083 million.

**3. Demand is projected to rise.** Non-urban demand for 1990 is about 2.3 million angler-days (2.9 million per one projection). Supply-limited use in 1990 is about 1.585 million days, a 17.8% increase over 1981. Demand is about 46% above the level the programs can support.

**4. Management concepts.** Seven concepts (Intensive Use, Basic Yield, Blue Ribbon, Featured Species, Wildfish, Urban, and Private/Reservation) define management. Intensive Use and Basic Yield together account for over 88% of projected 1990 demand. Wildfish waters are almost entirely streams.

**5. Supply versus demand by 1990 (Table 9, Arizona licensed anglers).**
- Intensive Use: potential supply 1,134,000 vs. 1990 demand 897,000 (surplus).
- Basic Yield: 750,000 vs. 901,500 (shortage of 151,500).
- Blue Ribbon: 97,000 vs. 126,500 (shortage of 29,500).
- Featured Species: 2,500 vs. 23,000 (shortage of 20,500).
- Wildfish: 98,000 vs. 80,500 (surplus, but about 50,000 days are in the Grand Canyon and largely inaccessible).
- Combined Intensive Use and Basic Yield: surplus of about 383,500 days, though Basic Yield alone falls short.

**6. Hatchery supply is the key constraint.** Hatcheries are described as antiquated and in disrepair. Achieving the 1990 demand would require roughly a 55.7% increase in hatchery-produced trout over 1981 production. Securing renovation funds will determine future supply, and accurate prediction is not possible.

**7. Management goals and objectives.**
- Protect and enhance cold water habitat; conserve native trout; improve angling quality; diversify opportunities; increase hatchery trout production.
- Increase hatchery trout available for harvest by 30% by 1987 and 55% by 1990.
- Add 20,000 Blue Ribbon angler-days by 1987 and 50,000 by 1990.
- Add 10,000 Featured Species angler-days by 1987 and 20,000 by 1990.
- Preserve Wildfish fisheries and add 10,000 angler-days by 1990.
- Add 300,000 Intensive Use and Basic Yield angler-days by 1987 and 600,000 by 1990.

**8. Problem areas and strategies.** The plan groups problems into four areas: general management (poor biological data, inconsistent creel and sampling methods, no central data repository), hatchery and stocking (insufficient production, predation, poor water quality at stocking, uneven distribution), habitat and water quality, and law enforcement (non-compliance, illegal stocking, and jurisdictional limits on shared waters). Research needs include survival and harvest rates for stocked trout, a standardized angler-use census, and optimal species and strain selection.

**9. Concept conclusion.** The plan concludes that water supply is currently adequate for present demand, but only Intensive Use waters can meet 1990 demand without conversions. The supply of waters is adequate only in the short term, and the hatchery supply of trout is inadequate now.
