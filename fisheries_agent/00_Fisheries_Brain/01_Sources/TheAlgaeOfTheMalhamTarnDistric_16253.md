# The Algae of the Malham Tarn District

#phycology #freshwater-biology #algae

## Executive Summary

This review, written by J. W. G. Lund of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], describes the algal flora of the [[Malham Tarn]] district in Yorkshire. The document concerns algae, not fish. It covers terrestrial, semi-terrestrial and aquatic habitats, with a focus on the calcareous conditions that shape the flora. It also reports long-term plankton observations on [[Asterionella Formosa]] and the nutrient and alkalinity dynamics of the tarn and its inflow. The article is a research-oriented guide that also sets out research priorities for the [[Malham Tarn Field Centre]] and includes methods for collecting, culturing, and identifying algae.

## Background & Description

The district has a rich algal flora within easy reach of field facilities. The [[Windermere Laboratory]] of the Freshwater Biological Association is about two hours away by road. The author notes that Britain has a few thousand algal species, that over a thousand could occur in the area, and that more than 450 genera are recorded in Britain. Several genera are very large: [[Chlamydomonas]] has about 550 species, [[Navicula]] has over 1,000, and [[Staurastrum]] has a similar number of specific and infra-specific taxa.

The article uses a referencing system to the standard floras of [[Fritsch and West Flora]] (1927) and [[Smith Freshwater Algae]] (1950), cited as F. and S. with page numbers. An appendix gives a generic checklist arranged by habitat letter codes (soil, peat, bark, rock, temporary puddles, permanent pools, streams, plankton). The checklist covers Chlorophyta, Cyanophyta, Chrysophyta, Xanthophyceae, Conjugatophyceae, Bacillariophyceae, Rhodophyta, Euglenophyta, Pyrrophyta and Cryptophyceae. Its main text is about algal ecology rather than a full species inventory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Terrestrial and semi-terrestrial habitats.** Most soils are calcareous, so [[Cyanobacteria]] and [[Diatoms]] are well represented. On the [[Tarn Moss]], peat supports algae such as [[Zygogonium Ericetorum]] (a dusky purple coating that resists drought), [[Chlamydomonas]], and [[Coccomyxa]]. The acid-tolerant [[Euglena Mutabilis]] can grow at pH 2 or less. Bark, walls, and limestone surfaces carry the [[Pleurococcus]] community, which the author describes as the most widespread alga in Britain. Its members look alike, so their distributions are poorly known. This community is eaten by [[Psocids]], whose ecology was first studied at Malham. Algae are also tied to lichens (for example [[Trebouxia]]), and moss tussocks host diatoms and filamentous algae.

**Rock and cliff surfaces.** Wet rock hosts [[Gloeocapsa]] and [[Phormidium]] communities. Gloeocapsa sheath pigment changes colour with substratum pH: bluish on alkaline limestone, reddish on acidic rock. This explains the bluish-black discolouration of Malham limestone. [[Scytonema]] and [[Petalonema]] stages are linked to drier and wetter conditions, respectively.

**Pools and puddles.** Limestone solution hollows are the main habitat for [[Stephanosphaera Pluvialis]] and [[Haematococcus Pluvialis]]. Both produce reddish cysts when dry. Cattle-trampled puddles on the Ha Mire and Great Close Mire support [[Euglenineae]] and Volvocales, and rare flagellates such as [[Mesostigma Viride]]. Many flagellates are "interface organisms" that live in anaerobic mud and aerobic water above it. [[Trachelomonas]] builds manganese- and iron-impregnated envelopes, with more than 250 described species, many separated on ornamentation the author considers too variable to be reliable.

**Streams and springs.** Calcareous streams are relatively neglected in British algology and offer a rich, varied flora. [[Oocardium Stratum]] is one of few desmids that grow in highly calcareous water. It is rare at Malham and was rediscovered by C. A. Sinker. [[Chrysonebula Holmesii]] is known from Gordale Beck and a branch of Cowside Beck, and nowhere else. It forms white jelly masses on stones in rapid flow. Calcite-depositing blue-greens such as [[Rivularia]] and [[Phormidium Incrustatum]] contribute to [[Tufa Formation]].

**The tarn.** The tarn has an area of 62 hectares, a maximum depth of 4.4 m, and lies at 380 m above sea level. Its main inflow is Tarn Beck. Rainfall averages about 1,500 mm per year. The drainage area is calcareous grassland with few trees. Shoreline algae include [[Cladophora Glomerata]], [[Ulothrix]], [[Chaetophora]], and epilithic diatoms that give a brown bloom in spring. The large sphere-forming [[Cladophora Sauteri]] ("Cladophora-balls") is found in the peaty area. Halogen analyses show high bromine in these balls, but analyses of other freshwater algae suggest that halogen content varies without relation to a marine ancestry. The tarn is eutrophic in plankton quality but sparse in quantity. Its plankton is not much richer in species than the oligotrophic north basin of [[Windermere]].

## Key Findings

- **Plankton is sparse for its trophic status.** Spring diatom crops in Malham exceeded 1,000 cells/ml only once (1952). Windermere produces about 5,000–12,000 cells/l each spring, and sometimes up to 30,000. Green algae rarely exceed 100 cells/ml in Malham, while Windermere green algae reach about 1,000 cells/ml or more.
- **Macrophytes compete with phytoplankton.** The tarn is shallow, so submerged plants and benthic algae can use light at all depths. This is a likely cause of low plankton abundance, and benthic production may dominate primary production in most British waters.
- **Silica controls Asterionella.** Crops do not increase below 0.4–0.5 mg/l dissolved silica, and large crops reduce silica concentration. Spring diatom growth on stones and sediments appears to compete for silica. Catastrophic declines occur even at silica levels that would not limit growth in Windermere, which suggests another nutrient is limiting.
- **Nitrogen is not limiting in spring.** Asterionella grows below 0.1 mg/l nitrate-N, while Windermere crops arise below 0.3 mg/l. Spring nitrate in Malham is typically above 0.1 mg/l.
- **Phosphorus is the likely limiting nutrient.** Phosphate-P is almost always extremely low in the tarn. The method is inaccurate below about 1.5 µg/l, which is close to the maximum concentration observed. Increased use of synthetic detergents at the Field Centre has probably raised phosphorus inputs in recent years.
- **Temperature does not restrict Asterionella.** Summer increases occurred at 16–20 °C, and spring increases began near 4 °C, or even during ice cover. Diatoms are therefore not cold stenotherms.
- **Long time series are essential.** Eight years of Asterionella records are described as more than twice as valuable as four years of records, which in turn are much more valuable than two. The author cites this as a case for permanent field stations.
- **Alkalinity cycles are biologically and physically controlled.** Tarn alkalinity falls from late May to June and rises from September to October. It rarely drops below 100 mg/l CaCO₃, though it fell to 60 mg/l in 1949. Tarn Beck shows the reverse pattern. The author proposes that CO₂ exchange with the atmosphere, tarn temperature, and macrophyte photosynthesis together explain the opposite curves, though the relative importance of each factor is unknown.
- **Stephanodiscus hantzschii is episodic.** It appeared in quantity in 1950, produced a large crop in 1952, and was not seen after late 1953. The cause of this time-and-space distribution is unknown.
- **Rare and unresolved taxa are numerous.** Examples include [[Ochromonas Malhamensis]], a famous culture organism of unknown natural occurrence, and [[Chlorobrachis Gracillima]], whose taxonomic status is uncertain. The author also notes that [[Asterionella Formosa var. acaroides]] is probably a growth phase of A. formosa, not a separate taxon.
- **Recommended research priorities.** The author calls for experimental tests of Zygogonium's effect on Sundews and Sedges, phenology of Stephanosphaera (absent in July 1956 despite full pools), growth rates of Rivularia, the ecology of Cladophora-balls (which are rare and should not be over-collected), and taxonomic revision of Trachelomonas and Chrysophyceae scales.
- **Methods appendices.** The appendices give procedures for collecting and keeping samples alive, culturing soil and mud algae, fixing and staining, and cleaning and mounting diatoms with acid, incineration, or potassium permanganate and oxalic acid treatment.
