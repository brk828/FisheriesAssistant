# Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) Revised Recovery Plan (Draft, April 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This draft revised recovery plan, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 by Abarca, Bagley, Hendrickson and Simms, addresses only the Gila topminnow, one of two subspecies of Sonora topminnow. The Gila topminnow was listed as endangered in 1967 without critical habitat designation. The [[Yaqui Topminnow]] is covered by a separate plan. Only nine natural populations remain in Arizona, and the plan states that delisting is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future. Its objective is downlisting from endangered to threatened once recovery actions succeed. The main threats are habitat alteration and introduction of [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which preys on and competes with the species.

The plan lists seven tasks: protect remaining populations, re-establish populations throughout historic range, monitor populations, develop a genetic protocol, maintain captive populations, study life history and interactions with nonnative fishes, and educate the public and managers.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described in 1853 as *Heterandria occidentalis* from the Santa Cruz River near Tucson; redescribed as *P. occidentalis* by Hubbs and Miller (1941). Minckley (1969) recognized two subspecies, distinguished mainly by snout length, mouth position and female lateral band pattern. Molecular data suggest the Yaqui form may warrant species rank, but the plan retains subspecies status.
- **Morphology:** Males seldom exceed 25 mm standard length; females average 30 to 45 mm. Breeding males turn black with bright yellow pelvic, pectoral and caudal fins. Females can reach about 50 mm.
- **Identification:** Mosquitofish have a dark suborbital spot and black spotting on the dorsal and caudal fins, and their gonopodium does not reach past the snout tip. Topminnow gonopodium reaches beyond the snout in the copulatory position.
- **Hybridization:** In Mexico, Gila topminnows coexist with the all-female hybrid *P. monacha-occidentalis*. In the Rio de la Concepción basin, the unisexual hybrid makes up 13% of all poeciliids.
- **Recovery priority:** 2, indicating high threat but high recovery probability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The species was historically widespread in the Gila River drainage below about 1,800 m, from the San Francisco River near Frisco Hot Springs, New Mexico, to the Gila near Yuma, Arizona, and possibly the lower [[Colorado River]]. Remaining U.S. populations are in the Gila and Santa Cruz systems, including [[Monkey Spring]], [[Sharp Spring]], [[Sonoita Creek]], [[Cienega Creek]] and [[Bylas Springs]]. Natural populations are also found in northern Sonora.

**Habitat.** The species prefers shallow, warm, quiet pools but tolerates:
- Temperatures from near freezing under ice to 37 °C
- pH 6.6 to 8.9
- Dissolved oxygen 2.2 to 11 mg/L
- Salinities from tap water to seawater
- Burrowing into mud to survive 1 to 2 days without water

Preferred habitat includes dense algae and debris mats along stream margins. Topminnows generally occupy the upper one-third of the water column.

**Flooding dynamics.** Topminnows tolerate moderate floods better than mosquitofish. In artificial streams, topminnows oriented to the flow and moved to low-velocity edges, while mosquitofish were swept downstream. At Sharp Spring, mosquitofish dropped from 11.5% to 0.7% of the fish fauna after moderate flooding. Coexistence with mosquitofish rarely lasts beyond 3 years in non-flooding habitats, but topminnows persisted with mosquitofish for over 29 years in the flood-prone Santa Cruz River.

**Life history.** Internal fertilization occurs. Large territorial males (14 to 24 mm) turn black and defend territories, while small males use a sneaking strategy. Sexual maturity takes 2 to 11 months. Breeding runs mainly January through August, with two to three overlapping broods. Brood size is 1 to 90 (average about 6, per the source's figures). Females produce 2 to 5 broods per year and can store sperm. Mosquitofish have larger brood sizes (1 to 315) and higher reproductive potential.

**Diet.** Topminnows are opportunistic omnivores, eating detritus, vegetation, amphipods and insect larvae. Mosquitofish are carnivorous and eat rotifers, snails, insect larvae, crustaceans, and fish fry, including conspecifics. The plan argues there is little diet overlap, so food competition is not considered a major cause of decline.

**Genetics.** Allozyme studies found that Monkey Spring and Bylas Springs populations were homozygous at all 25 loci tested, while Sharp Spring had 3.7% heterozygosity. Sharp Spring fish showed higher survival, growth and fecundity, so they replaced Monkey Spring stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1986. Later mtDNA work suggests the Gila basin historically held a basin-wide panmictic population, and that present differences reflect recent bottlenecks.

**Reasons for decline.** Two main causes are identified: habitat destruction and introduction of exotic species. Late 1800s overgrazing, wood cutting and the 1891–1893 drought led to arroyo cutting and lowered water tables. The spread of mosquitofish, first collected in Arizona in 1926, corresponds closely with topminnow declines. Gila topminnows lack the alarm reaction to injured conspecific skin that mosquitofish show, and they evolved in a naturally depauperate fish fauna. Mosquitofish eliminated some populations within 2 years (Havasu Creek reintroduction) to under 3 years (artesian ponds near Safford). Because mosquitofish are cosmopolitan, the threat is unlikely to be removed from the historic range.

## Key Findings

**Status and reintroduction record**
- Nine natural populations remain in the U.S. Of these, seven are in the Santa Cruz system; Monkey Spring, Cottonwood Spring and Cienega Creek are the only ones free of mosquitofish.
- Over 200 reintroductions have occurred at about 178 wild locations (including the figures given in the text). Of these, 19 populations persisted in historic range in 1992.
- Fewer than 11% of reintroduced populations remain extant, despite more than 340 documented stockings.
- Population losses since 1985 were attributed to desiccation (50%), flooding (20%), unknown causes (20%), mosquitofish (2%) and cattle, dredging or low oxygen (remaining 7%).
- Downlisting criteria (20 wild populations surviving three years) were met from 1985 to 1987 but fell below threshold in 1991. Delisting criteria were 50 populations surviving three years, or 30 surviving five years.

**Downlisting criteria (five)**
1. Secure the four metapopulations at nine locations: San Rafael Valley, Sonoita Creek, Cienega Creek, and Bylas Springs Complex.
2. Secure two additional metapopulations and two additional populations if verified.
3. Replicate and secure natural populations within historic range.
4. Improve and maintain genetic diversity through an exchange protocol.
5. Develop and implement monitoring and genetic integrity assessment.

A viable population is defined as at least 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers, whichever is greater), with all age classes and evidence of reproduction. Replicated populations are considered established after persisting at least 10 years.

**Recovery tasks**
- **Task 1 (Protect):** Survey for undiscovered populations, acquire habitat (including a proposed 25-year cooperative agreement with private landowners), remove or bar mosquitofish, and develop management plans. Bullfrogs may also need control.
- **Task 2 (Re-establish):** Adopt a revised reintroduction protocol: replicate each native population in at least two refugia; stock with hydrographically nearby stock; manage reintroductions as large-scale field experiments; distribute large numbers (>500) across diverse habitats; and use Simons' four-category site classification system.
- **Task 3 (Monitor):** Standardize protocols, sample at least once per year (ideally twice, March to April and September to October), and designate AGFD as the data repository. Define criteria for extirpation, including repeated failed sampling (per the text, a number of consecutive samplings for natural and reintroduced populations) during June to November.
- **Task 4 (Genetics):** Build a computer model of population genetics, run mesocosm and field experiments using Arizona stocks and Mexican hybrids, and monitor allozyme diversity every five years.
- **Task 5 (Captive populations):** Maintain captive populations meeting the 500-adult viability threshold, reassess genetic diversity every five years, and encourage parallel plans in Mexico.
- **Task 6 (Life history):** Research minimum viable population size, temperature thresholds, oxygen requirements, flood tolerance, disease, and interactions with native fishes such as [[Desert Pupfish]].
- **Task 7 (Education):** Produce bilingual English and Spanish outreach materials and provide training in sampling and species identification.

**Priorities and costs.** Actions are ranked in three priority levels, from Priority 1 (prevent extinction) to Priority 3 (full recovery). The cost table (Section IV) is blank in this draft, so no dollar figures are reported.

**Reference:** The plan cites approximately 150 references, including foundational papers by Minckley, Meffe, Schoenherr, Constantz, and Vrijenhoek.
