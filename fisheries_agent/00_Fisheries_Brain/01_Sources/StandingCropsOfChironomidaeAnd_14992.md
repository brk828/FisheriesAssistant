# Standing crops of chironomidae and tubificidae in two desert reservoirs, Central Arizona

## Executive Summary

This study by Rinne (1978) quantifies seasonal and spatial variation in the density and biomass of two dominant benthic macroinvertebrate taxa—[[Chironomidae]] (dipteran larvae) and [[Tubificidae]] (oligochaete worms)—in **Roosevelt Lake** and **Apache Lake**, two desert reservoirs in the Salt River system of Central Arizona. Sampling using Ekman and Petersen dredges revealed low mean densities/biomasses overall, with clear seasonal peaks in spring, declines in summer, and partial recovery in autumn. A striking **disjunct (complementary) distribution** pattern was observed between the two taxa: where chironomids were abundant, tubificids were scarce, and vice versa. This spatial segregation is attributed to a combination of factors including hypolimnetic oxygen depletion, food supply (chlorophyll-a/phytoplankton availability), lake morphology, substrate composition, and potential predator-prey interactions between carnivorous chironomid genera and tubificid worms. The study contributes foundational benthic data supporting a broader limnology-fisheries research program on bathymetric fish distribution in these reservoirs.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: Part of a chain of Salt River reservoirs in the Upland [[Sonoran Desert]] (Jaeger, 1957; Dunbier, 1968), initiated in summer 1970 as a combined limnology-fisheries investigation (Rinne, 1973, 1974, 1975).
- **Prior knowledge gap**: Benthic organisms of the Salt River reservoir system were poorly studied prior to this work. Earlier reports covered only the oligochaete *Branchiura sowerbyi* (Cole, 1966) and the invasive Asiatic clam *Corbicula* sp. (Rinne, 1974).
- **Taxa examined**:
  - **[[Chironomidae]]**: 5 genera identified — *Cryptochironomus*, *Procladius*, *Tanypus*, *Chironomus*, *Polypedilum*. Three genera (*Cryptochironomus*, *Procladius*, *Tanypus*) belong to subfamilies considered carnivorous (Wirth & Stone, 1968); *Chironomus* is the most common genus and is a filter-feeding herbivore, closely tracking chlorophyll-a levels.
  - **[[Tubificidae]]**: 2 genera — *Branchiura* (specifically *B. sowerbyi*, an inhabitant of well-oxygenated waters) and *Limnodrilus* (dominant genus in Roosevelt and Apache Lakes, more tolerant of anaerobic/stagnant conditions).
- **Excluded taxa**: Crayfish (*Oroconectes*, *Procambarus*) and the Asiatic clam *Corbicula* also inhabit these reservoirs (rocky canyon slopes) but were not quantified in this study; *Corbicula* is reported elsewhere (Rinne, 1974).

### Sampling Methodology
- **Dredge comparison**: Sampling began with a 12-inch (900 cm²) **Petersen dredge** (43 kg; two grabs per station). In spring 1971, a lighter 6-inch (225 cm²) **Ekman dredge** (5 kg; six grabs per station) was introduced alongside Petersen sampling.
- **Key methodological finding**: Ekman-derived density estimates were consistently ~3x higher than Petersen estimates, likely because the heavy Petersen dredge created a pressure wave on descent and ejected surface ooze/organisms upon impact (per Brinkhurst, 1974). Consequently, **the Ekman dredge was used exclusively after spring 1971**.
- **Processing**: Samples sieved through a #40 Tyler screen (0.35 mm mesh); organisms identified/counted live in the field, preserved in 10% formalin, blotted dry, weighed (fresh weight), then oven-dried at 80°C for 12 hours to obtain dry biomass.
- **Statistical approach**: Analysis of variance for unequal sample sizes (Woolf, 1968) compared density/biomass estimates across stations, transects, and seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Substrate**: Predominantly soft, finely divided silt-clay with little debris or rock in sampled areas; uplake areas contained greater proportions of organic debris.
- **Lake morphology contrast**:
  - Roosevelt Lake: broader (b = 2.08 km), gentler littoral slope (S = 7.0), predominantly shallow (<15 m).
  - Apache Lake: narrower (b = 0.37 km), steeper slope (S = 32.0), mostly precipitous sides except transects A-2/A-3.
  - Roosevelt Lake was at only **half total storage capacity** throughout the study, exposing rocky terraces and reducing bottom heterogeneity.
- **Oxygen dynamics**: Severe hypolimnetic/bottom oxygen depletion strongly influenced taxon distribution:
  - Roosevelt Lake summer 1971: bottom dissolved oxygen as low as **0.4 mg/l** — chironomids absent at downlake transects (R-2, R-3), tubificids persisted.
  - Apache Lake summer 1971: dissolved oxygen **0.8 mg/l**, water temperature **28°C**, reservoir at only **15% of maximum volume** — chironomids absent, tubificid densities increased over spring levels.
  - Roosevelt Lake autumn 1971: despite favorable conditions (homothermy at 16°C, DO 7.0 mg/l), chironomids remained absent at R-2/R-3, suggesting delayed recovery from summer die-off.
- **Trophic linkages**: Chironomid abundance (especially *Chironomus*, a filter-feeding herbivore) closely tracked chlorophyll-a (phytoplankton) concentrations, which were highest at both ends of Roosevelt Lake. Tubificid numbers were inversely related to chlorophyll-a.
- **Predator-prey/competition hypothesis**: Carnivorous chironomid subfamilies (*Cryptochironomus*, *Procladius*, *Tanypus*) may suppress tubificid densities in shallow inflow areas, contributing to the observed disjunct distribution (see Loden, 1974; Howmiller, 1977).
- **Fish predation context**: Chironomids serve as a major food source for both mature and immature fishes in these reservoirs (Rinne, 1973). However, densities of dipteran larvae were consistently higher in shallow zones despite greater fish predation pressure there, suggesting substrate type and dissolved oxygen are more important distributional determinants than fish predation.
- **Comparative regional data**: Broader downlake regions of the adjoining, lower reservoirs in this Salt River chain — **Canyon Lake** and **Saguaro Lake** — showed higher total benthos biomass than narrower uplake areas (unpublished data), and *Branchiura sowerbyi* was more common in these lower, presumably better-oxygenated reservoirs.

## Key Findings

### Density Patterns (organisms/m²)

**Roosevelt Lake** (Table 1):
| Season | Density Range (Grand Avg) |
|---|---|
| Autumn 1970 (Petersen) | 210/m² (11–495) |
| Spring 1971 (Ekman) | 999/m² (up to 2,931 for tubificids) |
| Summer 1971 | 473/m² |
| Autumn 1971 | 554/m² |

**Apache Lake** (Table 2):
| Season | Density Range (Grand Avg) |
|---|---|
| Autumn 1970 | 144/m² |
| Spring 1971 | 488/m² (tubificids up to 2,758 at A-3) |
| Summer 1971 | 328/m² (only A-4, A-5 sampled; A-1–A-3 dry) |

- Densities were generally **low in summer, higher in autumn, and highest in spring** — attributed partly to unsynchronized local "hatches" of chironomid species.
- **Disjunct distribution**: In Roosevelt Lake autumn 1970, tubificids were common where chironomids were scarce and vice versa — a pattern persisting across seasons.
- Chironomids were consistently more abundant at the shallow uplake/end transects (R-1, R-4) of Roosevelt Lake; tubificids predominated in deeper, clay-silt downlake areas (R-2, R-3).
- In Apache Lake, both taxa were densest at the mid-lake transect A-3 in spring 1971 (chironomids up to 647/m², mean 122; tubificids up to 2,212/m², mean 1,347).
- Continuing water-level decline **precluded any Apache Lake sampling in autumn 1971** (reservoir largely dry at uplake transects by summer).

### Biomass Patterns (kg/ha dry weight)

**Roosevelt Lake**:
- Autumn 1970: chironomids 0.27–10.0 kg/ha; tubificids 0.15–7.83 kg/ha (paralleled density; nonsignificant, P > 0.05).
- Spring 1971: chironomid biomass variation greater (0.86–16.83 kg/ha), higher at uplake transects; significant differences among transects for both taxa (P < 0.05, DF 17).
- Summer/Autumn 1971: complementary biomass distribution between taxa persisted; tubificid biomass differences among transects were statistically significant (P < 0.05 summer; and highly so in autumn), chironomid differences were not.

**Apache Lake**:
- Autumn 1970: both taxa biomass low uplake, peaked at midlake, declined downlake.
- Tubificid mean biomass **more than doubled** from autumn 1970 to spring 1971; chironomid biomass **decreased** over the same interval.
- Tubificid biomass continued increasing from spring to summer 1971 (nonsignificant, P > 0.05, DF 72).

### Statistical Significance Summary
- Differences among **stations within a transect**: consistently nonsignificant (attributed to uniform depth, bottom type, DO, and temperature along transects, and reduced habitat heterogeneity from Roosevelt Lake's half-capacity water level).
- Differences among **transects**: significant more often in spring for chironomids (linked to asynchronous hatching); significant **year-round** for tubificids (possibly due to interspecific clumping/aggregation behavior, per Brinkhurst et al., 1972).

### Overall Ecological Conclusions
1. **Complementary/disjunct spatial distribution** between Chironomidae and Tubificidae is a persistent, ecologically meaningful pattern across both reservoirs and most seasons.
2. Distribution is governed by an interacting suite of factors: **hypolimnetic oxygen depletion**, **chlorophyll-a-linked food supply**, **substrate composition** (coarse debris vs. clay-silt), **lake morphology** (depth, slope, broadness), and possibly **predator-prey dynamics** between carnivorous chironomid genera and tubificids.
3. Despite Roosevelt Lake having greater mean benthic biomass than Apache Lake, both reservoirs' values are **much lower than typical for lakes of comparable type** (cf. Brinkhurst, 1974), consistent with the generally low productivity of these desert impoundments.
4. Reservoir morphology strongly shapes benthos abundance: broader, shallower, gently-sloped basins (Roosevelt-type) support denser benthic populations than narrow, steep-sided basins (most of Apache Lake), except where Apache locally resembles Roosevelt morphology (e.g., transect A-3).
5. These benthic standing crop patterns have direct implications for [[Bathymetric Fish Distribution]] in these reservoirs, since chironomids constitute a major dietary resource for reservoir fish populations.
