# Agricultural Development and Stream Invertebrates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[Thomas F. Waters]] compares benthic invertebrates and invertebrate drift in two naturally forested-or-unmodified streams and two streams whose upstream catchments were altered by [[Agricultural Drainage]] and [[Stream Channelization]] in [[Southwestern Minnesota]]. Four streams were sampled over a 21-month period. The modified streams showed no significant differences in physicochemical conditions, and total benthos abundance, biomass, and drift were broadly similar across all four. Differences that did occur were mainly tied to individual streams, particularly [[Cottonwood River]], rather than to the natural versus modified contrast. The authors conclude that local substrate and site-specific factors, not agricultural drainage, best explain most faunal differences.

## Background & Description

Agricultural practices often conflict with natural resource management. Channelization and drainage of small streams have been studied for effects on pools, substrate, and turbidity, and these effects can extend downstream. Small headwater and tributary streams received relatively little attention from fisheries researchers, even though they feed larger systems and may affect downstream biota. The authors note that the general effects of sediment on stream life were well documented, but the effects of headwater drainage on benthic invertebrates in natural downstream reaches were not.

The study compared two pairs of streams chosen for similar physicochemical characteristics:

- **Natural streams:** [[Highwater Creek]] and [[Cottonwood River]]
- **Modified streams:** [[Fort Ridgely Creek]] and [[Ramsey Creek]]

Modified streams had upstream channelization and drainage development, while the natural streams had largely undisturbed riparian vegetation. Riparian areas of all four streams were heavily vegetated with trees, shrubs, and ground cover. Study reaches were shallow (0.05–0.15 m) with rock and gravel riffles, pools, and sand substrates, and watersheds were predominantly cultivated (86–95%).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Benthos were collected with a 0.1 m² [[Surber Sampler]] fitted with 475-µm mesh, with rubble and substrate scrubbed and debris preserved in formalin. Drift samples were collected with nets in midstream. Invertebrates were identified mostly to genus, counted, and weighed (dry weight after removal of excess water). Sampling was irregular because high flows, ice, and drought periods precluded access to streams at times.

**Physicochemical conditions.** Turbidity, suspended solids, dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, total alkalinity, hardness, pH, water temperature, and discharge were measured. Mean discharge was highest at Highwater Creek, largely because site visits coincided with high runoff. Turbidity was positively correlated with discharge (r = 0.70). Suspended solids stayed low (0–300 mg/liter), peaking during high discharge.

**Drought.** All study streams except Cottonwood River went dry for 3–10 months during a severe [[Drought]] in 1976. Faunal abundances were severely depleted, but invertebrates reappeared rapidly once flow returned, apparently recolonizing from upstream pools, deeper substrate layers, and other sources.

**Statistical approach.** Comparisons used [[Analysis Of Variance]] (one-way and two-way), t-tests, and regression, with a [[Bonferroni Correction]] applied to limit spurious significant results from the large number of comparisons (up to 30).

## Key Findings

**1. Physicochemical conditions did not differ among streams.** No significant differences were found in physicochemical characteristics among the four streams, and upstream agricultural drainage development did not appear to alter the magnitude or timing of discharge or turbidity.

**2. Benthos and drift were broadly similar.** Over the 21-month study, mean abundance, biomass, and drift of total insects and total benthos were similar in all streams. Monthly comparisons showed few significant differences between individual streams and none between natural and modified pairs.

**3. Specific taxa differed.** The abundance and biomass of Trichoptera and Coleoptera, and the drift of Plecoptera, varied significantly among streams. Most measures were greater in the natural streams, although noninsect drift and plecopteran drift were higher in modified streams. Dipterans (mostly [[Chironomidae]]), which made up roughly 40–60% of the fauna, were similar across all streams.

**4. Key taxa drove the few natural-versus-modified differences.** Of 22 abundant invertebrates (taxa comprising more than 1% of the fauna, including 16 genera, two insect families, and four noninsects), only four differences separated natural from modified streams. Each was largely driven by one stream. The hydropsychid caddisflies *Hydropsyche bidens* and *H. frisoni* and the elmid beetles *Stenelmis* sp. and *Optioservus* sp. were much more abundant at Cottonwood River than elsewhere. The [[Hydropsychidae]] and [[Elmidae]] at Cottonwood River may reflect the rough, pockmarked surfaces of larger stones, which offer more net-building and clinging sites.

**5. Drift differences were driven by two taxa.** Drift was greater in modified streams for *Allocapnia* sp., *Stenonema* sp., *Tipula* sp., [[Hyalella Azteca]], and [[Physa Gyrina]]. Several differences were due to sampling timing or to catastrophic drift events (e.g., a November storm at Fort Ridgely). The amphipod *H. azteca* and snail *P. gyrina* drifted in high numbers at Fort Ridgely and Ramsey, probably because they inhabited upstream drainage-ditch pools absent from the natural streams. At Fort Ridgely, periphyton on rocks supported *P. gyrina* populations, and drift may have been linked to high population density and reproduction.

**6. Sediment and turbidity did not differ.** Downstream reaches of modified streams experienced silt and turbidity loads similar to the natural streams. Upstream sediment in the drainage systems was largely flushed out during spring runoff, and in-channel erosion was limited by soil conservation practices, especially maintained vegetation.

**7. Local factors matter more than land use.** The authors conclude that differences among invertebrate faunas were mainly due to local substrate and other site-specific factors rather than agricultural drainage. They also suggest that benthic fauna in agricultural areas with erodible soils and discrete spring runoff may be adapted to brief, high turbidity events.
