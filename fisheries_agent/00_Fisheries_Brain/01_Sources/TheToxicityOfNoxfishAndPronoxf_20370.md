# The Toxicity of Noxfish and Pro-Noxfish to Eggs of Common Carp and Fathead Minnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #toxicology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

Laboratory experiments conducted at [[Auburn University]] examined the toxicity of two rotenone-based formulations—[[Noxfish]] (5% rotenone) and [[Pro-Noxfish]] (2.5% rotenone plus 2.5% Sulfoxide synergist)—to eggs of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). The study addressed concerns about incomplete fish eradication during pond reclamation, investigating whether fish eggs could survive rotenone treatment and subsequently repopulate treated waters. Results demonstrated that both egg stages were highly susceptible to rotenone formulations at concentrations similar to those lethal to fingerling fish, with [[Noxfish]] proving approximately twice as toxic as [[Pro-Noxfish]] for both species.

## Background & Description

The research was motivated by repeated failures to completely eradicate fish populations from ponds and lakes using rotenone-containing formulations, as documented by multiple researchers (Krumholz 1948, 1950; Clemens and Martin 1953; Ball 1948). Fish frequently repopulated treated areas without additional stocking or evident invasion through water courses, leading to speculation that fish eggs might survive the toxic period and hatch after poison dissipation.

**Species Studied:**
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): Eggs collected from brood fish in small concrete ponds using excelsior spawning mats
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*): Eggs collected from masonite board spawning devices placed in brood ponds

**Test Formulations:**
- [[Noxfish]]: Emulsifiable formulation containing 5% rotenone
- [[Pro-Noxfish]]: Emulsifiable formulation containing 2.5% rotenone plus 2.5% Sulfoxide as synergist

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addressed a critical fisheries management problem: the survival of fish eggs during chemical pond reclamation. Leonard (1939) had previously reported that eyed brown trout eggs survived 0.50 p.p.m. derris concentration, though fry perished upon hatching. This raised questions about whether egg survival could explain repopulation of chemically treated waters.

**Experimental Conditions:**
- Carp eggs: 24-48 hours old at treatment, tested at approximately 75°F
- Fathead minnow eggs: Multiple age groups tested (less than 31 hours old; 101-120 hours old), at 70-76°F
- Test containers: Plastic dishes (3.5 inches diameter, 1.25 inches depth, approximately 200 ml volume)
- Ten eggs per container with 100 ml pond water
- No aeration provided
- Control containers showed 90% hatch success for both species

## Key Findings

**Carp Egg Toxicity (24-48 hours old, ~75°F):**
- LD₅₀ for [[Noxfish]]: 0.091 p.p.m.
- LD₅₀ for [[Pro-Noxfish]]: 0.178 p.p.m.
- Control containers: 90% hatched, 88% appeared normal
- Hatching began 28 hours after treatment, completed within 3 days
- Tests terminated at 7 days

**Fathead Minnow Egg Toxicity (less than 31 hours old, 70-76°F):**
- LD₅₀ for [[Noxfish]]: 0.142 p.p.m.
- LD₅₀ for [[Pro-Noxfish]]: 0.233 p.p.m.
- Control containers: 90% hatched (120 eggs total)
- Hatching began 4 days after treatment, completed by day 9

**Age-Dependent Susceptibility:**
Fathead minnow eggs treated at different developmental stages (12-31 hours old vs. 101-120 hours old) showed equal susceptibility to both poisons, indicating no protective effect of developmental stage.

**Comparative Toxicity (Eggs vs. Fingerlings):**

*Common Carp:*
- Noxfish: Eggs 0.091 p.p.m. vs. Fish (1.5-2.5 inches) 0.081 p.p.m.
- Pro-Noxfish: Eggs 0.178 p.p.m. vs. Fish 0.163 p.p.m.

*Fathead Minnow:*
- Noxfish: Eggs 0.142 p.p.m. vs. Fish (1.0-2.8 inches) 0.159 p.p.m.
- Pro-Noxfish: Eggs 0.233 p.p.m. vs. Fish 0.191 p.p.m.

**Key Conclusions:**
1. [[Noxfish]] consistently proved approximately twice as toxic as [[Pro-Noxfish]] for both species
2. Fish eggs showed similar susceptibility to rotenone formulations as fingerling fish of the same species
3. Egg developmental stage (early vs. late) did not significantly affect toxicity
4. Abnormalities (crooked vertebral columns, abnormal swimming) occurred in both control and treated containers
5. Some eggs appeared to hatch prematurely in response to toxicant exposure

**Management Implications:**
The finding that fish eggs are as susceptible to rotenone as fingerling fish suggests that egg survival is unlikely to explain repopulation of chemically treated ponds. The similar LD₅₀ values between eggs and fingerlings indicate that standard rotenone application rates for pond reclamation should be effective against all life stages.
