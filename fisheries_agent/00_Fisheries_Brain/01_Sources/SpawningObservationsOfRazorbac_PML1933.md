# Riverine Spawning of Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) in the Lower Colorado River, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Paul C. Marsh reports the first well-documented spawning of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*) in a riverine (lotic) setting. Although the species was historically common throughout the [[Colorado River Drainage]], it is now very rare. Spawning records had come almost entirely from [[Reservoir Populations]], and riverine spawning had been suspected but never confirmed by collecting eggs or larvae. In 1984, the author used [[SCUBA Survey]] and [[Surber Sampling]] in the [[Lower Colorado River]] below [[Hoover Dam]] to document spawning activity, substrate use, flow conditions and egg and larval densities. The author concludes that razorback suckers spawn in flowing water, even though the habitat is shaped by reservoir releases.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The razorback sucker was common across the Colorado River drainage but is now very rare. Recovery work is led by the [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] under a 1981 cooperative agreement.
- **Knowledge gap:** Information on riverine spawning was limited because the species is scarce, underwater work is logistically difficult, and water visibility is often poor.
- **Study setting:** The river below Hoover Dam runs clear because of [[Hypolimnetic Release]] from [[Lake Mead]]. Underwater visibility commonly exceeds 15 m (the OCR reading is uncertain). Razorback suckers are common in [[Lake Mohave]], which the author identifies as the species' last stronghold in the lower basin.
- **Prior observations:** Spawning was seen in 1983 and 1984 in the river 4.8 km below Hoover Dam. Later spawning was also found downstream of [[Davis Dam]]. The author notes that both areas are strongly influenced by mainstream reservoirs and are therefore not natural, though they have riverine characteristics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Field methods (1984):**
- Sampling dates: 28 January, 11 February, 25 February and 11 March 1984.
- Methods: [[SCUBA Survey]] for observations and depth and velocity profiles, and [[Surber Sampling]] for eggs and larvae.

**Spawning habitat:**
- Spawning was confined to a roughly 300 m² area of scoured sands and gravels. The material had been deposited from a dry wash.
- Water depth ranged from 1.2 to 2.0 m.
- Scuba observations showed that repeated spawning disturbed the substrate and produced shallow depressions. Spawning activity flushed finer material and left depressions lined with larger gravel and cobble.
- The author mapped 18 depressions. Their size is reported as roughly 0.25 to 1 m (the OCR reading is uncertain).
- Water velocity within depressions (measured with a [[McBirney Flow Meter]]) ranged from 0 to 0.37 m/s.
- Near-bottom ("nose") velocities at 10 cm above the substrate, measured at 5 spawning sites, averaged 0.15 m/s (range 0.12 to 0.18 m/s).

**Egg and larval drift:**
- Twenty-three [[Surber Sampling]] samples yielded 103 eggs and larvae in total.
- The January and February samples contained eggs and larvae, but none were taken directly from spawning substrate.
- The March sample yielded 21 larvae.
- Samples from within depressions (N=19) yielded about 10.5 to 15.7 eggs/m² and 0.7 to roughly 10 larvae/m² (OCR values partly unclear).
- One sample from a large cobble immediately downstream of a depression yielded 52 eggs (about 273 eggs/m²) and a high larval density. The author interprets this as evidence of strong [[Egg And Larval Drift]].
- Three samples from finer material outside depressions yielded no eggs or larvae.

**Spawning behavior:**
- Behavior resembled that reported for [[Reservoir Populations]], but with differences.
- Most fish observed were small males. They stayed in fixed positions at the downstream end of the site, where they were less mobile than reservoir males.
- Spawning groups of three to eight fish formed and spawned over depressions, or swam around the area before dispersing.
- Larger fish, presumed females, moved periodically into the spawning position. Groups occasionally rolled in mass for several seconds; the cause was not determined.
- On several occasions, fish sheltered downstream of large boulders while maintaining their positions.
- Spawning was oriented with the current, as in reservoir populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Riverine spawning is confirmed.** Razorback suckers spawned actively in flowing water below Hoover Dam in 1983 and 1984, and also below Davis Dam.
2. **Spawning sites are structured.** Repeated spawning created depressions in scoured sand and gravel. Depression-lined substrate with cobble and boulders provided habitat with high flow structure.
3. **Velocity and drift matter.** Spawning velocities averaged 0.15 m/s, and the densest egg and larval concentrations occurred in and immediately downstream of depressions, which indicates substantial drift.
4. **Behavioral differences exist.** Riverine spawning males were less mobile than reservoir males, and small males dominated the observed fish. Spawning groups of three to eight fish were typical.
5. **Habitat is artificial but informative.** The author argues that the reservoir-influenced sites are not natural, but they resemble the structural habitat used by razorback suckers in the Colorado River before dam regulation. The observations should therefore be considered valid.
6. **Literature is inconclusive.** The author notes that published information on the range of spawning habitats used by razorback suckers is far from conclusive.

**Cited literature:** About 15 references are cited, mainly on razorback sucker status and reproduction (for example, [[Minckley Status Review]] 1983, [[Mueller Habitat Selection]] 1982, [[Paulson Dredging Study]] 1980, [[Langhorst Early Life History]] 1985, and [[Bozek Reproductive Success]] 1984). The references also cover Lake Mead and Mohave investigations and early studies of the Colorado River fishes.
