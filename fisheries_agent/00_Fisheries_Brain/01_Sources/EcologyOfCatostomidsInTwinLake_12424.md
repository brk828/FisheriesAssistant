# Ecology of Catostomids in Twin Lakes, Colorado, in Relation to a Pumped-Storage Powerplant

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #catostomidae #pumped-storage #baseline-study

## Executive Summary

This baseline study documents the ecology of [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) and [[Longnose Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*) populations in [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado, prior to operation of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. Research conducted from 1971-1978 examined age and growth, food habits, reproductive biology, distribution, mortality, and interactions with [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*). Both sucker species exhibited slower growth and delayed maturity compared to populations elsewhere, likely due to cold temperatures and short growing seasons at this high-elevation (2802 m) oligotrophic reservoir system. White suckers lived longer (to age XVII) than longnose suckers (to age VII), with females of both species outliving males. [[Chironomid Larvae]] dominated diets across all size classes, supplemented by [[Fingernail Clams]] (*Pisidium* spp.) and [[Cladocera]]. Spawning occurred in June-July at water temperatures of 8.4-10.0°C, with no use of inlet streams. The study provides critical pre-operational data for assessing future powerplant impacts on these catostomid populations and the broader aquatic ecosystem.

## Background & Description

[[Twin Lakes]] consists of two connected natural lakes modified by a dam at the eastern end of the lower lake and a dredged channel between basins. At maximum elevation (2802 m above sea level), the lower lake covers approximately 737 ha with maximum depth of 27 m, while the upper lake encompasses 263 ha with 28 m maximum depth. The system is classified as oligotrophic, second-class dimictic, with ice cover from mid-December to mid-May.

**Physical Characteristics:**
- Shoreline substrate ranges from boulder-rubble to sand, with steep to gentle slopes
- Deep areas contain fine, loosely compacted glacial flour
- Littoral zones support aquatic vegetation (*Potamogeton* sp., *Chara* sp., *Nitella* sp.)
- Upper lake sediments contain substantial woody material from marsh erosion

**Biological Community:**
- Benthic fauna: Two [[Fingernail Clam]] species (*Pisidium casertanum*, *P. pauperculum*), three chironomid species (*Chironomus* sp., *Phaenopsectra* sp., *Dicrotendipes modestus*)
- [[Mysis relicta]] (opossum shrimp) stocked 1957, now abundant
- Zooplankton: Four copepod species (*Cyclops*, *Eucyclops*, *Diaptomus* spp.), three rotifer genera, two cladoceran species
- Fish community: White sucker (native), longnose sucker, [[Lake Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], stocked [[Rainbow Trout]], occasional [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Kokanee Salmon]]

**Study Methods:**
Experimental multifilament gill nets (38 m × 1.8 m, five 7.6-m panels, 19-45 mm bar mesh) were set monthly May-October 1978 at 12 stations, plus February-March ice sets. Vertical gill nets (3 m wide, 10-20 m deep, 6-32 mm mesh) sampled June-October for vertical distribution. Electrofishing targeted shoreline areas and smaller fish. Opercular bones provided age determination (superior to scales for older fish). Food habits analysis used pooled gut contents from fish grouped by species, size, lake, and month.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Twin Lakes]] represents a high-elevation, cold-water ecosystem with limited productivity. Benthic organism biomass in Upper Twin Lake during 1974-1975 was the lowest reported in literature, while Lower Twin Lake showed moderate biomass. The oligotrophic nature and cold temperatures (surface temperatures reaching only 15-17°C in summer) create a short growing season that constrains fish growth rates.

**Trophic Structure:**
The benthic-pelagic food web centers on chironomid larvae as the dominant macroinvertebrate, with [[Mysis relicta]] occupying a critical mid-water niche. Suckers function as benthic omnivores, consuming primarily chironomid larvae but also fingernail clams, cladocera, and occasionally [[Lake Trout]] eggs. Young suckers (<100 mm) feed more on planktonic cladocera and small chironomids, transitioning to larger benthic prey with growth.

**Habitat Partitioning:**
White and longnose suckers showed distinct distribution patterns. White suckers were more abundant in the lower lake with highest catches at stations 4, 5, and 7 (shallow to moderate depth). Longnose suckers appeared more concentrated in the upper lake. Vertical distribution data indicated white suckers frequently occupied depths less than 2 m from bottom during June-October, with 54% found 3-6 m from bottom in July. Seasonal movements occurred, with fish moving to deeper water in fall, possibly following chironomid prey or avoiding spawning lake trout.

**Temperature and Limnology:**
Water temperature stratification influenced fish distribution. During spawning (early to mid-June), temperatures ranged 8.4-10.0°C depending on depth. By late July, surface temperatures reached 17.1°C while 15 m depth remained at 10.1°C. The upper lake maintained temperatures 2-4°C colder than the lower lake due to [[Lake Creek]] inflow.

## Key Findings

**Age and Growth:**

*White Suckers:*
- Opercular radius-length relationship: TL = 0.3830R + 6.9531 (r = 0.981)
- Females longer than males at all ages; maximum difference at ages VII-IX (males averaged 93% of female length)
- Females lived to age XII+ (some to age XVII); males maximum age XI
- Growth rapid first year (72 mm), declining with age (annual increments: 46, 46, 41, 42, 36, 33, 27, 25, 14, 15, 8 mm for ages II-XII)
- Slower growth and greater longevity than most reported populations
- Sex ratio: 40% male, 60% female (significantly different, X² = 36.75)
- Maturity: females at age VII, males at age VI

*Longnose Suckers:*
- Opercular radius-length relationship: TL = 0.3265R + 1.9561 (r = 0.972)
- Females slightly longer-lived (age VII vs. age VI for males)
- Growth pattern similar between sexes until age IV, then male growth declined
- Annual growth increments: 83, 47, 41, 36, 39, 33, 25 mm for ages I-VII
- Sex ratio: 46% male, 54% female (not significantly different, X² = 1.96)
- Maturity: both sexes at age III (20% mature at age II, 91% by age IV)

**Food Habits:**

*Fish <100 mm:*
- White suckers: Cladocera most numerous, small chironomid larvae dominant by volume, debris and silt comprised major volume
- Longnose suckers: More cladocera than chironomid larvae by number, but chironomids dominant by volume (33% of diet was silt/sand/debris)
- Both species showed seasonal variation: cladocera peaked in summer, chironomid larvae in fall

*Fish 100-200 mm:*
- White suckers: Chironomid larvae 50.8% by number, 59.1% by mass; cladocera 32.5% by number but only 4.6% by mass; fingernail clams 4.6% number, 4.7% mass; silt 15.4% mass
- Longnose suckers: Chironomid larvae 49.5% by number, 69.0% by mass; cladocera 48.7% by number, 7.9% by mass; little plant material; silt/sand and debris 18.7% mass
- Upper lake fish relied more heavily on chironomid larvae; lower lake fish had more diverse diets
- TU diversity index higher for lower lake fish in all seasons

*Fish 200-350 mm:*
- White suckers: Chironomid larvae 75.3% by number, 63.8% by mass; fingernail clams and cladocera nearly equal in abundance; 10 animal food species utilized; silt and debris 24% total mass
- Longnose suckers: Chironomid larvae 80.2% by number, 55.8% by mass; cladocera second by number (3.4%), minimal by mass (0.8%); fingernail clams 7.2% number, 4.4% mass; silt 21.3% mass
- Seasonal patterns: chironomid pupae in spring only (emerged mid-June), cladocera peaked in summer, fingernail clams important in fall
- Upper lake fish showed lower diet diversity (TU index 0.166-0.088) vs. lower lake (0.514-0.570)

*Fish >350 mm (White Suckers only):*
- Chironomid larvae 74.6% by number, 61.3% by mass
- 13 different food species consumed
- Cladocera 21.8% by number, 2.2% by mass
- Fingernail clams 4.7% number, 3.4% mass
- Amphipods 0.6% number but 2.4% mass (larger prey items)
- [[Lake Trout]] eggs found in fall (66 eggs total, 13.9% of food mass for upper lake fish >350 mm)
- Silt and debris 27.6% total mass

**Reproductive Biology:**

*White Suckers:*
- Fecundity: 10,888-21,970 eggs for fish 316-441 mm (365-844 g)
- Eggs per gram body mass: 24-33 (no trend with fish size)
- Length-egg count relationship: log Y = 2.29 log L - 1.70 (r² = 0.95)
- Mass-egg count relationship: Y = 20.74M + 2700 (r² = 0.93)
- Spawning: mid-June (first ripe fish June 5; 80% females ripe/spent by June 14-18)
- No use of [[Lake Creek]] inlet for spawning
- Larvae first appeared July 12 (33 days post-spawning)
- Highest larval densities at stations A, F, G (sheltered areas with sand/gravel substrate)
- Spawning depth: 0.5-2.1 m based on larval distribution

*Longnose Suckers:*
- Fecundity: 2,361-6,750 eggs for fish 176-264 mm (49-181 g)
- Eggs per gram body mass: 41-76
- Length-egg count relationship: log Y = 1.71 log L - 0.292 (r² = 0.95)
- Mass-egg count relationship: Y = 26.80M + 2126 (r² = 0.91)
- Ovary mass as percent body mass increased with fish size
- Spawning: June-July, possibly extending to August (longer period than white suckers)
- 59% females spent by June, 97% males spent
- Some females may not spawn in consecutive years

**Distribution and Movements:**

*White Suckers:*
- Lower lake: highest catches stations 4, 5, 7 (spring); stations 5, 2 (summer); stations 1, 6, 7 (fall)
- Upper lake: stations 9, 11 highest; station 12 (deep water) consistently lowest
- Vertical distribution: majority within 2 m of bottom June-October (exception: July 54% at 3-6 m from bottom)
- Home range: marked fish remained within 800 m of capture point
- Nocturnal inshore movements evident (nets effective only at night)
- Lowest catches near powerplant tailrace (station 1) except in fall

*Longnose Suckers:*
- Higher density in upper lake based on gill net catch rates
- Spring: highest catches stations 1, 7 (lower lake), station 11 (upper lake)
- Summer: increased catches stations 2, 7 (lower lake), 9, 10 (upper lake)
- Fall: highest catch station 1 (powerplant area, lower lake), station 9 (upper lake)
- Not captured in vertical gill nets
- Shared similar habitats with white suckers <300 mm

**Mortality and Population:**

*White Suckers:*
- Instantaneous mortality rate: 0.533 (95% CI: 0.496-0.570) by Robson-Chapman method using age data
- Alternative estimate: 0.597 ± 0.013 by Ssentongo-Larkin method using length data (n=409)
- Population estimate (lower lake): 8,366 (95% CI: 4,942-27,248) by Schnabel method
- Wide confidence interval due to small sample size and low recapture rate
- Estimate likely conservative; represents primarily south side of lower lake

*Longnose Suckers:*
- Instantaneous mortality rate: 0.532 (95% CI: 0.492-0.573) by Ssentongo-Larkin method (n=173)
- Average length at first capture: 170 mm; average length in catch: 237 mm

**Sucker-Lake Trout Interactions:**

*Suckers as Prey:*
- 4 of ~150 lake trout stomachs contained 5 suckers (3 white, 2 unknown)
- Lake trout 411-543 mm consumed suckers
- Previous study (Griest 1974-1975): suckers in 6.2% of stomachs but 49.2% of food volume
- Suckers more important to large lake trout (>400 mm)
- Habitat overlap: both species in similar areas spring/fall; more segregated in summer (lake trout in deeper, cooler water)
- Large lake trout (>550 mm) invaded warmer water, increasing predation opportunity

*Lake Trout Egg Predation:*
- 66 lake trout eggs found in large white suckers (>350 mm) from upper lake in fall
- 6 of 13 white suckers caught over spawning areas contained eggs
- Eggs confirmed as lake trout (not brown trout) by electrophoretic analysis
- Predation considered minimal; few suckers captured over spawning beds
- Eggs eaten likely in accessible locations with reduced hatching probability

*Food Competition:*
- Minimal overlap: lake trout fed primarily on [[Mysis relicta]], suckers on chironomid larvae
- Greatest similarity: young lake trout and suckers both consumed chironomid larvae and pupae
- Competition unlikely: mysids abundant as alternative prey for lake trout; chironomids not limiting

**Pumped-Storage Powerplant Impact Assessment:**

*Direct Effects - Adult Entrainment:*
- Low susceptibility based on distribution: lowest white sucker catches at powerplant tailrace (station 1) except fall
- Home range fidelity reduces entrainment risk for fish in other areas
- Nocturnal inshore movements coincide with pumping mode (night), increasing risk
- No attraction to currents during spawning (no inlet stream use observed)
- Longnose suckers: higher catches near powerplant in fall; overall higher density in upper lake

*Direct Effects - Larval Entrainment:*
- Low risk: minimal spawning near powerplant based on larval surveys
- Larvae abundant mid-July to early November in shoreline areas
- Model tests indicate water enters tailrace along north shore during pumping
- Very low larval densities along north shore
- Vulnerability duration: 3.5 months in shallow water; depth selection after November unknown

*Indirect Effects - Food Organisms:*
- Chironomid larvae adaptable to various substrates; moderate turbidity unlikely to severely impact
- Fingernail clams (family Sphaeriidae) tolerant of moderate turbidity
- Severe siltation could impair clam feeding
- Zooplankton (cladocera, copepoda) vulnerable to entrainment by intake currents
- High turbidity could reduce phytoplankton production, affecting zooplankton food base

*Indirect Effects - Feeding:*
- Suckers feed by tactile sense; moderate turbidity/turbulence unlikely to limit feeding
- Small fish (<200 mm) more dependent on visual feeding, potentially more affected
- Possible "chumming effect" from entrained organisms creating detrital food source
- Food organism populations may remain stable in areas minimally affected by operations

*Indirect Effects - Reproduction:*
- No attraction to flowing water during spawning
- Minimal spawning near powerplant
- Egg/larval entrainment dependent on current velocities and intake volumes

*Indirect Effects - General:*
- Trophic dynamics may shift if mysid populations reduced by entrainment (increased sucker predation by lake trout)
- Growth rates could respond to multiple factors affected by powerplant
- Unknown impacts difficult to predict; long-term monitoring needed
- Baseline data enables future comparison to identify causative factors for any observed changes
