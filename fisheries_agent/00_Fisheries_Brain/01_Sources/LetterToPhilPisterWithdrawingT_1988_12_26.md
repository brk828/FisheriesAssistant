## Executive Summary

This document is **not a primary scientific data source** but an internal organizational letter from Dean A. Hendrickson (Native Fish Biologist) to Phil Pister of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], dated December 26, 1988. It concerns the withdrawal of a formal resolution addressing the **Virgin River renovation project** and an associated "accident" during that project. No morphological, physiological, or quantitative ecological data on fish species are presented in the text. Instead, the letter documents a governance dispute over how the Desert Fishes Council should officially respond to a native fish conservation action (a "renovation," i.e., a piscicide-based fish community manipulation) on the [[Virgin River]], a critical desert stream supporting endangered native cyprinids in the Colorado River Basin.

No morphological, meristic, or anatomical data are provided in this source. The letter is purely administrative/political in nature. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The [[Virgin River]] is a warm-water, turbid, desert tributary of the Colorado River system, historically home to a distinct assemblage of native, often endemic, fish species adapted to fluctuating flow, high sediment load, and elevated temperatures.
- **Renovation Project**: The letter references a "Virgin River renovation project" — a term in desert fish conservation typically denoting active management interventions (e.g., piscicide treatment such as rotenone) intended to remove nonnative competitors/predators and restore habitat conditions favorable to native, often imperiled, species.
- **Ecological Concern**: The letter references a "resultant accident" tied to this renovation project, implying an unintended negative ecological consequence (e.g., unintended mortality of native fish, chemical spill, or incomplete containment during treatment) that motivated the original resolution before the [[Desert Fishes Council]].
- **Stakeholders**: The controversy involved multiple parties, including project directors referred to as "Denise and Don," and prominent desert fish biologists [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[Jim Deacon]], indicating broader scientific community concern over management practices affecting native desert fish populations.
