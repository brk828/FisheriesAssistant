## Executive Summary

This 1995 report to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation synthesizes literature on the swimming and leaping capabilities of non-native fishes threatening [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona — one of the last Arizona streams retaining an intact native fish assemblage (seven indigenous taxa). The work was commissioned to inform the design of **Exotic Fish Exclusion Structures (EFES)**, physical barriers mandated by a 1994 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion to prevent upstream invasion of non-native fishes via the Central Arizona Project canal system, which interconnects the Colorado River and Gila River basins. The Opinion identified jeopardy to four native/listed species — [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] — and mandated EFES construction on Aravaipa Creek and the San Pedro River. The authors conducted a literature review of swimming speed, leaping ability, and barrier effectiveness for seven target non-native species, concluding that data are insufficient to specify precise EFES engineering criteria but sufficient to establish general design principles emphasizing laminar flow, minimal turbulence, and shallow, non-impounded downstream conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Target non-native taxa** examined for swimming/leaping data: [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Rudd]] (*Scardinius erythropthalmus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*). Species-specific swimming/leaping data were located for five of these (marked with asterisks in source: carp, rudd, channel catfish, green sunfish, smallmouth bass).
- Based on body-form classification (Videler 1993), **none of the seven selected species is morphologically specialized for high-speed swimming** (i.e., none possess a fusiform, scombroid-type body optimized for sustained rapid locomotion).
- Maximum recorded freshwater burst speed among species considered: **~8.2 ft/sec (5.6 mph)** for a smallmouth bass of 24 cm total length (Webb 1983) — markedly lower than the >91 ft/sec (62 mph) recorded for the epipelagic marine [[Swordfish]] (*Xiphias gladius*), included as a comparative benchmark.
- Body size strongly correlates with swimming and leaping performance: **larger fish swim faster and leap higher than smaller conspecifics or related taxa**.
- Leaping performance is body-size dependent even at small scale: green sunfish as small as 3.4 in (TL) were captured leaping to heights of ~30 inches above a pond surface (Ellis 1974).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Aravaipa Creek** is a rare, relatively unmodified Sonoran Desert stream supporting seven indigenous fish taxa — more than any comparable Arizona habitat — attributable to an intact watershed/channel and general historical absence of non-native predators/competitors.
- The system is hydrologically connected to invasion risk via the **Central Arizona Project (CAP)**, an artificial canal linking the Colorado River mainstem to the Gila River system, including the lower San Pedro River (into which Aravaipa Creek drains), creating a corridor for non-native fish immigration.
- Non-native species of concern (carp, red shiner, rudd, channel and flathead catfish, green sunfish, smallmouth bass) represent a range of ecological guilds (benthic omnivores, generalist predators, competitors) that could displace or predate upon native and federally protected taxa such as [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Analogous invasion case studies** cited include:
  - [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) overland dispersal via sheet flow during rainstorms and ascent of inclines >3.0 ft in height, defeating multiple EFES (1.5–30 ft tall) at San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge over a 7-year period, threatening [[Yaqui Topminnow]].
  - Analogous mosquitofish/Gila topminnow interactions at Bylas Springs, Arizona (Marsh & Minckley 1990), including human-assisted transfer above barriers.
  - Failure of a ~10-ft log barrier on Ord Creek, AZ to exclude [[Rainbow Trout]] from areas managed for native trout, again potentially confounded by human intervention.
  - Natural vertical falls (~6.5 ft) effectively excluding non-native trout from habitats of endemic [[Gila Trout]] in New Mexico (McKenna and Spruce creeks).
  - Sea lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*) exclusion by barriers as low as ~1 foot in Great Lakes tributaries.
- Landowner and aesthetic opposition to impoundment-forming barrier designs constrains EFES options, since impounded "pool habitat" upstream would itself favor establishment of non-native fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Swimming speed categories** (per Beamish 1978): burst speed (<20 sec), prolonged speed (20 sec–200 min), sustained speed (>200 min). Burst speed is identified as most relevant to EFES design/passage scenarios.
- Reported swimming-speed values are considered **conservative estimates**, since body size, water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pollution, conditioning, and individual variation all influence performance (Beamish 1978; Kolok & Farrell 1994); most underlying studies were laboratory-based using small-to-moderate-sized fish, limiting field applicability.
- **Leaping ability** is highly variable across taxa and is assisted by vertical drops and turbulence: salmonid parr exploit kinetic energy from submerged waves below waterfalls to gain lift (Stuart 1962); reported upper leaping limit for salmon ~5.9 ft (Stringham 1924).
- **Barrier height thresholds** from prior studies:
  - ~1 ft sufficient to block sea lamprey.
  - ~6.5 ft natural falls excluded non-native trout in NM streams.
  - Dams >6.0 ft effectively blocked most fish passage in a 30-site Tennessee survey of old mill dams (4–30 ft range).
  - ≥8 ft height thought necessary to preclude upstream common carp movement (Ruhr 1957).
  - An 11.3-ft, ~230-ft-long, ~50-ft-thick "apron type" barrier on Roaring River, TN (Cordell Hull Reservoir) prevented upstream passage of seven reservoir fish species (including common carp) over 1.5 years, even during flows up to >4350 cfs (Bulow et al. 1988), though it created an undesirable ~1-mile silt-laden upstream pool.
- **Human intervention** (manual transport of fish above structures) repeatedly compromised barrier effectiveness in Southwest case studies (mosquitofish at San Bernardino NWR and Bylas Springs; rainbow trout at Ord Creek) — recommended countermeasure: public education.
- **Design recommendations for Aravaipa Creek EFES**:
  1. Keep downstream water as shallow as possible; avoid creating downstream pool habitat.
  2. Use a relatively expansive, minimally turbulent apron with **laminar flow**, avoiding any low-velocity or turbulent refuge zones that fish could use to rest or "launch" leaps.
  3. Curved (rather than angular/cornered) surfaces at apron-channel and apron-vertical junctions may be needed to maintain laminarity.
  4. Laminar high-velocity flow over the structure imposes drag forces up to **5× greater** than fish experience swimming below the surface (Videler 1993), which should limit upstream passage capability.
  5. A structure effective at low-to-moderate discharge (~<300–500 cfs) and on the descending limb of flood hydrographs is likely sufficient; at very high discharges (>1000–5000 cfs), upstream movement through constrained channel reaches is expected to be naturally precluded or limited regardless of structure.
  6. No design was identified in the literature that is simultaneously fully environmentally acceptable (non-impounding) and fully effective at excluding fish at all discharge levels — a fundamental design trade-off.
- The report concludes that available literature offers only general guidance, not sufficient quantitative design criteria, and recommends collaborative design work between Reclamation engineers/modelers and fisheries biologists.
