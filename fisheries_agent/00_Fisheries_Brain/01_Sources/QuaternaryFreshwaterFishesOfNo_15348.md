# Quaternary Freshwater Fishes of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoichthyology #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Robert Rush Miller]] of the University of Michigan, reviews the origin, geologic range and distribution of North American freshwater fishes, with emphasis on late-Pliocene and Pleistocene fossil records. The freshwater fauna is largely Cenozoic in origin, apart from a few ancient relicts such as sturgeons, paddlefish, gars and bowfin. The paper summarizes fossil sites, presents a first tabular summary of geologic ranges, and discusses the [[Idaho Lake Fauna]], the zoogeographic history of the [[Snake River]], lacustrine speciation of whitefishes in [[Bear Lake]], and the limited opportunity to detect faunal shifts because few Pleistocene sites are diversified. The author stresses that paleoichthyology needs closer collaboration with neontologists, geologists and paleontologists.

## Background & Description

The North American freshwater fauna contains roughly 940 species. It is divided into two groups:

- **Primary fishes** (strictly freshwater): about 90 genera and approximately 500 species, including undescribed species. Seven endemic families are recognized: Amiidae, Hiodontidae, Ictaluridae, Amblyopsidae, Percopsidae, Aphredoderidae and Centrarchidae.
- **Secondary fishes** (diadromous, salt-tolerant, marine-derived or sporadic): 25 families, about 100 genera and approximately 400 species, including [[Lepisosteidae]] (gars), [[Goodeidae]], [[Poeciliidae]], [[Cyprinodontidae]] and [[Cichlidae]].

Fishes long restricted to fresh water disperse mainly with slow physiographic changes of the land. Most freshwater fossil material is rare because of small body size, habitat preferences and the chance nature of preservation, so the fossil record helps little for many groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pleistocene climate change strongly shaped the fauna. A progressive trend toward aridity and seasonality caused extinctions and range contraction, especially in the arid [[Great Basin]], which is dominated by relict species and populations. Desiccation of streams and lakes destroyed habitat, and glacial cooling eliminated species whose spawning requirements could not adjust to new thermal regimes. Glaciation may also have promoted speciation in cold-adapted groups such as salmonids and sculpins (*Cottus*).

Faunal assemblages provide paleoclimate evidence. The Illinoian Berends fauna of Oklahoma (8 families, 9 genera, 12 species) suggests a cooler, moister climate. The yellow perch, whose natural range lies several hundred miles north of northern Oklahoma, marks the southern limit of that assemblage. The Mt. Scott fauna of southwestern Kansas (14 species, 7 families) indicates a more extensive aquatic habitat and cooler, wetter conditions similar to modern southern Wisconsin.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size and age:** Fifteen families are represented by 49 genera, which comprise 95 species (5 in four extinct genera). The 45 living genera contain about 90 known fossil species and 418 living species. Nearly half of the fossil species (41) are extinct. About 60% of the living fauna may be no older than late Miocene or early Pliocene.
- **Pleistocene record:** The Pleistocene data set comprises 14 families, 41 genera and 69 species of living genera. Most post-Nebraskan fossils are osteologically indistinguishable from living species. Only a few sites (sites 11, 14–17, 25–32 and 34 on Figure 1) are diversified enough to represent true faunas.
- **Families:** [[Cyprinidae]] has the most fossil species (24), and [[Catostomidae]] is the second most abundant group. Ten of the 14 sucker species occur in the western United States, mostly in the Idaho Lake fauna.
- **Idaho Lake fauna:** This Plio-Pleistocene lake (southern Idaho and adjacent Oregon) contained 6 families, 15 genera and at least 20 species. Several minnows, including *Mylocyprinus*, *Sigmopharyngodon* and *Diastichus*, went extinct when the lake drained, probably because of loss of lacustrine habitat and its molluscan food supply.
- **Paleohydrology:** The distribution of the sucker *Chasmistes*, which survives today at six localities, and of *Mylopharodon* and *Archoplites* suggests a former Snake River connection to the Pacific, possibly through the Klamath basin. The "Fishhook Pattern" of *Chasmistes* distribution suggests that the disjunct populations were formerly linked by rivers and lakes.
- **Bear Lake speciation:** Bear Lake contains four endemic species: three whitefishes (*Prosopium gemmiferum*, *P. spilonotus*, *P. abyssicola*) and the Bear Lake sculpin (*Cottus extensus*). The species coexist through differences in feeding, spawning time and place, and behaviour. Bright's (1963) reconstruction gives three opportunities for fish colonization, from the Snake River, Lake Bonneville, and after Bonneville overflowed.
- **Salmonids:** The first fossil record of *Prosopium* comes from the late-Wisconsin Bonneville terrace near Salt Lake City. The extinct *Salmo copei* from Plio-Pleistocene lake beds in southern Idaho may be close to the basal ancestor of Salmoninae.
- **Future research:** Priorities include revision of fossil nomenclature, new fossil sites, comparative osteology of living fishes, and ecological and life-history studies of extant species.
