# Status and Distribution of the Freshwater Fishes of Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzontidae #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Cottidae #Gasterosteidae #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in the Bulletin of the [[Southern California Academy of Sciences]] (vol. 92, no. 3, December 1993), reviews the status and distribution of the native and introduced freshwater fishes of southern California, from the Carmel River to the Mexican border, plus the California portion of the [[Colorado River]] drainage. The authors ([[Camm C. Swift]], [[Thomas R. Haglund]], [[Mario Ruiz]] and [[Robert N. Fisher]]) recognize 38 native freshwater taxa, many of which are endemic, extirpated, or in jeopardy. They document more than 100 non-native species now established in the region, which they describe as the highest number of established aliens of any area in the state. The paper concludes that the native fauna faces accelerating habitat loss driven by urbanization, flood control, water diversion, drought and introduced species, and recommends a set of conservation priorities.

## Background & Description

The region contains a sharp environmental contrast: wet, Mediterranean-climate coastal drainages versus arid inland basins (Mohave, Death Valley, Salton Sea, Colorado River). Coastal streams are steep and rocky, with a few lowland reaches, while inland systems are dominated by desert springs and terminal lakes. The native fish fauna is a depauperate southwestern extreme of a temperate western North American assemblage.

Freshwater taxa were defined to include species that spend all or a distinct part of their life cycle in fresh water, including primary, secondary and diadromous categories. Estuarine taxa were included only where they depend on lowered salinity. Records came from museum collections (chiefly the [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]]), [[California Department of Fish and Game]] files, and the [[U.S. Forest Service]].

Taxonomy follows [[Moyle and Williams]] (1990), with common and scientific names from Robins et al. (1991). Conservation status draws on [[Williams et al.]] (1989) and related sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geologic history:** In the late Miocene and early Pliocene, the region had a flatter topography, westward-draining rivers and large lakes. Tectonic compression, linked to the [[Pacific Plate]] subducting beneath the [[North American Plate]], uplifted the mountains and redirected drainages. Modern streams align with major faults, which force groundwater to the surface as springs.
- **Climate and hydrology:** Coastal drainages were historically well watered. Human population growth, channelization, water-table lowering, logging, flood control and water storage, plus the drought of 1986 onward, have converged coastal habitats toward the hydrology of the inland deserts.
- **Community structure:** The authors define four freshwater fish communities: lowland (low gradient), upland (high gradient), anadromous, and coastal lagoon. A foothill zone is poorly developed because steep mountains meet alluvial plains abruptly.
- **Lagoons:** Historically brackish or fresh and cooler than the ocean in summer. Most have been replaced by tidally maintained estuaries, and only a few small systems remain relatively unchanged (for example, [[Santa Margarita Lagoon]] and [[Morro Bay]]).
- **Lower Colorado River and Salton Sea:** Native fishes there have been extirpated or very rare since the 1950s. Desert pupfish persist in canals around the Salton Sea, and tilapias and sailfin mollies dominate warm waters.
- **Introduced species:** Dominant introduced stream fishes include [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Red Shiner]] and [[Sailfin Molly]]. Reservoir assemblages depend on continual stocking and include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Golden Shiner]].

## Key Findings

**Native fauna and status**
- 38 native freshwater taxa are recognized in the region. Only the Los Angeles Basin contains endemic freshwater fishes: *Rhinichthys osculus* (Santa Ana speckled dace), *Gila orcutti* (arroyo chub), *Catostomus santaanae* (Santa Ana sucker), *Lampetra* cf. *pacifica*, and one or two threespine stickleback taxa.
- Several taxa are extinct or extirpated in California, including the Pacific brook lamprey (last taken 1955 in the Santa Ana River) and the unarmored threespine stickleback *G. a. williamsoni* from the Los Angeles Basin (last records 1950s).
- Of the Colorado River natives, most have been extirpated, including the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- Steelhead and Pacific lamprey are still anadromous in several coastal streams, and the endangered [[Tidewater Goby]] (*Eucyclogobius newberryi*) survives at about 20 coastal localities (approximately 29 localities listed).

**Introduced fauna**
- More than 100 non-native species are recorded from southern California, the highest number of established aliens of any state area.
- Non-native fishes were introduced from eastern North America, the Central Valley, the Colorado River and Gulf of California stocks, often without documented sources.
- Introduced species include [[Rainbow Trout]] (planted since the 1890s), [[Brown Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Grass Carp]] and [[Tilapia]] (several species).

**Conservation priorities**
- Protect remaining coastal and interior habitats that still hold native elements, and rehabilitate some unsuitable areas.
- Prioritize existing habitat of the unarmored and partially armored sticklebacks, including the federally endangered form in the upper Santa Clara River, the Shay Creek stickleback and the San Antonio Creek stickleback.
- Maintain lagoons, spawning gravels and migratory corridors for anadromous fishes.
- Recognize that the authors propose 100 Aquatic Diversity Management Areas (ADMAs) for the southern California region, biased toward sites with native fishes.
- Emphasize that non-native genetic additions and introduced predators, competitors and hybridization are the principal threats to native integrity, and that protection must be implemented immediately.
