## Executive Summary
This short technical note from the New York State Conservation Department describes an inexpensive, practical method for individually identifying laboratory-held [[Common Carp]] during chemical toxicity and physiological experiments. Researchers at the Fish Laboratory at Livingston Manor, New York, faced the challenge of distinguishing among up to 18 fish housed together in large aquaria while recording rapid observations on chemical effects. The solution adopted was a repurposed commercial laundry tag — a spring steel clip with attached water-resistant colored paper — which could be quickly affixed to various external locations on the fish (fin, mouth, gill cover). The method proved cost-effective and time-saving, despite some minor drawbacks related to tag retention and fish irritation.

## Background & Description
- **Tagging apparatus**: A steel spring clip fitted with a tab of water-resistant colored paper (commercial "laundry tag" design).
- **Attachment sites on fish**: Fin, mouth, and gill cover were the primary anatomical locations used for clip placement, chosen for accessibility and minimal handling time.
- **Customization options**: Tags were available in a dozen distinct colors; further individual differentiation was achieved by:
  - Cutting the paper into different shapes
  - Adding pencil notations directly on the tag
  - Varying the anatomical placement location on the fish
- No modification to the fish's morphology was intended beyond the temporary external clip attachment; this is a marking tool rather than a permanent anatomical tag (e.g., not a fin clip or internal tag).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
This note does not describe a natural ecological niche, as the subject fish ([[Common Carp]]) were held in an artificial laboratory setting rather than a wild habitat. Context relevant to the experimental environment:
- **Housing density**: Up to 18 carp per large aquarium tank.
- **Institutional setting**: [[Fish Laboratory at Livingston Manor]], operated by the New York State Conservation Department.
- **Research purpose**: The tagging system supported experiments examining **the effects of chemicals on individual fish**, indicating a toxicological or physiological research context rather than a study of natural population ecology.

## Key Findings
- **Practical efficacy**: Laundry tags with steel spring clips proved effective for quickly marking and re-identifying individual carp in a multi-fish laboratory tank setting.
- **Speed advantage**: The tagging method allowed for **quick notation** of chemical effects on specific individuals, significantly improving experimental workflow.
- **Cost efficiency**: The tags were extremely inexpensive, costing **less than $3.00 per thousand units**, while yielding considerable savings in man-hours of labor.
- **Limitations identified**:
  - Tags could **fall off after several days**, reducing long-term reliability.
  - Attachment **always caused some degree of irritation** to the fish, though this did not appear to compromise the validity of the experiments.
- **Versatility**: The combination of 12 available colors, adjustable paper cutting, pencil notation, and variable placement location provided a flexible, low-cost system for identifying large numbers of individual fish without need for more invasive or expensive tagging technology.
- **Relation to broader tagging literature**: This method contrasts with more elaborate fish-handling and tagging devices discussed elsewhere in the same journal issue (e.g., pneumatic fish-holding devices for large-scale field tagging operations, as referenced in [[Salmon Stripper, Egg Counter And Incubator]] by Lindroth, 1956), highlighting a low-tech solution suited specifically to controlled laboratory conditions rather than field tagging of wild fish stocks.
