# The Salamanders of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by Charles H. Lowe, Jr. (University of Arizona) clarifies the taxonomy and distribution of the [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) in Arizona, a group previously in uncertain status. The author recognizes three subspecies, supported by approximately 30 verifiable localities: [[Arizona Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. nebulosum*, 21 localities), [[Utah Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. utahense*, 8), and [[Sonora Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. stebbinsi*, 1). The paper restores the name *nebulosum* to the yellow-spotted form of central Arizona, names the northern dark-spotted form *utahense*, and describes the southern form *stebbinsi* in its earlier 1954 publication. It also proposes standardized common names, provides a dichotomous key, and reports the absence of known plethodontid salamanders in Arizona. The author urges further study of the [[Colorado River Basin]] because of planned water development and the growing bait trade in live larvae.

The excerpt also includes a second, unrelated article on Rorschach color responses in children (Duncan), which is psychological rather than aquatic and is summarized only briefly below.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** Earlier work by Dunn (1940), Bishop (1943), Stebbins (1951), and Reed (1951) left Arizona populations poorly resolved. Dunn misapplied the name *nebulosum* to the dark-backed northern populations, contradicting Hallowell's (1852) original description of a conspicuously yellow-spotted salamander from San Francisco Mountain. Lowe concludes that the name *nebulosum* belongs to the central Arizona form. The northern form is left without a valid name, and Hallowell's 1857 name *maculatum* is treated as a probable synonym of it, but it is a secondary homonym of *Ambystoma maculatum* Shaw, 1802.

**Diagnostic characters.** Lowe emphasizes color pattern over structural traits, following Cope (1867) and Dunn (1940). Subspecies are reported as distinctive in 100% of individuals examined, with the exception of possible intergrades.

- *A. t. nebulosum*: Blackish-brown upper surfaces with 10–25 yellow spots or blotches on the dorsum and 10–35 on the tail; belly boldly patterned with yellow.
- *A. t. utahense*: Gray, brownish, or blackish-brown upper surfaces, with darker or no definite markings; not yellow-spotted. Holotype (MVZ 29481) collected May 1935 at Lapoint, Uintah County, Utah.
- *A. t. stebbinsi*: Black or nearly black upper surfaces with numerous small golden-brown to yellowish spots; 25–45 dorsal spots and 35–60 tail spots; belly brownish, not boldly patterned. Holotype collected November 4, 1950, at John A. Jones Ranch, Parker Canyon, south side of the Huachuca Mountains, Santa Cruz County, at about 5,000 ft.
- *A. t. mavortium* (New Mexico): Used for comparison; yellow-spotted with fewer dorsal spots than *stebbinsi*.

**Type material.** The *nebulosum* holotype (USNM 4702) is now darkened, but roughly 14–15 tail spots are still visible. Stejneger's (1890) topotype (USNM 16179), a female of 78 mm snout–vent length, has 14 tail spots and 24 dorsal spots.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat.** Specimens from the type locality and nearby sites came from Yellow Pine Forest. The northern *utahense* was collected from forest ponds and rim areas of the Grand Canyon, at about 7,000 ft.
- **Drainage patterns.** *A. t. nebulosum* is largely confined to the Little Colorado and Gila Rivers, tributaries of the Colorado River. *A. t. utahense* occupies drainages flowing directly into the Colorado, such as the San Juan River and Tapeats Creek. *A. t. stebbinsi* is the only subspecies in the Gila drainage south of the river, and it is restricted to Parker Canyon, which drains into the Santa Cruz River basin.
- **Range gaps.** No populations are known from the Rio Yaqui or Rio de la Concepción basins, which drain into the Gulf of California.
- **Plethodontids.** No plethodontid salamanders have been found in Arizona, despite suitable high-elevation habitat. Lowe attributes this to sampling timing and patchy relictual distributions. He expects them at roughly 8,000 ft and above in the north and 8,500–9,000 ft and above in the south, in fir and spruce forest.
- **Human pressures.** Planned Department of the Interior water projects and a growing commercial "water-dog" bait trade are moving larvae between localities. This is contaminating local breeding populations and spreading to several parts of the state.

## Key Findings

1. **Three subspecies in Arizona:** Approximately 30 localities (nebulosum 21, utahense 8, stebbinsi 1), with subspecies distinct in all but intergrade individuals.
2. **Nomenclatural correction:** The name *nebulosum* is restricted to the central Arizona yellow-spotted form. The northern dark form is treated as a single subspecies, *utahense*, pending further study.
3. **Sympatry or intergradation:** Pond populations on the south rim of the Grand Canyon include both *nebulosum*-like and *utahense*-like individuals. Their status (sympatry, hybridization, or intergradation) is unresolved and is marked with a question mark on the distribution map.
4. **Possible species split:** The light-spotted southern group (*nebulosum*, *stebbinsi*, *californiense*) and the dark-spotted northern group (*utahense*, *melanostictum*, *diaboli*) may represent two species rather than one.
5. **Proposed common names:** The paper proposes standardized vernacular names for nine U.S. taxa, including Arizona Tiger Salamander, Utah Tiger Salamander, and Sonora Tiger Salamander.
6. **Dichotomous key:** Separates the three Arizona subspecies by upper-surface coloration, dorsal spot counts, and tail spot counts.
7. **Conservation priority:** Recommends fresh, well-preserved specimens, especially from Navajo and Apache Counties and from New Mexico and adjacent Mexico, and calls for a basin-wide study of the Colorado River Basin.
8. **Plethodontid survey gap:** Suggests that absence of plethodontids in Arizona reflects inadequate sampling rather than true absence.

**Unrelated excerpt:** The second article, by Bertha K. Duncan (Kansas State Receiving Home for Children), discusses ego functions and color responses on the Rorschach test in disturbed and institutionalized children. It contains no aquatic or ichthyological data.
