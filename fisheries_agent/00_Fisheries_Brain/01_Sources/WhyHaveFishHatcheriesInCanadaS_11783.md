# Why Have Fish Hatcheries in Canada's National Parks?

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the Seventeenth North American Wildlife Conference, documents the historical rationale, operational evolution, and management value of fish hatcheries in Canada's Mountain National Parks (Banff, Jasper, and Waterton Lakes). Established beginning in 1913, these hatcheries stocked ~40 million trout (eggs, fry, fingerlings) between 1913–1943, transitioning after 1944 to rearing yearling and 2-year-old fish for more targeted stocking. The core scientific contribution of the paper is a fin-marking and recapture study (creel census plus test netting) conducted primarily in [[Jasper National Park]] from 1943–1952, quantifying recapture rates, survival, and growth of stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Eastern Brook Trout]]. The study demonstrates that hatchery stocking is essential in high-elevation, historically fish-free lakes with inadequate natural spawning habitat, and introduces experimental interspecific hybridization (Eastern Brook Trout × Lake Trout, i.e., splake) as a management tool suited to environments unfavorable to either parent species.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily a fisheries management study rather than a morphological survey, several size/age-class and growth metrics are reported that bear on morphology and life-history staging:

- **Age-class terminology used in stocking:** Fingerlings (F, including "advanced" or "number five" fingerlings), Yearlings (Y), and 2-year-olds (2Y).
- **Growth data** show substantial variability in weight gain depending on species, lake, and planting timing:
  - [[Eastern Brook Trout]] yearlings in Beauvert Lake: 0.6 oz → 1.7 oz gain over 16 weeks.
  - Eastern Brook Trout yearlings in Trefoil Lake: gained 10.4 oz over 17 weeks (May–September).
  - Advanced fingerlings (0.11 oz) in Trefoil Lake grew ~90-fold in weight (to 2.8–8.9 oz) within 37–49 weeks, including winter months — evidence of **under-ice winter growth**, contrary to the general assumption that freshwater fish cease growth in cold winter months at northern latitudes.
  - 2-year-old Eastern Brook Trout in Trefoil Lake: 5.8 oz → 88.0 oz over 46 weeks (dramatic growth).
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] in Edith Lake: 6.0 oz → 18.0 oz over 48–56 weeks.
- **Longevity/size record:** Two [[Lake Trout]] planted as yearlings in 1943 in Pyramid Lake were recaptured in 1951 (8 years post-stocking) at lengths of **17 and 20 inches**.
- **Hybrid morphology/behavior (Splake — Eastern Brook Trout × Lake Trout):** Described as growing faster and being better acclimatized to cold water than Eastern Brook Trout, and more readily taken on a fly than Lake Trout — an intermediate phenotype combining favorable traits of both parents. Spawns on gravel bottoms in late October, similar to Eastern Brook Trout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish fauna** of the Mountain National Parks prior to hatchery intervention: [[Lake Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Dolly Varden]], and [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]], restricted to main stream systems and a few connected lakes.
- **~120 previously fish-free ("virgin") high-elevation lakes** across Waterton, Banff, Yoho, Kootenay, Glacier, Mount Revelstoke, and Jasper Parks were stocked with hatchery-reared Cutthroat Trout, Rainbow Trout, and Eastern Brook Trout, converting them into sustained game fisheries — most notably the **Maligne Lake Region**, now noted for Eastern Brook Trout.
- **Stream vs. lake carrying capacity:** Stream stocking was largely discontinued because limnological studies showed limited carrying capacity due to steep gradients, rapid/fluctuating flow, and low temperatures typical of mountain streams. Lakes, by contrast, showed much better survival and growth outcomes for planted trout.
- **Co-occurring species:** [[Leach Lake]] supports a large population of chub minnows (*Couesius plumbeus*) alongside stocked Rainbow Trout, illustrating interspecific coexistence within stocked lake systems.
- **Winter ecology:** Evidence of significant fish growth occurring under ice during winter months challenges assumptions about northern freshwater fish dormancy, indicating productive winter limnological conditions in at least some park lakes (e.g., Trefoil Lake).
- **Environmental hazards:** Winter-kill events (e.g., total winter-kill in Trefoil Lake, spring 1948; partial winter-kill spring 1951) are identified as a recurring threat to lake fish populations, necessitating restocking as a management response.
- **Niche-matching via hybridization:** The Eastern Brook Trout × Lake Trout hybrid was developed specifically to occupy niches where neither parent species performs optimally — combining Lake Trout's cold-water tolerance with Eastern Brook Trout's superior fly-fishing appeal (i.e. surface-feeding behavior).

## Key Findings

1. **Historical scale of stocking:** 40,000,000 trout distributed as eggs/fry/fingerlings (1913–1943) from three park hatcheries (Banff est. 1913, Waterton Lakes est. 1928, Jasper est. 1941); post-1944 shift to rearing larger fish resulted in only 3,000,000 fish distributed to present, >10% of which were yearlings or older.
2. **Marking program (since 1943, expanded 1948):** Fin-clipping combined with creel census (voluntary angler reporting, begun 1933) and test netting used to evaluate hatchery contribution to angler catch, natural reproduction levels, survival, growth, population estimates, and optimal stocking procedures.
3. **Recapture rates highly variable by lake:** In lakes like Edith, Patricia, and Mina, marked Rainbow Trout comprised >75% of angler catch, indicating natural reproduction is inadequate and fishery is stocking-dependent. In Trefoil, Mildred, and Beauvert Lakes, marked Eastern Brook Trout similarly exceeded 75% of catch. Where marked-fish proportion is low, natural spawning is inferred to be adequate, reducing the need for continued stocking.
4. **Survival outcomes:** Ranged widely — e.g., Trefoil Lake Eastern Brook Trout 1948 planting showed 73% total recorded survival to anglers over two years; by contrast, some Rainbow Trout plantings (e.g., Patricia Lake) showed as low as 5–6% return.
5. **Weight-based survival:** In Mildred Lake, 71 lbs of planted Eastern Brook Trout (1949–50) yielded ~400 lbs in angler catch (1950–51); in Trefoil Lake, 190 lbs planted (1948–49) yielded ~300 lbs caught; 1949 Trefoil catch estimated at 30 lbs/acre.
6. **Growth-selection bias:** Discussion (R.H. Stroud, Massachusetts) raised concern that faster-growing individuals are preferentially caught by anglers, biasing growth studies toward remaining slow-growing fish in the population — a caveat relevant to interpreting hatchery growth/survival data.
7. **Species suitability:** Comparative planting trials (post-1952) established to directly compare Rainbow Trout vs. Eastern Brook Trout growth, survival, and angler return within the same lakes, informed by findings that Eastern Brook Trout often outperforms Rainbow Trout in Jasper Park waters.
8. **Hybridization as management tool:** Eastern Brook Trout × Lake Trout hybrids (second-generation) actively stocked where hybrid traits (faster growth, cold tolerance, fly-fishing readiness) outperform either pure parent species; described as easy to breed and highly attractive to anglers.
9. **Longevity record:** Lake Trout survival of at least 8 years post-stocking documented in Pyramid Lake (17–20 inch fish recaptured in 1951 from 1943 planting).
10. **Overall management conclusion:** Hatcheries remain essential in Canada's national parks for three primary reasons — (1) inadequate natural spawning in barren high-elevation lakes, (2) need to introduce better-suited species/hybrids to specific environments, and (3) need to restore populations after winter-kill events — with ongoing evaluation via [[Creel Census]] and limnological monitoring ensuring efficient, targeted use of hatchery resources rather than indiscriminate put-and-take stocking.
