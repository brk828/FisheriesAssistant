# Bullfrog Ecology in the Lower Colorado River, Arizona-California

#herpetology #freshwater-biology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Clarkson and co-author(s) (1986, *Journal of Herpetology*, vol. 20, no. 1) provides baseline ecological data on the [[Rana Catesbeiana|American bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) living along riverbanks of the [[Lower Colorado River]] in the [[Yuma Division]], between [[Laguna Dam]] and [[Morelos Dam]]. Fieldwork ran from July to September 1981. The aim was to predict biological effects of future changes in discharge and hydrology. Adult density averaged 9.1 frogs per linear kilometre, and frogs were most numerous where bank cover was dense reeds with some open bank. No larvae were found. Stomach analysis showed an opportunistic diet dominated by crayfish and wolf spiders, and it added new prey records of muskrat and rattlesnake. The authors argue that lotic (flowing-water) conditions probably alter the breeding and chorusing behaviour known from lentic (still-water) populations.

## Background & Description

*Rana catesbeiana* is one of the largest and most geographically widespread frogs in North America. Historically it occupied permanent ponds and slow-flowing streams east of the Rocky Mountains, from Canada to northeastern Mexico. It was introduced into California and elsewhere in the early 1900s and became established widely across the West. The authors note that several native amphibians were probably displaced or extirpated as a result, including the [[California Red-Legged Frog]] (*Rana aurora draytonii*), whose decline the literature links to overharvest and the bullfrog introduction.

Most earlier bullfrog studies came from pond populations, such as those at Crane Pond (Michigan) and Basking Pond (New Jersey). This paper focuses on riverbank populations, where unidirectional current and a one-dimensional bank habitat should alter reproductive and calling behaviour. Adults are both aquatic and terrestrial, which the authors suggest makes them especially sensitive to changes in river habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** Thirty-two 0.4 km transects were set at regular intervals across the study reach. Three separate night-time visual counts were made by boat with a 12-volt DC lamp, and frogs were then collected by gig and dip net. Snout-vent length (SVL) and weight were recorded before specimens were preserved. Stomach contents were scored using a modified point system of relative volume (Hynes, 1950). Bank vegetation was classified by timing how long it took to pass each cover category from a boat travelling at constant speed. Categories were [[Common Reed]] and [[Giant Reed]], emergents (cattails and bulrush), [[Salt Cedar]], riprap, and open bank.

**Physical conditions.** Discharge in the Yuma Division during the survey ranged from 350–609 cubic feet per second (cfs) below Laguna Dam, 590–883 cfs below the Yuma Main Canal wasteway near River Mile 27.5, and 3,400–3,700 cfs above Morelos Dam. Current velocities were typically less than 0.5 m/s. The exceptions were the area directly below Laguna Dam (not surveyed) and a short run near River Mile 24.

**Bank vegetation.** Reeds were the dominant cover, making up 49% of bank classifications (slightly more on the California side). Open bank accounted for 36%, salt cedar 6%, and riprap 4%. Emergents, salt cedar and riprap were limited overall but locally abundant.

**Thermal ecology.** Citing [[Lillywhite]] (1970), the authors report that bullfrog body temperatures are typically kept between 26–33 °C, with a critical thermal maximum of 38.2 °C. Summer night air temperatures in the Yuma area often approach that level, while mainstream water is much cooler. The authors suggest frogs may shuttle between land and water at night, which would lower the chance of counting all frogs at any one moment.

**Harvest pressure.** The bullfrog hunting season opens 1 July in both states, and no pre-survey harvest data exist. Creel data suggested light early-season harvest at the upper end of the division, though secondhand reports indicated some capture before the season opened at the lower end. Late-season collection remained easy, and the legal bag limit of 12 frogs could be taken per hour in vegetated areas.

## Key Findings

### 1. Density and distribution
- Mean adult density was **9.1 frogs per linear km** of river, counting both banks.
- Arizona bank averaged **4.7/km**, California bank **4.4/km**. Backwater frogs more than 5 m inland were excluded.
- Three visual counts between 10 July and 13 August 1981 did not differ statistically (two-tailed t-test, α = 0.05).
- Densities peaked near River Mile 30 and next to the dams.

### 2. Habitat associations
- Transects with **>50% reed cover** held **5.9 frogs/km** per bank, significantly more than transects with <50% reed cover (**4.6/km**; P < 0.05).
- Areas with **≥50% open bank** (including riprap) had only **2.3/km**. Areas with 25–50% open bank had 4.6/km, and areas with 0–25% open bank had 5.9/km.
- These patterns held for both banks and all repetitions.
- Frogs were seldom found inside dense cover. They were typically on short open-bank stretches between vegetation patches, which offered both water access and cover. The authors conclude that some open bank within dense cover is essential prime habitat.
- Adults were seen only at night, usually within 1 m of the water's edge and rarely beyond 2 m. They sat on bare mud-silt banks or flat boulders in riprap areas and rarely used floating reed mats.

### 3. Population structure
- **No larval bullfrogs** were observed despite extensive searches in mainstream and backwater habitats.
- Only **two leopard frogs** (*Rana pipiens* complex) were seen. Vitt and Ohmart (1978) had considered that species extinct from the lower Colorado River.
- The SVL histogram showed no obvious size classes, with considerable overlap in body size and age among adults.
- Two specimens (SVL 54 and 47 mm) were recently metamorphosed adults. Published transformation sizes range from 25–60 mm.
- A random sample of **61 dissected specimens** had a **1:1 sex ratio**. All females were spent or immature. Literature ratios are also 1:1.

### 4. Feeding ecology
- Stomachs of **100 adults** collected 15 August–24 September 1981 averaged **29% full**. Seventeen percent held 5% or less of their potential volume as food.
- Individual frogs averaged **1.9 identified taxa** each (excluding vascular plants). Across all specimens there were **32 taxa**.
- Diet was highly opportunistic. Each food category averaged only 2.4% relative volume and appeared in only 6.5% of stomachs. Nineteen categories (59%) were found in just two stomachs.
- Most important prey by relative volume:
  - [[Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*): 19.2% mean relative volume, in 24% of stomachs
  - [[Wolf Spiders]] (Lycosidae): 15.3% relative volume, the most frequent item at 36%
- Other frequent items: earwigs (20%), sowbugs (25%), German cockroaches (11%), crickets (16%), and vascular plant material (36%). Sand and gravel appeared in 11% of stomachs.
- **New prey records:** a young [[Muskrat]] (*Ondatra zibethicus*) and a [[Western Diamondback Rattlesnake]] (*Crotalus atrox*). Other unusual items included a kingsnake, a soft-shelled turtle (*Trionyx spiniferus*), several fish species, the Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*), a scorpion, and mostly beetles.

### 5. Breeding and life history
- The authors' general life-history model for the lower Colorado River is:
  - Breeding starts in early April, once daytime water temperatures exceed 20 °C.
  - Peak activity probably falls in late April–early May and again in late May–early June.
  - A small fraction of males call through July and stop by August.
  - Eggs hatch in about 2–4 days. Larvae stay near the hatching site for about three days before dispersing.
  - First-clutch larvae probably metamorphose in 3–6 months (August–October). Some second-clutch larvae overwinter and transform the following March–April.
- Lotic conditions raise several problems for the lentic breeding model:
  - Eggs are laid as a surface film and settle after about 20 minutes. In current they would not stay in a male's territory unless anchored to vegetation.
  - Territorial defence of oviposition sites should be limited by current.
  - A true "chorus" may only form in one dimension along a bank.
- Comparison with other ranids (e.g. [[Rana Boylii]], [[Rana Aurora]], [[Rana Pretiosa]]) shows that stream-dwelling frogs typically attach eggs to stones or vegetation with precise site selection.
- Because adults are widespread far from backwaters, the authors suggest mainstream breeding may occur. Yet tadpoles were never found there. If mainstream habitats are unsuitable, adults or larvae must move more than 10 km to explain the observed distribution.

### 6. Sources of bias and conclusions
- Dense overhanging reeds, cattail and bulrush may hide some frogs. These areas make up about 1.5% of reed and emergent distributions, so the counts may underestimate numbers.
- Thermoregulatory shuttling at night may also reduce the number of frogs visible above water.
- Hunter removal and boat traffic may have depressed densities near boat launch ramps, but the authors could not separate these effects from the cover effect.
- The authors conclude that bullfrogs have become established in the lower Colorado River. Their densities are comparable to those reported by Treanor (1975) in California canals. They tolerate extreme temperatures and turbid, lotic waters that are unlike much of their native range. Further data on lotic breeding behaviour are needed.
