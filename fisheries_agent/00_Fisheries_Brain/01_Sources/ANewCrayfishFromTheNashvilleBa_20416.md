# A New Crayfish from the Nashville Basin, Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cambaridae #freshwater-invertebrates #taxonomy #crayfish

## Executive Summary

This paper describes **Cambarus gentryi**, a new species of burrowing crayfish from the [[Nashville Basin]] in [[Tennessee]]. The species was first collected in the 1890s from a tributary of [[Stones River]] but remained undescribed for approximately 75 years. It was rediscovered in 1968 by Dr. Glenn Gentry in [[Cheatham County]]. The species is distinguished by its cobalt blue coloration with orange or yellow markings, linear areola, and distinctive first pleopod morphology. It inhabits boggy areas and shallow burrows where bedrock limits vertical burrowing depth. The known range encompasses portions of the [[Cumberland River]] ([[Stones River]], [[Harpeth River]], [[Yellow Creek]]) and [[Duck River]] basins in Middle Tennessee.

## Background & Description

### Historical Context
The holotype specimens were originally collected by Mr. Von Baer in November during the 1890s from a "tributary of Stone River, 20 miles from Columbia, Tennessee" and transferred to the [[Smithsonian Institution]] from the [[United States Fish Commission]]. The species remained undescribed until rediscovered by Dr. Glenn Gentry in April 1968 in a boggy area along a tributary to the [[Harpeth River]] in [[Cheatham County]].

### Morphological Description
**Cambarus (Jugicambarus) gentryi** is characterized by:

- **Body**: Pigmented, subcylindrical; eyes small but well-developed
- **Rostrum**: Convergent margins without marginal spines or tubercles; acumen very short and indistinctly delimited
- **Carapace**: Areola sublinear, comprising 40-44.5% of entire carapace length, bearing one or no punctations in narrowest part; approximately 50 times longer than broad in holotype; without cervical tubercles or spines
- **Postorbital ridge**: Prominent with conspicuous swelling caudally and subtruncate cephalically
- **Antennal scale**: Approximately three times longer than broad
- **Chela**: Single row of 5-8 tubercles on mesial margin of palm with 1-3 above marginal row; lateral surface with middle half subcostate; both fingers with moderately well-developed longitudinal ridges on upper surfaces
- **First pleopods** (Form I male): Corneous central projection recurved approximately 120°, tapering distally, lacking subterminal notch; mesial process non-corneous, submammillate, with simple or spinose tip directed caudally
- **Annulus ventralis**: Deeply embedded in sternum with short cephalomedian trough separated from broad caudal wall by asymmetrical median depression

### Measurements (Holotype, Form I Male)
- Carapace length: 27.6 mm
- Carapace width: 13.1 mm
- Carapace height: 11.7 mm
- Areola length: 11.5 mm (41.6% of carapace length)
- Areola width: approximately 0.23 mm (sublinear)
- Rostrum length: 4.0 mm
- Rostrum width: 4.4 mm
- Chela outer margin length: 22.1 mm
- Palm width: 9.7 mm

### Coloration
Distinctive cobalt blue carapace with:
- Hepatic and branchiostegal areas: cobalt blue
- Gastric region: blue suffused with greenish tan
- Margins of rostrum, postorbital ridges, and paired tubercles: orange tan
- Chelipeds: bluish green dorsally from distal merus to orange tan finger tips
- All tubercles on chela: yellowish cream
- Spines and mesial tubercle row: conspicuously orange-yellow
- Ventral abdomen, uropods, telson: pale blue
- Antennae: cream with orange tan setae

### Size Range
- Largest male (Form I): 48.3 mm carapace length ([[Concord Creek]], [[Rutherford County]])
- Largest female: 43.4 mm carapace length ([[Concord Creek]])
- Smallest Form I male: 30.8 mm carapace length (type-locality)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Characteristics
**Type-locality**: Boggy area below seepage along small tributary to [[Turnbull Creek]] ([[Harpeth River]]-[[Cumberland River]] drainage), one mile west of [[Kingston Springs]], [[Cheatham County]], Tennessee.

Habitat features:
- Shaded area adjacent to stream
- Water table virtually at surface
- Ground riddled with crayfish burrows
- Almost covered with mats of liverworts and mosses
- Shallow layer of soil, small rocks, and gravel overlying almost horizontally bedded rock
- Burrow depth limited to maximum 1.5 feet by bedrock

### Burrow Architecture
**Type-locality burrows**:
- Shallow, mostly horizontal passages
- 6-8 feet in length
- Branching with 2-3 openings to surface
- Easily excavated with garden rake rather than shovel

**Other localities**:
- More nearly vertical construction
- Branching with at least 2 openings to surface
- One passageway descending to approximately 3 feet depth
- Water table within 1 foot of surface after heavy rains

### Behavior
- First form males occasionally leave burrows and may wander into streams (suggested by Von Baer's collection of three Form I males from stream)
- Active at night at burrow mouths or walking on ground surface
- Can be collected with flashlight at night

### Geographic Distribution
Known range encompasses portions of [[Cumberland River]] and [[Duck River]] basins in Middle Tennessee:

**Specimens examined from**:
- **[[Cheatham County]]**: Type-locality (37 specimens total from multiple collections 1968-1969)
- **[[Davidson County]]**: Along creek at [[Montgomery Bell State Park]] (2 juveniles)
- **[[Houston County]]**: Along [[Yellow Creek]] at [[Dickson County]] line (1 Form I male)
- **[[Maury County]]**: Near [[Mt. Pleasant]] (1 Form I male, 1 female)
- **[[Rutherford County]]**: Along [[Concord Creek]] between Rockvale and Eagleville (1 Form I male, 3 females); "Tributary of Stone River" (3 Form I males, 1890s)
- **[[Williamson County]]**: Camp Mary Mount, 2.5 miles W of Nashville (1 Form I male)

## Key Findings

### Taxonomic Description
A new species of burrowing crayfish, **Cambarus (Jugicambarus) gentryi**, is described from the [[Nashville Basin]], Tennessee. The species is most closely allied to [[Cambarus carolinus]] within the subgenus [[Jugicambarus]].

### Distinctive Features
The most distinctive diagnostic features are:
1. Linear areola (approximately 50 times longer than broad)
2. Cobalt blue carapace with orange or yellow markings
3. Unique first pleopod morphology with corneous central projection recurved approximately 120°
4. Deeply embedded annulus ventralis with distinctive morphology

### Life History
- First form males collected in April, May, and November
- No ovigerous females or females carrying young observed
- Reproductive season not fully determined

### Geographic Variation
Specimens from [[Concord Creek]] and "[[Stone River]]" localities in [[Rutherford County]] show notable variations:
- **Size**: Concord Creek specimens larger (carapace 40.7-48.3 mm) than type-locality (maximum 35.3 mm female)
- **Areola proportion**: Concord Creek 43.5-44.5% vs. type-locality 40.4-43.2% of carapace length
- **First pleopod morphology**: Concord Creek male shows central projection less strongly recurved, mesial process terminates in 3-4 small spines, distal portion more strongly arched laterally
- **Stone River specimens**: Mesial process constricted distal portion longer and more flattened; distal pleopod portion strongly arched laterally

### Type Specimens
- **Holotype**: Form I male, USNM 130283
- **Allotype**: Female, USNM 130284
- **Morphotype**: Form II male, USNM 130285
- **Paratypes**: 24 Form I males, 78 Form II males, 22 females, 1 juvenile (USNM)

### Etymology
Species named in honor of Dr. Glenn Gentry, who rediscovered the species and provided extensive assistance in collecting specimens.

### Collection Methods
- Flashlight collection at night when animals at burrow mouths or on surface
- Excavation from burrows using garden rake (type-locality) or shovel (other localities)
- Possibly collected from streams (Von Baer specimens)

### Conservation Implications
The species has been known to exist for approximately 75 years but remained undescribed, suggesting potential for additional undiscovered crayfish diversity in the region. The restricted habitat requirements (shallow soil over bedrock, seepage areas) may make populations vulnerable to habitat alteration.
