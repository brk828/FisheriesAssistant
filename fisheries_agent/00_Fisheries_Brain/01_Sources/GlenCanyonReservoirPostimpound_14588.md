# Lake Powell Post-Impoundment Fishery Investigation, 1967 (Progress Report No. 5)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #reservoir-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This progress report from the Utah Division of Fish and Game, with contributions from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, covers the fifth year of post-impoundment fishery study on [[Lake Powell]] (Glen Canyon Reservoir) on the [[Colorado River]], for the biological year February 1967 to January 1968. It combines reservoir management data, physical and chemical limnology, fish stocking records, and population studies of the principal game and non-game species. The reservoir's fish community is dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Common Carp]], with [[Channel Catfish]] rising as a major angler species. Stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] returned very poorly (about 0.5 percent of 13.1 million planted), while bass recruitment and a strong 1964 year-class dominated the harvest. Dissolved oxygen minima in the thermocline and a late-1967 black crappie mortality were notable events.

## Background & Description

The project is a cooperative Section 8 program under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. Utah is responsible for physical and chemical regimes and fishery development; Arizona handles fishing pressure, harvest and plankton. The study began in July 1963, and this is the fifth progress report in the series. The reservoir was impounded in 1963, with morphology described in earlier reports.

Sampling used 17 fixed stations (14 for chemistry and temperature, three for temperature only), monthly temperature profiles, and multiple gear types: vertical and horizontal gill nets, a barge electro-shocker, a 100-foot beach seine, and creel sampling at access points. Age was determined from scales; condition was expressed as K = W x 10^5 / L^3.

Water level in 1967 ranged from elevation 3,502 ft (May low) to 3,533 ft (July high), ending at 3,526 ft in January 1968 with 8,684,000 acre-feet of storage and a 66,810-acre surface area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime and stratification.** The lake was monomictic in 1967, with a permanent thermocline forming in early June and persisting until late November. Minimum surface temperatures were 39 F in the upper 10 miles and 44 F near the dam in winter. Peak epilimnion temperatures reached 82 F in early August. The [[Colorado River]] inflow, carrying sediment, flowed as a density current along the bottom in winter and over the thermocline in summer.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Thermocline oxygen declined through summer, with a low band of 3.9 ppm about 20 feet wide at 60 to 80 feet depth from August to October. Winter oxygen was 9.0 to 10.6 ppm in the upper 80 feet and 5.2 to 7.3 ppm below.

**Water chemistry.** Alkalinity was almost entirely bicarbonate, peaking in December (157 ppm average surface in 1967, versus 144 ppm in 1966). Total hardness reached 465 ppm at the surface near the headwaters in late fall. Sulfates averaged 333 ppm in March 1967. Total phosphates averaged 0.35 ppm, down from 0.70 ppm in 1964, with an unexplained peak of 0.60 to 0.80 ppm at [[Hall's Crossing]]. Turbidity during runoff reached 1,425 ppm in the [[Colorado River]].

**Key habitat and forage.** Deep pelagic water is largely unused by game fish. The 10 to 50 foot zone during stratification held the greatest plankton and forage fish concentrations, favoring bass growth.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and trout performance**
- 600,000 rainbow trout (about 5 inches) were planted in late September and early October 1967 at four areas. Handling and temperature shock (plantings at 72 to 76 F, with aerial tanks at 35 to 44 F) caused hauling and planting mortality of 4 to 8 percent (truck) and 2 to 4 percent (air).
- No marked trout from the 1967 plants were recovered by the end of January 1968.
- Trout returns were very low: 3,001 fish in 1967 versus 37,733 in 1966. Overall, 68,151 of 13.1 million planted trout (0.5 percent) had been harvested.
- The authors recommend that future plants of larger trout occur in late October or November and that stocking be restricted to Wahweap Bay in late fall.

**Largemouth bass**
- Growth was slowest in the lowermost 50 miles and fastest between Aztec Creek and Tapestry Wall. Growth differences between areas have moderated since impoundment.
- First-year mean lengths were 231, 225 and 227 mm for the 1963, 1964 and 1965 year-classes. Shocker length-frequency data conflicted with scale-derived first-year growth, possibly due to misread scales or extended spawning.
- A strong 1964 year-class was identified; the 1966 year-class was the weakest recent class. Electro-shocker samples were 342 (1965), 504 (1966), 720 (1967) and 935 (1968) fish.
- Condition declined in larger fish since 1965: an average 425 mm bass had K values of 2.96 (1965), 2.71 (1966) and 2.47 (1967).
- The 1967 creel sample (278 fish) was dominated by the 1964 (46 percent) and 1965 (45 percent) year-classes. Estimated total harvest was 39,939 fish, down from 61,770 in 1966.
- Spawning occurred from late April into July, peaking in mid to late May. Spawning depths remained unknown, and nest observations in shallow water failed to confirm spawning.
- Bass diet was mainly fish, especially [[Green Sunfish]] in the lower reservoir and centrarchids in the upper reservoir. Crayfish and bluegill were found only in upper-reservoir stomachs.

**Rainbow trout biology**
- Of 292 trout sampled in 1967, 198 came from the dam to Antelope Canyon, 72 from Wahweap Bay and 22 from above Antelope Canyon.
- Natural reproduction was largely unsuccessful, with no spawning activity or young observed.
- During stratification, trout concentrated at 50 to 100 feet (greatest at 60 to 90 feet), in temperatures of 52 to 70 F. Winter distribution moved them to the surface to 40 feet, near the penstock depth.
- Diet shifted in 1967: fish (chiefly black crappie, young of the year) occurred in 26.0 percent of large-group stomachs and made up 70.9 percent of volume, versus 9.6 percent occurrence in 1966.

**Black crappie**
- Introduced to Wahweap Bay in 1964 and 1965, crappie spread to the Escalante to Rincon area (about 75 miles above the dam) by March 1967.
- Natural reproduction was first recorded in 1966 and appeared very successful in 1967. Young of the year made up about two-thirds of 183 fish sampled.
- A low-intensity winter mortality (December 1967 to February 1968) coincided with surface temperatures of 40 to 43 F. No pathogen was identified, and the population appeared not seriously affected.

**Non-game species and other fishes**
- [[Common Carp]] made up nearly three-fourths of the total fish sample and 89 percent of the non-game sample. Very few carp were captured below 60 feet.
- [[Green Sunfish]] fell sharply to 3.2 percent of the non-game sample (from 38 percent in 1966) and seining yielded about 90 individuals versus roughly 1,000 in 1966. Reproduction declined markedly.
- [[Bluegill Sunfish]] increased roughly 400 percent in numbers, with reproduction confirmed (33 young of the year seined from lower reservoir and 35 from upper).
- [[Channel Catfish]] (325 collected in 1967, versus 189 in 1966) had become second only to bass in angler popularity; the 1965 and 1966 year-classes were strong.
- [[Walleye]] was a natural introduction. In 1967, 27 fish were collected, grouped into year-classes 1964 to 1966, suggesting a slowly increasing population.
- [[Colorado Chub (Bonytail)]] (38 captured) were mostly taken in Wahweap Bay and the lower main channel; spawning condition was observed in June.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (11 captured, 420 to 602 mm) were all three-year-olds in the scale-aged subsample.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] made up 7 percent of the non-game sample and appeared to be increasing.
- [[Black Bullhead]] population was small and static (1.1 percent of non-game fish in 1967).
- The [[Red Shiner]] remained at low density compared with 1963 and 1964. Age-growth from 164 specimens gave mean lengths of 35.8, 44.0, 51.4 and 58.9 mm for ages I to IV.
- A [[Utah Chub]] (1 specimen) and [[Humpback Sucker]] (2 specimens) were recorded for the first time in Lake Powell.

**Management proposals and outlook**
- A [[Striped Bass]] introduction was under evaluation because deep pelagic habitat was largely unused by game fish. Literature suggested stripers would adapt well, mainly occupying the lower third of the lake in winter and the upper metalimnion in late summer. Natural reproduction was considered doubtful, so hatchery rearing studies were initiated.
- Threadfin shad, the only clupeid in the Colorado River, were introduced into Wahweap Bay in June 1968 as forage for striped bass.
