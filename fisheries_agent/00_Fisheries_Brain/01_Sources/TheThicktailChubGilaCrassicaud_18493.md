# Thicktail Chub (Gila crassicauda): An Extinct California Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game special publication (Inland Fisheries Endangered Species Program, Special Publication 80-2, May 1980) by Terry J. Mills and Kathy A. Mamika concludes that the [[Thicktail Chub]] (*Gila crassicauda*), once one of the most abundant fishes of the [[Central Valley]], is extinct. The species had not been collected for 22 years despite repeated sampling. The authors attribute its decline to (1) habitat alteration, chiefly drainage of large shallow lakes, loss of vegetative cover, and channelization caused by [[Hydraulic Mining]] and [[Flood Control]] works, and (2) predation and competition from [[Exotic Fish Introductions]]. The report reviews taxonomy, synonymy, hybridization with the [[Hitch]], historical distribution, and life history, and recommends delisting the species from the State endangered species list.

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** Slender-bodied minnow with a short, very deep and thick caudal peduncle (the basis of the name *crassicauda*, "thick tail"). Caudal peduncle depth into head length 1.5 to 2.05 (average 1.9); into standard length 6.2 to 7.5 (average 6.9).
- **Scalation and counts:** 40 to 60 lateral-line scales; predorsal scales 27 to 36 (usually 31 to 32); dorsal and anal fins usually 8 rays; pelvic fins usually 9 rays.
- **Dentition and gut:** Pharyngeal teeth 2,5-4,2, usually hooked and lacking grinding surfaces; single S-shaped intestinal loop.
- **Coloration:** Back greenish-brown to purplish-black; sides lighter with yellowish ground color; young have a black spot at the base of the caudal fin. Males develop nuptial puncticulations and tubercles during spawning.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Females are larger; the dorsal fin origin is slightly more anterior in males.
- **Taxonomy:** First described by Baird and Girard (in Girard 1853) from the Gila and Zuni rivers, New Mexico, hence the genus name. Over 20 synonyms are listed. Common names include Thicktail Chub, Sacramento Chub, and thicktail. The holotype of *Lavinia conformis* was found by Miller (1945) to be a large-scaled variant of this species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history and diet:** Little is known because the species received little study before it became rare. Based on Miller's (1963) study of 101 fish, it was probably carnivorous, feeding on small fish and large aquatic invertebrates. It likely occupied lowland lakes, sloughs, slow river reaches, and, in high-runoff years, surface waters of San Francisco Bay.

**Hybridization:** Five hybrid specimens with the [[Hitch]] (*Lavinia exilicauda*), collected between 1872 and 1926, were misidentified as *Lavinia conformis*. Their meristic characters, including gill rakers, fin rays, and pharyngeal teeth, were intermediate between the parents. Overlap in spawning habitat, since hitch spawn over gravel or mud in lakes (Kimsey 1960), may have allowed hybridization.

**Historical distribution:** Collection records ranged from the Sacramento River near Fort Reading (Redding) to Poso Creek in the lower San Joaquin Valley. Archaeological midden sites, notably at Stone Lake in Sacramento County, show the species formed a major part of the local Indian diet (Schulz and Simons 1973). Tables list roughly 12 historical collection sites and about 16 archaeological records.

**Historical habitat:** The [[Central Valley]] was a network of lakes, sloughs, tule marshes, and flood plains before reclamation. Early explorers described it as such (Fages 1772; Moraga 1808; Latta 1937). Sacramento Valley land in 1894 comprised 2,510 square miles of high land, 450 square miles of occasionally flooded land, 1,250 square miles of periodically submerged low land, and 38 square miles of perennial stream surface (Anon. 1912).

**Habitat alteration:**
- Hydraulic mining deposited more than 1.3 billion cubic yards of debris from the Yuba, Bear, American, and Feather rivers; by 1876 the Bear and Yuba channels were filled.
- Levee construction eliminated fish access to overflow areas and tule beds. About 700,000 acres of Delta overflow land were converted to agriculture after the 1850s (Skinner 1972).
- By the 1930s only 25% of the Sacramento Valley floor remained subject to periodic inundation.
- The flood control project comprises about 440 miles of channels, 1,000 miles of levees, and 95 miles of bypasses (Jones 1967).

**Exotic species:** Nearly 50 exotic fish species were introduced into California after 1871 (Moyle 1976b). Striped bass (introduced 1879) and American shad (1871–1881) became major fisheries, with shad harvests of 5.7 million lb in 1917. White catfish and brown bullhead were introduced in 1874.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The thicktail chub was last collected from the Delta in 1957 and from Clear Lake in 1938. With no specimens found in 22 years of sampling, the authors conclude it is extinct. Surveys of Central Valley streams (Aceituno et al. 1973) and resampling of likely habitat in the 2 years before the report produced negative results.
- **Abundance in the past:** Anglers caught it frequently at Clear Lake (Coleman 1930). It was sold in San Francisco markets in the late 19th century, though Lockington (1879) rated its occurrence as only "occasional." Collins (1892) reported it abundant in the Sacramento River in 1888, and Jordan and Gilbert (1895) listed it as common.
- **Causes of extinction:** The authors judge habitat reduction alone unlikely to have caused extinction. They conclude that habitat loss combined with predation and competition from expanding exotic populations (striped bass, catfish, and juvenile shad) was the most likely cause.
- **Sacramento perch parallel:** Declines of the native [[Sacramento Perch]] in the early 1900s, under similar pressures, suggest the thicktail chub was already declining at that time.
- **Recommendations:**
  1. Remove the thicktail chub from the State endangered species list.
  2. Department of Fish and Game and university personnel sampling Delta fishes should preserve any chub-like specimens for identification.
