# National Geographic Society Image Collection and Desert Spring Protection Notice

#ichthyology #marine-biology #media-archive

## Executive Summary

This is a promotional "Behind the Scenes" column from the August 1995 issue of [[National Geographic]]. It is not a research paper and reports no fish, invertebrate, or water-chemistry data. It covers three topics: how outside clients can license images from the [[National Geographic Society]] [[Image Collection]], how fees and contact channels work, and two short anecdotes. One anecdote describes how a photographer's assignment led to the legal protection of unusual desert springs at [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] in [[Coahuila]], Mexico. The other is a donor's tribute to the Society. No species are named.

## Background & Description

The column introduces the Society's picture archive, which holds more than 10.5 million photographs and artworks. According to [[Maura Mulvihill]], director of the Image Collection, subjects such as "fins, fangs, fur, stones, and bones" sell well. For the first time, editors, publishers, and art directors could draw on the full collection to illustrate books, articles, and advertisements.

Requests vary in specificity. Some ask for a named subject, such as a photograph of Julius Caesar. Others ask for a concept, such as "spirituality" or "the competitive edge." Fees follow standard industry rates and range from several hundred to several thousand dollars, depending on order size.

Contact details listed in the column:
- Toll-free telephone line for U.S. and Canadian callers (Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. ET), plus a TDD line for the hearing-impaired.
- Postal address at the Society's Washington, D.C. headquarters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The only ecological content concerns the springs near Cuatro Ciénegas, described as "unusual desert springs." The column does not describe their biota, water chemistry, or hydrology. Its aquatic relevance is limited to a conservation outcome:

- [[George Grall]], a photographer working for the Society, photographed the springs in Mexico in the fall of 1995 (the column says "last fall" relative to its August 1995 publication).
- The local mayor, [[Susana Moncada]], informed the governor of Coahuila, who was a long-time Society member. He persuaded the then president of Mexico to declare the springs a natural protected area.
- The column states that conservationists had been urging this protection for 30 years.

The column also mentions [[Irian Jaya]], Indonesia, where staff writer [[Tom O'Neill]] was briefly stopped by an armed soldier during a trip. This is a travel anecdote with no environmental content.

## Key Findings

- **Collection scale:** The Society's Image Collection contains more than 10.5 million photographs and artworks available for licensing.
- **Licensing scope:** Editors, publishers, and art directors can use the collection for books, articles, and advertisements.
- **Pricing:** Orders are charged standard industry fees, from several hundred to several thousand dollars.
- **Conservation outcome:** Photographic work on the desert springs near Cuatro Ciénegas contributed to their designation as a natural protected area in Mexico, a step conservationists had sought for about 30 years.
- **Visitor tribute:** Romanian businessman [[Dorel Jurcovan]] visited the Washington, D.C. headquarters to thank the Society, saying that *National Geographic* had given Romanians hope through communism, wars, and economic hardship.
