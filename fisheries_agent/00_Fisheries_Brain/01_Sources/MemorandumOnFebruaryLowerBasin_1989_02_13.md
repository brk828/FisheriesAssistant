# Memorandum on Lower Basin "Big River" Fishes Recovery Coordination Meeting (1989)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary

This 1989 memorandum from the Arizona Game & Fish Department outlines the planning agenda for a coordination meeting focused on the recovery of three imperiled big-river fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin: the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (now commonly known as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]), and, to a lesser extent, the [[Bonytail Chub]]. The document is not a primary research paper but a planning/administrative record that reveals the state of recovery efforts, known knowledge gaps, and a preliminary task list intended to form the basis of a "Lower Basin Recovery Implementation Plan." No direct morphological or population data are presented; the content is procedural and programmatic, reflecting the applied conservation science and hatchery management context of the era.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions, measurements, or anatomical data for the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], or [[Bonytail Chub]]. These are large-bodied, long-lived native cyprinid and catostomid species endemic to the Colorado River Basin, historically referred to collectively as "big river fishes," implying large adult body size relative to other basin natives — a trait relevant to their habitat needs and vulnerability to altered flow regimes and predation by introduced species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context**: The species of concern inhabit — or historically inhabited — large, mainstem riverine systems of the Lower Colorado River Basin, including the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and [[Gila River]], as well as reservoir habitats such as [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Horseshoe Reservoir]]/[[Roosevelt Reservoir]].
- **Stocking and hatchery dependence**: At the time of writing, wild self-sustaining populations were largely absent; recovery relied heavily on hatchery production and stocking programs, with monitoring conducted quarterly in riverine settings.
- **Observed ecological problem**: Stocked fish exhibited apparent high mortality and rapid dispersal after release, suggesting a mismatch between hatchery-reared fish behavior/conditioning and wild habitat demands, or unsuitable release-site habitat conditions.
- **Broodstock source**: The [[Bonytail Chub]] recovery effort depended on broodstock retrieval from [[Lake Mohave]], indicating this reservoir functioned as a critical refugial population source for the species.
- **Interbasin coordination**: The Lower Basin efforts were recognized as needing better integration with [[Upper Basin]] recovery programs, implying the species' range and management span multiple jurisdictional and hydrological basin units within the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Key Findings

- **Long-range goal**: Establish self-sustaining wild populations of razorback sucker and squawfish (and by extension bonytail) within Lower Basin habitats, moving away from dependence on hatchery stocking.
- **Current status (as of 1989)**:
  - High levels of hatchery production and stocking were occurring.
  - A quarterly riverine monitoring program was already in place.
  - Stocked fish showed apparent high mortality and rapid post-release dispersal.
- **Identified knowledge/management gaps**:
  - Inadequate understanding of the fate of stocked fish in the wild.
  - Inadequate understanding of species-specific habitat requirements (seasonal utilization and preference).
  - Inadequate representation of Lower Basin populations and needs within official federal/regional Recovery Plans.
- **Immediate research/management needs identified**:
  - Document causes and patterns of post-stocking mortality.
  - Document seasonal habitat utilization and preference.
  - Improve survivorship of stocked individuals via program modifications.
  - Formally incorporate Lower Basin strategies into official Recovery Plans (since funding priority follows tasks specified in Recovery Plans).
- **Proposed/short-range tasks** (categorized by species/program: r = razorback, s = squawfish, h = hatcheries, b = bonytail):
  - Radio telemetry studies in the Middle Gila River and Salt/Verde systems to track dispersal and habitat use of Razorback Sucker (RBS) and Colorado Squawfish (CSF).
  - Habitat preference and dispersal/mortality-cause studies.
  - Development of hatchery protocols to produce larger razorback sucker and squawfish for stocking.
  - A Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal growout proposal for razorback sucker.
  - Continued quarterly monitoring of Verde/Salt Rivers.
  - Food habits analysis of stocked fish.
  - Acquisition of additional grow-out ponds.
  - Installation of a computerized database system for stocking/monitoring records (BBS system).
  - Salt River habitat renovation and backwater renovation/growout habitat creation.
  - Investigation into imprinting/homing behavior in squawfish, relevant to stocking-site selection strategy.
  - Development of a hatchery production procedures manual.
  - Implementation of permanent individual marking of stocked fish, enabling future mortality/dispersal tracking.
  - Bonytail broodstock retrieval effort from [[Lake Mohave]].
  - Development of a sport fisheries implementation plan (secondary consideration).
- **Institutional/programmatic recommendation**: The author (Dean A. Hendrickson, Native Fish Biologist, Arizona Game & Fish Department) proposed compiling a consensus-prioritized task list into a formal "Lower Basin Recovery Implementation Plan," to be added as an official appendix to broader federal Recovery Plans — since tasks explicitly named in Recovery Plans received funding priority under Section 6 and other funding mechanisms.
- **Scope limitation**: This document is a planning/coordination artifact rather than a data-driven research report; it establishes the administrative and strategic groundwork for subsequent field studies on [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] recovery in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]].
