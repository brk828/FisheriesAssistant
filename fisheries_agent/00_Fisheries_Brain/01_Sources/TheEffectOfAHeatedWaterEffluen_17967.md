# The Effect of a Heated Water Effluent Upon the Macroinvertebrate Riffle Fauna of the Delaware River

#macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #thermal-effluent

## Executive Summary

This study examines how heated condenser discharge from the [[Martin's Creek Steam Electric Station]] affects the [[Macroinvertebrate]] bottom fauna of riffles in the [[Delaware River]]. Samples were collected with a [[Surber Sampler]] mainly during summer 1959, with additional sampling in 1960 and 1961. Within the maximally heated zone, the summer fauna was sharply reduced in both taxa and individuals, and [[Standing Crop Biomass]] fell by more than 90 percent. A riffle 4800 feet downstream showed no clear loss, and some taxa, most notably [[Hydropsyche]], were more abundant there. Cool-season samples showed partial repopulation of the heated zone, probably by organisms washed in from upstream during high water. The author estimates a tolerance limit of about 90 to 95 degrees Fahrenheit for most of the fauna.

## Background & Description

The study was written by [[Charles C. Coutant]] of [[Lehigh University]] and reprinted from the *Proceedings of the Pennsylvania Academy of Science* (Vol. XXXVI, 1962). It was partly supported by a [[National Science Foundation]] Undergraduate Research Grant.

The author notes that laboratory studies, especially on fish, define physiological temperature limits but say little about how organisms react under natural conditions. The Martin's Creek plant is a useful natural experiment. It returns about 120,000 gallons of water per minute to the Delaware, heated to about 20°F above intake temperature. The upper Delaware is described as one of the cleanest rivers in the East, so pollution should not confound the results.

The study area includes three main riffle sampling sites and several other stations:

- **[[Big Kaypush Rapids]]** (begins 1500 feet below the plant and runs 2300 feet to a railroad bridge). The discharge canal enters about 700 feet below the rapids' start. Jetties were built to aid mixing, but heated water still concentrates on the Pennsylvania side.
- **[[Little Kaypush Rapids]]**, below a 3500-foot pool.
- **Control station M-1**, upstream of the discharge.
- **Maximally heated station M-2**, midway between the discharge and the railroad bridge.
- **Station M-3**, on the Pennsylvania side of Little Kaypush Rapids, about one mile downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Riffles were coarse rubble with varying sand. Rooted aquatic plants were absent, but [[Benthic Algae]] were abundant, especially at M-2. The heated water had somewhat lower dissolved oxygen, but never reached levels considered restrictive to riffle organisms. Mineral content, measured as conductivity, rose downstream because of springs near Little Kaypush (250 to 500 micromhos, versus under 100 at the plant).

Sampling followed standard methods. Organisms were preserved in 10% formalin, sorted with a table sugar solution, identified mostly to genus, and weighed wet. Bivalves were weighed separately. Samples were collected on six dates in 1959 (July 9, 23, 30; August 20; September 3; October 6), with traverse sampling on August 27 and September 10. Additional samples were taken on March 5, 1960; July and early August 1960; and January 17, 1961.

Standing crop biomass was used rather than productivity, which the author acknowledges is a limitation. Biomass was considered especially useful for temperature tolerance work. The author also notes that larger, unevenly distributed taxa such as bivalves were treated separately, following [[Sanders]] (1960).

## Key Findings

**Summer reduction in the heated zone (M-2)**
- Using five-square-foot samples, July-August averages fell from 24.5 to 5.0 taxa and from 1763.8 to 186.0 individuals, compared with the control.
- Standing crop biomass fell from 1.04 g/ft² to 0.09 g/ft² (July-August average).
- Biomass was 79% below control on July 9. The reduction became greater than 99% on August 20, the date of the highest recorded temperature (107°F).
- Species numbers at M-2 were 34.6% of control on July 30 and 7.7% on August 20 (mean 22.3%).

**Downstream station (M-3, Little Kaypush)**
- Biomass was consistently above control, from 42.3% greater on July 9 to 5.8% greater on September 3 (mean 25.9%). These differences were significant at the 95% confidence limit.
- Including the predatory [[Corydalus]] on September 3 raised the difference to 47.2%.
- Species counts exceeded control by one to six taxa (average 4.3).
- The author cautions that M-3 differences may not be direct temperature effects. Explanations considered include mineral content, periphyton-driven plankton supply, and natural patchiness.

**Tolerant taxa**
- Organisms common at M-1 were mostly absent from M-2. Exceptions were [[Tendipedidae]] (midges), [[Berosus]] (water scavenger beetle), [[Physa]] (pond snail), and [[Argia]] (damselfly larva).
- Tendipeds averaged about 0.4 g per thousand individuals, so they contributed little biomass.
- Physa at M-2 were rarely large, suggesting inhibited reproduction.
- Temperatures of 105° to 110°F restricted even these tolerant taxa.

**Bivalves and Hydropsyche**
- Bivalves at M-3 included [[Elliptio Complanatus]] (25 to 30 g each; 1.5 to 8 per ft²), [[Sphaerium]] (finger-nail clam, 0.06 g each; 1 to 25.6 per ft²), and one [[Alasmidonta Undulata]] (about 25 g). The author concludes temperature had little demonstrable effect on bivalves.
- [[Hydropsyche]] averaged about 8.5 g per thousand. It was more abundant at M-3 than at M-1 on every date except September 3, 1959, and had the highest count of any riffle sampled in 1960. This suggests a direct or indirect heated-water effect a mile downstream.

**Temperature tolerance and traverses**
- Traverses from M-2 toward cooler water showed an increase in both number and variety of organisms, indicating temperature was the primary limiting factor.
- The author estimates a tolerance limit of 90 to 95°F for most of the fauna. The conclusions state a limit close to 90°F for normal population structure.

**Repopulation**
- On March 5, 1960, at 56°F, M-2 contained taxa absent or restricted in summer, including [[Ferrissia]], [[Gammarus]], [[Isonychia]], a stonefly, and Hydropsyche.
- On July 11, 1960, at 95°F, two square feet had a more typical summer population, but many Hydropsyche were dead or sluggish. Several days later, only tendipeds were found living.
- In January 1961, at 68°F, the population resembled the previous July sample and included forms not typical of summer M-2.
- Repopulation probably occurs mainly through winter and spring floods that carry upstream organisms into M-2, rather than immediately after autumn cooling. Rapid spring warming kills intolerant organisms rather than driving them out.

**Conclusions**
1. From July to October, the number, diversity, and biomass of riffle macroinvertebrates are substantially reduced in the part of Big Kaypush Rapids in the heated water path.
2. One mile downstream, population structure is essentially normal, except for a large increase in Hydropsyche and biomass, and some annual abundance cycles appear shifted.
3. Tendipedidae, Berosus, Argia, and Physa are particularly tolerant of elevated temperature.
4. A tolerance limit near 90°F is indicated for normal population structure.
5. Limited repopulation of the affected zone occurs in cooler seasons, probably by washed-in upstream organisms.

The author stresses that more data are needed before observations at M-3 can be confirmed as heated-water effects.
