# Recovery of Grass Carp, Channel Catfish, and Centrarchids in Artificial Central Arizona Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports fish community structure in seven small artificial ponds at [[Sun Lakes]], a retirement community about 32 km SSW of Phoenix, Arizona. The ponds were reclaimed with [[Rotenone]] in April 1978, and all fish were counted and measured. Mean total fish biomass was 532 kg/ha. The community was dominated by [[Grass Carp]] (67%) and [[Channel Catfish]] (30%), with [[Largemouth Bass]] (3%) and [[Green Sunfish]] (<1%) contributing little. The highest biomass, 1,159 kg/ha, occurred in a 0.44-ha pond. Grass carp and largemouth bass standing crops were positively correlated, while pond area and fish yield were unrelated. The authors suggest angler harvest and water quality as likely causes of yield differences among ponds.

The paper is a short research article in the *Journal of the Arizona-Nevada Academy of Science* (vol. 18). The same journal issue also includes a book review of an anthology on the Seri people of the Gulf of California.

## Background & Description

Grass carp were promoted for decades as a [[Biological Control]] agent for nuisance aquatic plants. They are banned in Arizona because of uncertain environmental risks, yet they have been imported and have escaped or been stocked in several places. Channel catfish and largemouth bass are popular introduced sport fishes in the southwestern United States. The [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGF) stocked both extensively in public waters, and the U.S. Soil Conservation Service and private individuals stocked private ponds.

At Sun Lakes, the three species were introduced together to meet both vegetation control and recreational fishing needs. The ponds lie within "green belt" and golf course areas, and the study assessed community structure and species biomass.

**Pond characteristics:**
- Eight artificial ponds, generally kidney shaped
- Surface area 0.44–1.02 ha (1.09–2.51 ac)
- Maximum depth 3.4–4.3 m (11–14 ft)
- Low shoreline development
- Sealed with clay or plastic liners, overlain by silt-sand substrate; gravel rare
- Interconnected by underground pipes

**Stocking history:** Unknown numbers of fingerling channel catfish (76–102 mm) and largemouth bass, and juvenile grass carp (152–203 mm), were stocked in 1971–1972. At least two [[Silver Carp]] were probably introduced as contaminants with grass carp. Green sunfish, [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Goldfish]] were also stocked at unknown dates and sizes.

**Methods:** In April 1978, [[Arizona State University]] personnel, working under AZGF direction, netted fish for one to three weeks to obtain brood stock (all grass carp were left in place). The ponds were then treated with 7.5% active-ingredient rotenone at 2 mg/l. Fish were collected for three consecutive days. All specimens from seven ponds were identified and counted, and day-one fish were measured (total length) and weighed. The eighth pond, originally fishless, held fish for restocking. Total biomass was estimated from mean day-one weights multiplied by total numbers over the three-day collection. Simple linear regressions (y = mx + b) tested relations between species biomasses and between pond area and biomass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biomass and standing crop:**
- Total biomass ranged from 174 to 1,159 kg/ha (mean 532 kg/ha).
- Grass carp comprised 50–83% of biomass (mean 67%).
- Channel catfish comprised 12–46% (mean 30%).
- Largemouth bass comprised 1–5% (mean 3%).
- Green sunfish comprised <0.1–1% (mean <1%).

**Comparison with other systems:** The authors compared Sun Lakes values with North American lake data (Carlander 1955). Mean standing crops in those lakes and reservoirs were approximately 15 kg/ha for channel catfish, 17 kg/ha for largemouth bass, 45 kg/ha for sunfishes, and 115 kg/ha for common carp. Sun Lakes channel catfish were at or above the reported maxima, and largemouth bass were about average. Sunfish were relatively low, while grass carp were exceptionally high compared with common carp.

Commercial polyculture ponds may reach tens of thousands of kg/ha, and under ideal monoculture conditions yields of up to 2 million kg/ha per year have been reported. The Sun Lakes yield is below the most productive commercial ponds but within the global aquaculture range of 55–6,000 kg/ha (31 countries). Standing stocks in natural low-desert southwestern streams ranged from 30 to 1,670 kg/ha, and in a southwestern canal from 490 to 1,020 kg/ha. Streams elsewhere rarely exceed 300 kg/ha. Sun Lakes biomass is therefore near the maxima recorded for other southwestern aquatic systems and well above values from other regions.

**Environmental drivers:** Pond 1 sits at the highest elevation and is the only pond receiving well water. It had the highest biomass per unit area. Biomass declined sequentially downflow through ponds 5, 6, 7, and 8, which the authors attribute to possible water quality deterioration or nutrient limitation in upstream ponds. Pond 4 received nutrient-enriched reclaimed wastewater, which may have boosted algal and macrophyte production and relative fish production. Grass clipping feeding by groundsmen and fertilizer runoff from golf course greens are additional uncontrolled variables.

## Key Findings

1. **Biomass and composition.** Mean total biomass was 532 kg/ha, with grass carp dominating. The highest pond biomass was 1,159 kg/ha in the 0.44-ha pond, comparable to some commercial culture operations.

2. **Size structure.**
   - Grass carp: mean total length 63–93 cm among ponds, limits 53–102 cm, mean weight 2.7–11.1 kg, low variability (CV ≤ 0.25 within ponds).
   - Channel catfish: numerically dominant in 4 of 7 ponds; mean total length 33–50 cm, individuals 13–56 cm; mean weight 0.310–1.060 kg. Small fish in two ponds suggested some natural recruitment.
   - Largemouth bass: most abundant species in 2 ponds; mean total length 19–27 cm; mean weight 0.054–0.226 kg.
   - Green sunfish: numerically dominant in one pond; mean weight 0.005–0.070 kg; biomass 0.1–4.4 kg/ha.

3. **Correlations.**
   - Largemouth bass biomass was positively and linearly related to grass carp biomass (y = 0.034x + 2.645, r = 0.87, N = 7, p = 0.025). This was the only significant species-biomass correlation.
   - Pond area was not significantly correlated with total or species biomass (r from −0.04 to −0.60).

4. **Mechanism proposed.** The authors attribute the largemouth bass–grass carp link to vegetation removal by grass carp, which made prey more vulnerable. Vegetation and forage fishes were virtually absent in the ponds, suggesting largemouth bass may have depleted their forage base.

5. **Angling effects.** Ponds 1–4 were closed to angling during daylight hours, and ponds 5–8 were open 24 hours daily.
   - Closed ponds averaged 745 kg/ha; open ponds averaged 373 kg/ha.
   - Largemouth bass: 19.5 vs. 11.5 kg/ha; channel catfish: 223 vs. 108 kg/ha.
   - Grass carp, which anglers did not take, showed a similar difference (500 vs. 252 kg/ha), so differential angling alone may not explain the pattern.

6. **Recruitment.** Grass carp had not recruited in any pond. Recruitment of channel catfish and sunfish was minimal, and small fishes of all species were largely absent. The authors conclude that the population as a whole showed low recruitment.

7. **Management implications.** Grass carp effectively controlled aquatic vegetation, but the study cannot evaluate interactive effects with other species. The authors note that grass carp growth followed the same among-pond trends as standing stock (Marsh and Dhaenens 1984), probably driven by similar factors.

8. **Related publication (book review).** The same journal issue reviews *Tales from Tiburon: An Anthology of Adventures in Seriland*, edited by Neil B. Carmony and David E. Brown. The anthology compiles first-hand accounts of four expeditions among the [[Seri People]] of the Gulf of California coast, from 1826 to 1921. The reviewer, Gary Nabhan, notes its value for documenting the Seri before the 1930s, while observing that the journals emphasize terrestrial wildlife more than marine life.
