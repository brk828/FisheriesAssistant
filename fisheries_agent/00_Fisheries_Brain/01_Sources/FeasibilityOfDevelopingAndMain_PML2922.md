# Feasibility of Developing and Maintaining a Sport Fishery in the Salt River Project Canals, Phoenix, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #ictaluridae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Report (No. 18, September 1995) by Brian R. Wright and Jeff A. Sorensen assesses whether the 61.4 km [[Arizona Canal]], part of the [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) canal system, can support a put-and-take sport fishery in metropolitan Phoenix. Monthly electrofishing (February 1992 to July 1994), stocking experiments with [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], water quality and benthic/plankton sampling, contaminant analysis, and a telephone public opinion survey were combined to evaluate biological potential, safety, and demand. The authors conclude that a canal fishery is feasible and recommend an [[Urban Canal Fishing Program]] with a stocking schedule and a special-use license, contingent on resolving liability concerns.

## Background & Description

The report opens with the expansion of canal-based recreational fishing in the western United States, citing large canal systems such as the [[Central Valley Project]], the [[California Aqueduct]], the [[All-American Canal]], and the [[Central Arizona Project]]. Marsh and Fisher (1987) estimated more than 11,000 km of canals in the desert southwest. Urban fishing license sales in Arizona rose from 2,500 in 1983 to 25,679 in 1994.

The SRP system includes 217 km of main canals and 1,497 km of lateral canals and ditches serving irrigation and municipal use across 10 cities and the Salt River Indian Reservation. The SRP canals are not managed as a sport fishery and are not stocked with game fish (except for research), though a statewide license is required to fish them. Fish in the canals originate largely from the Salt and Verde rivers above [[Granite Reef Diversion Dam]]. Since about 1989, SRP has stocked sterile [[White Amur]] to control aquatic vegetation, with about 15,000 stocked in 118 km of canals by March 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Arizona Canal flows west from Granite Reef Dam through Scottsdale, Phoenix, Glendale and Peoria. Five regular electrofishing sites (Sites 1, 2, 3, 5, 7) and alternate sites were sampled. Habitat was predominantly run (about 44 km), with about 13 km of pool and 4.4 km of riffle. Roughly 90.3% of the canal is concrete-lined.

**Barriers and inflows.** An [[Electric Fish Barrier]] at the canal head limits upstream movement of fish from the Granite Reef reach. Alum sludge from three municipal water treatment plants and periodic dewatering for maintenance affect habitat and fish populations.

**Water quality.** Temperatures peaked in August and water quality was generally adequate for warm-water species. Dissolved oxygen was lowest in winter at a partly dewatered section. Mean CHLA:PHEA ratios ranged from 1.1 to 1.4, indicating moderate primary production.

**Invertebrates and plankton.** Eighteen benthic macroinvertebrate taxa were recorded (standing stock generally below 200/m²), dominated by Pleuroceridae, oligochaetes and Corbicula spp. Eighteen zooplankton taxa were found, but densities were low (below 5 per 20 L).

## Key Findings

**Fish assemblage (Arizona Canal, Oct 1992 to July 1994)**
- 13,355 fish collected by electrofishing, representing 20 species in 10 families (stocked fish excluded from the resident count).
- Most abundant: Sonora sucker, desert sucker, threadfin shad, red shiner, white amur and largemouth bass; these six made up about 98% of the sample.
- Native suckers and forage fish were abundant (each more than 7,500 sampled), while game fish were scarce (about 3% of the sample; the Discussion cites 5.1%).
- Species richness was highest at the downstream end (Skunk Creek Drain, 19 species) and fell to 12 near Granite Reef Dam.
- Site 3 held about 35% of fish (about 25% at Site 1 and 9.5% at Site 7 in the summary).

**Immigration through the barrier (1991 to 1995)**
- 17 fish species were collected above the Granite Reef electric barrier, with 9 to 13 species per year.
- Most abundant above the barrier: desert sucker (39.7%), tilapia (16.5%), Sonora sucker (14.1%), channel catfish (13.0%), common carp (12.4%).

**Stocking experiments**
- 1,500 channel catfish were stocked in June 1993, 300 July catfish were tagged, and 2,200 rainbow trout were stocked in November 1993, all at Site 3.
- Recapture was highest within the first 6 weeks; some rainbow trout persisted at least 7 months and channel catfish up to 13 months.
- Neither species showed substantial growth or improved physiological condition over 5 to 12 months.
- Movement: about 99.4% of channel catfish and 95% of rainbow trout remained in their stocking reach within one year.
- Losses to lateral canals: 3 rainbow trout (0.1%) and 9 channel catfish (0.4%) were captured in five fish traps over 86 days.

**Contaminants**
- Analysis of 129 EPA priority pollutants found low or no concentrations; only minor pesticide residues, and mercury and PCBs were well below FDA Action Levels.
- Sample sizes were very small (typically 1 to 3 fish per composite), which the authors identify as a limitation.

**Public opinion survey (May 1994)**
- 68% of licensed anglers and 51% of the general public said they would be very or somewhat likely to use canals if a program were developed.
- Respondents preferred bass, catfish and trout; 62% of the combined sample was willing to pay for a special license, with a median of $10.71 among those willing.

**Projected use and revenue**
- Estimated annual use of about 753,100 person-use days, including 27,330 from licensed "very likely" users and 418,600 from non-licensed residents.
- Projected 129,538 new anglers, generating about $1.55 million at $12 per license (the authors note this is unlikely to be fully realized).

**Fishing access sites (5 recommended)**
- Riverwalk between Pima and Hayden roads (Scottsdale, Site 7).
- Paseo Racquet Park at 63rd Avenue and Thunderbird Road (Glendale, Site 2).
- G.R. Herberger Park (Phoenix, Site 5).
- Cortez Park/Metrocenter area (Alternate Site 3).
- Cortez Lake, Phoenix.

**Management recommendations**
- Administration: manage under the statewide program, the existing Urban Fishing Program, or as an independent [[Urban Canal Fishing Program]] with a special-use stamp; the authors favor an independent or stamp-based option.
- Enhancements: fishing access improvements, artificial habitat (tire-reefs, catfish houses), and reduced chemical and alum inputs.
- Stocking: channel catfish in summer and rainbow trout in winter (rainbow trout not expected to survive summer temperatures above 20 °C).
- Monitoring: creel surveys, seasonal water quality sampling, and electrofishing.
- Public safety: resolving liability between SRP, AGFD and the cities is identified as the first priority.
- Future research: extend surveys to the other SRP canals (for example, the South Canal), expand lateral trap coverage, and study sediments and alum effects.

**Conclusions**
- The authors conclude that the Arizona Canal's biological conditions can support a fishery and that the main barriers are public safety, liability, and conflicts with SRP operations.
