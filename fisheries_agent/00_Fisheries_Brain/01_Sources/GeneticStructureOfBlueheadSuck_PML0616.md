# Genetic Structure of Bluehead Sucker across the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR) draft final report, from [[Colorado State University]], examines the mitochondrial genetic structure of [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus (Pantosteus) discobolus*) across its range in the [[Colorado River Basin]] and the [[Weber River]], which drains into the closed [[Bonneville Basin]]. The work supports the Utah state management plan and the range-wide Conservation Agreement being developed among Arizona, Utah, and Wyoming. Because Utah's populations are disjunct, multiple management plans may be needed. The authors therefore used [[Phylogeography]] to assess how genetic diversity is partitioned among populations.

The central finding is that the Weber River population forms a distinct mtDNA lineage that should be protected regardless of its taxonomic status. Its formal taxonomic position remains unresolved and requires nuclear DNA analysis. Upper basin populations are genetically similar, though some drainages carry unique alleles. The report also documents a small amount of introgression from [[Desert Sucker]] and an unusually close mtDNA relationship with [[Mountain Sucker]].

## Background & Description

Bluehead Sucker is a specialized, mostly herbivorous catostomid in the subgenus *Pantosteus*. Its synapomorphies include broad, disc-shaped lips and strong cartilaginous jaw sheaths. The species ranges from the Colorado River headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado to the Grand Canyon in Arizona. It prefers high-gradient streams but can use larger rivers when conditions are favorable. Adults enter tributaries from spring through summer and spawn in April through May. Taxonomy has been contested: Smith (1966) placed the species in *Catostomus*, while Minckley (1973) used *Pantosteus*.

The report frames the study within the broader biodiversity debate. It argues that species-level criteria must be defined operationally before biodiversity assays begin ([[Sites and Crandall]], 1997). It also argues that [[Mitochondrial DNA]] alone is insufficient, and that independent [[Nuclear Intron Markers]] are needed to confirm species boundaries under the [[Genealogical Concordance]] principle. The authors combine the [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]] with life history and morphology. They note that the [[Biological Species Concept]] has limited phylogenetic utility.

Bluehead Sucker occupies only 45% of its historic upper Colorado River basin range (Bezzerides and Bestgen, 2002). It has not been federally listed, but Colorado, Idaho, Utah, and Wyoming classify it as "rare and of special concern." Principal threats are dams, habitat degradation, and non-native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biogeography:** All native Colorado River suckers belong to the tribe Catostomini, which diversified west of the Rocky Mountains. These lineages date to the mid-Miocene or late Oligocene (Minckley et al., 1986). The report notes that upper basin drainage histories may have allowed more gene flow between upper and lower basins than previously assumed.
- **Historic dispersal:** Desert Sucker haplotypes found in upper basin Utah drainages probably reflect ancient lower-to-upper basin dispersal, not recent movement.
- **Habitat and life history:** Blueheads show large morphological variation at single locations, particularly in caudal peduncle shape (Minckley, 1991).
- **Management context:** Utah's Bluehead populations are split between the Colorado River system and the isolated Weber River. Wyoming has three drainages (Green, Bear, Upper Snake). Funding came from the Endangered Species Mitigation Fund, matched 1:3 by a federal State Wildlife Grant.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and sequence characteristics**
- Populations sampled: 40 from 17 drainages across four states (Arizona, Colorado, Utah, Wyoming).
- Per-population sample size: 1 to 33 (mean 9.6, SD 6.67).
- In-group Bluehead Sucker: 384 individuals. Outgroups: 22 individuals across 10 OTUs. Total: 50 OTUs and 406 individuals.
- Markers: mtDNA ATP8 (168 bp) and ATP6 (673 bp), overlapping by 10 bp, for an 852 bp combined fragment.
- Variation: 796 monomorphic sites (93.4%), 56 polymorphic (6.6%), and 37 parsimony-informative (4.3%).
- Diversity: 43 unique haplotypes (one per 10.4 individuals); haplotype diversity h = 0.859 (SD 0.014); nucleotide diversity π = 0.0054 (SD 0.0004).
- Base frequencies: A = 0.28, G = 0.16, C = 0.26, T = 0.30.
- Neutrality and clock tests: the partition homogeneity test was non-significant (P > 0.47), so the two genes were combined. Sequences were neutral (HKA 0.32 < P < 0.94; McDonald-Kreitman 0.37 < P < 0.87) and evolved at a uniform rate. The best-fitting model was GTR.

**Drainage-level diversity**
- Lowest diversity: White River, with two haplotypes across 11 individuals (h = 0.182; mean pairwise difference 0.36 bp).
- Highest haplotype diversity: San Juan River (h = 0.839, k = 1.87) and upper Colorado River (h = 0.842, k = 1.96).
- Highest average pairwise difference: Weber River (k = 2.15), despite only moderate haplotype diversity (h = 0.514).
- Demographic history: significant Tajima's D and Fu's Fs values indicate population expansion in the upper basin Colorado River and the Little Colorado River.

**Divergence**
- Non-*Pantosteus* outgroups differ from *Pantosteus* by roughly 8–10% (the OCR text is partly garbled here).
- Most divergent *Pantosteus* OTU: Rio Grande Sucker, at 4.52%.
- Weber River form vs. other Bluehead: 1.44%.
- Little Colorado River (LCR) form vs. other Bluehead: 1.19%.
- Desert Sucker vs. other Bluehead: 1.1%. Desert Sucker vs. Weber River: 1.56%; vs. LCR: 1.62%.
- Weber River vs. LCR: 2.00%.
- Mountain Sucker vs. Bluehead: 0.01%, which is nearly indistinguishable.

**Phylogenetic structure** (majority-rule maximum parsimony consensus)
- **Clade 1:** Weber River Bluehead (haplotypes 12, 13, 32; all 15 individuals from two Weber River sites). It is sister to all other Bluehead haplotypes.
- **Clade 2:** Includes Grand Canyon haplotype 24 and haplotype 14 (sampled across Utah and lower basin sites), sister to two Desert Sucker haplotypes (42, 43). This clade makes *P. discobolus* paraphyletic.
- **Clade 3:** Haplotypes 29 and 31 from the Little Colorado River, representing the [[Zuni Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. d. yanowi*).
- **Clade 4:** The remaining 334 Bluehead individuals, with little geographic structure. Subclades 5 and 7 have reasonable bootstrap support.

**Management recommendations**
1. **Weber River form:** Clarify its status with nuclear DNA before a firm recommendation. It is clearly distinct from a management perspective and should be protected from habitat destruction. Formal taxonomic designation may help secure its long-term future.
2. **Integration of OTUs:** Analyze Desert Sucker and LCR populations together with Bluehead populations, using both mtDNA and nDNA.
3. **Upper Colorado River drainages:** Treat them as one management plan, but note that some drainages (Little Sandy and Henrys Fork in Wyoming; Duchesne, Yampa, San Rafael, and Westwater Canyon in Utah and Colorado) carry unique, low-frequency mtDNA alleles. These should be considered before any hatchery program.
4. **Mountain Sucker:** Assay the species across its western North American range to test for hybridization with Bluehead, using mtDNA and nDNA.
5. **Desert Sucker haplotypes:** Six of 384 Bluehead (1.6%) carried Desert Sucker haplotypes, all in Utah drainages. The authors judge this historic, low-volume dispersal to be of little management concern.

**Conclusions**
- The Weber River lineage is distinct and may be a subspecies or a separate species.
- Mitochondrial divergence alone cannot resolve species status, so nuclear intron markers are essential.
- The authors state that current data do not justify collapsing or expanding taxonomy.
