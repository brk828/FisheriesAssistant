# Trout Feed Costs, Hatchery Diet Change, and Stocking Survival in Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two linked Minnesota fisheries studies. The first, a cost review by [[Robert E. Schumacher]] (Aquatic Biologist, Fisheries Research Unit, Special Publication No. 3, February 1955), compares [[Trout Feed Costs]] and feed efficiency at state trout rearing stations over five years (1950 to 1954). It finds that a new [[Hatchery Trout Diet]] introduced in 1953 cut food cost per pound of trout produced by about 18 percent. The second study, a field report on the [[Survival Of Stocked Trout]], uses [[Creel Census]] and population estimates in southeastern Minnesota streams to show that fall-stocked [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] suffer high [[Winter Mortality]] and that anglers catch them soon after the season opens. The report recommends weighing hatchery costs against angler returns when choosing between fall and spring stocking.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Minnesota's state trout hatcheries raised [[Yearling Trout]] for stocking in streams and lakes. Feed is the major operating cost, so the study asked whether a changed diet and feeding method could lower cost per pound of trout produced without reducing fish size or growth.

**Stocked species.** Yearlings raised in 1950 to 1954 were composed of approximately 25 percent [[Brook Trout]], 50 percent [[Brown Trout]], 10 percent [[Rainbow Trout]], and 5 percent [[Lake Trout]]. The remaining share is not stated in the text. Most field work concerned brown and rainbow trout.

**Diet and feeding methods (1950 to 1953).** The diet was a wet mash of ground beef meat, a commercial trout pellet, and a fish cereal, averaging about 17 percent protein. The usual mix was 35 percent beef meat, 25 percent pellets, and 20 percent cereal, plus 2 percent salt. Fish were fed by hand from buckets or scoops, as much as they would eat, once or several times a day depending on fish size.

**Diet and feeding methods (1953 to 1954).** The new diet was a dry, tumbled mixture of 45 percent ground beef meat and 35 percent dry cereal, with particles about ⅛ to ⅜ inch across. Cereal ingredients included toasted soybean flour, cottonseed oilmeal, wheat middlings, dried skim milk, whitefish meal, dried brewers yeast, distillers dried corn solubles, and iodized salt. Feed was scattered over the pond and raceway area at or close to the recommended feeding rate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams.** The field component covered several southeastern Minnesota trout streams, including [[Dusche Creek]] (a tributary of the [[Root River]] about 40 miles southeast of Rochester), [[Diamond Creek]], [[North Branch Creek]], [[South Fork Crooked Creek]], [[Watson Creek]], [[Beaver Creek]], and [[Mill Creek]]. Stream lengths ranged from about 1 to 5 miles, and widths were generally between 12 and 32 feet.

**Survey methods.** Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]] with a direct-current portable unit (boat-mounted or hand-held electrodes). Populations were estimated by [[Mark-Recapture Estimate]] methods. Creel census days covered about one-third of the available fishing hours. Angler catch rates were calculated for each census day.

**Season and timing.** Fall stocking took place in September to October, and spring stocking in April to May. Creel surveys ran in the weeks after the legal season opened. Fall-stocked trout were mostly taken early in the season, when they were recently stocked and still concentrated.

## Key Findings

**Feed cost and conversion (hatchery study)**
- 1950 to 1952: average food cost was about $0.49 per pound of trout produced (the OCR-garbled value is probably $0.494), with an average feed conversion of 9.11 pounds of food per pound of trout.
- 1953 and 1954 (new diet): average food cost was $0.406 per pound, a reduction of 17.9 percent. Feed conversion was approximately 3.9 pounds of food per pound of trout (reading uncertain in OCR).
- Average size of yearling trout for stocking fell from 9.2 fish per pound (1950 to 1952) to 6.2 fish per pound (1953 to 1954), meaning larger fish were produced.
- Costs exclude food lost through spoilage or inedible waste, which the author did not attempt to correct.

**Stocking survival (field study)**
- Average winter mortality of fall-stocked brown trout across eight streams was 40.1 percent. Winter mortality for rainbow trout on three of these streams was reported as roughly 44 percent (OCR-garbled).
- Fall-stocked brown trout taken by anglers averaged 35.3 percent of the number stocked, compared with a higher share for spring-stocked fish.
- On Dusche Creek, unmarked resident brown trout had 62.4 percent winter mortality from fall stocking in October 1955 to April 1956. Standing crop in October 1955 was 124 trout per acre (164 pounds per acre).
- Diamond Creek: 42.5 percent of brown trout from summer stocking were lost over winter, and 33.3 percent mortality was recorded for rainbow trout. Standing crop was 14.5 pounds per acre.
- North Branch Creek: winter losses were 43.2 percent for brown trout and 43 percent for rainbow trout. Standing crop was 112.5 pounds per acre.

**Angler harvest and timing**
- Anglers caught about 55 percent of spring-stocked brown trout during the season on some streams, and 68.3 percent of fall-stocked brown trout on Dusche Creek were caught by anglers.
- Fall-stocked trout were caught quickly, often before the third week of fishing. Anglers on Dusche Creek took 86 percent of fall-stocked returns by the end of the third week.
- Spring-stocked fish of 15 months old were typically caught right after stocking, while fall-stocked 20-month-old trout could yield up to 50 percent more pounds to anglers in the following season.

**Economic trade-off**
- Holding fall-stocked trout over winter in the hatchery adds about three months of feeding. At a feed cost of $0.12 per pound and a conversion of about 1.0 pound of feed per pound gained, an added cost of approximately $0.12 per fish per pound gained was estimated.
- Assuming a 35.3 percent average catch, the author estimated an additional food cost of roughly $0.31 per fish caught (OCR-garbled reading).

**Overall conclusions**
- The new [[Hatchery Trout Diet]] lowered food cost and improved feed efficiency without reducing fish production.
- Fall stocking gives higher yields to anglers in the following season but carries substantial winter losses and extra hatchery cost. Management decisions should weigh these factors together.
- Smaller stocked fish (more per pound) are cheaper to raise, but the report does not directly assess their survival advantage.
