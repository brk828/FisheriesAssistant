# Feeding and Survival of Larval Razorback Suckers in Experimental Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether food limitation contributes to the recruitment failure of the endemic [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. In 1985, larvae were reared for eight weeks in 12 earthen ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, fertilized at three levels to create high, medium, and low food availability. Fertilization did not significantly change larval survival, which averaged 77.5 ± 6.3% across treatments. Growth, however, was greater at the two higher invertebrate densities. The authors conclude that nutritional deficiency is a plausible contributor to year-class failure at the lowest recorded densities of reservoir [[Zooplankton Density]] in Lake Mohave, but that starvation is unlikely at the higher densities observed there.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is an endemic fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its populations have declined, as documented by earlier researchers. Lake Mohave supports the largest known population, but the adults are old (average age over 30 years; McCarthy and Minckley 1987). Annual reproduction occurs, and larvae are produced in abundance, yet they disappear once they reach less than about 12 mm total length (TL; Marsh and Langhorst 1988). No successful year-classes have been documented.

[[Predation By Nonnative Fishes]] on eggs and larvae has been considered the most likely explanation for this failure. Food limitation is an alternative explanation, and it is well documented in marine fishes, where starvation or slowed growth increases predation risk. Similar year-class failures have been documented for [[Threadfin Shad]] in [[Lake Mead]], immediately upstream of Lake Mohave, under low nutrient inputs.

The experiment described here evaluated larval survival, growth, and feeding in artificial ponds where food availability was controlled through [[Pond Fertilization]]. Laboratory work by the same authors (Papoulias and Minckley 1990) complements the pond results.

**Methods (summary)**
- **Ponds:** 12 earthen ponds, 0.04–0.10 ha (mean 0.06 ± 0.01 SE), averaging 1 m deep, arranged in a stratified block design.
- **Fertilization treatments:** High (341 kg alfalfa pellets and 57 kg PO4 per ha), medium (170 kg and 28 kg per ha), and low (none). Ponds were filled with groundwater in February 1985.
- **Larval source:** Adult [[Razorback Sucker]] females from Lake Mohave were induced with [[Chorionic Gonadotropin]] (220 IU/kg). Eggs were fertilized with milt from wild males and incubated at 21°C.
- **Stocking:** On 25 February 1985, larvae of 9.4–10.7 mm TL (mean 9.96 ± 0.04 mm, n = 50) were stocked at 250,000 per ha. No supplemental feeding was provided.
- **Sampling:** Limnological and invertebrate samples were taken weekly from randomly selected ponds (three of four per treatment). Larvae were collected by plankton tows and night spotlight-and-dip-net sampling, which was attracted to their [[Phototaxis In Larval Fishes|phototactic]] behavior.
- **Diet analysis:** Gut contents were identified and measured. Prey selectivity was evaluated with the [[Strauss Linear Index]].
- **Statistics:** ANOVA with Tukey's HSD test; significance at P < 0.05.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water temperature and chlorophyll.** Surface water temperatures rose from 12°C in February to 17°C in April. Chlorophyll peaked within the first two weeks after filling, earlier in high-fertilization ponds. Differences among treatments were significant only in week 1.

**Invertebrate prey.** Invertebrate densities and biovolumes were highest in high-fertilization ponds, which peaked at week 6 for biovolume. Mean values were:

| Treatment | Invertebrate density (organisms/L) | Biovolume (mm³/m³) |
|---|---|---|
| High | 43.3 | 2,455 |
| Medium | 23.7 | 354 |
| Low | 12.5 | 257 |

Numbers in high-fertilization ponds exceeded those in low-fertilization ponds in weeks 2–3 combined and in week 4. Biovolumes did not differ significantly among treatments on any date. The dominant taxa in high-fertilization ponds were [[Cladocerans]], unidentified invertebrate eggs, and copepod nauplii and adults. Medium and low ponds were dominated by [[Rotifers]], nauplii, and ostracods, with cladocerans also present.

Compared with natural waters, all pond densities were relatively low.

**Salamanders.** [[Tiger Salamander]] larvae (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) colonized three of four ponds in each treatment. Salamander density and biomass varied widely and were not significantly correlated with treatment. Salamander biomass was positively correlated with razorback sucker survival (r = 0.66, increasing to r = 0.68 after adjustment), although this relationship was not statistically significant. Razorback larvae were found in some salamander stomachs.

**Lake Mohave comparison.** Zooplankton in Lake Mohave was low relative to these ponds in some years:
- 1982: 27.3–44.6 organisms/L at the sampling areas
- 1985: average 1.5 organisms/L where larvae were caught
- 1985 cutoff bay (larval habitat): less than 0.5 organisms/L

## Key Findings

**1. Survival did not differ among fertilization treatments.**
Survival after eight weeks was 67.4 ± 15.7% (low), 89.8 ± 6.8% (medium), and 77.0 ± 8.1% (high), with F = 1.60 and no significant difference. Survival was high even at the lowest invertebrate densities (about 12.5/L).

**2. Growth was greater at higher food densities.**
Larvae in medium- and high-fertilization ponds were significantly longer and heavier than those in low-fertilization ponds from week 3 onward, with few exceptions. High- and medium-fertilization treatments did not differ in size. Length-weight relationships were the same for all treatments: log10(WT) = −3.452 + 3.978 log10(TL), with r² = 0.98.

**3. Larval diets shifted with growth.**
- First foods were diatoms, detritus, and algae.
- By the end of week 1 (about 12 days post-hatch), rotifers, nauplii, cladocerans, eggs, and [[Chironomids]] appeared in the diet.
- Rotifers and chironomids dominated numerically in weeks 1–3, and cladocerans became more important from week 4 onward.
- Larger larvae selected cladocerans. Selection indices for other taxa were not significant.
- At week 1, 31 of 172 larvae (about 18%) had empty guts. Overall, empty guts were common in early larvae.

**4. Gut fullness was maintained through prey switching.**
Larvae longer than 15.0 mm TL had about 100 organisms in their guts, regardless of treatment. Food biovolume increased linearly with larval length (r = 0.54), while numbers of prey remained roughly constant. A larva needed to search only about 2.3 L (high fertilization) or 4.2 L (medium fertilization) to encounter 100 organisms.

**5. Mouth size and prey size were linked.**
Upper lip length (ULL) increased with total length for fish 10–26 mm TL: ULL = −0.16 + 0.04 (TL). Maximum prey width was smaller than the predicted maximum gape, suggesting that esophageal or other constraints limit prey size. Larvae smaller than 12.0 mm TL selected organisms about 0.1 mm wide. Larger larvae shifted to prey averaging about 0.3 mm wide by about 20.0 mm TL.

**6. Lake Mohave larvae showed evidence of food stress.**
- In 1985, 67% of 124 Lake Mohave larvae (mean 10.6 mm TL) had empty guts, compared with 20% of similarly sized larvae from the ponds.
- Lake Mohave fish contained about half as many prey as pond fish (13 vs. 25 per 10.8 mm larva; 50 vs. 100 per 13.9 mm fish).
- Larvae reached 16.0 mm TL in 69 days in the cutoff bay, compared with 49 days in unfertilized ponds, suggesting that low-density habitats still supported growth.

**7. Laboratory and other-species comparisons.**
Laboratory razorback larvae survived at food concentrations of 5–10 *Artemia* nauplii/L only at reduced rates during a [[Critical Period]] (about 8–19 days post-hatch at 18°C). The authors also compared survival across 14 fish species in a table of food density and survival. Razorback survival appeared high at relatively low food densities compared with several other species, suggesting that this species may use food efficiently.

**8. Critical period and temperature.**
Pond larvae still carried yolk 14 days after hatching, and none had yolk by 21 days. Pond temperatures were about 6°C cooler than laboratory containers, which likely slowed yolk assimilation. Larvae in Lake Mohave may therefore pass through the critical period later and survive longer on less food.

**9. Conclusions on recruitment failure.**
The authors conclude that year-class failure in Lake Mohave may well be attributable to nutritional deficiency at the lowest recorded densities of reservoir zooplankton. Starvation or food-related problems are unlikely at higher zooplankton densities, unless only a small percentage of organisms are of appropriate size to be eaten. Predation by nonnative fishes is not ruled out by these results.
