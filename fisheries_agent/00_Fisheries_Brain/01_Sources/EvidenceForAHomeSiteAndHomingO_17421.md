# Home Site and Homing Behaviour of Adult Yellowtail Rockfish (*Sebastes flavidus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sebastidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether adult [[Yellowtail Rockfish]] (*Sebastes flavidus*) in southeastern Alaska show a home site and homing ability. Fish were tagged at a shipwreck at [[Lena Point]] in [[Favorite Channel]], displaced to 11 release sites up to 35.0 km away, and recovered mainly by hook and line. Fish returned to the wreck from as far as 22.5 km, and the authors concluded that the species has a home site, that returns are directed rather than random, and that intensive localized fishing could cause long-term stock decline.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Yellowtail Rockfish]] (*Sebastes flavidus*, Ayres), a common demersal species along the western coast of North America, used in both sport and commercial fisheries.
- **Key concepts:** A *home site* is a locality to which an animal shows attachment or association (termed "localization" by Scott 1958). *Homing* is the return to a formerly occupied place rather than to equally probable alternatives, and implies some navigational ability (following [[Gerking 1959]] and [[Green 1971]]).
- **Management motivation:** Sport-diver and fisher observations suggested a disjunct distribution of adult schools in southeastern Alaska. If schools are discrete, as shown for [[Blue Rockfish]] (*S. mystinus*) in California, this would matter for fishery management.
- **Prior work:** Homing had been demonstrated for only six demersal marine fish species, mostly tidepool or reef fishes moving less than 100 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study site:** A large vessel wreck at Lena Point, Favorite Channel, near Juneau. Schools were known to occupy only sharply defined locations.
- **Study population:** 283 fish were scale-aged; the school was composed mainly of two year-classes (1960 and 1961). Captured fish measured 280-411 mm fork length (mean 355 mm) and were 7-16 years old (mean 9 years).
- **Release conditions:** Fish were transported by skiff in covered 114-litre barrels of sea water and released at 7-18 m depth, similar to capture depths. Distances from Lena Point ranged from 2.4 to 35.0 km.
- **Habitat contrast:** Release sites were either along the mainland coast (3 sites) or across open water (8 sites). Open-water stretches exceeded 100 m depth.
- **Fishing pressure:** Sport and commercial fishing effort in the area was substantial and, although unmeasured, exceeded the authors' effort in time and distribution.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Tagging used Floy FD-67 anchor tags in vinyl "spaghetti" tubing (7.6 cm), serially numbered and colour-coded.
- 337 fish were tagged and released between August 1969 and September 1970: 24 at Lena Point (controls) and 313 displaced to 11 sites.
- Holding treatments: about half released on tagging day; 127 held 3-13 days because of weather; 34 held 3 months to assess tag retention; 4 held 1 month for a November release.
- A US$2 reward was publicised to aid recovery.

**Recapture results**
- Overall, 74 of 337 fish (22.0%) were recaptured at the wreck; only 2 (0.6%) were recovered elsewhere (one at [[Vanderbilt Reef]], one near Barlow Point).
- Half of the control fish released at the wreck were recaptured there, some up to five times over spans of up to 9 months.
- Mainland release sites returned 45.9% (109 fish), with no recaptures elsewhere. Open-water sites returned 10.5% (228 fish), with 0.9% recovered at other sites.
- Fish released at [[Point Stephens]] and [[Point Terese]] (sites with existing resident schools) and fish held 3 months before release returned at high rates.
- Time to first recapture ranged from 4 to 42 days. The longest time at liberty was 428 days (released at Auke Bay, August 1969; recaptured at Lena Point, October 1970).

**Interpretation**
- **Home site:** Repeated recaptures of different fish from the same release over successive years, and of individuals up to five times, support Lena Point as a home site.
- **Homing:** Because fishing effort was roughly random yet returns concentrated at the wreck, the authors argued fish navigated toward home. Unpublished electronic tracking and diver observations were cited as further support for directed movement.
- **Strength of attachment:** High returns from resident-school sites and from captive-held fish suggest association is not merely due to accessibility or habitat suitability, and that memory persists for at least 3 months.
- **Barrier effect:** Recapture rates for mainland displacements were more than four times those for open-water displacements (45.9% vs 10.5%). The authors suggest deep open water is a partial barrier, either through traversal hazards or disorientation when visual contact with the substrate is lost, citing [[Quast 1968]] on visual dependence of California demersal fishes.
- **Taxonomic significance:** This is the first demonstration of home site and homing in the family Scorpaenidae among the few demersal fish groups studied. Earlier examples, such as [[Bathygobius soporator]], [[Opaleye]], [[Wooly Sculpin]], [[Tidepool Sculpin]] and [[Nassau Grouper]], involved displacements of generally under 100 m, whereas yellowtail rockfish returned from up to 22.5 km.

**Management implications**
- Slow growth and great longevity (Phillips 1964) combined with site fidelity mean an intensive localized fishery on an adult stock could easily cause long-term decline in abundance.
- Discrete, site-attached schools would need to be managed as separate units.
