# Tilapia Culture for Food and Business (Cultivo de la Tilapia para Alimentación y Negocio)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tilapia

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language extension bulletin, published in the *Boletín de Piscicultura Rural* (Vol. XII, 1963), reprints a practical guide by S. W. Ling (FAO-ETAP fish culturist) and Ariya Sidhtimunka (acting head of the Bangkhen Fish Station) on raising [[Tilapia]] in [[Thailand]]. The guide was produced by the Thai [[Department of Fisheries]] with [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) cooperation and equipment and technical support from the [[Mutual Security Agency]] (MSA). It presents tilapia as a fast-growing, prolific food fish that suits small-scale farmers, and gives step-by-step instructions for pond culture and rice-field culture, fertilization, stocking, supplementary feeding, harvest, and seed supply through community and family broodstock ponds.

The bulletin is the tilapia article within a larger issue whose other contents (water-hydrobiology, a commercial fish farm project, a State of Mexico fish plan, and water hyacinth control) are not included in the provided text.

## Background & Description

**Biological attributes claimed for tilapia** (as stated in the source):
- **Growth:** About four months from young fish to roughly 150 g, a good table size.
- **Reproduction:** Sexual maturity at about four months; breeding possible year-round at intervals of two to three months. One well-fed adult pair may produce up to 10,000 fish per year.
- **Yield:** About 3,300 kg of fish per hectare per year in small ponds.
- **Tolerance:** Lives in fresh or brackish water, and in ditches, swamps, tanks, and muddy surface water such as rice fields.
- **Diet:** Feeds on small animals and aquatic plants; accepts supplementary feed. Its foraging stirs bottom humus, releasing organic matter that breaks down into fertilizer.
- **Food quality:** Firm, tasty flesh with few bones; suitable for fresh consumption, drying, salting, smoking, and pickling.

**Origin and introduction:**
- The source states that tilapia is native to tropical waters of South Africa.
- About 20 years before the text, a few fish were introduced to Indonesia, where they multiplied quickly. The Indonesian government subsequently promoted their culture, and they have supplied thousands of tons of protein food each year since the war.
- After World War II, tilapia spread through Southeast Asia.
- The Thai Department of Fisheries imported fish from [[Penang]] on 18 August 1949 and about 200 from [[Singapore]] roughly two years before the text, and local farmers confirmed their suitability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond culture site requirements:**
- Ponds should hold water at least 30–40 cm deep year-round and must not flood during the rainy season.
- A typical farm or family pond is elongated, about 400 m² (one *rai*) and 50 cm deep.
- Inlets and outlets are screened with fine bamboo mesh to prevent escape and predator entry.

**Pre-stocking preparation:**
- Remove undesirable aquatic plants such as water hyacinth, water lettuce, water lilies, and lotus.
- Remove predators, including snakehead (*Ophiocephalus* sp.), catfish (*Clarias*), "plabu," and turtles, by trapping, netting, or draining followed by rotenone and lime. Wait at least 7–10 days after rotenone before stocking.

**Rice-field requirements:**
- Fields must hold at least 12 cm of water for a minimum of four months, be well bordered to prevent overflow and seepage, and ideally sit near the farmhouse and on slightly sloped bottoms for drainage.
- Each hectare needs a fish refuge ditch about 1 m wide and 35 cm deep along the edges; for fields under half a hectare, the ditch length should be about half the field's perimeter.

**Water quality:** Decomposing uneaten feed, especially in hot weather, degrades water quality and harms fish. The source warns against fertilizers containing toxic substances such as calcium cyanide.

**Ecosystem roles:** The text describes tilapia as a control agent for insect pests in ponds and rice fields, a partial check on mosquito larvae, and a grazer that restrains excessive growth of submerged aquatic plants.

## Key Findings

**1. Pond fertilization**
- Apply about 20 kg of animal manure per 1,600 m² every four to five days until the water turns green and submerged plants grow well, then once every 20 days.
- Keep two 30 kg manure heaps year-round at opposite pond corners, surrounded by three or four bamboo stakes to slow dispersal.
- Combine more than one type of manure (cattle, pig, poultry, bedding, kitchen waste, green manure).

**2. Stocking rates**
- For a 1,600 m² pond: 100–150 adults or 400–600 juveniles.
- Ponds with predators should be stocked with fish of at least 8 cm length.
- Stock on any day of the year except perhaps the very hot summer, preferably early morning or late afternoon. Acclimate fish by mixing transport water with pond water before releasing them slowly.

**3. Supplementary feeding in ponds**
- Use cheap feed such as rice bran, coconut meal, or peanut cake at about 2% of fish body weight per day to accelerate growth.
- Large fish may also be given duckweed (Lemnaceae).
- Plant water spinach (*Ipomoea*) along the banks for shade, refuge, and feed, covering no more than one-twentieth of the water surface.

**4. Harvest (ponds)**
- Fish reproduce quickly, so overcrowding is a risk. Carrying capacity is limited by natural food; overstocked fish grow slowly and may die in large numbers.
- Once the pond reaches capacity, harvest mature fish about every two weeks, a few at a time, so juveniles keep growing.
- Harvest begins about two months after stocking adults, or five months after stocking juveniles. Cast nets or seine nets are typical.

**5. Rice-field culture**
- Timing depends on rice transplanting and fry size. Stock 3–4 cm fry about three weeks after transplanting, or 5–6 cm fry at least four weeks after.
- Stocking density: 18,750–25,000 fish per hectare for 3–4 cm fry; 12,500–15,625 per hectare for 5–6 cm fry.
- Apply fertilizer about 20% above normal rice-field rates at the usual time. Supplementary fertilizer, when needed, is about one-quarter of the initial amount. Before applying chemical fertilizer, drain the field so fish retreat into the ditches, then reflood two days later.
- Supplementary feed is unnecessary for the first 1–1.5 months. Afterward, apply rice bran, peanut cake, or duckweed about once every two days at roughly 1% of total fish weight, applied in the morning and soaked if windy.
- Harvest coincides with rice harvest, when draining concentrates fish in the ditches. Large fish are taken first, leaving small fish to grow.

**6. Seed supply system**
- **Initial supply:** Government stations at Bangkhen, Bung Borapet, Kwan Payao, and Nong Harn hold stocks for distribution; requests go to the Department of Fisheries in [[Bangkok]].
- **Family broodstock ponds:** A pond of about 50 m² and 50 cm deep, located near the house, can supply a family with 20–30 pairs of good breeders.
- **Communal nursery ponds:** Cooperatively managed ponds can supply seed to village members. A 1,000 m² pond at 50 cm depth can meet the restocking needs of about 20 families.

**7. Technical outlook**
- The authors acknowledge that tilapia culture was new in Thailand and that the bulletin should be revised with results from farm operations. The Department invites practical feedback from farmers and extension workers.
