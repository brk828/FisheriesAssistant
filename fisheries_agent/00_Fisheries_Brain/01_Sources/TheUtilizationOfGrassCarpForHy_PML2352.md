# Grass Carp Control of Hydrilla in Lake Baldwin, Florida, with Notes on Brook Trout Ranching

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1981 *Journal of Fish Biology* paper by J. V. Shireman and M. J. Maceina (School of Forest Resources and Conservation, University of Florida) on the use of [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) to control the invasive submersed plant [[Hydrilla]] (*Hydrilla verticillata*) in [[Lake Baldwin]], an 80 ha lake in Orlando, Florida. The study tracked fish growth, capture success, consumption and plant standing crop from 1978 to 1980. Hydrilla was effectively controlled when grass carp biomass exceeded roughly 130 kg/ha. The authors propose three integrated vegetation-management strategies. The excerpt also contains the opening of a separate paper on [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) ranching in the Matamek River.

## Background & Description

Grass carp were introduced to Florida waters as a biological control agent for aquatic plants. Earlier fingerling stockings (100–150 mm total length) suffered heavy predation until fish exceeded about 300 mm. In 1974, fingerlings were stocked in Lake Baldwin, but hydrilla was not controlled. A selective [[Rotenone Sampling]] effort estimated the surviving population at only 3 grass carp per hectare. In 1978, 24 fish over 300 mm were restocked. Lake Baldwin was the only Florida lake where grass carp numbers, growth and hydrilla standing crop were monitored closely.

Lake Baldwin is adjacent to the U.S. Naval Training Center. The bottom sediment is 60% sand and 40% organic matter by area. Before 1980, submersed vegetation covered about 80% of the lake, dominated by hydrilla. Other plants included [[Vallisneria]] (*Vallisneria americana*), cattail, pickerelweed and water hyacinth. A water control structure at the outlet limited water-level fluctuation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors stress that the vegetation removed from a water body should match its use. Irrigation canals need full clearance, while multi-use lakes need management plans that protect recreation and fish habitat. Florida fishery biologists agree that aquatic vegetation supports sport fish, but the optimum amount for fish production is unresolved. Arkansas takes the opposite view and removes all submersed plants.

Laboratory work by the authors links vegetation cover to fish condition:
- [[Bluegill]] and [[Redear Sunfish]]: condition factors are unaffected until vegetation fills most of the water column.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (>250 mm): low condition factors occur when hydrilla coverage exceeds about 30%. Smaller bass are unaffected until coverage exceeds about 50% (Colle & Shireman, 1980).

Grass carp consumption rates for fish over 1 kg in natural lakes were largely unknown when this study was conducted. Smaller fish (under about 600 mm) are known to eat hydrilla in large amounts relative to body weight.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and capture**
- Between December 1979 and August 1980, 268 grass carp were captured in Lake Baldwin.
- A modified gill net–haul seine was the most efficient method when a school was located. One haul yielded 123 fish, and at least 75 more jumped the net.
- Electrofishing captured 29 fish, at rates of 0.5 to 3.3 fish per hour.
- Gill netting outperformed electrofishing in winter.

**Growth**
- Growth was rapid over the first two years. Fish from the 1978 stocking reached a mean of 9.15 kg and 876 mm total length after two years, from a stocking weight of about 0.09 kg.
- Growth by interval:

| Years after stocking | Weight (kg) | Length (mm TL) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (stocking) | 0.09 | N/A |
| ~1 | 0.79 | 408 |
| ~2 | 3.00 | 592 |
| ~3 | 5.25 | 720 |
| ~4 | 7.25 | 807 |
| ~5 | 9.15 | 876 |

- Weight-length slopes did not differ significantly from 3.0 (P < 0.05). Slopes differed between the sexes of fish over 200 mm, with females showing steeper slopes than males.
- The growth rate was slower than that reported for Lake Baldwin by Colle et al. (1978). Fish in Lake Wales also grew more slowly than those in Lake Baldwin (Shireman et al., 1980).

**Hydrilla consumption and control**
- Grass carp larger than 6 kg consumed about 20–25% of their body weight in hydrilla per day. The source OCR garbles the exact range.
- Gut analysis showed hydrilla made up 8–10% of total body weight. The authors estimate hydrilla passed through the gut in about 12 hours, so 16–20% of body weight per day was consumed.
- Grass carp standing crop was about 8,000 kg in April 1979, when hydrilla control was already evident. Control was effective at about 130 kg of fish per hectare, and hydrilla was clearly controlled once biomass exceeded about 130–136 kg/ha.
- Hydrilla volume declined steadily after June 1979. Coverage fell from about 62% (June 1978) to under 8% by September 1980.
- The winter 1979 hydrilla standing crop was expected to reach 1.2 million kg (normal for the area), but fell below 0.5 million kg.
- Hydrilla was removed within 22 months, and large areas were free of vegetation within 9 months.

**Management strategies proposed**
1. **Full clearance:** stock enough grass carp to remove vegetation completely (demonstrated at 34 fish/ha in this study).
2. **Slow reduction:** stock low numbers, reaching the desired level in three to four years, then adjust numbers. A stocking rate of about eight fish/ha could reduce hydrilla to roughly 50% of its standing crop within three years.
3. **Integrated control:** apply herbicides or mechanical methods to reach target levels in year one, then stock only enough grass carp to offset regrowth. Overstocking means costly and time-consuming [[Fish Removal]].

The authors assumed grass carp up to 3 kg eat 100% of their body weight per day, with rates declining with size. Hydrilla die-back in winter was taken as 30% of the October maximum, and grass carp mortality was assumed negligible in the first three years.

**Cautions from other studies**
- In Lake Wales, grass carp increased hydrilla initially by grazing. Control failed after a second stocking, and about 12 fish per hectare remained after a rotenone sample by the Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission.
- Where several plant species are available, food selectivity matters. Fowler & Robson (1978) found grass carp converted diverse communities to a few unpalatable species.
- Stott & Robson (1970) suggested a 50% plant reduction is desirable.

**Brook trout ranching (separate paper fragment)**
- An experiment by F. G. Whoriskey examined stocked brook trout in the Matamek River, using fish from spring and early summer releases. The excerpt is truncated.
- Results reported include that stocked fish in the 2+ and 3+ age classes showed the largest percentage of anadromous (sea-run) fish. Trout that were fed in the estuary showed more maturation and a shift toward sea-run behavior.
- Brook trout are known to be anadromous in some parts of their range, but in the Matamek River, suitable spawning habitat was limited.
