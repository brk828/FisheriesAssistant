# Red Cedar River Report: Chemistry and Hydrology

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #water-chemistry #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This 1968 report from the [[Michigan State University]] Museum (Biological Series, Vol. 4, No. 2) describes the chemistry and hydrology of the [[Red Cedar River]], a warm-water, highly buffered stream in south-central Michigan. It was written as the physical and chemical baseline for later papers on biological production. The authors (Ball, Linton and Kevern) studied chronic, low-level pollution across a 48-km study section divided into five ecological zones, tracking discharge, turbidity, conductivity, alkalinity, dissolved oxygen, and nitrogen and phosphorus over several years.

The central conclusion is that the river is clear at base flow but suffers excessive turbidity during rapid runoff. Its nutrient loads are high and are mostly flushed out during floods, when the biota can least use them. Declining base flow, combined with diurnal oxygen swings, threatens the biota. The authors judge that continued urban growth will keep increasing the nutrient burden unless waste disposal practices change fundamentally.

## Background & Description

The study rests on the premise that a stream is an open system. Its biota depends largely on allochthonous (externally supplied) nutrients, and it is exposed to regular inputs of human waste. The authors separated such wastes into three classes: acutely toxic, sub-lethal stress, and no apparent effect. They focused on the second class, chronic low-level pollution in a warm-water stream.

**Study design**

- The 48-km reach was divided into five contiguous zones:
  - Zone I: 5.6 km, from Farm Lane bridge (MSU campus) to Okemos Road.
  - Zone II: 13.7 km, from Okemos Road to Zimmer Road below Williamston.
  - Zone III: 4.0 km, from Zimmer Road to the Williamston dam.
  - Zone IV: 6.4 km, largely the reservoir from the dam to Dietz Road.
  - Zone V: 19.3 km, up to Van Buren Road.
- Five planned phases covered pollution sources, suspended-material transport, primary production on artificial substrates, bottom-fauna production, and fish populations. This report covers the physical and chemical components.
- Hypotheses tested included whether turbidity, conductivity, alkalinity, and nutrient concentrations relate to discharge, and whether tributary discharge scales with drainage area.
- The [[Sampling Schedule]] was a stratified random block design by Don W. Hayne. It set the wet season as February 15 to June 5, and the dry season as the rest of the year.

**River characteristics**

- The Red Cedar arises in Cedar Lake and flows northwesterly through Livingston and Ingham counties to join the [[Grand River]] at Lansing.
- Twelve major tributaries drain about 122,000 ha (472 sq mi). The study section drains about 92,000 ha (355 sq mi).
- Width ranges from about 8 to 24 m, with a mean gradient of about 0.45 m/km.
- Headwaters drain marsh and wetland and have been dredged and straightened. Downstream land use has shifted over 50 years toward intensive agriculture, urbanization, and industry.

**Methods (summary)**

- Phosphorus was measured by a modified acidified ammonium molybdate method, separating total, dissolved (0.45 µm filtered), and sestonic forms.
- Nitrogen was measured as ammonia by distillation and as oxidized forms by reduction, both read colorimetrically.
- Alkalinity and pH followed [[Standard Methods]]. Free CO2 was derived from pH and alkalinity.
- Dissolved oxygen used the Winkler method (later with the Alsterberg modification).
- Temperature was recorded continuously near Okemos. Conductivity and turbidity were measured with portable meters and a colorimeter.
- Discharge data came from the [[United States Geological Survey]] gauge at Farm Lane bridge. Tributary discharge was estimated with Price current meters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and discharge**

- Tributaries supplied about 90% of estimated total discharge at Farm Lane (Feb 1958 to Feb 1959).
- During the high-water stage (Feb 15 to June 5), tributaries contributed about 70%. Areas not drained by tributaries (about 20%) have low storage capacity.
- Base-flow tributary discharge totaled about 15% more than the Farm Lane record, implying groundwater recharge, irrigation withdrawal, and evapotranspiration losses.
- The annual mean discharge fell by about 75% over the 18 years to 1964. The authors attributed this to lower precipitation and urban groundwater pumping.
- Glacial drift underlying the basin has low storage and low intake. Intensive farming therefore produces rapid runoff. In the [[Deer Creek]] and [[Sloan Creek]] studies, about 60% of precipitation from a 3-hour storm became direct runoff within 11 hours. Deer Creek peaked at 27.72 tons of sediment per hour.

**Temperature and ice**

- Maximum temperatures typically occur in early or mid-August, with mean maxima in the mid-70s °F.
- The highest daily temperature recorded during the study was 87 °F in July 1964.
- Ice cover forms in late November or December and persists until late February or early March, breaking up with spring floods.

**Chemical setting**

- The river is a highly buffered alkaline system. Bicarbonate is the main contributor to alkalinity.
- Turbidity at base flow is typically 10 to 20 units. The peak reading was 387 units on September 4, 1961, after highway construction runoff.
- Suspended material selectively absorbs blue and green light more strongly than red. Red transmission always exceeded green and blue.

**Pollution sources**

- The [[Williamston Sewage Treatment Plant]] contributed about 2.48 metric tons of phosphorus per year.
- Two tributary categories were identified:
  - Sanitary- or septic-dominated tributaries, such as [[Lake Lansing Drain]] and [[Kalamink Creek]], with loads independent of discharge.
  - Agricultural or wooded tributaries, with loads proportional to runoff.
- [[Wilmarth Drain]], a tiled interceptor, added an estimated 3.31 metric tons of phosphorus, about one-third more than the sewage plant's annual contribution.

## Key Findings

**1. Physical and chemical baseline**

- Specific conductivity at base flow is generally 500 to 600 µmhos/cm and falls as discharge rises, reflecting dilution.
- pH ranges from 7.5 to 8.4. Lower values occur during rapid runoff and ice cover.
- Methyl orange alkalinity ranged from 160 to 300 mg/L and is inversely related to discharge. From July 12, 1957 to July 7, 1958, the mean was 237 mg/L (SD 42.3; range 112 to 298).

**2. Dissolved oxygen extremes**

- Strong diurnal oxygen pulses occur in summer. Dissolved oxygen reaches supersaturation in mid-afternoon, then falls to about 3.0 to 3.5 mg/L by midnight. Pre-dawn levels are about 2.5 to 3.5 mg/L.
- Reduced summer flow is making this situation worse every year. The authors warned the environment "may rapidly become uninhabitable for most of the present biota" if flow declines further.

**3. Nitrogen timing**

- Inorganic nitrogen loads peak during winter ice cover and high discharge, when biological uptake is lowest.
- During a July 3, 1958 storm, oxidized nitrogen (NO2 + NO3) rose from 0.11 to 2.70 mg/L at peak discharge, then fell to 0.06 mg/L by July 31.
- Ammonia builds to about 0.3 mg/L under ice and about 0.1 mg/L after heavy rain. Below the Williamston outfall it reached 0.16 mg/L and dropped to trace levels within the next 0.5 km.

**4. Phosphorus loading**

- The total annual load past Farm Lane was 9,763 kg (9.76 metric tons) of elemental phosphorus, equal to about 106 g/ha/yr. This is roughly 130 tons of 18% superphosphate fertilizer.
- Tributaries contributed about 50% of the total annual load. The load increased by 3.31 metric tons between Hagadorn Road and the terminal station.
- During February–March 1958, about 60% of annual discharge carried about 65% of the annual phosphorus load. Three floods lasting 31 days carried about 45%.
- About 89% of phosphorus in drift material moved through the river in under 60 days per year. Drift contained about 2.2% of the total phosphorus moving downstream.
- Regression models relating phosphorus to discharge were:
  - Total P (kg) = −4.93 + 32.84 × discharge (million m³)
  - Dissolved P (kg) = 0.62 + 14.48 × discharge (million m³)
- Mean annual phosphorus yields were 46.29 g/ha (total) and 27.95 g/ha (dissolved). Agricultural areas yielded 31.78 and 15.17 g/ha/yr respectively.
- Dairy watersheds (Coon and Herron Creeks) exported less phosphorus than corn and wheat watersheds (Middle and East Branches).

**5. Downstream enrichment and removal**

- Phosphorus enrichment increases downstream, but at low summer flow nearly all phosphorus from the sewage effluent was removed within the first kilometer. The authors say this exceeds what dilution alone would explain.
- Below 50 cfs in 1957, phosphorus concentrations exceeded 100 µg/L after the sewage outfall.

**6. Comparison with other streams**

- Total phosphorus transport in the Red Cedar (106 g/ha/yr) fell between the heavily polluted Yahara River (278 g/ha/yr) and agricultural drainages such as the Kaskaskia River (15 g/ha/yr).

**7. Conclusions and management outlook**

- The authors describe the Red Cedar as a warm-water, highly buffered alkaline stream that is usually clear but turbid during rapid runoff.
- Declining discharge has pushed the river to a critical base-flow level. Low flow, combined with oxygen extremes, threatens the biota.
- Williamston, Meridian Township and East Lansing improved waste collection, and a larger East Lansing treatment plant reduced the nutrient load. However, continued urban growth will keep increasing the nutrient burden unless waste disposal systems change fundamentally.
- The fish study was summarized in [[Linton and Ball]] (1965). Benthic and other biota were to be reported separately, and the fish themselves are not addressed in this report.
