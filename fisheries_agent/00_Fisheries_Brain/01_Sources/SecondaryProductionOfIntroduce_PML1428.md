# Secondary Production of an Introduced Asiatic Clam in a Central Arizona Canal

#malacology #freshwater-biology #bivalvia #corbicula

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies the [[Secondary Production]] and [[Life History]] of an introduced population of the [[Asiatic Clam]] ([[Corbicula Fluminea]]) in a small earthen canal in the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]], central Arizona. Sampling over a 12-month period (November 1981 to October 1982) found a mean density of 2,255 m⁻², a single annual spawning, a two-year life span and overlapping cohorts. Annual secondary production by the [[Size-Frequency Method]] was 25.62 g DM m⁻² and cohort production by a composite [[Allen Curve]] was 29.32 g DM m⁻². The author describes these as among the highest single-species estimates reported for molluscs, and argues that clams are a major functional component of [[Canal Ecosystems]] in the arid Southwest.

## Background & Description

The Asiatic clam is a freshwater bivalve native to Asia. It was first recorded in North America in 1938 and is now established in most major rivers of the subcontinent. In California it was first found in abundance in 1953, and it has been present in Arizona since 1956, when it appeared in Phoenix irrigation canals. It is now abundant in lakes, rivers and canals throughout the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]].

The species is an economic food resource in its native range. In introduced areas, however, it is a serious nuisance because it interferes with the efficient transport and use of water. Ecologically, it transforms detritus and dissolved CaCO₃ into animal biomass and shell, and it becomes an energy and nutrient source after death. Published life-history accounts vary with local conditions such as temperature, substrate and water chemistry.

Flowing water in the arid southwestern U.S.A. is increasingly carried in canals. These systems support diverse fish assemblages (see [[Fish Communities]]) and macroinvertebrate assemblages that differ from those of natural lotic systems in the region. [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] in these canals are dominated by Corbicula. The study aimed to estimate secondary production in a central Arizona canal and to improve understanding of clam ecology and of canal ecosystem function.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Sampling took place in a 100-m reach of Lateral 9.5 of the [[Consolidated Canal]] system in Mesa, Arizona. The 15.2 km earthen canal conveys irrigation and floodwater. It is trapezoidal in cross-section, with side slopes of 1.5:1, a mean water width of 7.7 m, a mid-channel depth of 0.95 m and a mean velocity of 0.07 m s⁻¹. Flow is relatively constant through an 11-month operational period. In autumn the canal is stilled and partly dewatered for maintenance, but it is not dried. The substrate is predominantly silt, with minor sand and rare fine gravel.

**Conditions.** Algae and macrophytes were sparse during the study. Midday water temperature ranged from 12.5 °C (January) to 30.5 °C (September).

**Sampling.** Benthos was collected on 10 dates from November 1981 to October 1982 using a Ekman grab (232 cm², hand-operated, to at least 15 cm depth). Each date yielded five samples (three mid-channel, one north side slope, one south side slope), which were preserved in 10% formalin and returned to the laboratory. Preliminary collections (N = 20) showed relatively uniform clam distribution, and N = 5 gave acceptable variance. Standard errors of monthly means rarely exceeded 50% of the mean, and no significant difference was found between mid-channel and side-slope samples.

**Comparison habitats.** Dense aggregations of Corbicula in sediment mounds, reported from the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] in California and from large Phoenix-area canals, were never observed in this small channel.

## Key Findings

**Population density and structure**
- Monthly mean density ranged from 215 ± 52 m⁻² (October 1982) to a February 1982 peak (the value is garbled in the scan). The annual mean was 2,255 m⁻².
- Density was low in November, rose rapidly to February, declined gradually to June and then stayed constant.
- Mean shell length (SL) stayed near 2 mm through the period of greatest abundance and into April. It then grew linearly to a maximum monthly mean of 18.0 mm in October 1982.

**Recruitment and growth**
- Recruitment occurred mainly from November to February, coinciding with peak numbers. Patterns suggest continuous recruitment in autumn and winter, with slow growth during that period.

**Reproduction and life cycle**
- Marsupial larvae occurred only in adult clams (>14 mm SL) and only in June and July. Reproduction therefore appears to be a single, discrete mid-summer event, with recruitment following several months later.
- Clams do not spawn in their first year of life. The author describes this as a once-per-year pattern, matching populations in Kentucky and southern California.
- Voltinism was less clear. Two size modes appeared in June and thereafter. The author concludes that the larger clams were a cohort recruited in 1980–1981 that remained in deeper sediment, where they escaped sampling, and migrated toward the surface in spring. Reported life span was two years.
- In a February 1983 sediment series (10 samples in 5 cm increments to 30 cm), clams >20 mm SL were most numerous at 15–20 cm depth. This supports the vertical migration hypothesis. Horizontal migration and transport by drift were judged unlikely.

**Secondary production (size-frequency method)**
- Annual secondary production (P) was 25.62 g DM m⁻² yr⁻¹ (256 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Mean annual standing crop (B̄) was 9.50 g DM m⁻², giving a turnover ratio (P/B) of 2.70.
- About 40% of P was attributed to clams >20 mm SL. The author treats P as an underestimate because large clams were poorly sampled during colder months.
- Assuming a 2.5-year life span would give P = 20.48 g DM m⁻². The author notes that the net effect of the two error sources is unknown.

**Cohort production (composite Allen curve)**
- The composite curve combined 0–1-year-olds from the 1981–1982 cohort with 1–2-year-olds from the 1980–1981 cohort.
- Cohort production (P_c) was 29.32 g DM m⁻², with P_c/B̄ = 5.81. This is slightly higher than the size-frequency estimate, and the close agreement is taken as support for the life-history interpretation.
- The author notes that the Allen-curve method has rarely been applied to macroinvertebrates, having generally been used for fish.

**Methods and data**
- Dry mass was estimated from the relation log DM = −2.118 + 3.117 (log SL), derived from Aldridge and McMahon. It was checked against a direct determination on a fresh subsample (SL 2–25 mm, N = 60; the verification error is garbled in the scan).
- Reproductive activity was assessed on 187 clams >10 mm SL, sampled from all months.
- Production was corrected for the cohort production interval (CPI = 730 days), following the [[Hynes Method]] modification for multivoltine populations.

**Discussion and ecosystem role**
- Lateral 9.5 clams differ from populations elsewhere in having a single annual spawning, reaching maturity at a larger size, and having a maximum size of about 28 mm (as reported in the Discussion). The author attributes the differences to local factors such as temperature, substrate and water chemistry.
- The high P/B values and the production estimates place Corbicula among the most productive molluscs reported. The author argues that the species has a substantial role in [[Nutrient Cycling]] and in the transfer of particulate organic matter. Filter feeding, pseudofaeces production and shell accumulation are all identified as pathways.
- Pseudofaeces may provide detrital food for fish, including [[Buffalo Fish]] species. The author notes that few other predators use clam tissue significantly.
- Arid southwestern streams experience periodic [[Flash Flooding]], which can greatly reduce or eliminate benthic populations. Canals are stable in this respect, which the author argues may favour the accumulation of large, heavy-bodied bivalves.
- The author concludes that canals should be recognized as important sites of secondary production, and that the role of clams in organic matter processing and sedimentation deserves further quantification in both canals and natural lotic systems.
