# Journal of Arizona Academy of Science, Vol. 1 (October 1959): Multi-Paper Excerpt

#arid-ecology #vegetation-change #arroyo-cutting #scorpion-venom #deceleration-biomechanics

## Executive Summary

This scanned issue of the *Journal of Arizona Academy of Science* (Vol. 1, October 1959) contains three unrelated items:

1. **A short immunization study on scorpion venom antiserum (Anavenom)** using guinea pigs, with two tables and a literature list.
2. **A long historical-ecological essay by James Rodney Hastings** on whether southeastern Arizona's grasslands, marshes, springs and streams have really changed since about 1880. The paper concludes that overgrazing played an important role, that climate cannot be ruled out, and that the "Arizona legend" of change is partly exaggerated but broadly correct.
3. **The opening of a biomechanics paper by Richard G. Snyder** on human vertebral injury from rapid deceleration, which breaks off before its main argument.

The most substantial content is the Hastings essay. The venom table gives small-sample results, and the Snyder piece is incomplete in the source.

## Background & Description

**Scorpion venom immunization (Anavenom).** The study uses guinea pigs (weights 590–900 g) to test whether pre-treatment with an antivenom preparation protects against a lethal venom challenge. The challenge dose is given as 15 MLD (minimum lethal dose). The cited literature covers South African scorpions (*Parabuthus*, *Hadogenes*, *Opisthophthalmus*) and the preparation of a specific anti-scorpion serum. Venom detoxification of cape cobra venom is also referenced.

**Vegetation change in southeastern Arizona.** Hastings examines the "Arizona legend" of ecological decline, meaning the image of tall grass, perennial streams, beaver ponds, springs and marshes before about 1880. The study region is bounded by the Gila River to the north, the International Boundary to the south, New Mexico to the east and the ranges west of the [[Santa Cruz River]] to the west. The essay is explicitly framed as a critique of "applied" history: historical sources must be evaluated critically before they are used as ecological evidence. The work is contribution No. 3 of the [[Arid Lands Program]] at the University of Arizona, supported by the Rockefeller Foundation.

**Deceleration and vertebral injury.** Snyder argues that protecting the human body from rapid deceleration has lagged behind advances in vehicle speed, especially in aviation and space research. He focuses on vertebral fracture, the most frequent injury in high-impact aircraft accidents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and arid variability.** Hastings stresses that aridity amplifies the effect of small climatic changes. Two inches of seasonal rainfall can alter plant cover dramatically, and variation occurs across space, across seasons and between years. Contemporary descriptions of the same site can contradict each other (for example, the junction of Sopori Creek and the Santa Cruz River in 1862 versus 1864).

**Grasslands.** Travelers' journals, army records, ranch records and newspapers generally suggest that grass cover has declined. Hastings qualifies this in two ways:
- Grass was never uniform in space or time. Machine-mowing of hay at Fort Grant in the 1870s–80s occurred only in wet years.
- Some areas show no change: the wetter mesa lands of the upper San Pedro Basin remained excellent, and the arid western Papagueria remained scant.

**Marshes and malaria.** Early army records show malaria was serious in the San Pedro Valley and around Tucson. In 1868, about 2,100 hospitalizations occurred near Camp Grant, with roughly 1,700 from malaria, which the author links to marshy river bottoms. Beaver ponds, trapping and fish (including "salmon trout" reported by Cooke) are documented along the San Pedro.

**Springs.** Of the springs with good historical descriptions, three are known to have dried up or diminished (Dragoon Springs, Stein's Peak and Sycamore Spring). Croton Springs still flows well, though mesquite encroachment is noted. Hastings notes that these trends are tentative.

**Arroyo cutting and channel trenching.** The essay treats channel trenching as a key mechanism. Lowered water tables after trenching would drain marshes and springs, which offers an alternative explanation to a climate change. Documented timelines:
- **Santa Cruz at Tucson:** no well-defined channel before 1890. Flood years 1885, 1886, 1887 and 1889 set up the transition. The present channel began cutting on August 5, 1890, along a ditch dug by Sam Hughes. Newspaper reports describe a cut 1.5 miles long within days.
- **San Pedro River:** trenching is more complicated. Kirk Bryan dated headward cutting from 1883 to 1892, but Hastings found evidence of a discontinuous trench in the 1850s and of a 12-foot bed lowering near Tres Alamos (1885–1889).
- **San Simon Creek:** no evidence of trenching before 1880. Trenching is linked to a settlers' ditch dated variously from 1883 to 1900. A dam below San Simon Cienega may have protected it from drying.

**Mesquite.** The "legend" claims mesquite was largely absent before about 1880. Hastings disputes this: mesquite was widespread before 1880, mostly in river bottoms, but also on mesa plains (Naco, the plains between San Simon Creek and Railroad Pass). Invasion of bajadas and mesas is documented near the Sierra San José, Croton Springs, Pearce and south of Charleston.

**Causal hypotheses.** Hastings groups explanations into three categories:
1. **Biological:** overgrazing altering grass, soil and runoff, with recent attention to effects on coyotes, prairie dogs and other rodents.
2. **Geological:** diastrophism.
3. **Climatic:** progressive desiccation, shifts in rainfall pattern, or more intense storms.

Bryan and Leopold favor a **[[Trigger-Pull Theory]]**: long-term erosion trends were already under way by 1880, and cattle acted as the trigger. Cattle numbers in Pima, Cochise and Graham counties rose from 68,000 (1883) to 156,000 (1886) to 253,000 (1890). Flood severity tracked this rise, though the author warns that mean precipitation alone is an unreliable indicator. Hastings concludes that overgrazing "probably" played an important part, but that climate cannot be rejected.

## Key Findings

**Scorpion venom immunization (Anavenom study)**
- **Anavenom 1:** Of four immunized guinea pigs, three survived and one (pig 3, 590 g) died. The immunization is given as three inoculations of 5 mg per kilogram. The control result for pig 5 is unclear in the scan. Controls pigs 6–8 died after the challenge.
- **Anavenom 2:** Of four immunized guinea pigs, two survived (pigs 2 and 3) and two died (pigs 1 and 4). Both control animals (pigs 5 and 6) died.
- Sample sizes are very small (four treated and two controls per table), so no statistical claim can be drawn from these results.

**Vegetation change (Hastings)**
- The "Arizona legend" of ecological change is partly exaggerated but broadly correct. Marked landscape changes have occurred.
- Reliable change is clearest in river bottoms and springs. Grassland decline is more ambiguous and depends on rainfall regime, with extremes (very wet or very arid zones) showing less change than the middle of the rainfall spectrum.
- Arroyo cutting is documented in the 1880s–1890s: the Santa Cruz cut its present channel in August 1890, and the San Pedro was trenching in the 1880s, possibly earlier.
- Mesquite was widespread before 1880 and is not a recent invader in general. Its spread is nonetheless documented in several bajada and mesa areas.
- The essay's central methodological warnings are the "good old days" fallacy and the confusion of spatial with temporal comparison (for example, Green comparing Arizona with Massachusetts).
- Overgrazing is the most widely accepted contributor, but the author argues that climate remains a possible "loaded gun" for the cattle "trigger."
- The author also notes that a map of pre-1880 mesquite locations was under way, and that further study is needed on the San Simon Creek ditch date and the San Pedro channel history.

**Deceleration and vertebral injury (Snyder, partial text)**
- Aircraft accidents are the most important cause of fatalities among USAF flying personnel (as cited in Moseley, 1958).
- More than 8,000 Air Force aircraft accidents occurred in the five years before 1958, involving over 21,000 individuals.
- In a study of crashes in 1953 and 1955 involving 8,416 individuals, 49.6% of fatal fractures involved vertebrae.
- Three of four vertebral fractures occurred in jet aircraft.
- Poppen (1958) found that over 90% of upper body mass lies above the fifth thoracic vertebra, with the center of gravity behind the manubrium, producing compression loads under high vertical and horizontal forces.
- Evans (1955) found lumbar failure at 978 lb maximum load, and anterior-flexion failure at 495 lb (one 79-year-old white male cadaver).
- German WWII ejection-seat studies gave a dynamic tolerance of 26 G for 0.0005 seconds, with breaking loads of 800 lb for the 12th thoracic vertebra and 1,000 lb for the first lumbar vertebra.
- The Aero Medical Field Laboratory has used anthropomorphic dummies and animals (bears, hogs, rats, mice, chimpanzees) plus human subjects since 1947, approaching limits in gradual increments.
- The source text ends mid-sentence, so the paper's conclusions and the "new approach" it proposes are not included.
