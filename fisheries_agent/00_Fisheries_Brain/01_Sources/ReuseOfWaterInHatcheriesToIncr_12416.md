# Reuse of Water in Hatcheries to Increase Capacity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonid

## Executive Summary

This conference talk by consulting engineer Ronald D. Mayo (Kramer, Chin & Mayo, Seattle) argues that hatchery capacity is limited by oxygen supply and by the ability of water to carry away metabolic wastes, so recirculating or reusing water can raise production where supply is short or where temperature control is needed. It was presented at the 18th Annual Convention of the [[U.S. Trout Farmers Association]] (October 28–30, Sacramento, California) and published in the January–February 1971 issue of *American Fishes and U.S. Trout News*. The talk uses the [[Dworshak Steelhead Hatchery]] as its main case study, describes the [[Sea Pool Industries]] salt-water trout and salmon facility, and reviews dissolved oxygen, dissolved nitrogen and [[Ammonia Toxicity]] as the key water-chemistry constraints.

## Background & Description

The presentation opens with four reasons a hatchery might adopt water reuse:

1. Insufficient water supply.
2. A adequate supply that needs to be used more efficiently.
3. The need to heat or cool water to grow fish on a required schedule.
4. Reducing influent treatment requirements or treating hatchery effluent, which the author calls an increasingly prevalent problem.

Reference examples cited:

- **Dworshak Hatchery, Idaho:** a 15,000 GPM water reuse system in service.
- **[[New England Aquarium]], Boston:** nearly 100 percent reuse practised, with a major portion of the $6,000,000 facility cost in water treatment. The author notes that almost every major US aquarium practises near-total reuse.
- **Informal reuse:** most small producers pass water from pond to pond, through aeration drops and large lagoons.

The author identifies a middle ground between informal reuse and multimillion-dollar systems, represented by the Sea Pool facility in Halifax, which uses reuse filters patterned on the Dworshak design.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water functions in a rearing system.** Water supplies oxygen to fish and removes excess oxygen, uneaten feed and metabolic by-products, including ammonia, carbon dioxide, nitrate and phosphates. The author derives a simple capacity relationship: available oxygen per GPM of flow divided by required oxygen per pound of fish gives pounds of fish per GPM. Because the removal of by-products is a second limit, adding oxygen alone does not guarantee greater capacity.

**Oxygen and elevation.** Figures 2–4 relate oxygen consumption by [[Trout and Salmon]] to fish size and temperature, and dissolved oxygen saturation to elevation and temperature. Using the author's example at 50°F with a minimum of 5 ppm:

- At sea level, saturation is about 11.4 ppm (OCR-corrected), leaving about 6.4 ppm available to fish.
- At 8,000 feet, saturation is about 8.1 ppm, leaving about 3.1 ppm for fish. The carrying-capacity fraction quoted in the scan is illegible.

**Nitrogen supersaturation.** Most natural waters are near 100 percent nitrogen saturation. The author gives about 18 ppm at sea level and 50°F, falling to about 15.8 ppm when water is warmed to 60°F. If heated water is not allowed to shed excess nitrogen, saturation can reach 115 percent, which "kills most trout very quickly." Heating systems therefore need a degassing step.

**Ammonia.** Only the unionized (gaseous) form of [[Ammonia Toxicity|ammonia]] is strongly toxic. Its proportion rises with higher pH and temperature. At 20°C and pH near 7, the gas fraction is essentially zero. This explains why some naturally buffered waters can be reused repeatedly, while in other cases a single pass produces dead fish. Field testing found occasional ammonia levels above literature toxicity thresholds, which the author attributes to this pH and temperature dependence.

**Dworshak context.** The [[North Fork Clearwater River]] is good natural habitat, but its water is unsuitable for hatchery rearing without temperature control. The [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] built the Dworshak Steelhead Hatchery as mitigation for Bruce's Eddy (later Dworshak) Dam, which was about half complete at the time.

## Key Findings

**Dworshak reuse system design.** The design follows the [[Abernathy Water Reuse System]] developed by Roger Burrows at the Abernathy Fish Cultural Laboratory. Its sequence:

1. Surface water is pumped through a mechanical filter to reduce turbidity and improve sterilization.
2. Water passes through ultraviolet sterilizers to remove pathogens.
3. A heat exchanger adds heat or cooling as required.
4. The flow combines with water from the reuse filter, passes through aeration, and returns to the raceways.
5. About 10 percent of flow is wasted to remove accumulating by-products.

**Reuse filter.** The filter is designed at 1 GPM per square foot of surface area. It consists of about one foot of oyster shells, several feet of rock and an underdrain. Water is aerated through aspirators. The oyster shells help control pH, and biological action removes most metabolic by-products. Each filter serves as many as 25 raceways. Clogged filters are cleaned by air-bubble backwashing.

**Sea Pool facility.** Sea Pool Industries in Halifax, Nova Scotia is reported to have an annual capacity of 4,000,000 pounds of trout and salmon raised in salt water. Heating accelerates growth. Reuse is used at nearly every stage, from hatching through rearing to holding adult fish.

**Drawbacks.** The Dworshak system is costly and has operational problems:

- Flow runs continuously at up to 1,500 GPM, including during backwashing.
- Backwash water carries high solids with high oxygen demand, so treatment may be required.
- Cost, operation and pollution are the main obstacles to building more units.

**Planned research.** The [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] planned a series of prototype plant operations in the following calendar year to compare reuse system types and improve their efficiency. The author expected the results to benefit public agencies and hatchery operators.

**Overall conclusion.** Reuse is a practical way to increase hatchery capacity, but only when oxygen supply, degassing, ammonia chemistry and solids handling are designed together.
