# Conservation Genetics and Genetic Diversity in Threatened Fishes of Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-genetics #threatened-fishes

## Executive Summary

This report by Anthony A. Echelle (Oklahoma State University, 1989 section dated 5 August) reviews genetic variation and relevant management practices in threatened fishes of western North America, with emphasis on those under U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region II. Part I reviews electrophoretic studies of genetic diversity, hybridization, introgression, and management. Part II proposes a program of [[Conservation Genetics]] for natural and captive populations in Region II. The central argument is that the best strategy for preserving management options and evolutionary flexibility is to maintain as many populations as possible while retaining natural patterns of genetic diversity. The review finds that less than 10 percent of the fishes of concern in western North America have been adequately surveyed genetically. The report stresses that genetic surveys should be conducted early, before populations are lost or captive stocks are reintroduced.

## Background & Description

The review centers on [[Protein Electrophoresis]] (allozyme analysis), the main tool for measuring genetic variation in natural populations since the 1970s. The threatened taxa considered were listed by [[Johnson (1987)]] and/or [[Williams et al. (1985)]] as receiving legal protection or special concern due to low numbers, limited distributions, or recent declines. Table 1 summarizes genetic diversity for roughly 27 threatened taxa, using measures of heterozygosity (H), polymorphism (P), and total gene diversity (Ht). Many values are crude approximations because sampling was limited.

Taxa discussed include:
- [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia nobilis*), endemic to the Pecos River drainage of New Mexico and Texas
- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*)
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)
- [[Pecos Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon pecosensis*)
- [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii henshawi*), including the Pyramid Lake population, reportedly much larger than introduced populations
- [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Woundfin]], which are subject to intensive management

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Human activities reduce genetic diversity in two main ways: by reducing population sizes and causing local or taxon-level extinctions, and by promoting hybridization, mainly through introductions of non-native forms. Introgression can quickly erode native fish populations over large areas.

Desert fishes are often strongly isolated in small springs or spring systems. Under the assumption of selective neutrality, genetic variability is governed by effective population size, so repeated bottlenecks and genetic drift strongly reduce diversity. Several desert taxa show zero detected variability. The review cites a Chihuahuan Desert comparison in which eight *Gambusia* species had significantly lower variability than 14 species in other regions. Spring habitat alone did not explain this pattern.

Hybridization is documented in several contexts:
- Rainbow trout introductions have introgressed or replaced native cutthroat trout across western basins, and are also largely responsible for declines of [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]].
- Introduced [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*) hybridized with the Pecos pupfish. A hybrid swarm covered about 150 river kilometers by 1985.
- Introduced [[Smallmouth Bass]] threatens [[Guadalupe Bass]] on the Edwards Plateau.
- Hybridization with the Rio Grande sucker (*Catostomus plebeius*) has introgressed the Zuni bluehead sucker.

## Key Findings

**Genetic diversity status**
- Heterozygosity in local populations of threatened fishes is low relative to marine species (marine mean H = .055; all-fish mean H = .051, per cited sources). Twenty-three of 27 threatened taxa in Table 1 had H below .050.
- Mean total gene diversity (Ht) in threatened fishes was about .041 (range .01 to .07, excluding single-sample estimates). This is similar to non-migratory freshwater fishes and somewhat lower than marine and anadromous fishes (.06 to .09).
- The highest diversity recorded was Ht = .11 in the Rio Grande sucker from three Mexican drainages, reflecting two strongly divergent forms.
- Zero variability was recorded in *Cyprinodon diabolis*, three of four isolated Gila topminnow populations, *Gambusia marshi*, *G. longispinus*, and *G. gaigei*.

**Population structure and gene flow**
- Migratory taxa carry most of their total genetic diversity within local populations less than do nonmigratory forms, reflecting gene flow.
- In the Pecos River drainage, *G. nobilis* (restricted to four isolated areas) carries only 40 percent of total Ht in its average local sample. Mosquitofish and Pecos pupfish, which are more continuously distributed, carry 81 and 77 percent respectively.
- Yellowstone cutthroat trout and Apache trout were exceptions to the migratory pattern, with the average local population carrying about 90 percent of Ht in these highly restricted taxa.

**Genetic conservation of *Gambusia gaigei***
- The Big Bend gambusia population passed through a severe bottleneck in 1956, reduced to one female and two males. Total absence of variability in extensive surveys suggests diversity was already low before that event.

**Captive propagation**
- Heterozygosity guided a change in Gila topminnow broodstock source from Monkey Spring (H = .000) to Sharp Spring (H = .057). Sharp Spring fish showed higher fecundity and growth.
- For Apache trout, the broodstock source had H = .007, about 2.5 times lower than the species mean and nearly four times lower than the maximum (.026, Flash Creek).
- Recommended founding stock minimum is 25 females and 25 males. Succeeding captive generations should contain at least 100 females and 100 males, with reproduction equalized across individuals (Allendorf and Ryman, 1987).

**Recommendations for Part II**
- Survey genetic structure of all species of concern in Region II within 10 to 15 years, sampling entire species ranges and at least 50 to 100 loci. Rank species by urgency and assess both phenetic and phylogenetic relationships.
- Protect the genetic integrity of all well-differentiated forms and naturally occurring geographic isolates. Rank populations by their value for long-term genetic potential, and designate gene banks for valuable populations.
- Eliminate human transport of live fish, such as bait and sportfish, in areas of concern. Consider eradication or replacement of introgressed populations, where feasible.
- Establish a single USFWS laboratory to monitor captive stocks, and periodically reassess broodstock genetic status with at least 50 loci.
- Maintain several captive populations to guard against catastrophic loss.

**Overall conclusion**
- Less than 10 percent of the fishes of concern in western North America have been adequately surveyed. Management decisions made without geographic genetic data risk being driven by chance, short-term cost, or convenience rather than by evolutionary potential.
