## Executive Summary
This document is a 1984 research proposal submitted by Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, requesting supplemental funding ($4,800) to expand an ongoing U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service–funded study of the early life history of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The proposal responds to a well-documented, range-wide decline in razorback sucker abundance, contrasted with the persistence of a large adult population in Lake Mohave that exhibits successful spawning but a near-total failure of larval recruitment. The core research need is to determine why larvae disappear within weeks of hatching, despite active reproduction, through daily monitoring, *in situ* enclosure experiments, a controlled/restrained larval population, and dispersal/movement studies using marked and unmarked hatchery-reared larvae.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Xyrauchen texanus* (Abbott), the razorback sucker, family [[Catostomidae]].
- Common name derives from a distinctive keeled/raised dorsal ridge behind the head (not explicitly described in this text but implied by taxonomic context).
- **Larval behavior trait**: Larvae are noted as **positively phototactic**, a key morphological/behavioral characteristic exploited in the proposed enclosure sampling design (attraction to artificial light at night).
- **Critical size threshold**: Larvae have consistently disappeared from the Lake Mohave population at approximately **10 mm** in size, marking a key ontogenetic bottleneck in early life history (Bozek et al. 1984; Marsh and Minckley unpubl.).
- No larvae have been documented recruiting beyond this size class in the open lake environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]] (Arizona-Nevada border), including open-lake spawning areas and isolated backwaters such as **Arizona Bay**.
- **Reproductive season**: November through April, requiring near-continuous (daily) field observation across this extended window.
- **Population status**: A large, apparently self-sustaining adult population exists in Lake Mohave, in contrast to declining populations range-wide (McAda and Wydoski 1980; Minckley 1983).
- **Recruitment failure**: Substantial monitoring effort since 1974 has failed to detect evidence of recruitment (i.e., survival of larvae to juvenile/adult stages) despite confirmed successful spawning and hatching.
- **Predation hypothesis**: Egg and larval predation by introduced (non-native) fishes was hypothesized as a cause of larval disappearance, but this hypothesis was **not supported** by 1984 field data (Marsh and Minckley unpubl.), leaving the cause of mortality unresolved.
- **Co-occurring species of concern**: [[Bonytail Chub]], another imperiled Colorado River native studied concurrently for reproductive success in Lake Mohave (Bozek et al. 1984).
- **Interaction with other agencies**: Study design involves cooperation with the Nevada Department of Wildlife, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the National Park Service, including clearing an isolated backwater (Arizona Bay) of other fish species to create a controlled experimental population.

## Key Findings
- **Recruitment bottleneck confirmed**: Despite consistent, successful spawning in Lake Mohave, wild-produced larvae vanish from the population within a few weeks post-hatch, well before reaching juvenile stages — a pattern first documented by Bozek et al. (1984) and corroborated by unpublished 1984 data from Marsh and Minckley.
- **Predation by introduced fish not supported**: Data collected in 1984 failed to substantiate the leading hypothesis that non-native fish predation on eggs/larvae drives this recruitment failure, indicating the true cause(s) remain undetermined and population dynamics of larvae fluctuate rapidly, necessitating fine-scale (daily) temporal monitoring.
- **Three-pronged proposed research design**:
  1. **Enclosure studies**: Polyethylene tube enclosures placed over known nest sites at varying depths/substrates to quantify larval yield per unit area, timing of emergence, and directional movement (exploiting larvae's positive phototactic response for nighttime sampling).
  2. **Restrained population study**: An isolated backwater (Arizona Bay) cleared of competing/predatory fish species to track a controlled larval cohort from hatching through juvenile stage, testing whether the same ca. 10 mm disappearance threshold persists absent open-lake escape routes or unsampled refugia.
  3. **Dispersal study**: Hatchery-produced larvae from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NM), some marked with vital stain (Bismark brown-Y), introduced to Lake Mohave to track dispersal patterns using visual observation and plankton net sampling over at least one week.
- **Funding structure**: The proposal sought to supplement an existing $15,000 USFWS budget (contract no. 14-16-0002-81-223) with $4,800 from the Bureau of Reclamation, primarily to fund full-time personnel (Daniel R. Langhorst) for 6 months of continuous field operations, totaling a combined program budget of $19,800.
- **Broader significance**: The study represents a critical early attempt to isolate the life-history stage and mechanism responsible for the region-wide recruitment failure of an endangered Colorado River endemic, directly informing later conservation and captive-rearing strategies for [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery programs.
