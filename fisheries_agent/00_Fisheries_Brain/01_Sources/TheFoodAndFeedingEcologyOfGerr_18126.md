# Food and Feeding Ecology of Gerreidae in the Estuaries of Natal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gerreidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[D. P. Cyrus]] and [[S. J. M. Blaber]] (University of Natal) examines the diet, prey selection and resource partitioning of five *Gerres* species (Gerreidae) in the estuaries of Natal, South Africa, sampled from 1978 to 1980. The main study area was the [[Kosi System]], an estuary-linked lake system with an abundance of Gerres. Four other estuarine systems were sampled for comparison. The fish feed mainly on benthic invertebrates, and the siphon tips of the bivalve [[Hiatula Lunulata]] were the dominant food item at most Kosi sites. The authors suggest that Gerres use optimal foraging strategies that reduce competition among co-occurring species.

## Background & Description

- **Family status:** Gerreidae had received little study. Prior work included [[Charles (1975)]] on *Eucinostomus gula* (USA), [[Austin (1971)]] on *Diapterus rhombeus* (Puerto Rico), and [[Prabhakara Rao (1968)]] on *Gerres oyena* and *G. filamentosus* (India). Southern African species had not been studied.
- **Regional fauna:** Six Gerres species occur in southern African coastal waters. They reach estuaries as far south as the Zwartkops River but are uncommon south of the Mtamvuna River in Natal. Five species occur in the Kosi system, where three are abundant over shallow sandy areas.
- **Economic importance:** Gerreidae are economically important in India and South-East Asia. Two Kosi species were classed by [[Wallace et al. (1971)]] as non-angling species of significance as human food. Long-term fishery management plans for Kosi created an applied need for this work.
- **Study focus:** Fish longer than 40 mm standard length (immature and adult) were examined. Because Gerreidae are benthic feeders, the benthos was sampled in detail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Kosi System.** The estuary mouth lies at 26°54'S, 32°53'E. The system comprises four connected lakes draining to the sea through a usually open estuary. The catchment covers 500 km², the estuarine area about 3500 ha, and the shoreline is 53.3 km long. Environmental condition was classed as good by [[Begg (1978)]]. Salinity varies among lakes:
- [[Amanzimnyama]]: always fresh.
- [[Nhlange]]: fresh to 5‰.
- [[Mpungwini]]: 3–6‰ during the study.
- [[Makhawulani]]: 7–14‰, with tidal influence.
- Estuary: lowest recorded 8.5‰; a salt wedge reaches about 6 km up to Makhawulani at high tide.

Temperatures ranged from 17 to 28 °C.

**Sampling sites.** Other localities were [[St Lucia]] (estuary-linked lake system), [[Mlalazi Estuary]], [[Fafa Lagoon]] and [[Mtamvuna Estuary]].

**Seasonal dynamics.** Three species (*G. acinaces*, *G. filamentosus*, *G. rappi*) co-occurred at the water level recorder (WLR), Makhawulani and Mpungwini sites in summer and autumn. In winter and spring, most Gerres left the shelf areas, and only *G. acinaces* remained in large numbers.

**Key benthic prey and co-predators.** Prey included polychaetes, bivalves, amphipods (*Grandidierella lignorum*, *Corophium*), the isopod [[Cirolana Fluviatilis]], the crab [[Hymenosoma Orbiculare]] and chironomid larvae. Other predators of bivalve siphons were [[Acanthopagrus Berda]] (Lake St Lucia, Durban Bay) and [[Pomadasys Commersonni]] (Kosi).

## Key Findings

**Field and analytical methods**
- Quarterly sampling at five Kosi sites from July 1978 to July 1980. Target was at least 30 fish per species per site per trip, though this was not always achieved.
- Diet analysed by four methods: frequency of occurrence, numerical occurrence, calorific contribution, and the Ricker (1968) points method.
- Prey selection assessed with [[Ivlev's Electivity Index]].
- Benthos sampled with a Zabolocki-type Ekman grab (0.0236 m²) and, for bivalves, a 30 × 30 cm box corer.

**Sample sizes (Kosi, 1979 diet analysis)**
- *G. acinaces* n = 555; *G. oblongus* n = 52; *G. oyena* n = 75; *G. filamentosus* n = 329; *G. rappi* n = 295.

**Energy values (J per animal, from Table I)**
- *Hiatula lunulata* distal 5 mm of siphon: 29.653 J, a value obtained in this study.
- *Musculus virgiliae*: 58.251 J.
- *Cirolana fluviatilis*: 26.847 J.
- Polychaeta: 6.462 J.
- Chironomidae larvae: 6.676 J.

**Diet at Kosi**
- *G. acinaces*: *H. lunulata* siphon tips made up 89%, 97% and 81% of energy intake at WLR, Makhawulani and Mpungwini. Chironomid larvae dominated at Nhlange.
- *G. oyena*: siphon tips supplied 56% of energy against 32% from polychaetes at the estuary.
- *G. filamentosus*: at the estuary, polychaetes supplied 66% of energy, siphon tips 15% and *H. orbiculare* 12%.
- *G. rappi*: the most varied diet, with *Musculus virgiliae* present in most estuary fish and chironomid larvae slightly more important overall.
- *G. oblongus*: mainly polychaetes and marine calanoid copepods.

**Diet in other Natal estuaries**
- Mlalazi: bivalve siphon tips supplied 98% of energy in *G. filamentosus*.
- Fafa (only *G. rappi*): *Grandidierella lignorum* was 88% by number, but siphon tips supplied 70% of energy.
- Mtamvuna: *G. lignorum* supplied 76% of energy; *Cirolana* 15%.
- St Lucia: only fry of *G. rappi* were collected.

**Prey selection**
- Few items were eaten in proportion to availability. *H. lunulata* siphon tips were positively selected at every site by every species.
- Positive selection at the estuary and Nhlange reflected the absence of *H. lunulata* from benthic samples, though siphons occurred in stomachs. The authors note that bivalves may occur in deeper water beyond the 2 m box-sampling depth.
- *Cirolana fluviatilis* was negatively selected in all instances.

**Seasonal predation on siphons**
- At WLR and Makhawulani, summer and autumn accounted for 78% and 76% of siphon predation, versus 22% and 24% in winter and spring.
- Mpungwini showed no marked seasonal pattern, likely because it held the highest *H. lunulata* densities.

**Conclusions and interpretation**
- Gerres feed predominantly on the most abundant preferred prey at each locality. Diets of four of five species were dominated by no more than three prey items.
- Coexistence is attributed to superabundant food, differences in minor prey, and feeding periodicity.
- Gerres appear to crop a renewable resource, so bivalves need time for growth, reproduction and siphon regeneration. The winter and spring emigration of *G. rappi* and *G. filamentosus* may reflect a feeding threshold, as seen in plaice predation on *Tellina* (Trevellion et al., 1970).
- Siphon tips are nipped from above the substrate, which the authors suggest lowers handling time and search effort relative to sorting prey from substrate. This is consistent with optimal foraging theory ([[Krebs (1978)]]).
- Gerreidae worldwide mainly consume polychaetes, bivalves, copepods (mainly by fry) and amphipods. [[Diapterus]] differs by eating aquatic macrophytes.
- The authors report the first record of any Gerreidae taking bivalve siphons.
