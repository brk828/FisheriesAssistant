# Trout Biomass and Stream Habitat Relationships in the White Mountains Area, East-Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study surveyed stream habitats and fish populations at 243 stations across 21 high-elevation trout streams in the [[White Mountains Area]], east-central Arizona, from 1986 to 1990. The area holds most of the historic habitat of the federally threatened [[Apache Trout]]. A generalized linear model relating trout biomass to stream, riparian, and geomorphic variables explained 68% of the variation (R² = 0.68) in the square root of areal trout biomass. The strongest management-relevant predictor was [[Bank Damage By Ungulates]], which the authors attribute mainly to domestic cattle grazing. The authors conclude that better [[Cattle Grazing Management]] is needed to improve trout habitat, while noting that the moderate model fit suggests trout may be limited by climate, predation, and competition as much as by physical habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Focal taxon:** [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), an endemic salmonid of the White Mountains, listed as threatened by the U.S. federal government.
- **Non-native competitors:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Brook Trout]] have displaced, replaced, or genetically swamped Apache trout in many streams.
- **Native associates:** [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]] occur in some streams, mainly in lower reaches of the Gila and Little Colorado basins.
- **Restoration:** Chemical renovation to remove non-native trout and reintroduce Apache trout has been underway since the early 1960s. Streams were used only if at least 5 years had passed since renovation and restocking.
- **Study area:** Streams straddle the [[Colorado Plateau]] and [[Basin and Range]] provinces, drain the White Mountains Volcanic Field, and range from about 2,000 m to 3,475 m elevation. Riparian vegetation ranges from willow and alder scrub and subalpine meadows to cottonwood-sycamore gallery forest.

**Background on prior models.** The authors review the [[Habitat Quality Index]] (HQI) developed in Wyoming (model I R² = 0.95; model II R² = 0.97 in development). Its transfer performance was inconsistent: it explained 6.7–9.2% of trout biomass variation in southern Ontario, and 87% and 71% in New Mexico streams. Southwestern modeling studies were rare, with [[McHenry 1986]] and [[Rinne and Medina 1988]] the principal examples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling framework**
- Streams were first- and second-order perennial streams, unstocked (except Apache trout reintroductions) and lightly fished.
- Reaches were classified as meadow (gradient <2%), canyon or headwater (gradient >6%), or intermediate, following [[Rosgen Stream Classification]].
- Each station comprised five transects. Station length was 152.4 m (1986–1987), 100 m (1988), or 50 m (1989–1990), with transect spacing of 30.5 m, 20 m, and 10 m, respectively.
- Fish were collected by three-pass electrofishing with block nets and estimated using the [[Zippin Removal Method]] in MicroFish 3.0.

**Habitat variables**
- More than 25 variables were measured, including channel width, water depth, gradient, riparian canopy density (concave densiometer), substrate classes, bank angle, undercut width, embeddedness, and bank damage by ungulates.
- Channel width, bank damage, and station elevation were rated or measured under standard [[Platts Stream Survey Methods]].

**Modeling approach**
- Trout biomass was expressed as g/m² and g/m³. Data included only nonzero responses (N = 223 in model building).
- Transformations (log, square root, inverse) were chosen using Box-Cox procedures. Model selection used Mallow's Cp, R², and MSE criteria in SAS.
- Validation used a 75/25 hold-out sample.

**Land use and ecosystem processes**
- Bank damage from cattle affects trout through higher summer temperatures, sedimentation that smothers eggs and fry, channel widening and anchor ice, channel trenching, and vegetation loss.
- Riparian width influences allochthonous inputs and production, linking riparian structure to salmonid food supply.

## Key Findings

**Model results**
- The selected model explained 68% of variation in sqrt(trout g/m²) (R² = 0.683; F = 37.357, P = 0.0001; error df = 156).
- Significant predictors (P < 0.001 unless noted): sqrt(channel width), bank damage by ungulates, log₂(station elevation), channel type, channel type × log₂(elevation) interaction. Log₂(riparian area width) was significant at P = 0.0026. Mean water depth was not significant in this model (P = 0.287).
- Validation: 89% (51 of 57) of new observations fell within 95% prediction intervals. The correlation between predicted and observed values was r = 0.52.

**Management implications**
- Bank damage by ungulates is the only significant variable directly influenced by land management, and the authors identify it as the main target for management.
- Channel type, station elevation, and riparian width are geomorphic and not directly manageable.

**Grazing contrast**
- Ungrazed or lightly grazed meadow reaches (Upper Ord, Pacheta, and Reservation creeks; 17 stations) had mean trout biomass of 29.6 g/m² (SE 3.32) and 242.6 g/m³ (SE 28.22).
- Grazed meadow reaches (31 stations) averaged 11.5 g/m² (SE 1.69) and 119.1 g/m³ (SE 24.28).
- Differences in ranked trout biomass between grazed and ungrazed meadows were highly significant (Kruskal-Wallis, P < 0.001).

**Interpretation and limits**
- The low R² (0.68) compared with HQI models suggests trout are constrained more by climatic events, predation, and competition than by physical habitat. The authors note that this is an inference, not a tested result.
- The study lacked controlled experiments and cannot establish causation between cattle use and trout standing crop. The authors recommend long-term controlled field experiments and multi-year population estimates.
- Generality is limited: the authors conclude that model generality and high precision may be mutually exclusive.

**Sample summary:** 21 streams, 243 stations, 1,201 habitat transects, 166 stations in the final pooled model.
