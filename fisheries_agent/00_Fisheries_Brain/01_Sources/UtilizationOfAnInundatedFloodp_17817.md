# Utilization of an Inundated Floodplain by Mississippi River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-fishes

## Executive Summary

This study examines how fishes of the [[Mississippi River]] main channel use an adjoining floodplain during seasonal flooding. Fish were sampled monthly from February to July 1973 with seines, trammel nets, and gill nets on an inundated backswamp near St. Francisville, Louisiana. Of 62 species collected, 24 were characteristic of the main channel and 15 occurred equally in floodplain and main-channel habitats. Juveniles and spawning adults indicate that flooded backwaters act as important nursery and spawning areas for riverine fishes. The author argues that floodplain drainage and loss of backwater access have contributed to declines in the lower river's fisheries and that floodplain protection should be a management priority.

## Background & Description

Fish life cycles on the [[Lower Mississippi River]] are closely tied to the annual flood pulse. As floodplains fill, many fishes move laterally from the main channel into inundated areas to spawn and feed. Such movements are predominantly lateral (toward the floodplain) rather than upstream or downstream.

Land drainage and development for agriculture and urban use have removed many floodplains from the influence of the main channel, altering the natural inundation cycle. The author links this to a decline in the river's fishery resources, citing earlier work by Gunter (1952, 1956, 1957) and Viosca (1927).

The study was built on the hypothesis that [[Floodplain Backwaters]] serve as feeding and nursery areas for main-channel fishes. It analyzes collections made during the 1973 flood. Funding came from Gulf State Utilities, and the work was administered by Louisiana State University.

### Study Area

- Location: 8 km south of St. Francisville, West Feliciana Parish, Louisiana.
- The western (leveed) bank is pasture with remnants of borrow pits.
- The eastern natural floodplain is about 2 km wide, lying between the natural levee and Pleistocene bluffs.
- The backswamp contains cypress-tupelo gum forest or pasture, dense rooted aquatic vegetation, and mud substrate with organic debris.
- Poorly defined channels drain into [[Thompson Creek]], a Mississippi tributary. Even at normal discharge, the shallow backswamp is often inundated with river water through lower Thompson Creek.

### Methods

Sampling ran monthly from February to July 1973. All gear was nylon:

- **Seine:** 3.0 m × 1.8 m, 0.48 cm mesh. Twenty-one collections were made in shallow shoreline areas, continuing at each site until no new species appeared.
- **Gill net:** 45.7 m × 1.8 m, mesh 2.5 to 7.6 cm, set in open water parallel to shore.
- **Trammel net:** 45.7 m × 1.8 m, 5.1 cm inner webbing, 25.4 cm outer walls.

Nets were set at dusk and retrieved the next morning. Effort was 5.5 net-nights for gill nets and 6 for trammel nets. Four seine collections were also taken monthly in Alligator Bayou, a nearby floodplain tributary, for comparison. Seine catches were preserved in 10% formalin, transferred to 45% isopropanol, then sorted and measured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper contrasts productive floodplain habitat with the main channel. The main channel has high current velocities, high turbidity, no aquatic vegetation, and unstable sandy substrate. Floodplain habitat has slackwater, heavy vegetation, and fine, organically rich substrate. The author cites the factors reviewed by [[Christenson and Smith]] (1965) and [[Schramm and Lewis]] (1974) that make flooded backwaters productive nursery areas: soil fertility, reduced current, food availability, and water-level fluctuation.

Reported fish yields tie production to the extent of backwater habitat:

- Illinois River (Richardson 1921): 199 kg/ha where about 90% of the acreage was backwater, 146 kg/ha at a lower backwater fraction (value garbled in the scanned text), and 78 kg/ha where backwaters made up 63%.
- Upper Mississippi (Barnickol and Starrett 1951): game fish declined where backwater area was reduced.
- Lower Colorado River (Beland 1953): draining adjoining backwaters produced a 100% reduction in fishery values.
- [[Atchafalaya Basin]] (Lambou 1963): the most productive sport and commercial fisheries lay where backwaters were most abundant.

Other studies cited document lateral movement into backwaters on rising water: [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] spawning migrations were linked to spring floodplain inundation (Johnson 1974), and [[Paddlefish]] were observed entering oxbow lakes as water rose (Stockard 1907).

## Key Findings

### Species Composition and Catch

- **Gill nets:** 9 species were taken, averaging 6.48 individuals and 5.09 kg per net-night. [[Gizzard Shad]] was most numerous at 1.43 individuals per net-night, followed by [[Carp]] and [[Shortnose Gar]] at 1.24 each. Carp contributed the most biomass at 1.84 kg per net-night. [[Bowfin]], shortnose gar, and bigmouth buffalo each yielded 0.67 to 0.76 kg per net-night.
- **Trammel nets:** 5 species were taken, averaging 2.70 individuals and 5.73 kg per net-night. Bowfin (1.06 individuals) and gizzard shad (1.00) were most frequent. Gizzard shad contributed the most biomass at 2.95 kg per net-night, followed by bowfin at 2.36 kg.
- **Seine:** 61 species were taken in 21 collections. The most abundant were [[Threadfin Shad]] (1,275 individuals, 26.8%), [[Silvery Minnow]] (746, 15.7%), [[Mosquitofish]] (357, 7.5%), [[Blacktail Shiner]] (279, 5.9%), and [[Golden Shiner]] (214, 4.5%).
- **Overall:** 62 species were recorded across all gear. Of these, 39 inhabit the Mississippi River, and 23 occur mainly in main-channel habitats. In seine collections, 53.5% of individuals belonged to species normally found in the main channel.

Note that the Results section gives 23 main-channel species, while the Abstract gives 24 characteristic species plus 15 occurring equally in both habitats. The source reports these figures without reconciling them.

### Evidence of Floodplain Nursery and Spawning Use

- Shortnose gar, bowfin, carp, and bigmouth buffalo were collected in spawning condition at the start of gill-net sampling, as floodwaters first reached the floodplain.
- Young-of-the-year and spawning adults were found in seine collections for about 30 species, including bowfin, threadfin shad, gizzard shad, carp, silvery minnow, several shiners, sunfishes, and crappies.
- The author concludes that many riverine fishes use the floodplain as a nursery.

### Temporal Pattern With Flood Stage

- Before flooding, characteristic river fishes stayed below 12 species on the floodplain.
- They rose to a peak of 32 species in July as water levels climbed. Their percent composition followed the same trend.
- As discharge fell and water receded, both species number and percent composition declined.
- Nineteen species were not recorded on the floodplain before the flood, including skipjack herring, goldeye, sauger, freshwater drum, [[White Bass]], [[Yellow Bass]], and channel and blue catfish.

### Migration and Entrapment

- During spring floods, river fishes move laterally, perpendicular to flow, into quiet backwaters.
- Juveniles and spawning adults in the floodplain point to its use as nursery habitat. Fish also return to the main channel as water falls, contributing to river recruitment.
- Continued presence of river fishes in floodplain seine catches after rapid water-level drops suggests that some are trapped in sloughs.

### Management Implications

The author concludes that floodplains are central to sustaining riverine fisheries. Little effort has gone into preserving floodplains along large rivers. Any physical destruction of floodplains, or any manipulation that blocks access to them, could harm the fishery. The author recommends that floodplain protection, and even restoration, be a top priority in large-river fisheries management, because the aim of management is sustained yield of sport and commercial fisheries.
