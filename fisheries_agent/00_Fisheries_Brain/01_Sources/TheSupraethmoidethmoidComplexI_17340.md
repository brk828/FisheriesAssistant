# Supraethmoid-Ethmoid Complex in American Catfishes: Ictalurus and Pylodictis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the [[Supraethmoid-Ethmoid Complex]] (the anterior-most part of the upper skull) across 10 species of American catfishes in the genera [[Ictalurus]] and [[Pylodictis]]. The authors, F. E. Calovich and B. A. Branson of Kansas State College, Pittsburg, measured skull structures on articulated specimens and grouped the species by shape. They conclude that the complex offers reliable [[Taxonomic Characters]] for identifying species and may help clarify evolutionary relationships. A dichotomous key based on the complex is provided. The sample sizes are small (1 to 7 specimens per species), and the authors state that larger samples are needed before the minor differences can be evaluated.

## Background & Description

The supraethmoid-ethmoid complex forms from the fusion of two distinct elements in adults. It sits at the front of the upper skull and partly protects the [[Central Nervous System]]. Earlier work by Suttkus (1961) and Smith (1956) had noted that cornual branching and the development of the diastema (the gap separating the cornua) carry taxonomic significance in these catfishes.

The structure is described using several terms:

- **Supraethmoid stem:** the elongated or broadened central element.
- **[[Cornua]]:** the paired branches (anterior and posterior) of the ethmoid.
- **[[Median Cleft]]:** the notch between the cornua, varying from deep and narrow to broad or nearly absent.
- **Anterior fontanelle:** an opening used as a landmark for measurements.

Specimens were collected with gill nets, baited fyke nets, rotenone and hook and line. Skeletons were prepared by immersion in hot water, soaking in two per cent [[Potassium Hydroxide]], and washing. One specimen each of *I. lupus* and *I. pricei* was stained with [[Alizarin Red]]. Measurements were taken with dividers, and ratios and angles were calculated. Nomenclature follows Moore (1957). Specimens are deposited at Kansas State College.

Sample sizes and size ranges (standard length) were:

- *Ictalurus punctatus*: 7 specimens, 111–352 mm
- *I. furcatus*: 5 specimens, 232–275 mm
- *Pylodictis olivaris*: 2 specimens, 324–462 mm
- *I. nebulosus*: 5 specimens, 214–263 mm
- *I. natalis*: 2 specimens, 184–304 mm
- *I. melas*: 2 specimens, 129–263 mm
- *I. platycephalus*: 3 specimens, 105–123 mm
- *I. catus*: 3 specimens, 185–274 mm
- *I. lupus*: 2 specimens, 156–505 mm
- *I. pricei*: 1 specimen, 88 mm

Collection localities included [[Lake Texoma]] (Oklahoma and Texas), Denison Dam, Lake Seminole and Lake Worth (Georgia), the Mountain Fork River (Oklahoma), the Ogeechee and Chattahoochee rivers, the Clear Fork of the Brazos River (Texas), and sites in Coahuila and Rio Sinola, Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is a morphological and taxonomic study. It contains no direct data on habitat use, diet, behavior or water chemistry. The only ecological remark is the authors' suggestion that the flattened, fused skull of *Pylodictis olivaris* is "probably a bottom dwelling specialization." The species occur in freshwater systems, including reservoirs, rivers and lakes across the southern and central United States and northern Mexico.

## Key Findings

**Four morphological groups**

1. ***Ictalurus punctatus*, *I. lupus*, *I. pricei*, *I. platycephalus*:** Narrowly elongated supraethmoid stems, compact cornua, and deep, narrow median clefts with well-defined extensions. *I. punctatus* has a median ridge on the dorsal surface, which *I. platycephalus* lacks.
2. ***Ictalurus furcatus*, *I. catus*, *I. nebulosus*:** Short, broadened supraethmoid stems and broadly branched cornua. *I. furcatus* and *I. catus* have broad clefts, while *I. nebulosus* has a shallow cleft that is nearly absent in one 214 mm specimen.
3. ***Ictalurus melas* and *I. natalis*:** A broad complex. In *I. melas* the cleft is broad and shallow in sexually mature specimens and very shallow in others. In *I. natalis* the cleft is shallow and broad, with broadly rounded bony extensions.
4. ***Pylodictis olivaris*:** Unique among the species studied, with a broadened and flattened ethmoidal stem, broadly branched cornua and a very wide cleft. The authors state that it is "unmistakably" distinguished from all other American catfishes studied.

**Key to species**

The authors provide a dichotomous key based on the complex. It separates the species using cleft shape and size, the presence and position of bony extensions, and ratios of supraethmoid width to total cornual width and of ethmoid width to the base-to-fontanelle distance. Separating *I. melas* from *I. nebulosus*, for example, depends on whether the least ethmoid width equals the distance from the base of the cleft to the anterior fontanelle.

**Conclusions and implications**

- The supraethmoid-ethmoid complex provides excellent taxonomic characters and may help in studying evolutionary tendencies.
- A depressed head does not necessarily mean a broad ethmoidal complex, which supports the earlier findings of Suttkus (1961).
- The morphology of the anterior skull appears to reflect phylogenetic relationships.
- The phylogeny of the whole group "is in need of thorough study."
- Numerous smaller differences were observed, but they cannot be evaluated until a much larger sample is available for each species.
