# Notropis Germanus Reinterpreted as an Intergeneric Hybrid (Hybognathus hankinsoni × Notropis heterolepis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Carl L. Hubbs (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) removes the nominal species [[Notropis Germanus]], described by O. P. Hay in 1887 from a single specimen collected in the Smoky Hill River, Kansas, from the system of nominal species. He reinterprets it as an interspecific hybrid between [[Hankinson's Minnow]] (*Hybognathus hankinsoni*) and [[Notropis Heterolepis]], a species that is now extirpated or greatly depleted across much of its southern range. The paper also recommends that names based on interspecific hybrids be treated as unavailable for either parent species. The work is a taxonomic revision combining morphological comparison, historical distribution records, and ecological reasoning about hybridization in rare species.

## Background & Description

**Nominal species history.** Hay (1887) described *Notropis germanus* on one specimen (U.S.N.M. No. 37949), with expressed uncertainty. [[Meek]] (1894) misidentified it as *Notropis heterodon*, and that error propagated through successive editions of Jordan's *Manual of the Vertebrates* and other manuals, placing Kansas within the range of *N. heterodon*. Elimination of Kansas from that range, in later works, removed *N. germanus* from its synonymy.

**Type specimen characters.** Key features used in the reidentification include:
- Two definite, loosely compacted S-shaped intestinal loops, unlike any eastern North American cyprinid. Hubbs noted a long gut suggests a *Hybognathus* parent, while short-gutted genera supply the other parent.
- Pharyngeal teeth with a 4-4 formula, intermediate in hooking and grinding surfaces between the parental genera.
- Jaw structure resembling *Dionda nubila*, with a symphyseal knob; upper jaw length 4.3 times in head length.
- Anal fin rays: 9 in the type, the only count departing from the hybrid expectation of 8. Hubbs notes the crowded, fine rays suggest an abnormally high count.
- Caudal peduncle scales: 12 (5 above and 5 below the lateral line), compared with 15 or 16 in *H. hankinsoni*, *N. d. missuriensis*, and *N. topeka*. This pattern fits *N. heterolepis* (12 scales).
- Body depth about 5.0 in standard length; head depth 1.7 in length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution decline of *Notropis heterolepis*.** The species formerly occurred south and north of the Smoky Hill River but is now depleted or extirpated in much of its southern range. Recorded declines include:
- Kansas: a single reported southern-subspecies record from the Arkansas River at Wichita (Jordan, 1891); no later Kansas records found.
- Nebraska: surveys from 1939 to 1941 found no evidence of continued existence, apart from relict populations in the Sand Hills.
- Colorado: only 1903 specimens from the South Platte system at Longmont; repeated 1950 collection attempts failed.
- Iowa: very scarce, if it survives at all.
- Illinois: extirpated or nearly so.
- Missouri: still common but local and scattered; Tennessee: a single series at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.
- Indiana: extremely rare except in glacial lakes; Ohio: extirpated over most of its primeval range.

**Habitat deterioration.** The species prefers cool, clear, weedy waters. Hubbs attributes its depletion to agricultural practices that eliminated these conditions south of the limits of Wisconsin glaciation.

**Co-occurrence in 1885.** The 1885 Hay collection also contained other cool-water species, including *Nocomis biguttatus*, *Semotilus atromaculatus*, *Notropis cornuta frontalis*, and *Hybognathus hankinsoni*, suggesting that cool, clear conditions existed in the Smoky Hill River before intensive cultivation of the Plains.

**Hybridization and rarity.** Hubbs proposes that scarcity of a freshwater fish, whether from recent establishment or impending extirpation, promotes hybridization with other species. Both parental species prefer cool, weedy waters and frequently co-occur, possibly sharing reproductive timing, habitat, and behavior.

## Key Findings

- ***Notropis germanus* is a hybrid, not a valid species.** The author interprets the type as *Hybognathus hankinsoni* × *Notropis heterolepis*, with most characters intermediate between the parents. [[Hybridization In Fishes]] is a recurring theme in this interpretation.
- **Other species excluded.** Among candidate parents from the same locality (*N. deliciosa*, *N. topeka*, *Semotilus*, *Nocomis*, *Phenacobius*), the author excludes most on fin-ray, scale, body-shape, and pigmentation grounds. *N. heterolepis* best fits all parameters.
- **Subspecies identity is uncertain.** The subspecies most likely involved is *N. h. heterolepis*, based on the absence of a distinct pore in the axial scale series, though the evidence is limited.
- **Naming policy.** Names based on interspecific hybrids are "unavailable for either parental species," on the grounds that type specimens are not representative of true populations and that this is consistent with botanical and zoological practice.
- **Systematic correction.** Removing *N. germanus* from the synonymy of *N. heterodon* leaves the range of *N. heterodon* compact and consistent.
- **Conservation implication.** The author presumes that rarity of *N. heterolepis* by 1885 promoted hybridization, and suggests that this propensity may accelerate extirpation of species made rare by environmental deterioration.
- **Broader context.** Hubbs situates this case within the extensive interspecific hybridization documented among American Cyprinidae, and cites a similar cyprinid hybrid, *Hybognathus placita* × *Notropis girardi* (Cross, 1950).
