# Correspondence on Spikedace and Loach Minnow versus Red Shiner Interactions

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #native-fish-conservation #species-displacement

## Executive Summary

This 1987 correspondence from an Arizona State University zoology professor addresses questions from the [[Gila Fish and Gun Club]] regarding the status of [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) populations in relation to the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). The letter documents the distribution of these native cyprinids in Arizona streams, the history of red shiner introduction and spread through the [[Colorado River Basin]], and the mechanisms by which introduced species displace native fishes. The author notes that while species replacement is documented, the specific mechanisms of red shiner displacement of native minnows remain unknown, prompting planned research beginning April 1987.

## Background & Description

[[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]] are native cyprinid fishes of the southwestern United States, particularly Arizona and New Mexico stream systems. Both species were subjects of conservation concern by the 1980s due to declining populations associated with habitat modification and invasive species.

[[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is characterized as highly tolerant of environmental extremes, possessing high reproductive rates and strong dispersal tendencies. The species was originally introduced into [[Colorado River]] mainstem reservoirs in the early 1950s as forage for game species and presumably as unused bait. By the early 1960s, it appeared in central Arizona ([[Salt River]]) through unauthorized baitfish stocking, spreading upstream through natural dispersal and fishermen transfer. The species likely reached [[San Carlos Reservoir]] in the late 1960s and spread into New Mexico waters by the early 1980s.

The letter provides comparative examples of other problematic introduced species: [[Carp]] (introduced from Eurasia in the 1800s), [[Bullhead Catfish]] (native to Mississippi River valley but problematic when introduced elsewhere), and [[Blue Tilapia]] (African species spreading in lower Colorado River basin waters).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Aravaipa Creek]] maintains populations of both [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]] as abundant as in the 1970s. [[Red Shiner]] has not yet accessed this system, presumably due to a persistently dry area near the mouth limiting dispersal except during floods, and active education programs discouraging public fish transfers. No sport fisheries exist in Aravaipa, eliminating baitfish transport.

[[Spikedace]] persists in three Arizona streams: [[Aravaipa Creek]], upper [[Verde River]] mainstem, and [[Eagle Creek]]. It co-occurs with [[Red Shiner]] only in the Verde River. [[Loach Minnow]] shows slightly wider distribution: [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Blue River]] and tributaries, [[White River]], and perhaps [[Eagle Creek]].

Reservoirs fundamentally alter river systems both at impoundment sites and substantial distances downstream, acting as storage places for introduced fishes. Species abundant in reservoirs typically invade upstream. [[Red Shiner]] may have "reservoir-hopped" throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]].

The [[Verde River]] presents a unique situation where [[Spikedace]] resists [[Red Shiner]] displacement, unlike patterns observed elsewhere. This resistance formed the basis for research planned to commence April 1987.

## Key Findings

**Current Distribution Status (1987):**
- [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]] remain as abundant in [[Aravaipa Creek]] as in the 1970s
- [[Spikedace]] occurs in three Arizona streams: [[Aravaipa Creek]], upper [[Verde River]], and [[Eagle Creek]]
- [[Loach Minnow]] distributed in [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Blue River]] and tributaries, [[White River]], and possibly [[Eagle Creek]]
- [[Red Shiner]] co-occurs with [[Spikedace]] only in the [[Verde River]]

**Red Shiner Invasion History:**
- Early 1950s: introduced into [[Colorado River]] mainstem reservoirs as forage
- Early 1960s: appeared in central Arizona ([[Salt River]]) via unauthorized baitfish stocking
- Late 1960s: likely reached [[San Carlos Reservoir]], Arizona
- Early 1980s: spread into New Mexico waters

**Species Displacement Mechanisms:**
- Pattern of rapid establishment in modified environments followed by slow spread to adjacent habitats
- Reservoirs facilitate spread by acting as storage and source populations
- For [[Red Shiner]] versus [[Spikedace]]/[[Loach Minnow]]: specific mechanisms unknown
- Hypothesized interactions: competition for food or space, or predation on eggs/larvae of native species

**Comparative Examples of Problematic Introductions:**
- [[Carp]]: reproduce prolifically, disturb bottom, roil water, compete for food and space, feed on eggs and young
- [[Bullhead Catfish]]: overpopulate and become stunted in ponds, create space and food limitations
- [[Blue Tilapia]]: displace catfish, sunfish, and [[Largemouth Bass]]; swarm nesting bass to decimate eggs

**Research Plans:**
- April 1987 research project to test hypotheses regarding [[Spikedace]] resistance to [[Red Shiner]] in [[Verde River]]
- Planned sampling in [[Gila River]], New Mexico, in cooperation with Dr. David Propst and [[New Mexico Game and Fish Department]]
- Focus on determining competitive versus predatory mechanisms of displacement

**Conservation Implications:**
- [[Conner Dam]] presence would have provided additional foothold for [[Red Shiner]] populations, likely catastrophic for upstream native fish populations
- Active education and information programs effective in preventing fish transfers to protected areas like [[Aravaipa Creek]]
- Coordination with state agencies (New Mexico Game and Fish Department) recommended for ongoing monitoring
