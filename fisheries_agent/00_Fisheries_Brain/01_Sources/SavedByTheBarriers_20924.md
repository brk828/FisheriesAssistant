# Trout Magazine, Summer 1991: Brook Trout Restoration, Hydropower Threats, and Environmental Politics

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Summer 1991 issue of a trout-focused magazine combines a policy essay, advertising, and four feature pieces. The central story follows [[Steve Moore]], fisheries biologist for [[Great Smoky Mountains National Park]], through the decline and attempted restoration of the native [[Salvelinus Fontinalis]] (southern Appalachian brook trout). The park's problem traces to logging, a [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking program, and competitive displacement. Restoration relies on [[Electrofishing]] and natural barriers, and a genetic question about whether the southern fish form a distinct lineage remains open. Other pieces cover [[Trout Unlimited]] restoration projects in the Southeast, a looming wave of federal hydropower license expirations in 1993 (including the [[Deerfield River]]), a letter by Christopher Camuto criticizing environmental policy inaction, and product advertisements.

## Background & Description

**Issue contents (summarized):**

- **Camuto letter (Virginia and national politics):** Argues that environmental progress is blocked by campaign funding and weak federal conservation leadership. Criticizes the [[National Acid Precipitation Assessment Program]] (NAPAP) as politicized, and criticizes state approval of small coal-fired cogeneration plants that add SO₂ and NOx emissions.
- **Advertising:** Heritage Rods (custom fly rods, $695 priced model), Kingfisher drift boats, a St. Croix Legend rod ad, and a Trout Unlimited Combined Federal Campaign notice.
- **Feature 1, "Pushed to the Brink" (Smoky Mountains brook trout):** Describes the park's brook trout decline, restoration work, and taxonomic questions.
- **Feature 2, Trout Unlimited Southeast projects:** Covers restoration work in Tennessee and North Carolina.
- **Feature 3, "Dam It All" (hydropower relicensing):** Introduces the Deerfield River. The text ends mid-article.

**Species profile, *Salvelinus fontinalis*:** The document describes brook trout as the only salmonid native to the Southeastern United States. Their natural range extends from Hudson's Bay south through the Great Lakes and New England, down both sides of the Appalachians to northern Georgia and South Carolina. Local anglers describe southern fish as smaller than northern strains, with more and brighter red body spots, longer pectoral fins, jaw and snout, and bigger eyes.

**Park context:** [[Great Smoky Mountains National Park]] was established in 1934 after a piecemeal land acquisition campaign. It covers roughly 70 miles along the North Carolina–Tennessee border, has been designated an International Biosphere Reserve, and contains well over 1,000 miles of streams, most of which once held wild native brook trout. The fish inventory cited is 58 species of fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Logging and habitat loss:** Mechanized industrial logging, which reached the southern Appalachians around 1900, was the most destructive force. Companies such as the Little River Logging Company and Champion Coated Paper Company were active. By the time the park was established, about 70 percent of its timber had been cut. Three and a half decades of logging destroyed nearly 50 percent of brook trout habitat. Canopy removal raised stream temperatures (the Little River reached the 80s °F), and eroded silt covered spawning gravel. Splash dams, which released log-filled ponds downhill, scoured streams and left trout stranded on the banks.

**Overfishing and human settlement:** Mountain residents exploited brook trout for food, profit, and barter. A 1934 park report recorded new regulations (artificial flies only, 10-inch minimum, 10-fish daily bag limit), but enforcement was weak in remote terrain. By the mid-1920s, 1,200 farms and 7,300 people lived in the area that became the park.

**Rainbow trout displacement:** Rainbow trout were chosen for stocking because of their tolerance of warm, silty, polluted water and their larger size. Between 1936 and 1975, over 1.5 million rainbows were planted in park waters, and more than 800,000 brook trout were stocked in an attempt to restore the native fishery. Federal managers assumed hatchery rainbows could not establish breeding populations and expected forest regrowth to favor brook trout. Instead, rainbows established viable breeding populations and moved upstream, displacing brook trout. Moore notes the average invading rainbow "weighs 1.8 times more and is two to three inches larger" than the average brook trout. Brook trout were pushed into smaller pools, side pockets, and faster water, and mortality rose, especially among young of the year.

**Population status:** The Park Service halted brook trout angling and stocking in 1975. By 1977, brook trout had lost 70 percent of their former park habitat. A 1980 [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] survey found brook trout in only 123 of the park's roughly 1,000 miles of fish-bearing water, and only 40 of those miles were considered safe from rainbow invasion. Local stocks of pure streams now total about 150 miles, and the stated goal is to double that figure.

**Hydropower threats (Deerfield River):** The Deerfield drops 2,900 feet over 73 miles and drains a 632-square-mile basin shared equally by Vermont and Massachusetts. It is the second largest basin of the [[Connecticut River]]. Eight conventional plants and an 800-megawatt pumped storage project at Bear Swamp are operated by [[New England Power Company]]. Fisheries scientist Mark B. Bain calls the project "one of the most extensive on a medium-sized river in the nation." Its steep grade provides cool, oxygenated trout habitat, but also makes the river attractive for power generation. A federal relicensing wave is due: 225 federal hydropower licenses across the country expire on December 31, 1993, many on important trout and salmon streams.

**Regional pollution context:** The Camuto letter frames air and water pollution as national problems. It cites the Bush-Sununu energy strategy (February 1991) as favoring accelerated exploration over conservation, and describes Virginia coal-fired cogeneration approvals as ignoring public health and environmental testimony.

## Key Findings

**1. Native brook trout have been displaced from most of their former Smokies range.**
- Logging destroyed nearly 50 percent of habitat, and about 70 percent of the park was logged before establishment.
- By the 1930s, brook trout could not be found below 3,000 feet, though historically they occurred down to about 1,600 feet.
- Only a handful of high-elevation headwater streams still hold this native fish.

**2. Rainbow trout stocking and competition were the main drivers of the recent decline.**
- Over 1.5 million rainbows were stocked between 1936 and 1975.
- Rainbows outcompete brook trout largely because they grow bigger, which limits brook trout to less desirable habitat.
- Brook trout declined a further 15 percent by the mid-1950s.

**3. Electrofishing removal has worked in some streams but not others.**
- On Taywa and Silers Creeks, rainbows were removed for six consecutive years. By 1981 they made up under 10 percent of trout present, and by 1987 they were gone.
- Taywa Creek brook trout biomass rose from about 2 pounds per acre (1976) to nearly 30 pounds per acre in some sections, while rainbow biomass fell from over 20 pounds per acre to zero.
- Sams Creek rainbow populations increased, because not enough fish were captured to reach a critical mass.
- Restoration of two streams has taken 14 years, and the method is labor-intensive and limited to small, high-elevation streams.

**4. Barrier identification remains unresolved.**
- Parker and Moore measured nearly 70 waterfalls and cascades (4 to 40 feet high) in a finclip and homing study completed in 1988.
- Vertical falls 8 feet or higher appear to be reliable barriers, but lower falls, sheet-flow slides, cascades with resting pools, and side channels passable at high water are inconsistent.
- Trout can leap off standing waves at the base of falls, and "some of the obstructions we thought were barriers weren't and some that weren't, were."
- National Park Service policy prohibits constructing artificial barriers in park streams.

**5. Genetic distinctness of southern brook trout is still disputed.**
- A Cornell study (about 1976) concluded Bunches Creek fish were at least a different subspecies. A 1978 study found the populations were the same. A 1981 Penn State study agreed with Cornell.
- Parker and Moore found that earlier studies ignored stocking records. Their preliminary 1987 starch gel electrophoresis analysis of five streams showed native populations "very different" from stocked ones.
- Eleven streams in the park appear never to have been stocked with hatchery brook trout, including Bunches Creek, Flat Creek, Sams Creek, Silers Creek, and McGinty Creek. A National Science Foundation funding proposal has been submitted with University of Tennessee collaborators.
- A distinct lineage could raise questions about hatchery-origin fish and possible federal listing.

**6. Trout Unlimited is directly supporting restoration efforts.**
- Chapters in Tennessee have removed rainbows from brook trout streams using electrofishing, toxins, and artificial barriers. Brookshire Creek in the Cherokee National Forest is now a brook-trout-only stream.
- In North Carolina, TU helped establish a fish refuge on 20 miles of Panthertown Creek. The land was transferred from Duke Power through The Nature Conservancy to the U.S. Forest Service, and catch-and-release regulations took effect July 1, 1990.
- A proposed Tellico Wildlife Management Area nursery, in partnership with the Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency, would propagate native brook trout and seed Tennessee streams. A $8,000 matching grant from the Sport Fishing Institute is pending.

**7. Hydropower relicensing is a major conservation opportunity.**
- The 1993 expiration of 225 federal licenses offers anglers a chance to restore coldwater habitat and wild populations.
- The Deerfield River hosts wild brown trout near Monroe, Massachusetts. A 22-pound brown trout is reported from near Sherman Dam in 1956, and the official Massachusetts record is 19 pounds 10 ounces.

**8. Policy critique.**
- Camuto argues that effective environmental progress requires campaign funding reform and federal leadership, and that scientific findings such as NAPAP results are being politically misused.
