# Effect of Incubation Temperature on Survival of Embryos of Native Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cypriniformes

## Executive Summary

This study experimentally tested how incubation temperature affects embryo survival and hatching in four endemic "big river" fishes of the [[Colorado River]]: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. All four are near extirpation in the wild. Embryos were incubated at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30°C. Survival and hatch peaked at or near 20°C, while total mortality occurred at 5, 10 and 30°C. The author concludes that optimal development is probably near 20°C, that cold water from hypolimnetic reservoir releases may limit reproduction, and that other causes of decline, such as predation by introduced fishes, must also be examined.

## Background & Description

The four species form the endemic fish fauna of the Colorado River, and all are listed as threatened or endangered by one or more western states. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service considers all but the razorback sucker endangered.

Historic ranges covered the larger rivers of the basin, or the middle and upper Colorado and Green rivers for the humpback chub. Present distributions are sharply reduced:

- **Razorback sucker:** found above Grand Canyon and in [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Bonytail chub:** persists in the Green River and Lake Mohave.
- **Humpback chub:** persists near the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] and in Desolation Canyon of the Green River.
- **Colorado squawfish:** restricted to the upper river in Colorado, Utah and Wyoming.

Declines have been attributed to dewatering, mainstream dams, altered flow and water quality, and introduced fishes. Lowered water temperatures below hypolimnetic reservoir releases have been repeatedly proposed as a cause of reduced reproduction. This study was designed to quantify the relationship between incubation temperature and embryo survival directly.

**Study system:** Water came from two wells at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. It was high in salts, especially sulfate (about 1900-1930 mg/L), with dissolved solids of 3340-3720 mg/L, total hardness of about 2067-2183 mg/L as CaCO3, and nitrate-N of 11.1-12.0 mg/L. The author notes that these ionic concentrations are not notably different from those of historic and present natural habitats at summer flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regimes.** The author compares historic and modern temperature records:

- Before closure of [[Hoover Dam]] in 1935, mainstream Colorado River temperatures ranged from about 10-30°C, similar to unregulated reaches today.
- Below Hoover Dam, tailwater temperatures from 1941-80 ranged from 9.0-15.0°C.
- Below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], temperatures after the 1963 formation of [[Lake Powell]] were 9-15°C year-round, against a pre-dam range of 2-26°C. Lee's Ferry (7-10°C) lies 24 km downstream.
- On the Green River at Greenlee, below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], the pre-closure range of 0.6-21.1°C narrowed to 2.7-12.2°C after closure.
- Below Davis Dam the range was 10.0-19.0°C, and below Parker Dam 8.5-26.0°C, the greater range being attributed to epilimnetic penstocks.
- In the Salt River (1959-1965, above reservoirs), temperatures exceeded 15°C from April to mid-October.

**Reservoir-related reproductive failure.** Bonytail chub and razorback sucker still occur in Lake Mohave, but no successful recruitment by either species has been documented since about the mid-1950s. The author suggests cool water may contribute, but notes that fertile ova and fry of razorback sucker have been collected recently, so other factors are probably important.

**Unexplained extirpation.** Native fishes are largely extirpated from reaches that appear thermally suitable for completing the life cycle. The author argues that thermal effects of dams have contributed to decline, but that [[Predation By Introduced Fishes]] and other interference competition must also be investigated.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**

- Eggs were fertilized at 18°C and incubated in six 22 L glass aquaria at nominal 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30°C. Five aquaria received continuous flow at about 1.0 L/h; the 30°C aquarium was static and tests there ended within 24 h.
- Each replicate chamber received 100 fertilized ova. Chambers were 76 mm PVC pipe with nylon mesh bottoms, with six to eight suspended per aquarium.
- Adults came from wild stocks (Lake Mohave, Little Colorado River) or hatchery stock from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Brood fish were not acclimated to test temperatures.
- Development rate was calculated as V = 1000/p, where p is hours to 50% hatch.

**Hatch and survival by species**

| Species | Hatch at 15°C | Hatch at 20°C | Hatch at 25°C | Hatch at 5, 10, 30°C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Razorback sucker | 19% | 35% | 29% | 0% |
| Bonytail chub | 35% | 32% | 0.5% | 0% |
| Humpback chub | 0.8% | 60% | 2% | 0% |
| Colorado squawfish | 0% (trial I and II) | 27% (I), 2% (II) | 0% (I), 9% (II) | 0% |

- Razorback sucker hatch at 15°C was significantly lower than at 20 and 25°C.
- Bonytail chub showed statistically equivalent hatch at 15 and 20°C.
- Humpback chub hatch was greatest at 20°C.
- Colorado squawfish was the only species with total mortality at 15°C in its first trial, and mortality at 5-15°C in both trials. Its results varied between trials, which the author attributes to unusual initial results.
- Most mortality occurred before embryos became motile within the chorion, for all species.

**Developmental anomalies**

- Razorback sucker: stunted or deformed fry were 8.1% of hatch at 20°C, 14.5% at 15°C and 32.2% at 25°C.
- Bonytail chub: abnormal fry ranged from 0 to 4% and were not clearly related to temperature.
- Humpback chub: abnormal fry were 33% at 15°C, 13% at 20°C and 17% at 25°C.
- Colorado squawfish: abnormal fry were 26% at 25°C, more than twice the 11% at 20°C.

**Hatchling size and timing**

- Hatched prolarvae were 0.2-1.3 mm TL longer at 20°C than at 15 or 25°C.
- Time to peak hatch fell as temperature rose. For razorback sucker it dropped from 216 h at 15°C to 84 h at 25°C.
- Swimup (neutral buoyancy) took 103-312 h for razorback sucker, depending on temperature.

**Development rates**

- Development rates (V) were similar across species: 4.4-6.1 at 15°C, 8.4-9.9 at 20°C and 12.4-18.8 at 25°C.
- Slopes of the linear rate-temperature relationships differed significantly between some species pairs (Colorado squawfish vs. razorback sucker, humpback chub vs. razorback sucker, and Colorado squawfish vs. bonytail chub; F-test, p < 0.05).

**Principal conclusions**

- Survival and hatch are maximal near 20°C, and mortality rises sharply with departures of less than 10°C from that temperature.
- Low survival and high anomaly rates at 15 and 25°C suggest that optimal temperatures for development and hatching are probably near 20°C.
- Reproduction may be limited in the wild by low water temperatures below reservoirs, although hatching is not precluded.
- Predation on young and interference competition by introduced fishes remain untested explanations for the extirpation of these species from thermally suitable reaches.

**Caveats:** The author notes that the experimental system may not reproduce natural incubation conditions, because microhabitat characteristics of natural spawning sites are unknown. Factors such as fungus on eggs, broodfish condition, wild versus hatchery origin, handling and induced spawning may have affected absolute survival values more than the relative differences among temperatures. Development rate estimates are independent of net hatch estimates. Adult acclimation effects could not be assessed. The [[Endangered Species Act]] framework provides the regulatory context for these species.
