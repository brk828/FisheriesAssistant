# Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: Distribution, Status, and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled study, edited by William F. Miller and colleagues and published by the Western Division of the American Fisheries Society, reviews the distribution, relative abundance, and conservation status of fishes in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The authors recorded 55 fishes (52 species and 3 subspecies) in the basin, of which 42 are exotic, 4 are native and endemic-listed as threatened or endangered only in part, and a small group of native species is threatened or endangered. Data were drawn mainly from recent state and federal agency surveys and compiled by hydrologic subregion, tributary, or reservoir. The study concludes that several large-river natives, including the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], face serious decline, while exotic sport fishes dominate reservoirs and lower zones.

## Background & Description

The document is a regional fisheries assessment of the Upper Colorado River Basin, covering the Upper Main-stem (Green River confluence upriver), the Green River, the Yampa, White, Duchesne, and San Rafael systems, the San Juan-Colorado subregion, and the San Juan River. Classification of status followed these categories:

- **EN (endemic):** found only in the Colorado River Basin.
- **NA (native):** native to the basin, present before human introductions.
- **EX (exotic):** introduced by humans.
- **TH (threatened)** and **ED (endangered)**, based on [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listings and [[Colorado Wildlife Commission]] regulations; where sources disagreed, the most restrictive classification was used.

Scientific and common names followed [[Robins et al. 1980]], with the exception of the bonytail. Abundance was rated as Abundant (A), Common (C), Rare (R), or Incidental (I). Ratings were inferred from varied source material, so they are relative rather than quantitative. Distribution maps (Figures 2 to 46) show species occurrence in the basin, and most source data came from state wildlife agencies in Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, New Mexico, and Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fishes occupy a wide range of habitats, from cold headwater streams and mountain lakes to large river channels and reservoirs. Table 5 summarizes preferred habitat and associated species for each taxon. Examples include:

- **Headwater streams and lakes:** [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Mountain Whitefish]] occupy cold, clear waters with gravel or cobble substrates.
- **Main-stem river channels:** [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] use deep runs, eddies, and swift canyon reaches.
- **Reservoirs:** 42 species occur in reservoirs, most of them exotic. Reservoir fishes such as [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] are typically restricted to lentic habitats.

Exotic fishes are concentrated in reservoirs and lower zones, while salmonids and native cyprinids are concentrated in upper zones. The document notes that exotic fishes have altered native communities, and that competition and predation by exotics are plausible causes of poor native recruitment (see [[Razorback Sucker]]).

## Key Findings

**Species inventory and status**
- The basin supports 55 fish taxa (52 species and 3 subspecies).
- The status summary lists 42 exotic, [number not stated in text] native, 1 additional threatened species, and 5 endangered fishes. Joseph et al. (1977) listed 27 native fishes, 4 endemic, and fewer exotics, which the authors attribute to differences in treatment and to new introductions.
- Recent reports list 10 additional fishes from the Upper Colorado River Basin (Table 6, hybrids), though identification was not validated.

**Exotic fishes**
- Fifteen exotic fishes were probably introduced into the upper basin, mostly through pond and reservoir stocking. Some are restricted to reservoirs, and some are widely distributed in the major tributaries.
- Only three native and exotic fishes were classified as abundant; the document reports a total of 19 exotics classified as abundant in some tributaries.

**Native and abundant species**
- Fathead minnow was the most widespread common fish, found across many habitat types, but restricted largely to one habitat type.
- Red shiner was designated abundant.
- Three native fishes (including [[Roundtail Chub]]) were classified as abundant in some major tributaries. Common natives included [[Mountain Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].

**Threatened and endangered fishes**
- Recent listing changes: the bonytail chub and Kendall Warm Springs dace were listed as endangered; the razorback sucker's proposed threatened listing was withdrawn; Colorado has listed the Colorado River cutthroat trout as threatened and the razorback sucker as endangered.
- The [[Colorado Squawfish]] has a much reduced historic range, is now restricted to the Upper Colorado River Basin, and requires a wide distribution for life-history needs.
- The [[Humpback Chub]] has shown a significant decline in the Green River, with only two taken from the Yampa River in over two years of sampling; new concentrations were found in Westwater and Cataract canyons.
- The [[Bonytail Chub]] is now the rarest endemic fish in the Upper Basin, with only a few old adults and one restricted location (Gray Canyon, Green River) producing recent adult collections. The recovery plan aims to prevent extinction rather than achieve recovery.
- The [[Razorback Sucker]] is declining, young are rarely found, and the authors recommend listing it as threatened or endangered.
- The [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] was introduced into Florence Creek and not recorded afterward; it is classified as exotic in the Upper Colorado River Basin.

**Conclusions**
- Rare native fishes are threatened by continued water development, reproductive failure, and predation or competition from exotics.
- Abundance data should be interpreted cautiously, since point collections of rare fish do not indicate commonness.
