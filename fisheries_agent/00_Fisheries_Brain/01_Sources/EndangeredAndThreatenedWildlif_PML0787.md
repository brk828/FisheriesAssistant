# Virgin River Fishes Critical Habitat Proposal (1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service) proposed in the *Federal Register* of April 5, 1995 (Vol. 60, No. 65; RIN 1018-AD22) to designate [[Critical Habitat]] for three fishes endemic to the [[Virgin River]] Basin: the [[Woundfin]], the [[Virgin River Chub]], and the [[Virgin Spinedace]]. The woundfin and Virgin River chub were listed as endangered. The Virgin spinedace was proposed for listing as threatened on May 18, 1994, but that listing had not been finalized. The proposal covers the mainstem Virgin River, its tributaries, and their 100-year floodplains. The document also includes an economic analysis of the designation, a public hearing notice, and a legal description of each reach. A court order required the proposal by April 1, 1995 and a final rule by December 1, 1995.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The Virgin River flows about 320 km (200 mi) southwest from south-central Utah to [[Lake Mead]] and follows the [[Hurricane Fault]], which marks the boundary between the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Great Basin]]. Before [[Boulder Dam]] was completed in 1935, the [[Muddy River]] joined the Virgin River before it reached the [[Colorado River]].

**Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*).** Adults occupy runs and quiet waters next to riffles with sand or sand/gravel substrates. They are generally found at depths of 0.15–0.43 m and velocities of 0.24–0.45 m/s. Juveniles prefer slower, deeper water, and fry use backwaters and stream margins with filamentous algae. Spawning occurs during declining spring flows. Historically the species ranged from near Tempe, Arizona, to Yuma, Arizona, and into the Virgin River and La Verkin Creek. It now survives in the mainstem Virgin River from Pah Tempe Springs to Lake Mead and in the lower La Verkin Creek.

**Virgin River Chub (*Gila seminuda*).** The species was described in 1875 and first collected near Washington, Utah, in the 1870s. Taxonomic status has shifted. Ellis (1914) treated it as a subspecies of the roundtail chub. DeMarais et al. (1992) argued for full species status, and the Service and fish-name committees later accepted this. The Muddy River form is included in *G. seminuda* but is excluded from this proposal because its status is still under review. Adults and juveniles use deep runs or pools with slow to moderate velocities, boulders, or other instream cover over sand. Chub are found in velocities up to 0.76 m/s. The species is now found from Pah Tempe Springs downstream to at least the Mesquite Diversion.

**Virgin Spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*).** Historically the species occurred in most clear-water tributaries and several mainstem reaches. Over the last 50 years its range has declined by about 32–40 percent, which the authors attribute to human impacts. Several former reaches, including the East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash, the Santa Clara River below Gunlock Reservoir, and Ash Creek, are now dewatered. Spinedace occupy runs and pools in clear streams with cover from vegetation, boulders, debris, or undercut banks. They use depths of 0.1–0.9 m and velocities of 0.1–1.0 m/s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floodplain energy.** The Virgin River carries little aquatic vegetation and produces little autochthonous organic matter, so its fauna depends on allochthonous energy from the floodplain. Seasonally inundated uplands support fish production, provide nutrients and terrestrial food, and offer spawning, nursery, and refuge habitat. The document cites Karr and Schlosser (1978) for a reduction in fish production of as much as 98 percent following floodplain loss. Native fishes in the basin are linked to a natural flow regime of high spring and autumn/winter flows.

**Threats.** The three species have declined because of cumulative effects: dewatering from many diversion projects, proliferation of [[Nonnative Fishes]], and changes to natural flow, temperature, and sediment regimes. The Service identifies water diversions, flow changes, nonnative species, contamination, and habitat loss from development as the main threats. Activities such as recreation, some farming, and ranching are judged unlikely to adversely modify critical habitat.

**Proposed habitat.** The Service proposes the following totals (Table 1; state totals are not additive across species):
- Woundfin: 151.7 km (94.8 mi), about 13.5 percent of its historical range.
- Virgin River chub: 151.7 km (94.8 mi).
- Virgin spinedace: 201.9 km (126.2 mi).
- Combined total: 330.8 km (206.8 mi).

Most of the land is in federal or private ownership. Designated reaches include the mainstem Virgin River, the East and North Forks, La Verkin Creek, Ash Creek, Beaver Dam Wash, Shunes Creek, Santa Clara River, and Mogotsu Creek.

**Economic setting.** The [[Economic Analysis]] (Brookshire et al. 1995) covers Washington and Iron Counties in Utah, Clark County in Nevada, and Mohave County in Arizona north of the Colorado River. Washington and Clark Counties are among the fastest-growing areas in the United States. The analysis covers a 45-year period (1995–2040). It uses input-output models and compares three scenarios:
- **WOFBA** (baseline, "without fish"): water is allocated to urban and industrial uses without fish protections.
- **WFST** ("with fish," structural): the fish are protected through new water projects built earlier and conversion of agricultural water.
- **WFCO** ("with fish," conservation): the same goals are met mainly through water conservation.

## Key Findings

- **Designation scope.** The proposal would add reaches to 50 CFR 17.95(e) for the Virgin River chub and woundfin and to 17.95(e) for the Virgin spinedace, along with the Muddy River and other exclusions.
- **Economic effects under WFST.** Present-value output losses for Washington County are about -$13.7 million (0.00046 percent of baseline), and for the full region about -$20.9 million. Annualized, Washington County output falls by -$1.95 million (0.0016 percent) and Clark County by -$0.43 million. The region-wide annualized output effect is -$2.45 million.
- **Factor payments.** Washington County's present-value change in value added is -$24.62 million under WFST and falls to -$8.15 million with the conservation measures. Clark County's is -$4.65 million.
- **Conservation mitigates most impacts.** The WFCO scenario nearly fully offsets economic effects, leaving negative effects mainly in agriculture and construction by the end of the study period. The authors note these later effects may be overstated.
- **Regulatory consequences.** Section 7 requires federal agencies to consult with the Service on actions that may destroy or adversely modify critical habitat. Designation does not create a wilderness area, a management plan, or restrictions on private land without federal involvement. Section 4(b)(2) allows exclusion of areas when economic or other benefits outweigh conservation benefits, provided exclusion does not cause extinction. The proposal has no significant effect on small entities under the Regulatory Flexibility Act.
- **Public process.** Comments were accepted until June 5, 1995. Requests for a hearing were due within 45 days. A public hearing was held on May 8, 1995, 5–9 p.m., at the [[St. George Hilton Inn]] (registration 4:30 p.m.). The Service also released a draft economic analysis and a biological overview (Maddux et al. 1995).
- **Contacts.** Primary authors are Henry R. Maddux and Janet A. Mitchell (Salt Lake City Field Office), Selena J. Werdon (Nevada State Office), and Lesley A. Fitzpatrick (Arizona State Office). The Field Supervisor is Reed E. Harris.
