## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary research paper but a set of fax-transmitted administrative records and a **draft meeting summary** from a March 2, 1994 scientists' meeting held in Las Vegas, Nevada, convened to discuss the Draft Biological Opinion on **Glen Canyon Dam** operations under the Endangered Species Act (ESA). The meeting, moderated by Dr. Duncan Patten, brought together federal and academic researchers (USFWS, NPS, GCES, Arizona Game and Fish, Bio/West, Arizona State University, Utah State University) to discuss the ecological "drivers" affecting the endangered **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) and other native fish in the Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam, and to evaluate flow management alternatives — the Draft Biological Opinion flow recommendation versus the **Modified Low Fluctuating Flow (MLFF)** alternative. No morphometric or taxonomic data are presented; the content is ecological/hydrological management discussion relevant to endangered fish recovery in the Grand Canyon ecosystem.

## Background & Description

No morphological, meristic, or taxonomic description data are present in this source. The only quantitative fish-size reference given is a size threshold used to distinguish life stages for management purposes:

- **Adult threshold**: fish >175 mm (total length) are considered adults, largely insensitive to short-term flow/flood magnitude effects.
- Life stages tracked as "responding parameters": egg/larval, young-of-year (YOY), juvenile, adult — reflecting an ontogenetic framework rather than morphological description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The meeting summary frames the **[[Humpback Chub]]** and other native Colorado River fishes within a highly regulated, dam-controlled riverine ecosystem. Key habitat/niche elements discussed:

- **Habitat types**: backwaters, eddy return channels, tributary mouths (including the **[[Little Colorado River]]**, referred to as "LCR"), and shoreline habitat.
- **Backwaters** are identified as critical nursery/rearing habitat for native fish; younger backwaters are noted as less productive than mature ones.
- **Thermal regime**: The Colorado River mainstem below Glen Canyon Dam is cold due to hypolimnetic reservoir releases; the LCR (a warmer tributary) is considered near carrying capacity for native fish, implying mainstem temperature is a limiting factor for broader population expansion.
- **Flow variability**: Both daily (diurnal) and annual flow fluctuations affect habitat stability; minimal diurnal fluctuation is identified as ecologically preferable since fish movement in response to flow changes is energetically inefficient.
- **Non-native species interactions**: Non-native fish populations recover rapidly after flow-driven declines, suggesting flow manipulation alone (even large-magnitude floods) is unlikely to control non-native competitors/predators in the [[Grand Canyon]] reach.
- **Sediment and turbidity dynamics** are linked to flood events and are important for maintaining backwater and shoreline habitat structure.
- **Food base**: Under current dam operations, food base is not considered limiting for adult native fish, though effects on earlier life stages (egg, larval, YOY) remain unknown.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory goal clarified**: The purpose of a "reasonable and prudent alternative" (RPA) under the ESA is to **remove jeopardy**, not to achieve full species recovery — an important legal/ecological distinction guiding the flow recommendations.
- **Flood classification** (by discharge, in cfs) established for management purposes:
  - *Maintenance flow*: <31,000 cfs (≈ power plant capacity); ~annual occurrence.
  - *Habitat-building flow*: ~45,000 cfs (±); recurrence ~1-in-8 years.
  - *Uncontrolled flood*: >31,000 cfs plus tributary flow, turbid water; recurrence ~1-in-100 years.
- **Life-stage sensitivity**: Flood magnitude, duration, and hydrograph shape scarcely affect adult fish (>175 mm) but significantly affect **eggs and larvae**, indicating research and management focus should prioritize recruitment and early life stages rather than adults.
- **Flood timing**: Controlled high-flow releases should mimic a natural hydrograph and, where possible, be scheduled **one year following a strong year class**, prior to May, to maximize benefit to recruitment.
- **Reservoir thermal management**: Water released for controlled floods should be drawn from the reservoir surface (not bypass tubes) to achieve warmer release temperatures — though this requires a full reservoir and may entrain pelagic reservoir fish downstream (considered low-risk since these species typically fail to survive in riverine conditions).
- **Non-native control limits**: An estimated 3 orders of magnitude increase in flow above baseline would be needed to meaningfully suppress non-native fish in the Grand Canyon — a magnitude considered unrealistic under any operational scenario.
- **Cumulative impacts**: Effects of dam operations on fish are cumulative across drivers (flooding, sediment, temperature, food base, predation, disease/parasites, non-native competition) and should not be evaluated in isolation.
- **Temperature vs. flow**: Temperature modification of the mainstem is likely more important than flow manipulation alone for increasing native fish populations; jeopardy likely **cannot be removed without mainstem temperature modification**, since the LCR tributary population is believed near carrying capacity.
- **Backwater maintenance**: Annual habitat-maintenance floods are not necessary every year to sustain backwaters or eddy return channels used by native fish.
- **Flow alternative comparison**: Both the Draft Biological Opinion flow recommendation and the **[[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow]]** alternative fall within the general physiological tolerance range of the Humpback Chub, and **neither scenario was expected to fully remove jeopardy**. Due to the complexity and data limitations of the system, rigorous statistical comparison between alternatives was considered infeasible, requiring reliance on best professional judgment.
- **Scientific consensus**: Most researchers present favored the reasonable and prudent alternative as having a higher probability of benefiting **[[Humpback Chub]]** and native fish populations compared to MLFF, and recommended that the final Biological Opinion also explicitly address **critical habitat** designation.
