# Ecological Studies of Flora and Fauna in Glen Canyon (1959)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 compendium, issued as the [[University of Utah]] Anthropological Papers (No. 40, Glen Canyon Series No. 7), presents the first biological baseline for the canyon region before the planned [[Glen Canyon Reservoir]] inundated it. The work was part of a salvage program run jointly with the [[National Park Service]] and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. It combines eight papers on vegetation, mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, parasites, insects, and the aquatic ecology of the [[Colorado River]], with the fish and aquatic survey by Gerald R. Smith, Guy G. Musser, and Donald B. McDonald as its core. The fish findings are the main focus of this note.

The aquatic survey found that the river itself is sediment-laden, swift, and very low in productivity, while its tributaries are far more biologically productive. Of 17 fish species recorded, only six are native, and introduced species such as [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] dominate collections in the river.

## Background & Description

The program was initiated after Congress authorized upper Colorado River storage development in 1956. The biological work was organized by [[Angus M. Woodbury]], Biological Editor, under the [[Division of Biological Sciences]] at the University of Utah. The volume was also meant to supply a working plan for long-term ecological studies, planned over roughly 20 years in four phases:

1. Preliminary planning
2. Inventory of the biota before filling
3. Changes during filling
4. Effects of the artificial lake on surrounding vegetation and animals

Field work consisted of six components between August 1957 and August 1958:

- Reconnaissance flight over the Glen Canyon region (August 19, 1957)
- Preliminary boat trip, Hite to Lees Ferry (September 1957)
- Lake Mead boat trip on siltation at the Virgin River mouth (September 1957)
- Survey of Wahweap to Kane Creek (March 23–26, 1958)
- Preliminary trip to Hite (June 8–10, 1958)
- Full expedition of 19 men, Hite to Lees Ferry (June 30–August 9, 1958)

The aquatic work was done during the summer 1958 expedition by Smith, Musser, and McDonald, the last representing the [[Utah State Department of Fish and Game]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Proposed reservoir.** The planned dam lies about 13 stream miles below the Utah–Arizona line. It would be a 580-foot concrete arch structure, and at full pool (about 3,700 ft elevation) the reservoir would extend about 186 miles up the Colorado River. Reported figures include:

- Surface area of about 162,000 acres at full pool
- Capacity of about 28 million acre-feet
- Average annual fluctuation of about 32 feet
- Sediment input of about 100,000 acre-feet per year, implying filling in roughly 280 years
- Gates planned to close in late summer 1962, with about ten years to fill

**Setting.** The region is a rugged, arid landscape with July temperatures averaging 82°F and January 35°F at Lees Ferry. Annual precipitation averages 5.2 inches, mostly from summer thunderstorms, and evaporation is about 50 inches per year. Sparse vegetation causes flash floods that carry large loads of sand and clay into the river. The total silt load passing Lees Ferry is estimated at roughly 100 million tons per year.

**Expected impacts.** The authors expected the reservoir to replace the terrestrial flora and fauna below the flood line with aquatic biota. They also predicted:

- Changes in the river from turbid to clear, cold, and thermally stable water downstream of the dam
- Loss of river species and emergence of new lake-dwelling populations
- Deposition of coarse sediment in deltas at tributary mouths

## Key Findings

**Survey scope.** The aquatic survey made observations at 46 stations, 15 of them on the river and the remainder in tributaries. Reported collections include:

- River: 116 fish specimens and 242 insect specimens of 16 species from the 15 river sites
- Tributaries: 869 fish specimens of 12 species and 2,069 insect specimens of 77 species (six orders) from 42 tributary sites
- Algae: a list of genera and species from Glen Canyon, given in Appendix D

**Fishes.** The annotated checklist (Appendix B) records 17 species. Only six are native to the Colorado River system. The native fishes include the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The introduced species include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], and [[Yellow Perch]].

- In the river, 72 channel catfish from five of 16 stations, one black bullhead, and 34 carp from two stations far outnumbered the eight flannelmouth suckers from four stations and a single speckled dace.
- In the tributaries, native speckled dace and introduced fathead minnows compete closely for dominance in small creeks. Native mountain suckers and introduced carp compete for second place, with carp apparently ahead.
- Young fish of all species were common in tributaries but scarce in the river. The authors suggest that the river's sandy effluent limits survival there.

**Food habits.** Fish stomachs were examined to assess feeding:

- River: 21 of 44 stomachs were empty or nearly so.
- Tributaries: only one of 16 stomachs was empty.
- Channel catfish (35 from the river, nine from [[Aztec Creek]]) were omnivorous, taking camp refuse, aquatic and terrestrial insects, and small cyprinids.
- Flannelmouth suckers and carp had similar gut contents, mostly amorphous material from muddy pools, suggesting competition for the same food.

**Physical and chemical conditions.**

- River temperature ranged from about 72°F at Hite (July 1, about 22,000 cfs) to about 80°F at Lees Ferry (August 8, about 4,000 cfs). Tributary temperatures ranged from 63°F to 92°F.
- River pH ranged from about 7.6 to 8.8. Tributary pH ranged from about 7.4 to 9.0.
- Dissolved oxygen was 7.3–8.8 ppm in the river and 4.8–8.8 ppm in tributaries.
- Free carbon dioxide was negligible. Bicarbonates were modest, and the water was described as relatively "soft," with greater variability among tributaries.

**Productivity.** The river is extremely low in productivity because of unstable, continuously moving sand. Productive microhabitats occur mainly at rapids, riffles, whirlpools, backwaters, and the lee sides of rocks. Tributaries are "overwhelmingly" more productive per unit volume, because they offer clearer, calmer water and stable substrates.

**Insects.** Specimens belong to eight orders. Aquatic stoneflies (Plecoptera) and grasshoppers (Orthoptera) were known from the region but were not collected. Notable records include:

- Mayflies: [[Homoeoneuria]] (possibly a new sand-dwelling species), [[Traverella albertana]], and [[Lachlania powelli]], with mating flights observed.
- Dragonflies and damselflies: abundant in side canyons. A large pale-winged mayfly-associated group and the caddisfly families were also documented.
- Beetles: 22 species, the largest number of any order, with none recorded from the river.
- Most sand in the river was found to be practically sterile of macroscopic life.

**Management recommendations.** The authors called for range management to reduce silt from overgrazed tributary drainages (such as the Paria River), a sediment-catching structure on the Paria River, and long-term monitoring of the aquatic biota through the transition from river to reservoir.
