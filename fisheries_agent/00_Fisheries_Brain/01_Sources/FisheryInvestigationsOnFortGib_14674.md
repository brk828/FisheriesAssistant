# Fishery Investigations on Fort Gibson Reservoir for the Year 1959

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-management #reservoir-ecology #population-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This job completion report documents comprehensive fishery investigations conducted on [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]], Oklahoma, from January through December 1959 under Federal Aid Project F-6-R-3. The study encompassed three major components: (1) rotenone population sampling in three dissimilar coves to assess standing crop and species composition, (2) experimental evaluation of trap nets, trawls, and whip trammel nets for rough fish removal, and (3) food habit analysis of buffalofish species. The investigations revealed extremely high young-of-the-year [[Gizzard Shad]] densities (53,000 fish per acre), successful reproduction of sport fish including [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[White Crappie]], and demonstrated the potential effectiveness of various commercial fishing gear for capturing undesirable species. Project leader Phillip B. Summers conducted this work for the Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation's Federal Aid Division.

## Background & Description

The 1959 investigations built upon previous year's work (1958) at [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]], utilizing identical sampling sites to enable year-to-year comparisons. Three dissimilar coves were selected to represent the reservoir's diversity: Duncan Cove (3.0 acres, maximum depth 20 feet, treated August 26), North Bay (12.0 acres, treated September 16), and Yonkers Cove (8.0 acres, treated September 23). The rotenone sampling methodology involved blocking cove entrances with small-mesh nets, releasing fin-clipped marked fish captured from trap nets to estimate recovery percentages, and conducting intensive first-day pick-up efforts to maximize fish recovery for processing.

The experimental gear evaluation component tested multiple capture methods: large trap nets (600-foot lead, 16 feet high) and small trap nets (200-foot lead, 4 feet high) fished continuously throughout the year; a 20-foot-wide, 30-foot-long one-inch mesh otter trawl constructed of Nycot synthetic material; and a 600-foot by 6-foot nylon whip trammel net with 2.5-inch stretched mesh and 10-inch outside hanging walls. The whip trammel technique involved setting nets from electrically-powered boats to minimize disturbance, then driving fish into nets using surface and underwater disturbance methods.

Food habit studies focused on buffalofish (either [[Black Buffalo]] or [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], not differentiated due to taxonomic uncertainties). Fifty specimens ranging 12.3-16.0 inches (average 15.2 inches) were collected March-December 1959 using whip trammel nets. The visceral section including the muscularized gizzard and anterior intestinal tract was extracted immediately upon capture, flushed with tapwater, and preserved in 10% formalin. Contents were examined at 37.5× magnification for macroscopic forms and 150× for microorganisms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Fort Gibson Reservoir]] experienced significant hydrological variation during 1959, with a notable water level rise in October that failed to subside until late November. This high water event decidedly influenced trap net effectiveness, resulting in sharp declines in catch rates. The reservoir's habitat diversity included mud-flat coves (abandoned field or grassland-type inundations) that consistently revealed greater fish concentrations, and deep rocky coves that were less productive for buffalofish capture.

The reservoir supported a complex fish community including sport fish ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]]), rough fish ([[Carp]], [[River Carpsucker]], [[Buffalo]] species, [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Redhorse]], [[Drum]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Spotted Gar]], [[Longnose Gar]], [[Black Bullhead]]), and forage fish ([[Gizzard Shad]]). The dominance of young-of-the-year [[Gizzard Shad]] (53,000 per acre in 1959 versus 11,211 per acre in 1958) indicated extremely successful reproduction and highlighted this species' role as the primary forage base.

Plankton communities supporting the fish populations included copepods (dominant at 61.75% occurrence in buffalofish diets), cladocerans (13.84%), and various other microorganisms. The excellent preservation condition of organisms in buffalofish stomachs suggested active feeding on live prey rather than detritus consumption.

## Key Findings

**Population Sampling Results:**

- Young-of-the-year [[Gizzard Shad]] estimates: North Bay 3,499/acre (70.0 lbs/acre), Yonkers 7,153/acre (71.5 lbs/acre), Duncan Cove 7,564/acre (75.6 lbs/acre)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] young-of-the-year: 30 fish/acre weighing 6.0 lbs/acre (1959) versus 19 fish weighing 0.7 lbs/acre (1958), indicating improved reproduction
- Adult [[Largemouth Bass]]: 8 fish/acre weighing 5.4 lbs/acre (1959) versus 38 fish weighing 19.65 lbs/acre (1958), showing population decline
- [[White Crappie]] young-of-the-year: 232 fish/acre (1959) versus 74 fish/acre (1958), representing substantial increase
- Adult [[White Crappie]]: 63 fish/acre weighing 19.0 lbs/acre (1959) versus 76 fish weighing 12.0 lbs/acre (1958)
- [[Channel Catfish]] young-of-the-year: 85 fish/acre weighing 2.0 lbs/acre (1959), a notable appearance after previously negligible recoveries
- Adult [[Channel Catfish]]: 41 fish/acre weighing 16.2 lbs/acre (1959) versus 31 fish weighing 13.1 lbs/acre (1958)

**Rough Fish Populations:**

- [[Carp]]: 58 fish/acre weighing 84.0 lbs/acre (1959) versus 55 fish weighing 22.8 lbs/acre (1958), showing increased adult biomass
- [[River Carpsucker]] young-of-the-year: 187 fish/acre (1959) versus 1 fish/acre (1958), dramatic increase
- [[Buffalo]] species: 12 fish/acre weighing 20.3 lbs/acre with minimal successful reproduction
- [[Drum]]: 152 adult fish/acre (1959) versus 90 fish/acre (1958); young-of-the-year 385 fish/acre weighing 3.2 lbs/acre (1959) versus 14 fish weighing 0.2 lbs/acre (1958)

**Growth Rates (Average Calculated Total Length at End of Each Year):**

- [[Largemouth Bass]] (32 specimens, 4 year classes): 6.6", 11.5", 15.8", 17.6"
- [[White Bass]] (3 specimens, 2 year classes): 6.7", 10.5"
- [[White Crappie]] (26 specimens, 3 year classes): 1.9", 5.8", 8.6"
- [[Channel Catfish]] (49 specimens, 5 year classes): 3.4", 6.9", 9.9", 12.0", 16.9"
- [[River Carpsucker]] (26 specimens): 4.7", 8.1", 11.3", 12.7", 13.6", 12.9"
- [[Buffalo]] species (28 specimens): 5.3", 7.9", 10.0", 12.1", 13.7", 14.8"
- [[Carp]] (19 specimens): 5.4", 8.8", 11.1", 14.0", 17.9", 17.6", 21.0", 22.1", 22.9"

**Trap Net Evaluation:**

- Large trap net (12-month average pounds per lift): [[Channel Catfish]] 42.0 lbs (highest), [[Buffalo]] species 25.1 lbs, [[White Crappie]] 22.0 lbs, [[River Carpsucker]] 19.0 lbs, [[Carp]] 10.0 lbs, [[Drum]] 6.4 lbs
- Small trap nets (12-month average pounds per lift): [[Channel Catfish]] 26.1 lbs (highest), [[Carp]] 23.0 lbs, [[River Carpsucker]] 12.3 lbs, [[Buffalo]] species 11.0 lbs, [[White Crappie]] 6.8 lbs, [[Drum]] 6.2 lbs
- Seasonal pattern: increased effectiveness during colder months (November-February) with sharp decline during October 1959 high water event
- Shift from 1958 when [[White Crappie]] and [[Channel Catfish]] dominated catches to 1959 when rough fish ([[Carp]], [[Buffalo]]) ranked higher, suggesting potential for rough fish control

**Trawl Evaluation:**

- Seven hauls over five months, average 18.0 lbs per 15-minute tow
- Average pounds per tow: [[Carp]] 9.2 lbs, [[White Crappie]] 3.2 lbs, [[River Carpsucker]] 2.2 lbs, [[Drum]] 1.5 lbs, [[Channel Catfish]] 0.7 lbs
- Size range: largest specimen [[Carp]] at 15.7 inches; captured young-of-the-year and yearlings of nearly all major species except [[Largemouth Bass]]
- Average total lengths: [[River Carpsucker]] 14.7", [[Flathead Catfish]] 12.0", [[Carp]] 11.8", [[Drum]] 8.0", [[Buffalo]] 8.0", [[Channel Catfish]] 6.0", [[White Crappie]] 5.6", [[Bluegill]] 4.0"
- Operational difficulties: bottom irregularities, foreign materials (beer cans, batteries, rocks, tree stumps), insufficient power, lack of towing speed control

**Whip Trammel Net Results:**

- Twenty-seven sets April-July captured 2,025 lbs total; 2,010 lbs (99.3%) classified as rough fish, only 15 lbs (0.7%) game fish
- Total catch by species: [[Carp]] 1,158 lbs, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] 542 lbs, [[River Carpsucker]] 279 lbs, [[Drum]] 14 lbs, [[Channel Catfish]] 9 lbs, [[Largemouth Bass]] 5 lbs, [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] 4 lbs, [[Gar]] 4 lbs
- Average pounds per set varied 17.0-221.0 lbs depending on month and location
- High selectivity for rough fish demonstrates potential value for commercial fishery

**Gill Net and Trammel Net Summary (11-month period):**

- Total catch 2,891 lbs: [[Carp]] 1,077 lbs (dominant), [[Channel Catfish]] 391 lbs, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] 340 lbs, [[Flathead Catfish]] 309 lbs, [[Gar]] 227 lbs, [[River Carpsucker]] 192 lbs, [[Drum]] 128 lbs, [[White Bass]] 76 lbs, [[Spoonbill]] 60 lbs
- [[Carp]] remained most abundant rough fish throughout 1959 collections
- Increased [[River Carpsucker]] catches October-December attributed to reduced 2.5-inch mesh size better suited to this species' maximum size

**Food Habits of Buffalofish:**

- Fifty stomachs examined (ten empty); fish averaged 15.2 inches total length
- Percentage occurrence of food items: copepods 61.75%, cladocerans 13.84%, plant material 8.0%, filamentous algae 4.4%, oligochaetes 3.14%, ostracods 3.0%, chironomids 2.1%, diatoms 1.5%, rotifers 1.0%, desmids 1.0%, miscellaneous 0.53%
- Frequency of occurrence (percent of fish containing item): copepods 96.0%, cladocerans 60.0%, plant material 44.0%, filamentous algae 20.0%, chironomids 18.0%, ostracods 16.0%
- Spring/summer collections revealed female copepods with intact egg-filled sacs and nauplius stages
- Excellent preservation condition of organisms indicated consumption while alive
- Plant material included unidentifiable small twigs, leaf particles, and various seeds
- Buffalofish appeared capable of seeking out and consuming copepod concentrations in mud-flat habitats

**Recommendations:**

1. Continue rotenone cove sampling for reproduction, species composition, and growth rate information
2. Divide sampling into three seasonal categories (spring, summer, fall) and average recoveries for more accurate reservoir representation
3. Continue trap net evaluation directing investigations toward maximum effectiveness for capturing undesirable fishes
4. Conduct further trawling experimentation with larger nets and adequate boats rigged for power handling
5. Establish cleared trawling areas prior to reservoir impoundment
6. **Strongly recommended:** Approve and adopt whip trammel net fishing for commercial operators in Oklahoma (currently prohibited by 100-foot shoreline restriction)
7. Conduct more detailed food habit studies and compile information on less desirable fishes in Oklahoma waters

The 1959 investigations demonstrated that [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]] supported robust fish populations with variable year-class strength. The dramatic increase in young-of-the-year [[Gizzard Shad]] (from 11,211 to 53,000 per acre) and improved reproduction of several sport fish species indicated favorable environmental conditions. However, the continued dominance of rough fish species, particularly [[Carp]], and the demonstrated effectiveness of experimental gear (especially whip trammel nets with 99.3% rough fish selectivity) supported the potential for expanded commercial harvest programs to improve sport fishing quality.
