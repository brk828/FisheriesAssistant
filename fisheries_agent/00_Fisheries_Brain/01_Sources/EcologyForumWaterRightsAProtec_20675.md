# Water Rights: A Protection Tool for the West

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This Ecology Forum piece by David L. Harrison and Robert Wigington argues that [[Water Rights]] acquisition can protect stream habitats and native fishes that cannot be secured by buying adjoining land alone. Because western water law follows the [[Appropriation Doctrine]], upstream diversions can dry out protected reaches, so the authors describe how [[The Nature Conservancy]] has used purchase, donation, and conversion of water rights to [[Instream Flow Rights]]. Examples come from Arizona, Idaho, and Colorado, with the [[Gunnison River]] and [[Yampa River]] as the central cases for endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** In the eastern United States, [[Riparian Rights]] give shoreline owners the right to the reasonable continuation of a stream's flow, so no one may "kill" a stream by diverting or impounding its total flow. The West operates under the appropriation doctrine ("first in time, first in right"), established in practice by the 1876 Colorado case [[Coffin V. Left Hand Ditch Company]]. Senior diverters may take their full entitlement, while junior claimants, including riparian owners, may go without. Historically, no one could require water to remain in a stream.

**Changing law.** Western water law has been evolving to recognize "instream uses." Instream flow rights can be acquired to leave water in a river. The senior right still prevails, so a junior instream right can suffice where flows are adequate and upstream diversions do not threaten habitat.

**Legal fragility.** In Colorado and Idaho, apparently only a state agency may hold instream flow rights. In Idaho each instream right must be ratified by the legislature and may be reviewed after 15 years. In Colorado the agency holding instream rights also promotes water development.

**Property nature of water rights.** Western water rights are property. They can be bought, sold, donated, or traded, and they are not tied to riparian land, so they can be separated from surface ownership. A right can generally be changed in place or in use if other rights are not injured. The authors call acquiring strategic diversion rights and converting them to instream use an "ideal protection method."

**Conditional rights.** In Colorado, [[Conditional Water Rights]] secure priority for a future project, such as a dam, as long as work proceeds with "reasonable diligence." Many healthy river segments remain exposed to development under such filings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Gunnison River]]:** A "spectacular and biologically crucial" 13-mile reach (the [[Gunnison Gorge]]) faces three major dam proposals, the largest of which could fully inundate it. The Gunnison enters the Colorado just above the only confirmed spawning area for the [[Humpback Chub]]. Upstream development could also "dewater" the habitat even if the dams are never built.
- **[[Yampa River]]:** Described as a pristine example of the original Colorado River system and possibly the most critical aquatic habitat in the Upper Colorado River Basin. It is a refuge for the endemic [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], both federally listed as endangered, and probably the most significant confirmed spawning site for the federally protected [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Conditional rights for two large dams upstream would regulate its entire flow.
- **[[Boulder Creek]] (Colorado):** A water right appropriated in 1862 for irrigation was bought by the Conservancy and sold to the state for instream flow, subject to reversion if used for other purposes.
- **[[Minnie Miller Spring]] (Idaho):** One of the last of the famous Thousand Springs of the [[Snake River]]; the Conservancy sought an instream flow right here through the Idaho Water Resources Board.
- **Arizona preserves:** The Conservancy obtained instream flow permits for its [[Ramsey Canyon Preserve]] and [[Canelo Hills Preserve]].

## Key Findings

1. **Land purchase alone is insufficient.** Upstream diversions can strand a protected stream reach, so water rights must be secured alongside land.
2. **Junior instream rights can protect adequate flows.** Even a recent junior right may suffice where diversions do not threaten habitat.
3. **Instream rights are politically fragile.** State-only holding in Colorado and Idaho, legislative ratification in Idaho, and conflicting agency roles in Colorado weaken protection.
4. **Water rights are tradable property.** They can be bought, donated, traded, or converted to instream use, as shown by the Boulder Creek transaction.
5. **Conditional rights are a major threat.** Future-project filings on the Gunnison and Yampa could dewater critical habitat for endangered chubs and the Colorado squawfish.
6. **A donation agreement protected the Gunnison Gorge.** The Conservancy secured an agreement that the largest proposed dam will not be built in the 13-mile reach, with part of the conditional rights converted to instream flow. Proposals to trade remaining rights for water from an existing federal reservoir followed.
7. **Protection requires a larger package.** The Gunnison deal may combine water rights with federal reservoir operation, wild and scenic designations, and federally reserved rights. A broader program involving state, federal, and private parties is emerging for the Upper Colorado River Basin's endangered fishes.
8. **Water allocation is contentious.** Deeply held views on western water make these efforts emotionally charged, but the authors contend that water rights acquisition could be the "extra ingredient" needed to save the basin's native fishes.
