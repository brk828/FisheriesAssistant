# Develop and Refine Year-Round Flow Needs for Endangered Fish in the Upper Colorado River (Grand Valley / 15-Mile Reach)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This FY93 annual project report (Colorado River Recovery Program, Project #13) documents efforts to quantify and validate **year-round flow recommendations** needed to maintain and enhance riverine habitat for two endangered fishes — the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — in the **15-mile reach** and broader **Grand Valley** section of the Upper [[Colorado River]]. The study combined aerial videography, ground-truth habitat mapping, and permanent transect bed-profile surveys across eight key sites to relate habitat availability (backwaters, chute channels, bottomlands) to river discharge. Preliminary findings indicate that historic reductions in spring flow magnitude — more than changes in summer or winter flows — have driven major habitat degradation, including reduced bottomland flooding, sediment infilling of backwaters, and loss of channel complexity, all of which correlate with poor reproductive success of Colorado squawfish and increased nonnative fish proliferation. The report recommends increasing the frequency of high-magnitude spring flows to restore ecosystem function.

## Background & Description

This report is a hydrological/habitat management study and does not provide direct morphological descriptions of [[Colorado Squawfish]] or [[Razorback Sucker]]. Relevant physical/habitat-linked characteristics noted include:

- **Colorado squawfish**: Large-bodied [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] (North America's largest minnow); described in this report primarily in terms of adult habitat association (deep runs, backwaters) and spawning ecology tied to spring discharge magnitude.
- **Razorback sucker**: [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] species associated with the 15-mile reach as a known spawning area; no morphometric data reported.
- No length/weight, fin-ray counts, or other taxonomic morphology data are presented in this source; the paper focuses instead on **habitat morphology** — i.e., channel bed profiles, backwater depth/area, and chute-channel geometry as functions of discharge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types monitored**: backwaters, chute-channel backwaters, bottomlands, and main-channel bed profiles across **eight study sites** and **26 established transects** in the Grand Valley reach of the Colorado River.
- **Spawning habitat**: The 15-mile reach is a documented spawning area for both [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and is identified as critical adult squawfish habitat.
- **Flow–habitat relationship**: Spring discharge magnitude is the primary driver of habitat quality — high spring flows historically maintained bottomland flooding and scoured chute-channel backwaters, while decreased spring flows have led to sediment infilling and loss of nursery/wintering habitat.
- **Seasonal flow shifts**: Historic data show spring flows have significantly decreased, summer flows have decreased, and winter flows have increased relative to historic patterns — altering the seasonal habitat mosaic available to native fish.
- **Nonnative species interaction**: Proliferation of nonnative fish species appears linked to years of low spring runoff, suggesting altered hydrology favors non-native competitors/predators over native endangered fishes.
- **Study area context**: Findings pertain specifically to the [[Upper Colorado River]], including the **15-mile reach** (most vulnerable to further water depletion) and adjacent **18-mile reach** below the Gunnison River confluence.

## Key Findings

- Reproductive success of [[Colorado Squawfish]] correlates with the magnitude of spring discharge — low-runoff years produce poor reproductive success.
- Changes in summer/winter flows have likely **not** been major factors in adult squawfish and razorback sucker decline, though summer flows below optimum during drought years still threaten habitat, underscoring the need to secure instream flow rights.
- Changes in spring flow regime are believed to have caused **major, lasting changes** to year-round riverine habitat structure, including:
  - Cessation of routine bottomland flooding
  - Sediment infilling of chute-channel backwaters (loss of adult, spring, and wintering habitat, plus nursery habitat)
  - Reduced channel complexity and habitat diversity via sedimentation and vegetation encroachment
- Preliminary recommendation: **increase the frequency of high-magnitude spring flows** to restore a healthy ecosystem for native Colorado River fishes.
- Methodology: Combined aerial video-imagery (flown by Bureau of Reclamation) with concurrent ground-truth habitat mapping and transect-based bed-profile monitoring (1990–1993) to develop an empirical, incremental flow-habitat relationship — an alternative/complement to earlier PHABSIM computer-modeled summer flow recommendations (1989).
- Final 1991 report (Osmundson & Kaeding) established **October–June flow recommendations** for the 15-mile reach; the FY93 videography/habitat study was designed to validate and refine these, plus extend analysis toward summer flow needs and the 18-mile reach.
- Project tasks related to habitat quantification and bed-profile/sedimentation analysis were behind schedule as of FY93; four consecutive years (1990–1993) of transect bed-profile data were collected, exceeding the original 2-year scope.
- No dedicated funding was available for FY94, threatening completion of remaining analytical tasks (relating habitat change and sedimentation to discharge) despite continued data collection.
