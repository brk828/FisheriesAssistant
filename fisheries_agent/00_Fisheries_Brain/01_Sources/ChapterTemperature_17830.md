# Temperature as an Ecological Factor in Marine Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #marine-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Gordon Gunter (Gulf Coast Research Laboratory, 1957) reviews temperature as "the most important single factor governing the occurrence and behavior of life" in marine systems. It links [[Temperature Physiology]] at the cellular level to whole-organism performance, reproduction, growth, [[Thermal Tolerance]], and large-scale distribution. The chapter argues that temperature gradients and duration matter as much as averages, that [[Acclimatization]] is slow in nature, and that temperature shapes latitudinal patterns in body size, abundance and species diversity. It also flags the difficulty of applying laboratory physiology to field ecology.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens by noting that temperature acts directly on protoplasm and indirectly by altering the physical and biological environment. Protoplasm is destroyed at 100°C and by intracellular ice formation. Life occurs across a narrow band of the total range: plants in hot springs reach at least 85°C, while some deep-sea animals live at near-constant subzero temperatures (Figure 1).

Gunter stresses that raw temperature readings are often misleading. The rate of change (the [[Temperature Gradient]]), duration, and range can be as ecologically important as the mean.

**Core physiological framework:**
- For most marine organisms, life processes operate between about 0° and 35°C, and a 10°C rise roughly doubles their rate. Individual processes may accelerate 1 to 6 times per 10°C. This is the basis of the [[Temperature Coefficient Q10]].
- Three models have been used to describe temperature effects: [[Van't Hoff Rule]], [[Arrhenius Equation]], and [[Krogh Formulation]]. The Krogh form, in which the speed of a process is a linear function of temperature, is judged the best ecological generalization, though still valid only within medial temperatures.
- Q10 values near 2 suggest that life processes are governed mainly by chemical rather than physical reactions.

**Temperature responses of marine eggs:**
- Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*), from Reibisch (1902): development time fell from 42 days at the lowest tested temperature (about -1°C) to 8.5 days at 14°C.
- Herring eggs develop in 40 to 50 days at 0.5°C and in 6 to 8 days at 16°C (Hesse et al., 1937).
- Sea-urchin egg development rate increased about 2.5 times per 10°C rise between 2.5° and 25°C (Peter, 1906).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Optima and lethal limits**
- Mammals and birds are [[Homoiotherms]]; all lower vertebrates and invertebrates are [[Poikilotherms]], with fishes showing only crude regulation.
- Terrestrial poikilotherms have thermal death points near 42° to 45°C. For marine animals the figure is usually 30° to 35°C, though intertidal species often tolerate temperatures close to land forms.
- Table 1 compiles lethal temperatures for marine molluscs (Broekhuysen, 1940; Evans, 1948; and others). Examples: *Venus mercenaria* 45.2°C (sublittoral); *Littorina littorea* 46.6°C (Halifax); *Crassostrea virginica* 48.5°C (level not stated); *Octopus vulgaris* 36.0°C (Naples). Upper-intertidal and mid-tidal species generally withstand higher temperatures than sublittoral ones.
- *Limulus* has a thermal death point of 46.3°C in Florida and 41°C at Woods Hole (Mayer, 1914).

**Extreme events**
- The [[El Niño]] current off Peru averages about 25°C, roughly 7°C above normal, and causes severe mortality.
- Cold waves kill fishes and invertebrates in Florida, Bermuda and Texas. Storey (1937) and Gunter found that cold-spell mortality in Florida and Texas recurs at intervals of about 10 years. Ice kills littoral organisms in northern shallows, and fluctuations in invertebrate larval abundance in Danish waters have been linked to "severe ice winters."
- Death from cold is generally less lethal than death from heat. Sudden thawing and rapid temperature change can be more damaging than freezing itself.

**Gradients and tolerance**
- Most fishes cannot survive a sudden change of more than 6° to 8°C, but some tolerate changes of 30° to 35°C if gradual (Norman, 1936).
- Fishes with higher breeding temperatures suffer heavier mortality in cold spells. Cold resistance in West Florida fishes correlates with their temperate, subtropical or tropical distribution (Storey, 1937).
- [[Stenothermy]] and [[Eurythermy]] are the two tolerance strategies. Most reef-forming corals will not live where temperatures fall below 18° to 19°C, and all reef formation occurs within the 20°C surface minimum isotherm. The European oyster (*Ostrea edulis*) tolerates 0° to 20°C; the American oyster (*Crassostrea virginica*) tolerates 4° to 34°C on the Gulf Coast. Shallow-water penaeid shrimp are restricted to warmer seas, while pandalids are restricted to cool waters.

**Reproduction and seasonality**
- Reproduction usually has narrower temperature limits than bodily processes. *Crassostrea virginica* can feed and grow across a wider range than it can spawn in; northern oysters may spawn only every 4 years.
- The European lobster larvae require at least 15°C. Eelgrass (*Zostera marina*) grows between 10° and 20°C but reproduces only between 15° and 20°C.
- [[Orton's Rule]] holds that organisms breed either at a physiological constant temperature or at a particular temperature change (rising or falling). Species are therefore divided into rising-temperature and falling-temperature spawners.
- Coastal Texas fishes show spring and summer spawners outnumbering fall and winter spawners by more than 4 to 1.

**Latitude, size and metabolism**
- Cold-water organisms grow larger, grow more slowly, mature later and live longer. Fishes in cold waters have more vertebrae, spines and bony plates, and smaller heads.
- Arctic invertebrates at 0°C have a three- to tenfold lower metabolic rate than tropical forms at 30°C (Scholander et al., 1953), indicating metabolic adaptation to cold.
- Viscosity doubles when water cools from 25° to 0°C, which helps explain why tropical plankton develop longer spines that keep them afloat.

**Diversity and gradients**
- Warm-water stenothermal species are the most numerous in the world's seas. Life is most abundant in cold waters, with greater plankton density and bottom fauna, but species diversity increases toward the tropics. Hawaii has 90 reef coral species compared with 10 at the Bermuda limit of their range.
- Jordan (1891) recorded 72 fish species in one seine haul at Key West, Florida, versus 48 species in a week's work in Massachusetts.
- Thorson (1950) found no planktonic prosobranch larvae in the high Arctic and Antarctic. The proportion of species with pelagic larvae rises toward the tropics, reaching about 85% in some areas.
- [[Bipolarity]] reflects parallel evolution of cold-water biotas at both poles rather than shared species.

## Key Findings

1. **Temperature gradients and duration matter as much as absolute values.** Averages and mercury readings can mislead; rate of change strongly affects organisms.
2. **Q10 near 2 indicates chemical control of most life processes.** The [[Krogh Formulation]] is the most useful ecological model, but only within limited temperature ranges.
3. **Lethal limits are lower in the sea than on land.** Marine animals usually die at 30° to 35°C; intertidal species tolerate more, and tropical species live closer to their thermal limits than northern ones.
4. **Cold kills are recurrent events.** Florida and Texas show cold-induced fish die-offs roughly every 10 years, and sudden extremes can shape distribution more than normal years.
5. **Acclimatization in nature is slow and seasonal.** Lethal temperatures of Canadian lake fishes rise through spring (Brett, 1944). Cold hardiness must be re-acquired each season.
6. **Reproduction is the most temperature-sensitive stage.** Breeding windows, not adult survival, often set range limits (for example, *Zostera marina* and the European lobster).
7. **Latitudinal size and form gradients are consistent.** Colder-water animals are larger, slower-growing and longer-lived, and fishes there show more meristic features. These patterns could help paleontologists infer past temperatures.
8. **Diversity declines poleward while biomass rises.** Species richness increases toward the tropics, whereas cold seas support denser bottom fauna and plankton, with fewer species.
9. **Temperature drives seasonal migration and abundance cycles.** On the Texas coast, temperature governs fish and invertebrate movements, and shrimp and clam populations peak seasonally.
10. **Gunter's broader message:** the physiological literature is complex and still incomplete, but temperature remains the leading control on marine life. Field ecologists must consider fluctuation, history and interacting factors, not only constant-temperature experiments.
