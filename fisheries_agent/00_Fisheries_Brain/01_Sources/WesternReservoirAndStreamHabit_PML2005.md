# Western Reservoir and Stream Habitat Improvements Handbook

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service handbook (FWS/OBS-78/56, October 1978) is a guide to the performance of fish and wildlife habitat and population improvement measures accompanying water resource development projects, mainly [[Dams]] and [[Reservoirs]] in the western United States. It was prepared under the [[Biological Services Program]] by Enviro Control, Incorporated (R. Wayne Nelson, Gerald C. Horak, and James E. Olson) under the Western Water Allocation project, with Lee S. Ischinger as project officer. The handbook catalogs 60 categories of measures, each evaluated with a standardized five-part format (Purpose, Illustration, Limitation, Performance, Cost/Output) and a success-and-confidence matrix drawn from project investigations and literature.

## Background & Description

The Biological Services Program supplies scientific information and methods on environmental issues affecting fish, wildlife, and their ecosystems, with priority areas including coal, power plants, geothermal and oil shale development, water resource analysis, coastal ecosystems, and systems inventory such as the [[National Wetlands Inventory]]. The handbook's purpose is to document successful and potentially successful measures accompanying western dam and reservoir projects, including diversion dams, so that Fish and Wildlife Service and state fish and game agencies can contribute effectively to project planning, design, and licensing.

The research followed six tasks: (1) identifying measures recommended by the Fish and Wildlife Service; (2) critically evaluating implemented measures; (3) preparing an interim handbook; (4) identifying additional proven or promising measures; (5) reviewing the interim guide through three-day interagency technical review teams; and (6) producing the final handbook. Projects were drawn from Fish and Wildlife Service regions 1, 2, and 6, with an appendix listing about 100 investigated dam, canal, and watershed projects across California, Idaho, Oregon, Washington, Arizona, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota, Utah, and Wyoming.

Success was classed as successful, marginally successful, or unsuccessful, using paired habitat and population criteria, and confidence in each assessment was rated high, medium, or low. Outcomes were judged against predicted increases in output; cost was expressed as a surrogate where direct costs were unavailable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The measures address reservoir flood basins, conservation pools, dam discharge systems, streamflows, riffles and pools, streamside protection, general practices (food and cover plantings, grazing control, ponds, wetlands, zoning), fish propagation and passage, and wildlife protection at canals. Key environmental concerns include:

- Post-impoundment decline of sport fish production, linked to decomposition of inundated material.
- Dewatering of benthos, fish spawn, and riparian vegetation from rapid pool or flow changes.
- Thermal and dissolved-oxygen stratification, winterkill, and nitrogen supersaturation below spillways.
- Loss of migrating fish at dams and canals, and drowning of big game in open canals.
- Siltation of spawning gravels and degradation of streambanks.

Related concepts include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Steelhead Trout]], [[Canada Goose]], and [[Kokanee Salmon]].

## Key Findings

**Habitat measures (Part One)**

- **Selective clearing** of reservoir flood basins: 11 of 12 applications reported successful; the only unsuccessful case was one of 19 reviewed. Little direct cost unless trees are marked and saved.
- **Brush shelters**: most assessed applications were successful; one was marginal. Typical lifespan 20 to 25 years with oak and other long-lived wood. Recommended placement at 10 to 20 feet of water, at least 150 feet apart, on weed-free, hard-bottom sites.
- **Tire shelters**: considered by some authorities to have the most potential among fish shelters. One Virginia reef used 7,000 tires over 9,500 m², with a total cost of $4,998, much of it donated.
- **Raised spillways**: at Cold Brook Dam, South Dakota, a 7-foot raise added 10 surface acres (about 30 percent); the single application was judged successful.
- **Stage filling**: at Melvern Reservoir, Kansas, a 3-year incremental fill produced good year classes of northern pike. Benefits include delayed habitat loss and sustained sport fish production; the measure remains relatively untried.
- **Reservoir water level (seasonal) manipulation**: a warmwater plan raises levels in spring for spawning, holds levels for nursery habitat, drops them in July to expose shoreline for revegetation, and refills in October for waterfowl. At Council Grove Reservoir, Kansas, results included increased walleye and other piscivore recruitment.
- **Minimum pools**: 9 applications; minimum pools were typically about 1,300 acre-feet or more. The cost analysis used a derived dollar value per acre-foot of storage; total costs were reported as $1.8 million with a range of implied values.
- **Aeration-destratification**: usually successful for dissolved oxygen, reduced hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, and zooplankton and fish survival. Methods include compressed-air diffusers, aerohydraulic guns, and high-volume pumps.
- **Low-level intakes**: 5 applications, none unsuccessful; 4 successful (Navajo Dam: $1,704,000 capital cost). Multi-level intakes (Oroville Dam) are considerably more expensive but allow temperature selectivity.
- **Spillway deflectors** ("trip-tips"): effective at reducing nitrogen supersaturation; mortality in trout and salmon reported above roughly 110 percent of normal saturation.
- **Minimum instream flows**: 4 classes of reserved flow sizes were compared; out of 90-plus cases (Exhibit 4), most high-implementation cases were successful. Legal recognition of fish and wildlife as a beneficial use is a major constraint.
- **Flow fluctuation control**: over half of cases were judged successful; two were unsuccessful for population improvement. Palisades Dam (Idaho) releases were refined to protect Canada goose nesting, with an 8,000-cfs target March 15 to May 15 or 20.
- **Current deflectors and check dams**: Barton and Winger found 35 of 45 structures effective on the Weber River, Utah; poor siting caused most failures.
- **Bank stabilization**: none of three applications judged successful; two marginal. Riprap at Parker Dam was too small and silted in.
- **Food and cover planting**: of twelve applications, most successful and none unsuccessful; costs roughly $26 to $175 per acre initial and $1 to $53 per acre annual maintenance (dollars as given in exhibit).
- **Grazing control**: most applications successful; none unsuccessful. Typical three-wire fences at 19, 28, and 40 inches; a 1970 Fort Gibson survey found only seven of 39 leased sites properly grazed.
- **Land acquisition**: successful in 14 of 14 cases shown; acquisition ranged from 27 leased acres at Angostura Dam to 35,000 acres at Eufaula Reservoir.
- **Reservoir and flood plain zoning**: population improvement successful in three of four cases; enforcement is difficult and depends on voluntary compliance.

**Population measures (Part Two)**

- **Fish hatcheries**: 14 assessed projects judged successful, with confidence uniformly high. Cost per pound of fish released was roughly $0.75 to $1.90 (1977 dollars) in the exhibit.
- **Nursery and rearing ponds**: an Oregon experiment with about five million fry showed a five-fold size increase over five weeks; 94 percent were recovered.
- **Spawning riffles**: rehabilitation on the Trinity River, California, was rated marginally successful despite full implementation.
- **Artificial spawning channels**: the Tehama-Colusa channel on the Sacramento River was judged successful.
- **Trap and haul systems**: nearly all fully implemented systems successful; annual operation and maintenance $9,000 to $40,000; construction $1.2 million to $2.7 million.
- **Fishways**: pool-and-orifice ladders were judged successful in two of four cases; the two failures occurred at a single project due to poor entrances.
- **Fish stocking, screens, barrier dams, eradication**: fish screens were largely successful when fully implemented, with failures mainly from debris clogging; barrier dams showed mixed results; eradication is temporary, with conditions expected to return within three to ten years.
- **Wildlife measures**: nesting structures and islands are readily accepted; passable fences allow antelope to pass under a 40-inch three-strand fence; 7.5-foot fences with woven wire are cited to eliminate deer access to canals; wildlife crossings should be at least 8 feet wide.

**Overall conclusion**

The handbook concludes that many habitat and population measures are successful when fully implemented, while success depends heavily on operational flexibility, site suitability, legal water rights, and enforcement. Costs are often difficult to isolate and are frequently expressed as surrogate values.
