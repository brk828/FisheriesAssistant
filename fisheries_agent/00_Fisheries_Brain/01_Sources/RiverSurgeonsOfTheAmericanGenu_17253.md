# River Sturgeons of the American Genus Scaphirhynchus: Characters, Distribution, and Synonymy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision by [[Reeve M. Bailey]] and [[Frank B. Cross]] re-examines the North American shovelnose sturgeons of the genus [[Scaphirhynchus]]. The authors confirm that the two species, [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*S. platorynchus*) and [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*S. album*), are distinct, while supporting their placement in a single genus. They provide meristic counts, morphometric comparisons, diagnostic ratios, new locality records, nomenclatural corrections, and a summary of distribution, habitat, and decline. The material is largely from the [[Missouri River]] in South Dakota, collected in August 1952.

## Background & Description

*Scaphirhynchus* is an endemic North American freshwater genus of [[Acipenseridae]] (sturgeons) confined mainly to the large rivers of the interior lowlands. Its members have a greatly elongated, depressed, fully armored caudal peduncle, a broad shovel-like rostrum, four fringed barbels, no spiracle or pseudobranchium, and short fan-shaped gill rakers. These features are unique among living sturgeons. A shovelnose sturgeon from the Upper Cretaceous of Fort Peck, Montana, shows that these specializations were already present by the Cretaceous.

The genus is closely allied to the Asian [[Pseudoscaphirhynchus]], which forms the subfamily Scaphirhynchinae with it. The remaining Recent genera, [[Acipenser]] and [[Huso]], form the Acipenserinae. The authors found that the resemblance between the two shovelnose genera most likely reflects common ancestry rather than convergence, although parallel modification to life in silty rivers cannot be ruled out.

Forbes and Richardson (1905) described *Parascaphirhynchus albus* as a new genus. The authors reject that generic separation and follow [[Berg]] in placing both species in *Scaphirhynchus*. Their reasons are the shared fundamental distinctions from other acipenserids and the close alliance between the species. The authors also note that the specific name should be spelled *album*, because *Scaphirhynchus* is neuter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species are bottom-dwelling fishes of large river channels. The Shovelnose Sturgeon favors strong current over sand or gravel and is almost excluded from [[Lake Keokuk]] and [[Lake Pepin]]. The Pallid Sturgeon is almost restricted to the [[Missouri-Mississippi River System]], which the authors describe as probably the most turbid large stream on the continent. It occurs over firm sand in strong current. Among other fishes, *Hybopsis meeki* seems most closely associated with it in habitat and distribution.

Several pressures have reduced populations:
- Reduced flow from [[Irrigation Diversion]] in the Rio Grande and North Platte River.
- Impoundment behind [[Dam Impoundment]] structures, which combines reduced current with a silt-covered bottom.
- [[Commercial Fishing]] and local pollution.
- Blocked spawning migrations.

The Shovelnose Sturgeon remains abundant in the Missouri River, but planned dams there were expected to reduce that population. Turbidity itself is not considered directly harmful to the genus. No Rio Grande specimens were known after 1874. Along the Ohio River, the species declined after impoundment began about 1911, and it is now rarely taken upstream of Scioto County, Ohio.

## Key Findings

**Species status and nomenclature**
- The authors confirm the specific distinctness of *S. album* and *S. platorynchus*, and retain them in one genus.
- *Acipenser cataphractus* Gray (1834) is a synonym of *S. platorynchus*. The London type and a New Orleans specimen both fit this species.
- *Scaphirhynchus mexicanus* Giltay (1929) is provisionally referred to the synonymy of *S. platorynchus*, pending examination of the type.

**Diagnostic characters**
- **Fin-ray counts** separate the species best. *S. album* has 37 to 48 dorsal rays and 24 to 28 anal rays. *S. platorynchus* has 30 to 36 dorsal rays and 18 to 23 anal rays. The dorsal and anal ranges do not overlap.
- **Barbels:** the inner barbel length is 5.3 to 8.2% of standard length in *S. platorynchus* and 3.7 to 5.0% in *S. album*. This is the only measurement with no overlap. The outer barbels lie behind the inner pair in *S. album* and level with or ahead of it in *S. platorynchus*.
- **Head and snout:** in fish over 200 mm, 92% of *S. album* have a head at least 30.5% of standard length, while 96% of *S. platorynchus* have shorter heads. Above 300 mm, the snout of 10 of 11 *S. album* is at least 17.4% of standard length, while 25 of 27 *S. platorynchus* have snouts no longer than 16.9%.
- **Mouth width:** 8.4 to 9.6% of body length in *S. album*, versus 7.1 to 9.1% in *S. platorynchus*.
- **Lateral plate height:** a tenth-plate height of 3.4% of body length provides a convenient separation line for larger fish.
- **Gill rakers:** *S. platorynchus* has more highly modified, fan-like rakers, usually with 3 or 4 blunt tips. *S. album* usually has 2 blunt tips. The authors regard *S. platorynchus* as the most highly specialized acipenserid in this character.
- **Ventral scutes and color:** *S. album* has no belly scutes at any age and is grayish white. *S. platorynchus* develops belly scutes in adults and ranges from light to dark brown.
- **Ribs and pelvic radials:** the earlier rib count (20–21 vs 10–11) is considered an artifact of counting method. Both species have 8 pelvic radials.

**Ratios**
- The ratio of snout tip to outer barbel versus mouth to inner barbel ranges from 1.27 to 2.19 in *S. platorynchus*, and from 2.29 to 3.26 in *S. album*.
- The inner barbel is 1.17 to 1.48 times as long as the outer barbel in *S. platorynchus*, and 1.64 to 2.41 times in *S. album*.

**Growth and size**
- The air bladder of *S. platorynchus* increases from 8–9% of body length at 150 mm to about 16% above 300 mm.
- Male *S. platorynchus* in the Mississippi reach maturity at 19.5 to 22 inches. The smallest adult female was 25 inches, and the mean weight was 1.3 pounds.
- *S. album* grows much larger. The largest specimen examined, from Fort Peck Reservoir, measured 58 inches total length and weighed 31.5 pounds at capture. Only about a dozen North American freshwater fishes exceed it in maximum size, excluding anadromous forms.

**Abundance and habitat**
- *S. album* is rare overall, but it is most frequent in very turbid water. It made up about 8% of specimens in the Kansas River at Lawrence, 5% (3 of 62) of sturgeons taken in the Missouri River of South Dakota in August 1952, and 3 of 4 identified New Orleans specimens.
- Forbes and Richardson reported *S. platorynchus* outnumbering *S. album* 500 to 1 at the mouth of the Illinois River, the type locality. Forbes and Richardson (1909) gave 300 to 1.

**Distribution**
- *S. platorynchus* occurs in most of the larger rivers of the Mississippi basin and in the Rio Grande. The authors list about 40 museum localities across Alabama, Arkansas, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Dakota, and Wisconsin. Records from Lake Winnipeg and Eagle Mountain Lake, Texas, are doubtful and not mapped.
- *S. album* ranges in the Missouri-Mississippi from Fort Peck Reservoir, Montana, to New Orleans, Louisiana, and up the Kansas River to Lawrence, Kansas. The reported upstream record at Keokuk, Iowa, needs verification.

**Conservation context**
- The authors call for more geographic comparison, since most material came from the Missouri River drainage.
- The decline of *S. platorynchus* in the Ohio and Rio Grande, and the rarity of *S. album*, point to the need for protection of the remaining large, turbid channel habitats.
