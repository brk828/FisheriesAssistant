# Water Temperature Criteria to Protect Aquatic Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #thermal-pollution #estuarine-ecology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 review by Mihursky and Kennedy synthesizes the ecological effects of temperature on aquatic organisms in the context of thermal pollution from steam electric station (S.E.S.) power plants, particularly the anticipated shift toward larger nuclear facilities producing greater waste heat per kilowatt. The authors outline fundamental thermal-biology principles—lethal limits, acclimation, thermal shock, critical thermal maximum (C.T.M.), metabolic effects, and reproductive/behavioral temperature dependencies—and argue that single-variable laboratory studies are insufficient to predict field impacts, advocating multivariate approaches (temperature × salinity × dissolved oxygen). The paper concludes with a comparative regulatory analysis of three ecosystem types: [[Cold-Water Salmonid Streams]], [[Warm-Water Centrarchid Environments]], and [[Estuaries]], using the Patuxent River estuary (Chesapeake Bay) as a primary case study, and warns that existing state temperature standards (e.g., Maryland, Pennsylvania) are likely inadequate to protect sensitive species such as [[Neomysis Americana]] and [[Trinectes Maculatus]].

## Background & Description

- **Context**: Rising electricity demand (doubling every 6–10 years) combined with a shift from fossil fuel to nuclear power generation will substantially increase waste heat discharge into aquatic systems, since nuclear plants are less thermally efficient per kilowatt and require larger cooling water volumes.
- **Scale of the problem**: Projected 1980 U.S. power needs could consume one-fifth to one-sixth of total national freshwater runoff for cooling; in some northeastern watersheds, S.E.S. withdrawal already exceeds 100% of river flow during low-flow periods.
- **Thermal discharge characteristics**: Individual plants may pump up to 0.5 million gallons/minute; discharge temperatures recorded between 100–115°F, with elevated river temperatures detected nearly 5 miles downstream of some installations.
- **Trend toward estuarine siting**: Due to cooling requirements, S.E.S. siting in estuaries is projected to increase from 22% (1950) to 32% (1980), raising concern for organisms with pelagic larval stages vulnerable to plant intake/discharge cycling.
- Basic thermal-biology concepts introduced:
  - **LD-50 (lethal dose 50%)**: standard metric for thermal tolerance, showing sigmoid mortality-temperature curves.
  - **[[Thermal Acclimation]]**: physiological adjustment to ambient temperature; organisms acclimate upward faster than downward (e.g., [[Placopecten Magellanicus]] acclimates upward at 1.7°C/day but takes up to 3 months to lose high-temperature acclimation).
  - **[[Thermal Shock]]**: acute stress from abrupt temperature change without acclimation time.
  - **[[Critical Thermal Maximum]] (C.T.M.)**: point of locomotor disorganization and loss of escape ability, increasing predation risk even absent direct lethality.
  - **Ultimate incipient lethal level** (Fry, 1947): maximum acclimation capacity boundary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Comparative thermal tolerance**: Seven estuarine species acclimated to 59.0–60.8°F showed markedly different LD-50 levels under 24-hr exposure:
  - [[Neomysis Americana]] (opossum shrimp) — least tolerant; near southern range limit in Chesapeake system.
  - [[Crangon Septemspinosa]] (sand shrimp) — similarly temperature-sensitive.
  - [[Monoculodes Sp.]], [[Gammarus Fasciatus]], [[Gammarus Mucronatus]] — intermediate tolerance; deep-channel Monoculodes less tolerant than shallow-water Gammarus spp.
  - [[Rhithropanopeus Harrisi]] (mud crab) — high tolerance; increases in abundance near S.E.S. outfalls (Naylor 1965).
  - [[Chrysaora Quinquecirrha]] (sea nettle polyp) — most tolerant, LD-50 18°F above Neomysis despite identical acclimation.
- **Metabolic effects**: Oxygen consumption increases with temperature across four freshwater fish species (brook trout, brown trout, bullhead, and a fourth species; Beamish 1964), directly linking temperature to activity levels via metabolic rate.
- **Reproductive dependency**: Temperature governs gametogenesis and spawning timing independent of salinity, light, or biological cues (Kinne 1963); narrower thermal windows apply to egg/larval cleavage stages than later larval stages.
- **Life-stage-specific thermal requirements** (Brett 1958, 1960 — Pacific salmon): Thermal tolerance ranges narrow progressively from survival → growth → reproduction → egg/hatching stages, visualized via a **thermal polygon** plotting upper/lower LD-50 against acclimation temperature, including "loading" (energy expenditure exceeds replacement) and "inhibiting" (impaired normal function, e.g., blocked spawning) levels.
- **Multivariate interactions**:
  - [[Sesarma Cinereum]] larval mortality modeled via fitted response surfaces across 12 temperature-salinity combinations (Costlow, Bookhout & Monroe 1960), showing life-stage-specific sensitivity (zoeal vs. megalops stages).
  - [[Homarus Americanus]] (American lobster) lethal boundaries mapped across temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen (McLeese 1956), showing zones where each factor acts independently vs. in combination.
- **Patuxent River estuary case study**:
  - [[Trinectes Maculatus]] (hogchoker) shows distinct spatial life-history structuring: spawning in lower estuary, nursery in upper estuary; post-larvae (8–13 mm) must migrate upriver past a 700-MW power plant (pumping 500,000 gal/min, +11.5°F warm-season differential). Three years of sampling failed to collect any 8–12 mm specimens, suggesting a lethal or avoidance barrier at the plant.
  - [[Roccus Saxatilis]] (striped bass) spawning/nursery grounds lie upstream of the plant; juveniles depend heavily on Neomysis americana as prey, which does not survive above 87.8°F—a threshold occasionally reached/exceeded in the Chesapeake during summer, posing indirect food-web risk even if striped bass itself tolerates higher temperatures.
  - Diurnal vertical migration behavior of Neomysis (epibenthic by day, rises at night) could interact adversely with thermally stratified water columns near plant discharges.

## Key Findings

- **Species-specific and stage-specific tolerance**: Thermal tolerance varies enormously across taxa and life stages; fewer than 5% of ~1,900 fish species in the U.S./Canada (per American Fisheries Society 1960 list) had been specifically tested for temperature response at time of writing—an inadequate basis for national criteria.
- **Cold-water salmonid streams** ([[Salvelinus Fontinalis]], brook trout):
  - Avoids water >68°F; final preferendum ~57–61°F; temperature of maximum spontaneous activity ~59°F.
  - Normal gametogenesis unimpeded between 47.3–60.8°F.
  - Upper lethal limit ~77.5°F (acclimated to 77°F); fry tolerate 74.3°F for 12–14 hrs.
  - Key prey (Plecoptera/stoneflies) optimal at 50–60°F; become DO-sensitive above 60°F.
  - Regulatory implication: ORSANCO (1956) and Pennsylvania rulings prohibit added heat to trout waters or cap effluent at 58°F in cold months—appropriate given narrow tolerance windows.
- **Warm-water centrarchid environments**:
  - [[Micropterus Salmoides]] (largemouth bass): nest-building begins 56°F, spawning at 66°F; field-preferred temp 80–82°F; upper lethal ~97.5°F (acclimated 86°F).
  - [[Micropterus Dolomieui]] (smallmouth bass): less tolerant than largemouth; spawning begins 62°F; preferred temp 68.5–70.3°F (field), 82.4°F (lab).
  - Macroinvertebrate food base (Trichoptera, Tendipedidae) generally tolerant to 86–103°F depending on genus, but riffle community structure collapses above 90°F (Trembley 1960, Coutant 1962), with major loss of taxa/biomass above this threshold.
  - Maryland's 99.9°F allowance (beyond 50 ft from discharge) and Pennsylvania's 93°F cap both risk eliminating substantial portions of vertebrate/macroinvertebrate diversity.
- **Estuaries** (Patuxent River/Chesapeake Bay model):
  - Observed power plant operations produce +11.5°F (warm season) to +23°F (cold season) differentials, pumping 250,000–500,000 gal/min.
  - Species acclimated to 95°F tolerated only 4–7°F additional rise before 50% mortality in 24-hr trials (tested on one crab, one shrimp, one fish species).
  - Pelagic life stages face disproportionate risk due to entrainment through plant intakes/discharges.
  - Allowable Maryland estuarine temperatures (90–99.9°F) are predicted to suppress or eliminate sensitive species, with overall ecological impact contingent on the volume and spatial (vertical/horizontal) distribution of the heated plume.
- **Overarching conclusion**: Given rapid growth in S.E.S. capacity and insufficient species-specific and multivariate thermal data, the authors call for a precautionary, conservative regulatory stance, warning that ecological damage from thermal loading is difficult to reverse once it occurs.
