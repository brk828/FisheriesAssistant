# The Tilapias of Kenya Colony

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 review by Fish Warden Hugh Copley, published in the [[East African Agricultural Journal]], surveys the [[Tilapia]] of Kenya Colony. It covers their evolutionary origin, species identification, breeding, feeding, growth, economic value, and their potential in farm ponds. The author argues that Tilapia can supply food in farm dams, but that stunting from over-reproduction ("runting") is the main obstacle. He also proposes practical management measures and identifies the research still needed.

## Background & Description

**Origin and biogeography.** The Cichlidae form a specialized family with roughly 140 species in the Americas and about 150 in Africa south of the Sahara, plus a few in Madagascar and three in southern India. Copley reasons that an American-African land bridge allowed early cichlids to spread to Africa. Later faulting in the Pliocene isolated lakes, swamps, and river valleys, and the cichlids diverged into the small, distinct species seen today. Other families with similar histories include the Siluridae, the Characinidae (including [[Tiger Fish]]), and the lungfish *Protopterus*. Barbus, Labeo, and Barilius are described as of eastern origin. The [[Nile Perch]] is described as descended from estuarine ancestors.

**Species and distribution.** The review lists the following Kenyan Tilapia:

- [[Tilapia Esculenta]] and [[Tilapia Variabilis]]: two three-spined species in [[Lake Victoria]].
- [[Tilapia Nilotica]]: found in [[Lake Rudolf]] and [[Lake Baringo]], reflecting ancient Nile connections.
- [[Tilapia Grahami]]: a small, brightly coloured relict of [[Lake Magadi]], shaped by its soda environment.
- [[Tilapia Nigra]]: native to the [[Athi River]] and [[Tana River]], with four anal spines.
- [[Tilapia Mossambica]]: the Coast Tilapia, also found in Lake Jilore and the hot, salty waters of Formosa Bay.
- [[Tilapia Jipe]] and [[Tilapia Girigan]]: two species in Lake Jipe that closely resemble T. mossambica.
- [[Tilapia Hunteri]]: from Lake Chale, possibly a relict of the old Tsavo-Athi River system, with four anal spines.
- [[Tilapia Melanopleura]]: introduced from the Belgian Congo in recent years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat conditions.** The lakes differ greatly. Lake Rudolf covers 6,848 sq. miles, has no aquatic vegetation, and has a pH well above 10. It contains predatory fish, but the crocodile is present in both lakes. Lake Baringo is a small lake of 62.8 sq. miles, with dense blue-green algae (*Microcystis*) and a pH of 8.2 to 8.8. It has no predatory fish.

**Feeding.** Most Tilapia feed on [[Phytoplankton]]. The Athi and Tana Rivers have very little phytoplankton, so T. nigra there eats a wider range of food, including molluscs, underwater insects, and fish fry. T. melanopleura is a weed eater only in its older stages.

**Breeding.** Tilapia are [[Mouth Brooding]] or nest-building. Copley notes that all species build nests, so "nest maker" is a poor term. T. nigra, T. esculenta, T. variabilis, and T. nilotica are mouth-brooders. T. grahami, T. mossambica, and T. melanopleura build nests and provide no parental care after hatching. Nothing is known about T. hunteri's breeding.

**Disease vectors.** Copley proposes that Tilapia could help control [[Malaria]] and [[Bilharzia]]. Clearing emergent weed creates ripples that deter mosquito larvae and reduces the habitat of the snail that carries bilharzia.

## Key Findings

**Identification.** The simplest field method is to count anal spines. T. nigra has four and T. melanopleura has three. In the Athi and Tana, a three-spined fish is T. mossambica. Scientists rely on the [[Pharyngeal Teeth]], especially the lower pharyngeal plate, for definitive identification. Young T. melanopleura, T. esculenta, and T. nilotica from Lake Baringo are nearly indistinguishable and can only be told apart by habitat.

**Sexual characteristics.** Males of several species develop breeding colouration with age. The male T. grahami shows brilliant peacock-blue colouring at spawning time. Old male T. hunteri have large heads and blue-black colouring. Ripe male T. variabilis have a blood-red genital papilla.

**Growth and runting.** Growth rate is central to pond culture. Food supply can increase either growth or reproduction. Over-reproduction produces stunted "runts." In Lake Rudolf, fish reach up to 12 lb, partly because predators keep down the population. In Lake Baringo, where there are no predators, the average weight is 9 ounces. A T. nigra population from the Athi, averaging 1 lb in the wild, produced fish averaging 2 ounces after two years in a 4-acre dam with no predators. In one case, spawning began at 4 inches at about four months of age, after which growth fell to one-fifth of its earlier rate, possibly from bodily exhaustion.

**Management proposals.** Copley lists three approaches to runting:
1. Introduce a predator. This may work in large dams but risks eliminating the Tilapia in small ones, as happened in a test pond.
2. Stock one sex only, to prevent breeding and maintain continuous growth. This is untested.
3. Produce sterile hybrids through cross-breeding, so that only growth matters.

He also raises [[Lebensraum]], the idea that each fish needs a certain volume of water to reach its best growth. The required area varies by species and food supply. The proper stocking rate per acre is still unknown because stocking has followed European practice.

**Economic value.** The [[Gill Net Fishery]] of Lake Victoria began in 1905, when the Norwegian Aarup introduced the 5-inch gill net. In 1951, about 2,400,000 Tilapia (approximately 3,000,000 lb) were exported from Kisumu, worth £82,500 to fishermen and £127,500 at retail. Local consumption is excluded. Annual catch is estimated at 12,000 tons, and 8,000 gill nets are set nightly in Kavirondo Gulf. Nets last 6 weeks to 2 months, which makes net supply a substantial trade. Lake Rudolf yields about 250,000 sun-dried fish per year.

**Farm pond potential.** Copley judges that commercial fish farms are impractical in most of Kenya, since suitable land with water available without pumping is scarce. Draining a farm pond to harvest fish is also ruled out because water is more valuable than the fish. He calls it unrealistic to expect 1,000 to 2,000 lb of fish per acre. He considers 400 to 500 lb of marketable fish per acre per year achievable. Earlier trials of [[Bluegill]] were disappointing and were abandoned. A Tilapia–[[Black Bass]] combination showed promise in some areas.

**Proposed integrated pond design.** A future farm dam might combine T. melanopleura to control weeds, T. nigra to use extra phytoplankton, and [[Gambusia]] in the shallow edges to control mosquito larvae. Predators such as Black Bass or [[Clarias]] might be added. In coastal areas, T. mossambica with Clarias is suggested.

**Research gaps.** Copley identifies several open questions: the cause of early spawning, the protein requirements of Tilapia, the proper stocking density, and the breeding habits of T. hunteri. He states that "only time will tell" on several of these.
