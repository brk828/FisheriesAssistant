# Asian Tapeworm Eradication and Habitat Redesign at San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a 1997 cover letter, a draft proposal, a phased work plan, a cost outline, and a refuge memorandum from the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (Douglas, Arizona). It proposes eradicating the nonindigenous [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) and reducing exotic [[American Bullfrog]] populations from a 2,309-acre refuge that protects the [[Rio Yaqui]] fish fauna. The project would drain and treat ponds, then replace them with a lined, stream-like habitat over an estimated three years. Principal investigators were Kevin Cobble and Anthony Velasco (refuge) and Jerry Landye ([[Pinetop Fish Health Center]]).

## Background & Description

The parasite was introduced to the United States about 20 years before the document, with imported [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*) used for vegetation control. It spreads rapidly through cyprinid populations, and heavy infections can block the digestive tract and compromise fish health.

The refuge supports several rare native fishes:
- [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), endangered and confined to the refuge and a ranch in the Chiricahua Mountains, where it was introduced in the 1980s.
- [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*), threatened; reintroduced to historic refuge habitat in 1990.
- [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonorensis*), with the refuge holding the only US populations.
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).

Of eight native fish species originally recorded on the refuge (about one quarter of Arizona's native fishes), five currently occur on San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon NWRs. [[Yaqui Catfish]] and [[Yaqui Sucker]] are held at Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center in New Mexico.

In December 1995, Pinetop Fish Health Center personnel began a disease survey requested by the refuge over concern about yellow grub (*Clinostomum complanatum*) on Yaqui topminnow. The survey found Asian tapeworm in Yaqui chub and beautiful shiner in four ponds, confirmed at the Pinetop center.

Exotic bullfrogs have caused the disappearance of [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] populations and nearly eliminated the refuge population of [[Mexican Garter Snake]]. Recent studies found that small bullfrogs feed mainly on native fishes, and young bullfrog dispersal raises concern that they may carry fish pathogens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The refuge is a 2,309-acre site in southeast Arizona along the US-Mexico border, established in 1982 to protect the Rio Yaqui fish fauna. It consists mainly of man-made impoundments fed by artesian well flows ranging from 5 to 30 gal/min. Stream habitat is limited to Black Draw and several natural springs on the refuge and adjacent private land.

The project is intended to benefit other rare species, including the Chiricahua leopard frog, Mexican garter snake, [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]], and the endangered [[Huachuca Water Umbel]] (*Lilaeopsis recurva* var. *Schaffneria*). Cooperators include the Arizona Game & Fish Department, Arizona State University, the University of Arizona, The Nature Conservancy, the Arizona Ecological Services Field Office, the Pinetop Fish Health Center, and Dexter National Fish Hatchery. The plan also raises the risk of tapeworm spread into Mexican waters of the Yaqui drainage.

## Key Findings

**Project scope and objectives**
- Remove Asian tapeworm from all fishes and impoundments on the refuge, estimated at up to three years.
- Objectives: (1) eradicate the tapeworm from native fish populations; (2) redesign aquatic habitat; (3) reduce or eradicate exotic bullfrogs; (4) monitor treatment effectiveness.

**Phased approach**
- Phase I: North Pond and Bunting Spring, a test of the protocol.
- Phase II: Mesquite Pond and Oasis Pond.
- Phase III: Twin Pond and Robertson Cienega. Twin Pond is the only pond to be reused after treatment.
- Phase IV: off-refuge waters and remaining refuge waters.

**Treatment protocol (per pond)**
- Fence the pond and begin drawdown, shutting off the water supply. Collect turtles and snakes for possible captivity and treatment.
- Build an artificial stream lined with polypropylene and filled with sand and gravel, with deep and shallow pools, riffles, backwaters, and undercut banks. The planned North Pond stream is about 200 m long.
- Seine fish using depletion sampling, then hold them in tanks and treat with Droncit for 24 hours at high concentration, with supplemental oxygen and 1% salt to reduce stress.
- Stock the treated fish into the new stream. Dry the old ponds to sun-sterilize sediments, then lime them.
- Restock garter snakes, turtles, and leopard frogs, and possibly plant Huachuca water umbel.
- Sterilize collecting equipment between ponds and document all procedures.

**Rationale for the liner-stream design (memo of 22 January 1997)**
- Advantages cited: efficient water use, closer resemblance to historic Yaqui fish habitat, fewer disease and vegetation problems, easier bullfrog control, easier monitoring, and lower maintenance.
- Filling time is about 4 days at 10 gal/min, compared with up to a month at 20 gal/min for a pond.
- Excess water would be piped into Black Draw to create riparian habitat.

**Monitoring and deliverables**
- Monitoring every six months to confirm eradication before the next phase proceeds. Fish and leopard frog populations are monitored monthly for breeding, growth, and condition.
- The Principal Investigator will provide annual reports to all participating parties.

**Costs and status**
- The cost sheet lists per-pond items, including fencing, a 7,200 sq ft liner at $0.70 per square foot (about $5,000), and sand, gravel, and fencing. Most figures are illegible in the scan, so the budget is not reliably reproduced here.
- The proposal is a draft. The January memo invites critical comments on the design, and the February letter notes that NRCS soil surveys will determine infiltration rates.
