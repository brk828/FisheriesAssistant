# An Evaluation of Feeds for Raising Razorback Sucker, *Xyrauchen texanus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This study tested artificial diets for rearing [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) fry from hatching to juvenile size. The work was conducted at the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] and was published in *Journal of Applied Aquaculture* (1992). Because the species is protected and its wild populations are declining with little recruitment, the authors sought a dry, formulated diet regime that would support both growth and survival. Fry were reared for 126 days on a sequence of commercial feeds, with the [[BioKyowa Diet Series]] performing best. The authors concluded that razorback sucker can be raised successfully on this feeding sequence, but that vitamin deficiency signs (spinal and vertebral deformities) appeared in fish on all diets and may relate to vitamin C loss during feed storage.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The razorback sucker was protected by several state statutes and was proposed for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Populations are declining, and recruitment of young fish into wild stocks is poor (Lanigan and Tyus 1989; Marsh and Minckley 1989).
- **Conservation need:** Maintaining separate genetic stocks requires hatchery production of fish large enough to carry permanent marks. Earlier work had shown that razorback sucker fry could be cultured for 45 days on live or prepared first exogenous foods (Tyus and Haines 1990), but growth and survival declined for larger fish, which suggested a combination of diets was needed.
- **Knowledge gap:** Successful intensive culture on dry formulated foods had been scarcely documented, and the dietary requirements of the species were not well understood.
- **Objective:** To identify a combination of artificial diets that would allow rearing from fry to fingerling with high growth and survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Source stock and incubation.** Eggs and milt were stripped from wild adults from the [[Green River]] in May 1989. Eggs were fertilized in the field, transported to the Ouray hatchery, disinfected, and incubated at a controlled temperature. Hatching began 71 hours after fertilization and was complete at 95 hours. Fry reached swim-up after five days.

**Rearing conditions by phase:**

| Parameter | Phase I (fry in wooden tank) | Phase II (tubes in compartments) |
|---|---|---|
| Water temperature | 19.3 °C (average) | 20.0 °C (average) |
| Dissolved oxygen | 5.4 mg/L | Not fully reported |
| pH | Reported (value unclear in source) | Reported (value unclear in source) |
| Total hardness | 440 mg/L | Not reported |
| Water source | Well water, flow-through | Well water, flow-through |

Fry in Phase II were held in mesh-bottomed PVC tubes (about 3.9 L of water each), suspended in 29-L compartments. Water exchange was through the tube walls and side slots, and the tanks were drained by surface standpipes.

**Diets tested.** Five commercial diets were fed: [[BioKyowa Diet Series]] B-250, A-250, A-400, and AP-100; [[Silver Cup Salmon Starter]] (SCSS); and BioDry. [[Proximate Analysis]] showed:
- Protein ranged from about 45% (BioDry) to 57.2% (B-250).
- Fat was highest in the BioKyowa-A diets used in Phases II and III.
- The diet producing poorest growth (SCSS) had the highest protein (55.3%) and lower fat, showing that crude composition alone did not explain performance.

## Key Findings

### Phase I (Days 5–25)
- Fry were fed B-250 six times daily at 3.25% of body weight. Feeding was not adjusted because fish were small (average weight 9.995 mg; the source text's unit is unclear).
- Survival was 67% (N = 3,359 fry).
- Mean weight increased from the initial to the final size, with an average daily weight gain of about 4% ([[Specific Growth Rate]], G).
- The authors noted that survival here was higher than in the earlier 45-day study (Tyus and Haines 1990), probably because the daily feeding rate was greater.

### Phase II (Days 26–52)
- Fish fed A-250 had significantly higher growth rates and were longer and heavier than fish fed other diets (p < 0.05).
- Survival of fish fed A-250 was significantly higher than survival of fish fed SCSS (p < 0.05).
- Growth and survival of fish on the remaining diets were similar. A reported survival value above 100% was attributed to counting errors with small larvae.
- Fish were fed six times daily at 10% of body weight, with the ration adjusted every two weeks.

### Phase III (Days 53–126)
- Statistical tests could not be applied in this phase because of unequal replication.
- Fish fed BioKyowa-A grew faster and had better survival than fish fed other diets.
- Fish maintained on SCSS were smaller and had lower survival (value reported in the source is unclear).
- Fish switched from A-250 to SCSS had high survival (97%), but the switch to SCSS was associated with slower growth.
- Fish switched from SCSS to BioKyowa-A had survival of 88% and grew faster than fish kept on SCSS alone.
- Fish switched from A-250 to SCSS in the middle of Phase III averaged 30.5 mm in length.
- Fish switched to A-400 later in the trial were given a 400-micron formulation of the same diet.

### Overall performance
- BioKyowa-A was the top performer in Phases II and III, showing the superiority of this diet had been established early in the trial.
- Growth on the BioKyowa diets was linked to diet sequence rather than to single nutrient values.

### Deformities and Vitamin Concerns
- Some fish from every diet showed spinal deformities, including displaced vertebral columns, fused vertebrae, and displaced muscle.
- Deformities were noted in all treatments; the source reports the proportion for SCSS but the value is unclear in the scan.
- These abnormalities are typical of [[Vitamin C Deficiency]] in fish.
- Commercial feed in this study was stored beyond the 90-day recommended shelf life. Feed stored at 21 °C is reported to lose about 50% of its vitamin C within the storage period (Piper et al. 1982, cited in the study). Vitamin C loss during storage is therefore a plausible cause of the deformities.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- BioKyowa diets appear capable of producing satisfactory growth and survival of razorback sucker fry during the first 126 days of life.
- Feeds should be given extra vitamins, or stored for only short periods (under 90 days), to avoid deformities.
- Further work is needed to evaluate formulated diets more thoroughly or to develop new diets for the species.
- The study was supported by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, with histological examination done by the [[Bozeman Fish Technology Center]].
