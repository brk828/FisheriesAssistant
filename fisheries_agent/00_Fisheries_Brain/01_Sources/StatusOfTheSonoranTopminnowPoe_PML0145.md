# Status of the Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department special report assesses the status of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in Arizona, based on field surveys from June 1989 to April 1990 and a review of historic records. Both species were once widespread and abundant in southern Arizona but declined sharply after habitat loss and predation by non-native fishes, especially [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Recovery criteria for the [[Gila Topminnow]] were met for downlisting, yet the authors argue downlisting is premature because natural populations continue to decline and long-term persistence is unproven. The report concludes that protecting natural populations should be the main focus of recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Taxa:** Each species has two Arizona subspecies. The Sonoran topminnow includes the Gila topminnow (*P. o. occidentalis*) and the Yaqui topminnow (*P. o. sonoriensis*). The desert pupfish includes the Lower Colorado River form and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*), which had a single historic locality at Quitobaquito Springs.
- **Listing:** Federally listed as endangered in 1967 (Sonoran topminnow) and 1986 (desert pupfish).
- **Causes of decline:** Habitat modification and loss, and predation by non-native fishes, particularly mosquitofish.
- **Recovery framework:** A 1981 Memorandum of Understanding among USFWS, USFS and AGFD set introduction guidelines. The 1983 [[Gila and Yaqui Topminnow Recovery Plan]] defined downlisting and delisting criteria for each subspecies. A draft [[Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan]] was under review at the time, so its criteria are not discussed.
- **Definitions used:** "In the wild" excludes captive sites with a mailing address (universities, museums, zoos, hatcheries, backyards). An "introduced population" results from transplanting fish to a site without a natural history of the species. A "natural population" is one at a site that held fish before human transplants and has not been mixed with other stocks. Sites were numbered by [[Simons]] (1987) to avoid confusion over duplicate names.
- **Site categories (Simons 1987):** Category 1 (persistent without human support), Category 2 (persistent only with support), Category 3 (not expected to persist long), Category 4 (mosquitofish present).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Desiccation:** The main threat to introduced populations. Surveys were scheduled for June–September 1989 to capture drying conditions.
- **Precipitation:** Central Arizona was wet in 1982–1983 (6.2 and 8.6 inches above normal), when most stockings occurred. Many sites dried during the following dry years, and 1989 precipitation was more than two inches below normal.
- **Genetics:** Gila topminnow populations differ genetically and in fitness ([[Vrijenhoek]] et al. 1985; [[Quattro]] and Vrijenhoek 1989). The report groups natural populations by drainage: Bylas Spring; San Rafael/Sheehy Spring/Sharp Spring/Santa Cruz River; Sonoita Creek; and Cienega Creek.
- **Mosquitofish interactions:** Topminnows coexist with mosquitofish at some sites, often where flooding periodically displaces the non-native fish or where non-random distribution occurs.
- **Habitat examples:** Sites range from large creeks such as Cienega Creek to pools smaller than a kitchen sink. Many are stock tanks or artesian-fed ponds, and some are fenced exclosures overgrown with cattail (*Typha*).

## Key Findings

**Introduced populations (Gila topminnow)**
- More than 56 wild sites were stocked before 1982, 90 in 1982 and 24 in 1983. In total, Gila topminnows were introduced into **192 wild habitats** and more than 102 captive sites, with more than 340 total introductions.
- **32 introduced wild populations were successful** as of September 1989, about 17% of the 192 wild introductions. Twenty-four had persisted three or more years, 19 five or more years.
- **15 wild populations failed between 1987 and 1989**, mostly from desiccation, with some from mosquitofish invasion, flooding or unknown causes (Table 2).
- Only five new introduced populations were established between April 1988 and August 1989. Two arose from unauthorized stockings, one from dispersal.
- Of the 32 successful introductions, **7 are Category 1**, 10 Category 2, 9 Category 3 and 6 Category 4.

**Natural populations (Gila topminnow)**
- Eight of eleven natural populations successful in 1987 persisted in 1989.
- Three natural populations failed in 1989: Sheehy Spring, Middle Spring and North Fork of Ash Creek.
- **Cienega Creek** is the largest natural population in Arizona, with no mosquitofish collected. Its 1989 acquisition by the Bureau of Land Management was described as probably the single most important conservation action since recovery began.
- Bylas Spring, renovated several times, was reinvaded by mosquitofish (98% of fish in 1987) and had only one topminnow in 1989. Sixty individuals were moved to [[Arizona State University]] as a refugium.

**Yaqui topminnow**
- Eight wild populations were successful in 1989. Only Tule Pond (Site #142) can be considered a natural population.
- Three introduced populations were eliminated in 1989 by mosquitofish.
- One of three delisting criteria is met: the San Bernardino Ranch was acquired by USFWS as a National Wildlife Refuge. Mosquitofish removal and long-term persistence criteria are not met.

**Desert pupfish**
- Four wild populations were successful and four failed in 1989. Two successful sites held Quitobaquito-like fish of questionable genetic integrity (Bog Hole and Finley Tank), which are proposed for renovation.
- The only natural population is at Quitobaquito Springs, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, which was large and free of exotics in 1989.
- Lower Colorado River pupfish were eliminated from Arizona before the 1950s.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- The authors consider downlisting of the Gila topminnow premature. Recovery criteria were met in 1985 and 1987, but they fail to address species needs and long-term persistence. Eleven populations that had survived four or more years before 1987 were lost by 1989.
- Natural populations are declining "at an alarming rate" and should be the focus of recovery.
- The Yaqui topminnow's habitats are federally owned, so mosquitofish removal is described as achievable.

**Recommendations**
1. Secure natural populations through mosquitofish removal, fish barriers and habitat management, with site-specific actions in Appendix C.
2. Conduct genetic studies on all natural populations to guide management of diversity.
3. Introduce fish into high-quality, persistent habitats, renovating non-native-occupied sites where needed.

The authors note these recommendations were made repeatedly since the early 1980s without being implemented.
