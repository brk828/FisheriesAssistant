# Desert Fish of Central Australia: Surviving a Changeable Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Australian Geographic feature (April–June 1996) profiles the freshwater fishes of inland Australia's deserts, written as a field narrative. It follows [[Hamar Midgley]] and [[Mary Midgley]] at the [[Finke River]] waterhole in the Northern Territory, and [[Bryan Pierce]] and [[Peter Unmack]] on a week-long survey of [[Mound Springs]] in South Australia's north-east. The article argues that desert fish persist through permanent refuges during droughts and disperse with floods, that springs and waterholes are vital and vulnerable habitats, and that long-term monitoring is needed to protect them. Quantitative details are drawn from the fieldwork and from the species accounts within the text.

## Background & Description

The Midgleys have worked on freshwater fish of northern and Central Australia for about 30 years, and collaborated on studies at more than 500 sites since the 1960s. [[Hamar Midgley]] is self-taught, funds many expeditions personally, and received an Honorary Doctorate of Science from the University of Queensland in 1994. Three taxa bear his name: the shovel-nosed catfish or silver cobbler (*Arius midgleyi*), Midgley's carp gudgeon and Midgley's grunter. The source describes the Midgleys' record as among the best biological records in the country.

The article profiles several fish, summarized below from the text:

- **[[Bony Bream]]** (also hairback herring): tolerates salinity near that of seawater and temperatures of 9–38°C. Grows to 40 cm but is usually 12–25 cm. Eats algae, is absent from the south-east, south-west and Tasmania, and is the major food fish of inland systems. Rarely targeted by anglers.
- **[[Spangled Perch]]**: widely distributed in coastal and inland areas, rarely over 25 cm, eats small insects, crustaceans and molluscs, and tolerates saltier water and wider temperature extremes than bony bream.
- **[[Yellowbelly]]** (golden perch or callop): can live in water nearly as salty as the ocean and at 4–37°C. Females may produce up to 500,000 eggs when spawning is triggered by rising water levels and temperatures. The Lake Eyre Basin callop is now classed separately from Murray-Darling yellowbelly and can spawn within three days of rainfall.
- **[[Desert Goby]]**: the most common fish in South Australian mound springs, about 6 cm long. Tolerates water from 5–41°C and low-oxygen conditions. Produces fewer than 300 eggs per spawning. Males guard eggs.
- **[[Lake Eyre Hardyhead]]**: slightly larger than the goby, with a similar egg count; its numbers in springs rise and fall.
- **[[Cooper Catfish]]**: grows to 80–90 cm and is an aggressive predator, unlike other Cooper system catfish.
- **[[Inland Rainbow Fish]]**: disperses well in floodwaters, preys on mosquito larvae, and has established populations in bore drains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Refuges and floods.** Permanent waterholes such as the Finke River pool, about 400 m long and 110 km south of Alice Springs, act as "gene pools" through drought. Fish survive there and repopulate rivers and lakes after floods. Henry Nix, of the Australian National University, stated that fish distribution and abundance depend more on rainfall than on any other factor, and that losing permanent waterholes to degradation or overfishing would "effectively blow the whole system." Floods over [[Cooper Creek]] can inundate about 300,000 sq. km.

**Cold stress.** During the Midgleys' winter visit the water was 13°C, and fish were in torpor. Pelicans, cormorants and even grey teal, normally non-fish-eaters, fed on 3–10 cm bony bream. Weaker individuals of a given size succumbed to cold.

**Springs.** The [[Great Artesian Basin]] feeds mound springs with steady flows at near-constant temperature and salinity. Fish are sensitive to habitat change. Bryan Pierce and Peter Unmack examined 32 springs over one afternoon and the next morning, recording size, location, temperature, chemistry and signs of life. Some contained snails and crustaceans but no fish. Peter Unmack suggested Mulligan Springs may once have held permanent hardyhead populations, which may have been lost as bore-sinking reduced spring pressure.

**Threats.** Trampling by stock and feral animals, reduced spring flow from bores, pastoralism and tourism were identified as pressures. [[Coward Springs]] was being fenced to protect it, following protection of [[Blanche Cup]] and the Bubbler since 1986. Stock damage was also noted at Freeling Springs.

**Indicator value.** Henry Nix noted that freshwater fish are good indicators of environmental health, and fish species numbers generally decline as water quality falls. The Midgleys' repeated site revisits allow change over time to be documented.

**Dalhousie Springs.** Dalhousie has an average annual rainfall of 130 mm and an evaporation rate more than 20 times that. About 60 springs flow freely, and about half of these contain fish. Dalhousie's [[Dalhousie Springs]] fauna has been isolated for at least 10,000 years.

## Key Findings

1. **Permanent waterholes are critical refuges.** Fish survive droughts in permanent pools and recolonize rivers and lakes after floods. Degradation or overfishing of these pools could devastate regional fish populations.

2. **Bony bream dominate the Finke River waterhole.** The Midgleys recorded one freshwater prawn and five fish species at the site: bony bream, banded grunter, spangled perch, Lake Eyre hardyhead and desert rainbow fish. Only bony bream were abundant.

3. **Desert fish show adaptations to variable conditions.** Yellowbelly tolerate salinity and temperatures from 4–37°C and can spawn within three days of rainfall. Desert goby tolerate 5–41°C and low-oxygen water, gulping air when needed.

4. **Spring-dwelling fish invest less in reproduction.** Desert goby and Lake Eyre hardyhead produce fewer than 300 eggs, far fewer than yellowbelly. The article links this to the stable spring environment, compared with ephemeral watercourses.

5. **Dalhousie Springs supports unique fauna.** Of six fish species there, four are found nowhere else: a sleek black 10 cm catfish, two hardyheads and a goby. Dalhousie hardyheads have been seen entering water as hot as 43°C.

6. **Desert goby spawning recorded in the wild.** At Freeling Springs, a clump of about 150 eggs was found under a rock, described as the first goby spawning site recorded in the wild. Males guard eggs and reportedly prevent fungal growth on them.

7. **Blanche Cup goby population disappeared.** Desert gobies introduced to Blanche Cup 30 years earlier were abundant in 1992, but none were found a year later. The cause is unknown.

8. **"Fish falling with rain" is probably explained by dispersal.** About 50 "rains of fishes" were logged across Australia from 1879 (per a 1972 *Australian Natural History* issue). [[Terry Sim]] of the South Australian Museum is sceptical, and suggests fish dispersing through sheet floodwater are stranded when the water drains away. Pastoralists' claims that fish come up from groundwater were tested by netting outflows, which caught none.

9. **Fishing pressure is significant in floods.** Pastoralist-turned-fisherman Denis Overton took more than 200,000 yellowbelly from the 35 sq. km Lake Hope after Cooper Creek floods in 1989, 1990 and 1991. Millions of fish, mostly bony bream, rotted when the lake dried in 1994.

10. **Conservation priority.** Bryan Pierce argues that fisheries management should prioritize conservation of the resource, and suggests Cooper Creek permanent waterholes be managed individually to ensure breeding fish survive long droughts. The authors conclude that protecting springs and waterholes is essential for these fish to persist.
