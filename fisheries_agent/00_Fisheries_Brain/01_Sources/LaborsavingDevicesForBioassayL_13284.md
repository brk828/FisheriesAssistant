# Labor-Saving Devices for Bioassay Laboratories

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-control

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife publication (Resource Publication 38, April 1967, Investigations in Fish Control No. 21) describes three low-cost devices built to reduce manual labor in [[Bioassay]] work at the [[Southeastern Fish Control Laboratory]] in [[Warm Springs Georgia]]. The authors, [[Robert J Hesselberg]] (Chemist) and [[Ralph M Burress]] (Fishery Biologist), report that the devices save "hundreds of man-hours" and reduce the hazards of handling heavy, slippery jars. The devices are a [[Jar Rinsing Rack]], an [[Automatic Liquid Measuring Vessel]], and a [[Jar Emptier]]. The document is an equipment and methods note, not a fish biology study, and it contains no species data.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Nearly all indoor bioassays at the Fish Control Laboratories use 5-gallon glass jars. Handling the large number of jars used daily was a significant labor burden, which motivated the development of these devices.
- **Publishing body:** [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Department of the Interior. Series: Investigations in Fish Control.
- **Series reference:** [[Lennon and Walker 1964]] (Investigations in Fish Control 1: laboratories and methods for screening fish-control chemicals, Circular 185). The jar cleaning procedure used with the rack follows a method outlined in that work.
- **Design priorities:** Availability of materials and ease of working them were the main factors in material choice. Materials are not critical unless highly corrosive wastes are involved, and dimensions can be adapted to other laboratories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not report any fish species, aquatic habitat, water chemistry, or ecosystem observations. Its environmental relevance is limited to the laboratory setting:

- Bioassay jars were filled with water and emptied into floor drainage. The rack sits over a floor trench into which waste water drains.
- The jar emptier uses a screened intake (1/8-inch mesh) to prevent loss of fish as water is removed, so fish retention during handling is a stated design concern.
- Highly corrosive wastes were noted as a material consideration, but no specific wastes are described.

## Key Findings

### 1. Jar Rinsing Rack
- **Dimensions:** About 15 feet 6 inches long, 28 inches wide, 18 inches high; dimensions can be varied.
- **Capacity:** Rinses 26 jars simultaneously. When fewer than 26 jars are rinsed, a board is laid over unused nozzles to direct water into the floor trench.
- **Construction:** Two-inch lumber and large nails for load-bearing parts; 1-inch material for pipe supports and grate-retaining rim; wooden parts given two coats of high-grade enamel; aluminum grating salvaged from the floor trench cover.
- **Plumbing:** 3/4-inch plastic pipe and fittings (1/2-inch pipe adequate for a small rack), 3/4-inch valves (one per pipe), 1/4-inch brass spray nozzles, 1/4- by 1 1/8-inch all-thread nipples.
- **Cost:** $54.20 for materials, exclusive of gratings.
- **Performance:** Cuts washing and rinsing time to less than half the time required when jars were cleaned individually.

### 2. Automatic Liquid Measuring Vessel
- **Purpose:** Automatically measures 15 liters of water into each jar.
- **Construction:** Polyethylene pipe with 1/4-inch walls and inside diameters of 3, 4, and 8 inches. The main body is a 17-inch section of 8-inch pipe with heat-fused top and bottom plates. The neck is an 8-inch piece of 4-inch pipe, and the drain is a 1 1/2-inch piece of 3-inch pipe.
- **Plate design:** Convex top plate (1/4 inch thick) and concave, weight-bearing lower plate (1/2 inch thick). Four radial grooves (1/2 inch wide, 1/4 inch deep) on the underside of the lower plate allow air to escape from the jar during filling. Drain side walls are tapered at 11°.
- **Filling and overflow:** Water enters through a 1/2-inch hard-rubber stopcock near the bottom. Excess drains through a 1-inch plastic overflow pipe. A threaded plastic coupling allows calibration of the measured volume.
- **Emptying:** A No. 13 rubber stopper on an aluminum rod acts as the valve and handle. The rod has a bent offset and a hand grip. A stainless-steel rod and soft rubber toilet-tank ball were used in a later build.
- **Use:** Two vessels run concurrently, fed through a "Y" connector. Filling 15 liters takes about 25 seconds. Emptying takes less than half that time, so one vessel can be moved while the other fills.
- **Cost:** $37.20 for materials.

### 3. Jar Emptier
- **Mechanism:** A U-shaped 1 1/2-inch plastic pipe passes through a rubber seal made from the upper section of a plumber's force cup. A 3/8-inch copper tube with a flared inner end supplies compressed air.
- **Operating pressure:** Low-pressure (5 p.s.i.) air from aquarium aeration supplies.
- **Performance:** Nearly 15 liters of water are expelled in 11 seconds. The resulting 30-pound reduction in weight makes lifting jars from troughs easier and safer.
- **Safety testing:** When the outlet screen was deliberately plugged and pressure allowed to build, the seal was forced harmlessly out of the jar neck, showing an adequate safety margin.
- **Cost:** $2.35 for materials.

### 4. Overall Conclusions
- The three devices are inexpensive (materials total about $94.75 excluding rack gratings: $54.20 + $37.20 + $2.35), easy to build, and adaptable.
- Reported benefits: hundreds of man-hours saved, reduced handling hazards with heavy, slippery jars, and improved working ergonomics.
- The authors note that the vessel and rack designs can be changed to meet different volume or space needs.
