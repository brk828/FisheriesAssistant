# Life History of the Utah Sculpin (*Cottus bairdi semiscaber*) in the Logan River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the life history of the [[Utah Sculpin]] (*[[Cottus Bairdi Semiscaber]]*) in the [[Logan River]], Utah, based on 801 specimens collected from 1949 to March 1951. The work aimed to clarify the sculpin's relationship to [[Game Fish]] and to supplement existing knowledge of the species. It covers habitat preference, [[Age And Growth]] estimated from vertebrae, the [[Length-Weight Relationship]], the [[Coefficient Of Condition]], [[Food Habits]], sex determination, and spawning. The sculpin fed mainly on aquatic insects, chiefly [[Diptera]], and spawned in spring. Predation on trout eggs was slight, and the author concluded that competition with trout is limited because the sculpin feeds mainly on the bottom while trout feed mainly at the surface.

## Background & Description

The Utah sculpin is economically unimportant and is regarded as a nuisance in many areas. The Wyoming Game and Fish Department prohibited transferring the species across watershed divides because bait fishermen had spread it carelessly, even though it is native to most mountain streams. Earlier work on sculpin food habits and spawning was published by [[Koster]] (1937), [[Pearse]] (1918), [[Smith]] (1922), and [[Simon And Brown]] (1943). This study extends that work to a Utah population.

Two sculpin species occur in the Logan River drainage. [[Miller And Sigler]] (1950) separated the Utah sculpin from [[Belding's Sculpin]] (*Cottus beldingi*) by its two or more preopercular spines, compared with one or none in Belding's sculpin.

Sampling used an electric shocking machine generating 600 watts at 220 volts. Catches reached 150 fish per 0.1 mile of stream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The [[Logan River]] flows largely through the [[Cache National Forest]], with drainage formations of dolomitic limestone and calcareous sandstone from the Bear River Range. Weathering of these rocks contributes to the river's alkalinity. The study reach ran from 10 miles downstream of the Idaho state line to a point half a mile below the mouth of [[Logan Canyon]]. The reach has few deep pools. Channel depth in July through September was usually less than 3 feet. The bottom is a mix of silt, coarse gravel, rock, and boulders. Springs and snowmelt supply the water, and mean velocity from April to October was 2.6 feet per second.

**Temperature.** Shade and spring inflow keep summer temperatures low. [[Brown]] (1935) reported a seasonal maximum of 80°F and minimum of 49°F. In 1950, summer water temperatures ranged from 48°F (July) to 55°F (August), while air temperatures ranged from 60°F to 72°F.

**Fish community.** Four native species occur in the river proper: [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), the Utah sculpin, and, rarely, the redside shiner (*Richardsonius balteatus hydrophlox*). Three species were introduced: [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta fario*), [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis fontinalis*), and [[Coast Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri irideus*).

**Habitat preference.** The Utah sculpin is widespread in the Rocky Mountain region, especially the Upper Snake River and Bonneville systems. It was most abundant where the bottom was coarse gravel and small rock. Few fish were collected from silted areas. Sculpins chose nest sites regardless of current speed, using rocks in both rapid and slow water. Preferred depths in July through September ranged from 6 inches to 3 feet. No sculpins were collected above 7,000 feet.

## Key Findings

**1. Sample and methods.** Food was analyzed from 275 stomachs. Stomachs were preserved in 10 percent formalin, and intestines were discarded because their contents were almost fully digested. The sampling period ran from 1949 to March 1951.

**2. Food habits.** The diet consisted almost entirely of aquatic insects, principally Diptera. Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, and Trichoptera were eaten in nearly equal numbers. Key taxa included:
- Diptera: [[Chironomus]], *Tipula*, [[Simulium]], and *Tanytarsus*. Chironomus larvae were often the only item in a stomach, with counts sometimes reaching 40.
- Ephemeroptera: *Ephemerella grandis* was the most frequent mayfly, followed by *E. doddsi*.
- Plecoptera: *Acroneuria pacifica* was the most abundant stonefly, and *Pteronarcella badia* contributed about one-eighth of stonefly volume.
- Trichoptera: [[Hydropsyche]] occurred more often than any other caddisfly, followed by *Brachycentrus*.

Fish, stones, wood, and plant material occurred rarely. The grand total was 1,431 food items. Total food volume was 51.1 cc across the three periods (13.5, 14.4, and 51.1 cc by period as reported in Table 1). Most stomachs were not full. Full stomachs contained either fish or large insects such as Tipula and Plecoptera larvae.

**3. Food availability and preference.** Bottom samples ranked the most available aquatic insects as Diptera, Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, and Trichoptera, in that order. The sculpin's preferred food followed the same order.

**4. Predation on trout.** All fish eaten were young sculpins. Only three trout eggs and two muddler eggs were found. The author judged that the trout eggs may have washed out of nests, so the loss was unlikely to harm reproduction. Predation on trout eggs was described as very slight.

**5. Competition with trout.** The sculpin is a potential competitor with game fish for food, but competition is reduced because trout feed mainly at the surface and sculpins feed almost entirely on the bottom. Food is scarce in winter, yet predation on trout remained low.

**6. Age and growth.** Ages were determined by counting annuli in vertebral centra. Annuli are dark, heavy, translucent bands indented into the centrum, alternating with broader, opaque summer bands. Accessory marks were fainter and could be told apart from true annuli. Ages from vertebrae agreed closely with length-frequency modes in histograms of 495 fish (see [[Vertebral Annuli]]). Growth was rapid in the second year.

**7. Length-weight relationship.** The fitted equation is W = 4.236 × 10⁻⁴ L^2.900, where W is weight in grams and L is standard length in millimeters. The fit is based on 601 fish (Figure 2). Standard lengths ranged from 32 to 134 mm across collection periods, with period means between 91.84 and 98.12 mm.

**8. Condition factor.** K increased with length up to about 60 mm standard length and then decreased in larger fish. The greatest variation occurred in fish under 50 mm (or under 55 mm, as stated elsewhere in the text). Removing stomach contents changed K by more than 6 percent in many individuals.

**9. Sex determination.** External sexing was difficult except in adults. Adult males show a cream or orange band on the first dorsal fin, a trait that rarely appears in females. Mature ovaries are nearly round in cross section, while testes are flattened and triangular. Nearly all fish over 60 mm could be sexed. Fish under 35 mm required microscopic examination.

**10. Reproduction.** Efforts to find nests in the Logan River failed. Examination of female reproductive organs showed a spawning period from February to May, indicating that the sculpin is a spring spawner in this river. Nesting behavior and habitat resembled those reported from Michigan and Wyoming, where eggs are laid on the undersides of rocks in both rapid and slow water.

**Conclusion.** The Utah sculpin in the Logan River is a bottom-dwelling insectivore that spawns in spring, lives in coarse-gravel habitats below 7,000 feet, and exerts little predation pressure on trout eggs. Its feeding overlaps with trout only partially, so its effect on game fish appears limited.
