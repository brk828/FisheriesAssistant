# Origin of the Drainage of Southeastern Arizona

#geology #geomorphology #drainage-evolution

## Executive Summary

This geomorphic study, produced under the [[Arid Lands Project]] at the [[University of Arizona]], examines how the modern drainage of southeastern Arizona developed, with a focus on the [[Safford Valley]] in Graham County. The writer asks how the [[Gila River]] came to cross the [[Mescal Mountains]] and [[Dripping Springs Mountains]] through transverse gorges, and how these gorges came to align with one another. The paper rejects the textbook [[Lake Overflow Theory]] and the headward-capture hypothesis, and favors an [[Antecedent Drainage]] model in which pre-orogenic rivers crossed the region before the [[Basin and Range Orogeny]] uplifted the ranges. The paper also proposes a sequence of post-orogenic events and identifies a regional erosion surface, the [[Tortolita Surface]], as independent evidence. The document is a research paper rather than a biological study, so it contains no species data.

## Background & Description

The study concerns the physiography and sediment history of the Safford Valley and its surroundings. The writer's aims were to determine the changes of course and discharge of older rivers, the source areas of river sediment, and the causes of changes in river regimen. The work was developed with colleagues at the University of Arizona and with [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Ground Water Branch staff, and the writer takes sole responsibility for errors.

The modern drainage has five key characteristics:

- Rivers alternate between structural valleys and transverse erosional gorges. These gorges cut almost at right angles to the mountain trends and record two or three episodes of downcutting.
- Several transverse segments are closely aligned with one another or with gaps that no longer carry rivers.
- No transverse gorge crosses a major granitic or gneissic range (such as the [[Santa Catalina Mountains]], [[Pinaleno Mountains]], or [[Chiricahua Mountains]]). Dry passes such as [[Redington Pass]] do exist in these ranges.
- Recent and Late Pleistocene sediments in the longitudinal valleys are only slightly deformed and often lie in depositional contact with mountain fronts.
- Elevations are: Mescal Mountains near the Gila gorge about 5,000 ft; [[Coolidge Dam]] (Gila River exit) about 2,286 ft; and perimeter low points of 4,730, 4,390, 4,200, and about 3,600 ft.

Earlier explanations were reviewed, including those of [[Waibel 1928]], [[Sauer 1930]], [[Davis 1931]], [[Howard 1942]], [[Gilluly 1956]], and [[Tuan 1959]]. Waibel's model is the basis of the lake-overflow theory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper does not address biological communities. Its environmental context is geomorphic and sedimentary:

- **Fine-grained "lake beds"** (lacustrine deposits) that lie against mountain fronts, locally with lime and gypsum accumulations, for example near Mt. Triplet in the Safford Valley.
- **Pediments and bajadas** that formed as mountain masses eroded.
- **Tertiary volcanic material** (including the Apache Leap-Pickert Post Mountain volcanic pile and a basalt sheet north of San Carlos) that buried and redirected former channels.
- **Regional tilting** to the northwest, which drove renewed downcutting.
- **A wetter climate phase** invoked in the lake-overflow model.

The paper argues that fine sediments did not come from uplift-related lakes. Instead, they were deposited when the mouth of the major drainage coincided with a center of volcanism that raised base level across the system.

## Key Findings

**Rejected theories**
- The [[Lake Overflow Theory]] fails because the transverse gorges cut through ranges that are not the lowest parts of the basin rims, because lake deposits postdate uplift, because it cannot explain the alignment of transverse segments, and because fine lake sediments lie directly against mountain fronts without coarse facies.
- Headward capture of an originally longitudinal system is rejected because no cross-fault zones of weakness were found and the theory does not explain alignment.
- Regional superposition from a post-orogenic cover is considered unlikely because no evidence for such a cover was found.

**Favored theory: Antecedence**
- At least four pre-orogenic rivers flowed across southeastern Arizona before the present ranges were uplifted. The writer favors southwesterly flow, though northeasterly flow has been suggested by S. C. Creasey (U.S. Geological Survey, personal communication).
- Three smaller tributary rivers are also inferred: one from the Pinal Mountains area across the Mescal Mountains and [[Troy Gap]]; one across the [[Galiuro Mountains]] where [[Aravaipa Creek]] now crosses; and one north of the [[Winchester Mountains]] through Redington Pass.
- The rivers maintained their courses through orogeny and were captured after downcutting by northwest-flowing tributaries following structural troughs.

**Independent evidence: the Tortolita Surface**
- A compound erosional and constructional surface extends from near the New Mexico–Arizona line at San Simon westward at least to Gila Bend. Its level declines from about 4,950 ft near the Peloncillo Mountains to about 4,200 ft at Tucson, 3,950 ft near Phoenix, and perhaps 3,000 ft near Ajo.
- Its mean gradient from the Peloncillos to Ajo is 9 ft per mile, comparable to the Gila River gradient of about 10 ft per mile from Safford to Gila Bend.
- The surface appears restricted to the area crossed by the hypothetical rivers. The writer concludes it supports antecedence and argues against lake overflow and headward capture.
- A possibly older surface near 6,100 ft is noted in the [[Huachuca Mountains]], [[Canelo Hills]], [[Santa Rita Mountains]], [[Sierrita Mountains]], and [[Mazatzal Mountains]]. The writer cautions that elevation correlation alone is hazardous.

**Post-orogenic sequence (skeleton history)**
1. Establishment of a dominant southwest-trending drainage, influenced by northeast-trending Precambrian structures.
2. Uplift of the ranges across the rivers and downcutting.
3. Development of the Tortolita Surface.
4. Downcutting of 800 to 1,200 ft, driven by regional tilting to the northwest.
5. Headward growth of northwest-flowing tributaries (Winkelman, Clifton, ancestral Aravaipa); the [[Winkelman River]] was diverted first, and the [[Rockinstraw River]] was diverted near Superior by the Apache Leap–Pickert Post Mountain volcanic pile.
6. Completion of capture and establishment of longitudinal drainage in the San Simon and Aravaipa valleys.
7. Damming of the ancestral [[Salt River]] by the volcanic pile south of the Mazatzal Mountains, raising base level and producing the lake beds. Independent damming occurred on the [[Verde River]] and [[San Pedro River]].
8. When the Safford Valley level reached 3,600 to 3,800 ft, the Gila reoccupied its old course through the Mescal Mountains and began rapid downcutting, with about 100 ft of elevation difference between the Safford and San Pedro valley levels.
9. Six or more stages of reduced downcutting are recorded as terraces and pediment levels in the Safford Valley.

**Conclusions**
- Drainage history in desert basins does not necessarily begin with mountain uplift and undrained basins. A pre-existing system persisted through orogeny and was disrupted by capture, not by diastrophism.
- Desert basin fills are not always products of local mountain erosion alone. Rivers could import fine sediment when mountain erosion was slow.

**Unresolved questions and proposed research**
- The relation between internally drained basins in northern Chihuahua and valleys in eastern Arizona and western New Mexico.
- Why some valleys are filled to their margins with fine sediment while others have coarse facies.
- The origin, direction of flow, and destination of the hypothetical transverse system.
- Recommended studies: heavy-metal distribution in fine sediments, rock-type provenance mapping, geochemical dating of volcanic rocks, geophysical mapping of basement configuration, clay-mineral analysis, and morphological study of mountain fronts.
