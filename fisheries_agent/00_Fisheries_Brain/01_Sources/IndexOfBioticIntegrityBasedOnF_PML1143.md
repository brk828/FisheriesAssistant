# Index of Biotic Integrity Based on Fish Assemblages for West-Central Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

This study presents a preliminary [[Index Of Biotic Integrity]] (IBI) built from [[Fish Assemblages]] in streams and small rivers of west-central Mexico. The index adapts North American IBI versions to regional conditions. It has 10 metrics that relate to environmental quality, ecosystem integrity, and biodiversity. Sampling covered 27 sites in the [[Sierra De Manantlán]] and the upper [[Rio Duero Basin]], and index scores tracked independent ratings of habitat and water quality. The authors recommend independent testing before wider use. They also present the index as a model for other Mexican and Central American regions.

## Background & Description

The authors frame [[Biotic Integrity]] as a system's ability to maintain a balanced, adaptive community with native, self-sustaining organization. They argue that [[Conservation Biology]] should protect ecosystem structure and function, not only species composition, and should be assessed against a benchmark of natural condition. The original IBI was developed for small streams in the north-central United States (Karr 1981). It has since been adapted to other regions and to fish-plus-amphibian or macroinvertebrate assemblages.

West-central Mexico, defined here as the Pacific slope between the Tropic of Cancer and 18°N, covers roughly 15% of the country's area. Its fish fauna includes about 100 species, with a high proportion endemic. The [[Lerma-Santiago River Basin]] is the richest basin in the region, with 66% endemic fishes. Human activities have caused at least five fish extinctions in the region and reduced many populations. Mexican IBI development has been hindered by limited community-ecology data, low natural species richness, species tolerant of extreme conditions, and high endemism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** The [[Sierra De Manantlán]] is a small coastal range about 30 km inland, now part of a biosphere reserve. Its streams are small and rocky, with high gradient. Some have excellent habitat, while others suffer from livestock grazing or poor forestry. Downstream lowland reaches are used heavily for irrigation, carry high sediment and nutrient loads, and receive industrial, municipal, and agricultural discharge. The [[Rio Duero Basin]] is a small, irrigated agricultural river in northern Michoacán that is part of the Lerma basin. Two cities and many towns discharge wastes to it. Its spring-fed tributaries are important water sources, and some remain in good condition.

**Sampling.** Field work took place in March 1986, February 1990, November 1991 (Manantlán), and November 1991 (Duero). Sampling used seines, dip nets, and direct observation in all years, a cast net in 1990, and a gasoline-powered pulsed-DC backpack [[Electroshocking]] unit in 1991 at most sites. Sites were sampled until new species stopped appearing. Fish were checked for disease, deformities, eroded fins, lesions, and tumors. Voucher specimens went to several Mexican and US collections.

**Site distribution.** The 27 sites comprised 21 sites on 15 streams and small rivers in the Sierra de Manantlán and 6 sites on 4 streams and small rivers in the upper Duero basin. The Manantlán sites were distributed among the Ayuquila (11), Purificación (3), and Marabasco (7) basins. The [[Rio Ayuquila Basin]] had the greatest species diversity. Piscivores, invertivores, and carnivores were absent from the Purificación and Marabasco basins.

**Environmental rating.** Each site received a habitat score and a water-quality score on 10-point qualitative scales. The lower of the two was used as the environmental quality score (EQS).

## Key Findings

**Index structure.** The IBI sums 10 metrics, each scored 10 (good), 5 (fair), or 0 (poor). The total ranges from 100 (best possible) to 0 (worst):

1. Number of native species (separate stream and small-river criteria)
2. Percent of individuals that are native benthic species (replacing the benthic species count, which had little utility in the region)
3. Number of water-column species (separate stream and small-river criteria)
4. Number of sensitive species
5. Percent of individuals that are tolerant species
6. Percent of individuals that are exotic species
7. Percent of individuals that are omnivores
8. Percent of individuals that are native livebearers (the only trophic/reproductive metric retained; the original version used three trophic metrics, reduced to one here)
9. Number of fish per half-hour of sampling (separate stream and small-river criteria)
10. Percent of individuals that are diseased, deformed, eroded-finned, lesioned, or tumorous

Metrics 9 and 10 were included on conceptual grounds, scored by professional judgment rather than the empirical data.

**Fish fauna of the study sites.** The authors found 15 species in total across the sites (Table 1 in the source). Native benthic taxa were scarce, with only six native benthic species, one to three per basin. The only probable simple lithophilous species was *Scartomyzon austrinus*, found only in the Duero and Ayuquila basins. Diadromous species *Agonostomus monticola* and *Sicydium multipunctatum* occur in the Manantlán basins but not in the Duero basin. *Oreochromis aureus* was an exotic species present at several sites, and lamprey ammocoetes were also recorded.

**Response to degradation.** Heavily degraded sites had significantly fewer native species (F = 8.42, p = 0.0124) and fewer native benthic species (F = 4.44, p = 0.0524). Sensitive species were usually absent (F = 14.16, p = 0.0019). Tolerant species were more common (F = 8.53, p = 0.0119). Native livebearers varied widely among heavily degraded sites, with proportions from 0.28 to 1.00. Fish in poor physical condition appeared only at one heavily degraded site (Arroyo La Cidrita).

**Index validation.** Index scores ranged from 35 to 90 across the 27 sites. Scores correlated positively with the environmental quality score (Spearman rank correlation = 0.71, P < 0.0001). The least-degraded sites all had high scores, and most poor-quality sites had low scores. Notable cases include:

- Arroyo Tecolote (Purificación basin): index 65 but EQS 2, an outlier driven by three tolerant native livebearers and herbivores at a livestock feedlot site
- Upper Rio Ayutla: index 45, degraded by siltation, livestock use, and organic pollution
- Pueblo Corcovado: index 65 after a recovery canyon
- Sugar-processing plant outfall (Ingenio Melchor Ocampo): index 35, indicating poor integrity
- Arroyo Manantlán and two sites in the canyon reach: scores of 75–80 and 70–80, indicating good integrity

**Caveats.** The authors state that the correlation is partly circular, because the same data were used to build and test the index. The validation is therefore an internal check rather than an independent test. The index also may not detect episodic toxic pollution, altered seasonal hydrology, barriers to migration, or introductions of exotic species. Sample size was small, and the species-level natural history data needed for accurate metric assignment are incomplete.

**Recommendations.**

- Test the index against independent fish and environmental data, including sites outside the Sierra de Manantlán and Duero basin.
- Collect life-history, trophic, reproductive, and pollution-sensitivity data for west-central Mexican fishes.
- Study how sampling gear biases affect metric values. The electroshocker was probably the least biased and most efficient method, but it is not widely available in Mexico.
- Consider basin-specific or region-specific criteria, and a simplified index for northern Mexican streams with low richness.
- Consider adding [[Macroinvertebrates]], amphibians, or lake and estuary indices, since macroinvertebrates respond to different stressors than fish do.
- Use the index for long-term monitoring and to identify priority reaches for protection or restoration, including the upper Rio Ayuquila, Manantlán Cupatzitzio, and Cuzalapa systems.
