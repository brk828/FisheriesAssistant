# Largemouth Bass in Ridge Lake, Coles County, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Vol. 26, Art. 2, November 1954) by [[George W. Bennett]] reports a ten-year study (1941–1951) of largemouth bass population dynamics in Ridge Lake, a newly impounded 18-acre artificial lake in Coles County. The lake was stocked with 435 [[Largemouth Bass]] in 1941, 129 [[Bluegill]] in 1944 and 138 [[Warmouth]] in 1949, and was managed through biennial spring draining censuses, public creel census fishing, and monitoring of spawn and growth. The study challenges the prevailing belief that "normal" predator–prey weight ratios govern bass populations, and concludes that controlling the numerical size of other fish populations, especially [[Bluegill]], is the key to sustained bass yields.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** [[Ridge Lake]] was completed in 1941 by damming Dry Run Creek, a tributary of the Embarrass River, within [[Ridge State Park]]. At overflow level it covered about 18.1 acres with a maximum depth of 25 feet. Its watershed is 902 acres (about 1.41 square miles), and the lake was set aside as a study area.
- **Species introduced:**
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*): 435 wild adults and yearlings from [[Craborchard Lake]] (58), [[Lake Chautauqua]] (42) and [[Lake Glendale]] (335), stocked April–June 1941.
  - [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*): 107 fish from Lake Chautauqua plus 29 from a strip-mine pond near Danville, released in 1944 (129 total).
  - [[Warmouth]] (*Chaenobryttus coronarius*): 138 fish in three 1949 groups, used to test establishment alongside a dominant bass population.
- **Native fauna of Dry Run Creek:** A poison application before impoundment killed species including stoneroller, creek chub, silverjaw minnow, green sunfish, longear sunfish, white sucker and two bullheads. Green sunfish and black/yellow bullheads later re-entered the lake in small numbers; a few carp and one black buffalo also appeared.
- **Management history:** The Illinois regulations of 1923 (closed season, 10-inch minimum length, creel limit of 10) are discussed as largely ineffective, given the study's findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Limnology and hydrology**
- The lake receives heavy silt loads from steep, overgrazed slopes. An average annual capacity loss of 1.29% (about 4.36 tons of soil per acre of watershed) was recorded by Stall et al. (1951). The upper end, where about 4 acres were under less than a foot of water in 1950, is heavily silted.
- A [[Tower Spillway]] draws water from the lake bottom (capacity about 25 cubic feet per second), while a [[Surface Spillway]] handles overflow during heavy rain.
- Summer thermal stratification produces oxygen-free bottom water. Heavy rain flushes this stratum out, temporarily raising dissolved oxygen (for example, 6.3 ppm at 12 ft on July 11, 1942), but oxygen was again zero from late July until fall overturn (September 27).
- Transparency fell below 2 feet after rains of about 1 inch or more. Maximum transparency was 14 feet (June 4, 1942).
- Soils are of low fertility; local corn yields average at most 25 bushels per acre, compared with about 75 bushels farther north. Lake fertility was judged intermediate in Illinois.
- The growing season for bass was about 6 to 6.5 months (water above 55°F from mid-April to late October in 1942).

**Aquatic vegetation**
- Pre-impoundment marsh flora included bulrush, cattail and sedges. Submerged and emergent vegetation (leafy pondweed, sago pondweed, elodea, naiad, arrowheads) developed after 1942.
- A midsummer vegetation die-off, beginning in 1946, was judged beneficial to fish management because it forced small fish out of the plant mats into open water where larger bass could eat them.

**Biota**
- Crayfish were abundant and were an important bass food (cited in the Discussion as a reason bass did not suppress bluegill numbers).
- Plankton in summer was dominated by blue-green algae.
- Midge larvae (*Chaoborus*) inhabit the oxygen-deficient bottom.

## Key Findings

1. **Predator–prey "normal ratios" rejected:** Largemouth bass are relatively omnivorous and can maintain high numerical and poundage levels without their supposed principal prey.
2. **Bass population and yield:**
   - Draining censuses recorded bass weights of 48.2, 50.4 and 49.9 pounds per acre in 1943, 1949 and 1951, suggesting a carrying capacity of about 50 lb/acre.
   - Angling yields ranged from 10.9 to 30.0 lb/acre (18-acre basis), with fishing pressure from 90 to 320 man-hours/acre.
   - In 1949, 25.6 lb/acre was caught in 1948 and yields were 2 to 10 times those of unmanaged Illinois waters.
3. **Total removals:** More than 11,000 bass and 154,000 bluegills were permanently removed in 1941–1951, including 3,743 bass and about 9,000 bluegills taken by anglers.
4. **Bass spawn:**
   - Mean fry estimates were 50,500 in years when small fish were culled by draining, versus 5,900 in alternate years (about 8.6 times greater).
   - Schooling fry were seen before June 1 in only 3 of 11 years.
   - Spawn production was inversely related to the number of small bluegills, indicating that bluegill nest-robbing and fry predation limit bass reproduction.
5. **Survival:** Fry-to-recruit survival ratios of 29:1 (1941), 40:1 (1943), 30:1 (1947) and 34:1 (1949) were recorded, rising to 195:1 in 1945.
6. **Growth:** Bass averaged 8.1, 10.3, 12.6 and 13.7 inches at ages 1 to 4. Bluegills averaged 4.7, 6.2 and 7.1 inches at ages 1 to 3. Draining-induced thinning accelerated growth.
7. **Interspecific competition:** The largest bluegill poundage (193.3 lb/acre, 1947) coincided with the lowest bass poundage, and the reverse held in 1949. Bass and bluegills compete, and the authors suggest bass rarely control bluegill numbers.
8. **Catch rate and wariness:** Catch rates fell sharply within days of opening; on the second day of the 1949 season the rate was 0.18 lb/man-hour, versus 2.96 in preseason fishing. Bass lost vulnerability quickly, while skill strongly affected success.
9. **Bait and lure efficiency:** Artificial lures caught more bass than natural baits. Minnows and crayfish were the most efficient natural baits, requiring about 4.0 and 5.3 man-hours per desirable bass, respectively.
10. **Marking losses:** Unaccountable losses of marked bass were 86.7% for the original 1941 stock, attributed largely to transport injury, versus 5.4 to 9.8% in later periods.
11. **Exploitation:** Rates for marked bass were 31.0 to 62.4% per year (1944–1950), higher than rates reported for Shoe Lake, Indiana (20%) and Norris Reservoir (18.5%).
12. **Cost of fishing:** In 1949, about $1.22 per hour, or $8.66 per pound of bass, with 7.1 hours needed per pound.
13. **Management conclusions:**
    - Control of the numerical size of non-bass species, especially bluegills, is the key to successful bass management.
    - The 10-inch minimum length limit and the creel limit of 10 showed no detectable benefit.
    - Biennial draining and culling produced dominant year-classes, improved angling success and removed surplus small fish.
    - Bass fishing was rarely limited by spawner numbers, because one male can guard enough nests to replace fishery losses.
