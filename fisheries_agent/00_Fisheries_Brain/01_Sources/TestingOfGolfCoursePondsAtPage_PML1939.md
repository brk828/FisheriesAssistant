# Testing Golf Course Ponds at Page, Arizona, as a Grow-Out Facility for Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, by Gordon Mueller (US Geological Survey, Denver) and Ed Wick (National Park Service, Fort Collins), evaluates whether the two lined ponds of the [[Lake Powell National Golf Course]] in Page, Arizona, can serve as a low-cost grow-out facility for endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] using surplus hatchery fish from the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]]. Fish stocked in May 1996 grew from an average of about 115 mm to about 360 mm (383 mm in the August 1997 sample) over roughly 12–15 months, with good health and high apparent survival. Water quality was generally good but dissolved oxygen and pH could become marginal under some conditions. The authors estimate 300–500 thirty-centimeter fish could be raised per year and recommend a formal agreement with the City of Page and the National Park Service and Fish and Wildlife Service as lead agencies.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), in serious decline across the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Source stock:** Fish hatched in 1993 at the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]], Utah, originating from [[Green River]] brood stock. Fish had been designated surplus and held at high densities, which slowed their growth.
- **Problem addressed:** Millions of small stocked suckers have produced little or no survival, largely due to predation. Stocking philosophy has shifted toward fewer, larger fish, but hatcheries lack pond space and funding to rear them to the 40 cm target set by the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]].
- **Facility:** The Lake Powell National Golf Course is owned and operated by the City of Page. Two ponds (1.1 and 2.3 acres; about 3.4 acres total, just over 2 m deep) are interconnected by underground pipe, have plastic liners, and are fitted with spray aerators. They serve as ball traps and irrigation reservoirs, fed by a mix of [[Lake Powell]] water and treated wastewater.
- **Methods:** Fish were stocked into the southern pond on May 16, 1996. Sampling used seine and trammel net; water parameters were measured with a hydrolab. Water quality data are reproduced in Appendix A.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality**
- Overall "fairly good," with shifts linked to season, irrigation demand, and water source.
- Aeration is critical to survival in summer, mixing the water column and influencing dissolved oxygen (DO).
- Summer vertical profiles showed stratification near the bottom, where DO dropped to 2 mg/L, probably near the detection range of the equipment.
- Temperatures ranged from 12.1 to 26.7 °C; conductivity from 946 to 1,364 µS/cm; pH from 7.8 to 9.3 (fine-scale logs in the appendix extend to 10.4).
- Surface and mid-column DO was generally above 5.0 mg/L, with saturation typically 80–150%.

**Pond dynamics and hazards**
- Roughly 25–35% of pond volume is pumped daily for irrigation in summer.
- Lawn chemicals entering after heavy rain, interrupted water supply, and aerator failure were identified as principal die-off risks.
- An algal problem (floating mats) is common at municipal ponds. Grass carp and tilapia were considered but are poor algae consumers; Boulder City manually removes mats.
- Iceberg Canyon's perched lake suffered a large fish kill when reconnected to the reservoir, attributed to oxygen depletion from inundated vegetation.

**Related sites**
- San Juan inflow area (Farmington, N.M. stocking site; last wild brood collection in 1991).
- Colorado River inflow area near the Dirty Devil River confluence and [[Canyonlands National Park]] (Farley and White Canyons).
- Grand Canyon reaches below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and below [[Hoover Dam]], where water near 12 °C still allows spawning.
- [[Lake Mead]], where Nevada biologists are rearing naturally spawned larvae.

## Key Findings

**Growth and survival**
- 296 fish stocked on May 16, 1996, averaging 115 mm and 17 g.
- July 1996 partial drawdown: 24 fish captured by 15-m seine, averaging 194 mm; several dead suckers observed.
- March 1997: 11 fish averaging 347 mm (trammel net).
- May 2, 1997: 82 fish measured, mean 359 mm and 641 g (range 320–450 mm; 410–1,270 g).
- August 19, 1997: 7 fish averaging 383 mm and 645 g, in good condition with no external parasites. Small sample suggests little growth since May.
- More than 130 fish captured in May 1997 suggest the ponds still held over 150 fish.
- Health inspection found fish healthy, free of tapeworm and external parasites, with good visceral fat.

**Production estimate**
- Carrying capacity could not be calculated from available data. Speculative range: 300–500 kg (equivalent to 300–500 thirty-centimeter fish at 1 kg each).
- Supplemental feeding could raise production.

**Constraints**
- Fish under 100 mm should not be stocked: the irrigation intake pumps may draw in larvae or small fish and clog nozzles and valves.
- The 1993 cohort was about four years old in 1997 and approaching sexual maturity; spawning and larval production could create the problems above.
- Recapture method should be improved to reduce stress; trap or fyke nets are used successfully at Lake Mohave and Boulder City ponds.

**Stocking opportunities**
- Augmentation of [[Lake Powell]] reservoir populations, including the San Juan Arm (a 1996 Memorandum of Agreement covers surplus fish but not golf course ponds).
- Moving fish from the ponds into Utah requires four special-use permits and sacrifice of at least 50 fish for health certification, a major administrative barrier.
- Stocking the [[San Juan River]] to meet the draft augmentation plan (several thousand large suckers), and the Grand Canyon and Lake Mead reaches.
- Iceberg Canyon perched lake as a potential natural-recruitment site, pending reservoir elevation changes.

**Recommendations**
1. Identify a lead agency (NPS and FWS proposed) and formalize an agreement with the City of Page.
2. Develop a budget for pond maintenance, PIT tags, fish removal, transport, and stocking.
3. Complete NEPA compliance and state and federal permitting.
4. Produce a flexible management plan with goals, release sites, fish sources, and schedules.
5. Expand public education through media coverage, student involvement, and an information sign near the ponds.

**Caveats**
- The study covers only about 15–16 months and one pond system, so disease and equipment-failure risks remain substantial.
