# Attempted Chemical Renovation of an Arizona Springbrook for Management of the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by Gary K. Meffe, published in the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*, examines the decline of the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) after colonization of [[Bylas Springs]] by non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). The author applied [[Antimycin A]] to First Spring in an attempt to remove all mosquitofish. Mosquitofish were eliminated in the initial treatment, and reintroduced topminnows expanded rapidly, but mosquitofish reappeared within months. The author concludes that single chemical treatments are unlikely to succeed against livebearing invaders and that repeated treatments combined with downstream barriers may be required.

## Background & Description

The Sonoran topminnow is a small (<50 mm) viviparous poeciliid native to lowland streams, springs and marshes of the American Southwest. It was formerly widespread in the Gila River drainage of Arizona and New Mexico, but has declined in the United States to a few isolated populations in southeastern Arizona and is listed as endangered (U.S. Department of the Interior 1980). The author attributes the decline to habitat alteration and interactions with introduced fishes, particularly mosquitofish, which can eliminate topminnows within one to two years of introduction.

The editor's note clarifies that "Sonoran topminnow" is the local name covering two subspecies, *P. o. occidentalis* and *P. o. sonoriensis*, and that the correct common name is Gila topminnow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Three small springbrooks, collectively called Bylas Springs, rise from alluvial fill and flow toward the [[Gila River]] within the Southwestern Desertscrub Formation of the Lower Sonoran Lifezone at about 800 m elevation. The main site, First Spring, has two springheads (Bos Spring and Medicine Spring) in channels 0.2–1.0 m wide and 2–20 cm deep. Discharge is about 1 L/s, with mean current velocity of about 0.2 m/s. Water disappears into alluvium 800 m from Bos Spring and 600 m from the Gila River channel.

**Physico-chemistry.** Springheads are uniform, with low pH, high dissolved CO₂ and a constant temperature of 29–32 °C. Downstream variability is greater. Temperature at downstream sites changes seasonally. Medicine Spring had conductivity of about 1,350–1,700 µmhos/cm, compared with about 4,000–4,400 µmhos/cm at Bos Spring (Table 1, 18 August 1981).

**Vegetation.** Dominant aquatic plants include sedges (*Eleocharis* spp., *Scirpus americana*), pondmat (*Zannichellia palustris*), naiad (*Najas guadalupensis*), *Vaucheria* algae, and lizardtail (*Anemopsis californica*). Stonewort (*Chara* sp.) periodically chokes channel sections. Riparian vegetation includes velvet mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*) and introduced saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*).

**Invertebrates.** Endemic hydrobiid snails occur in the system, and amphipods (*Hyalella azteca*) and zygopterans were present. Aquatic macroinvertebrates are described elsewhere (Meffe and Marsh 1983).

**Colonization history.** Bylas Springs were isolated from the Gila River by an alluvial fan since at least 1917. Mosquitofish probably entered when the fan was flooded in winter 1978–1979. Topminnows were the only fish from 1968 to 1978.

## Key Findings

- **Decline after colonization.** Within about 1.5 years, topminnows had disappeared from lower Salt Creek and were outnumbered by mosquitofish in Medicine Spring. Over the next two years they were extirpated from most of Salt Creek and most of First Spring. At Bos Spring they still made up 62–72% of fishes.
- **Refugia.** Springheads with low pH, low O₂ and high CO₂ appear to inhibit mosquitofish and act as refugia. An inverse abundance relationship between the two species was found.
- **Fin damage as indicator.** Of 121 juvenile and adult topminnows from Medicine and Bos springs, 79 (65.3%) had moderate to severe caudal fin damage. Little or no damage was found in 1968 specimens or in Second Spring fish (1981–1982), where mosquitofish were absent.
- **Second Spring.** This system, not colonized by mosquitofish, retained stable topminnow populations throughout.
- **Renovation treatment.** On 27 March 1982, 157 topminnows were collected and held as a safeguard. Antimycin A at about 10 µg/L was applied over 2 hours. Mosquitofish were affected earlier than topminnows. In the first half hour, topminnows were 9.3% of catch, rising to 33.0% in the second half hour. Essentially all fish were dead one hour after application.
- **Invertebrate impact.** Some amphipods and zygopterans died during treatment, but all major invertebrate groups were abundant three weeks later.
- **Failure.** No live fish were found on 16 April 1982. The 26 males, 37 females and 4 juveniles were restocked. On 10 July 1982, several mosquitofish were collected at Station 8, and no mosquitofish were in the springheads. Restocked topminnows had reproduced and expanded into the hundreds. Reproducing mosquitofish appeared 15 weeks after treatment.
- **Comparable failures.** A 1-hectare pond in Cochise County reinvaded after 3 months. Ord Creek trout removal failed on first treatment but succeeded after a second antimycin application (Rinne et al. 1981).
- **Management recommendations.** The author proposes repeated antimycin applications over months or years, combined with 1-m downstream barriers near stations 8–10 to block flood-borne reinvasion. Laboratory comparison of toxicity between mosquitofish and topminnows is recommended, since mosquitofish appear more susceptible.
- **Reinvasion mechanism.** A single gravid female escaping in an isolated pool could restart colonization quickly, which the author considers the probable cause at First Spring.
