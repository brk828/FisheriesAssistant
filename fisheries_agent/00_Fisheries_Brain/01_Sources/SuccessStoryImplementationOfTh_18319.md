# Implementation of the 1995 Biological Opinion on CAP and Nonnative Aquatic Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a success-story and implementation guide for the April 15, 1995 [[Biological Opinion]] on the potential for the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) to introduce and spread nonnative aquatic species in the Gila River Basin. It was written and negotiated by the Arizona Ecological Services Field Office (AESO) of the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] and is being implemented jointly with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The opinion is framed as an ecosystem-level [[Conservation Strategy]] rather than a traditional species-by-species consultation, and it targets four jeopardized native fishes. The document also explains the funding, roles, and project-selection process used for implementation, and it notes delays, litigation, and early results.

## Background & Description

The opinion addresses the threat that CAP water deliveries pose to native fauna by moving and spreading nonnative aquatic species. Its scope is intentionally broad: measures are meant to reduce nonnative impacts on all fish species in the basin, both listed and candidate, as well as listed and candidate amphibians and aquatic invertebrates. The consultation began in 1989, and the opinion was designed to function over the 100-year life of the CAP project.

The four species identified as jeopardized by CAP are:

- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

Implementation funds are generally restricted to these four species. They may also be spent on other Gila Basin listed and candidate fishes if the expenditure can be shown to relieve jeopardy to the four species, for example by improving understanding of the native fish community as a whole.

The implementation framework has three main funded components, plus information and education:

- **Recovery fund (RPA 3.0):** recovery actions for the jeopardized species, guided by the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] and the recovery plans for the three species with signed plans and the draft razorback sucker plans.
- **Nonnative control fund (RPA 4.0):** management against nonnative species, including a contingency fund for emergency responses, managed through an agreement with the [[Fish and Wildlife Foundation]].
- **Information and education (I&E) program:** developed with Reclamation to raise public awareness.

Other reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs) cover barrier construction on Aravaipa Creek and the San Pedro River (RPA 1.1), operation and maintenance of existing [[Electric Barriers]] (RPA 1.2 and 1.3), and baseline study and monitoring (RPA 2.0).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the Gila River Basin as a vanishing aquatic and riparian ecosystem in the southwestern United States. The main nonnative threats it names are:

- **[[Red Shiner]]:** described as a noxious nonnative whose recent invasion of Aravaipa Creek increased support for barriers there.
- **[[Crayfish]]:** the subject of a Reclamation-funded study of predation on native fishes.

Management tools include physical [[Fish Barriers]] (drop barriers and electric barriers on the Salt River Project and Florence-Casa Grande Canals) and monitoring to detect new incursions early. The document also cites a feasibility study on species- or family-specific toxins, noting that a toxin had worked against lamprey in the Great Lakes, as a possible way to remove nonnative fishes without harming natives.

Stakeholders include the states of Arizona and New Mexico, the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (NMDGF), the [[Nature Conservancy]], the [[Forest Service]] (four National Forests), the [[Bureau of Land Management]], Arizona State University, the Arizona Department of Water Quality, the San Carlos Apache Tribe, the White Mountain Apache Tribe, and private landowners.

## Key Findings

**Program outcomes reported as successes**

- The opinion is described as a successful collaboration. Early in the process, Reclamation and the Service disagreed strongly about the extent of the nonnative threat and CAP's role in it. The document reports that the agencies have since built trust and a productive working relationship.
- Reclamation's Phoenix Area Office hired a fish biologist to work exclusively on native fish problems. Beyond the opinion's requirements, Reclamation funded a crayfish predation study, a refugium and study population of Gila topminnow at Arizona State University, loach minnow propagation at Alchesay National Fish Hatchery, and fish barrier engineering assistance for the White Mountain Apache Tribe and Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests.
- Awareness has grown. In 1989 only a handful of biologists knew of the threat. A symposium on fish barrier construction and use was presented at the Desert Fishes Council meeting in fall 1997.
- Monitoring of nonnative fish distribution is underway and is intended to provide early warning of new invasions.
- The opinion's "umbrella" protection is used to streamline section 7 consultations in the central Gila basin. Projects such as groundwater recharge, created wetlands, and aquaculture can often proceed through informal consultation or "no effect" determinations.

**Delays and obstacles**

- Implementation was slower than planned. Work on funding transfers did not begin until about three months after delivery because Aravaipa barriers were given highest priority.
- The lump-sum funding approach failed because of Reclamation policies and regulations, so the first year's projects were chosen ad hoc. An annual project-selection process was set up only after the first year's projects were nearly complete.
- Congressional and other political "holds" delayed the funding transfer. The timeline of first-year interagency agreements runs into 1997.
- The electric barriers remain in operation, but minor operational "glitches" are being identified and corrected.
- The opinion is under lawsuit from two parties. One believes the opinion goes too far toward protecting native fish, and the other believes it does not go far enough. The document presents this as evidence of a middle ground and says the best defense is a strong record of implementation.

**Funding and governance rules**

- Transferred Reclamation funds may not be used for overhead by either agency. For year one, Reclamation allowed $50,000 for Service salaries tied to actual recovery and management work. Oversight of other RPAs is not covered.
- A portion of the annual management funds goes into a contingency account held with the Fish and Wildlife Foundation. Not all transferred funds are expected to be spent each year.
- The test for any implementation action is whether it contributes to removing the jeopardy that CAP-caused nonnative introduction and spread creates in the Gila basin, excluding the Santa Cruz basin.
- The annual project cycle runs as follows:
  1. The Desert Fishes Recovery Team recommends actions.
  2. A four-biologist Technical Committee (AGFD, NMDGF, Reclamation, and the Service) ranks them.
  3. A Policy Committee reviews the list.
  4. The Service Regional Director approves and forwards it to Reclamation, with comments from AGFD and NMDGF.
  5. The interagency agreement is amended and signed, and funds are transferred.
  6. Scopes of work are approved, and the Service carries out projects.
  7. An annual report and briefing document how expenditures removed jeopardy.

**Service responsibilities**

The Service's implementation duties include overseeing barrier design, siting, and operation; carrying out NEPA and section 7 compliance; administering contracts and agreements; reporting annually to Reclamation; and supporting the defense in litigation by maintaining the administrative record and assisting the Solicitor's office and the Justice Department.

**Coordination**

The document says implementation must stay consistent with a separate formal consultation on CAP in the Santa Cruz River basin, with other recovery efforts for the four jeopardized species, and with other section 7 efforts on nonnative species.
