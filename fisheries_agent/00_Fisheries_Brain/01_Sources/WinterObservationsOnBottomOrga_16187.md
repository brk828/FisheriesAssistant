# Winter Observations on Bottom Organisms and Trout in Bridger Creek, Montana

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Salmonidae #Trichoptera #benthic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by Sidney M. Logan in a 900-foot section of [[Bridger Creek]] (a tributary of the [[East Gallatin River]]) in southwestern Montana from October 1959 to September 1960, examined the effects of temperature, ice formation, and fluctuating water levels on benthic macroinvertebrates and [[Rainbow Trout]] movement. Ninety-six bottom samples and 27 drift samples revealed that [[Trichoptera]] (caddisflies, primarily family [[Limnephilidae]]) dominated the benthic community throughout the year. Surface ice cover had negligible effect on benthic organism abundance, while high spring flows caused significant reductions in situ and corresponding increases in drift samples. Trout tagging data showed strong site fidelity (61% recaptured within 150 feet) but also documented remarkable long-distance dispersal events, including a 55-mile downstream movement into the Missouri River. Seasonal habitat shifts were observed, with trout moving from pools into riffle areas under surface ice during winter months.

## Background & Description

- **Primary study species**: Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*), the most numerous game fish in the study section.
- **Size threshold for study**: Only rainbow trout exceeding 7 inches total length were included in movement analysis.
- **Long-distance migrants**: Four trout exhibiting wide-ranging movement (up to 55 miles) measured between 8.5–9.9 inches total length, estimated at 3–4 years old based on [[Purkett (1950)]] age-length correlations for the same stream.
- **Co-occurring fish species** (secondary presence, not focal to movement study):
  - [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) - small numbers
  - [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) - small numbers
  - [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) - small numbers
  - [[Mottled Sculpin]] (*Cottus bairdi*) - abundant
  - [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) - present in small numbers
- **Benthic invertebrate composition**: Dominated numerically by [[Trichoptera]] (50.5–85.6% of bottom samples), followed by [[Diptera]] (4.5–25.1%). Other groups present in lower abundance included [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Plecoptera]], [[Coleoptera]], [[Acari]], [[Gastropoda]], and [[Tricladia]] (flatworms).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical Habitat**
- Study section: 900 feet long, mean width 17 feet, average depth 15 inches, composed of sand, gravel, and rubble substrate with numerous riffles and few deep pools.
- Riparian cover: Dense willow, chokecherry, and rose, with occasional Douglas-fir and aspen.
- Water temperature range: 32–65°F annually; winter average 33.5–35.0°F (Nov–Mar).
- Turbidity: 3–18 ppm during low water (July–Feb); 45–96 ppm during high water (March–June).
- Water chemistry: Total dissolved solids 205–244 ppm; pH 7.6–8.2; alkalinity 175–202 ppm; dissolved oxygen 9.1–11.9 ppm.
- Ice types observed: [[Surface Ice]] (up to 2.5 in. in study section, 4.5 in. upstream) and [[Anchor Ice]] (absent in study section but present upstream); frazil ice not observed.

**Seasonal Habitat Use by Trout**
- Spring, summer, autumn: Trout primarily occupied **pools**.
- Winter: Many trout relocated to **riffle areas** under surface ice cover (average 14 trout/month movement into two riffle-dominated units, >50% originating from pools within 100 feet).
- Spring ice-melt: Trout returned to pools as streamside vegetation cover was still absent, creating a temporary cover deficit.
- This pattern parallels findings by [[Cooper (1953)]] for brook trout in Michigan streams, which similarly shifted from pools to higher-cover winter habitat.

**Benthic Organism Ecology**
- Bottom organism abundance highest in November (285/ft²) and lowest in May (104/ft²), inversely correlated with stream flow (scouring effect + insect emergence during high spring flow).
- Drift organism abundance peaked in May (29.3/sample) and was lowest in August (8.0/sample), positively correlated with stream flow.
- Surface ice cover produced no significant difference in benthic abundance between ice-covered near-shore samples and ice-free mid-channel samples, contrasting with findings of [[Brown, Clothier, and Alvord (1953)]] and [[Reimers (1957)]] in other Montana/California streams where prolonged ice reduced periphyton and grazing organisms.
- Only 9 dead limnephilids were found frozen in edge ice — a minor loss compared to other studies.
- Floating surface ice did **not** significantly increase drift organism numbers (13.0 organisms/sample pre-ice vs. 12.4 during ice-covered periods), unlike anchor ice dynamics reported elsewhere (e.g., [[O'Donnell and Churchill (1954)]] recorded ~9,480 organisms/hour dislodged by anchor ice release in the Brule River, Wisconsin).
- Absence of anchor ice in the study section (due to continuous upstream ice bridging) likely prevented sudden flow surges and associated drift spikes seen in other systems.

## Key Findings

1. **Benthic community stability under ice**: Long periods of low water temperature and intermittent surface ice cover had no significant negative effect on the abundance of bottom organisms; [[Trichoptera]] remained dominant (50.5–85.6%) across all months.

2. **Spring flow as primary disturbance driver**: High spring stream flows (associated with snowmelt) reduced in-situ bottom organism abundance via scouring while simultaneously increasing drift sample counts — flow, not ice, was the dominant physical driver of benthic community fluctuation.

3. **Anchor ice absence prevented flow surges**: Because surface ice continuously bridged the stream area upstream, anchor ice dams did not form, thereby preventing the sudden water releases and drift surges documented in comparable systems (Brule River, Pigeon River).

4. **Trout site fidelity**: 61.6% of tagged trout remained within the 900-ft study section one month post-tagging; over 35% of recaptures occurred within 50 feet of original capture location. This supports the concept of defined "home territories" as previously suggested by [[Allen (1951)]], [[Miller (1954)]], and [[Miller (1957)]].

5. **Downstream movement bias**: Across all months, more trout were recaptured downstream (mean 37.7%) than upstream (mean 26.6%), consistent with general downstream movement tendencies reported by [[Stefanich (1951)]] and [[Funk (1955)]].

6. **Enhanced winter movement**: Trout movement exceeding 50 feet was more frequent during December–February (70.4% average) compared to other months (60.5% average), coinciding with low temperatures and surface ice presence — possibly reflecting redistribution from pools to riffle refugia.

7. **Long-distance dispersal capacity**: Despite strong overall site fidelity, a subset of trout demonstrated extensive movement — up to 2 miles upstream and 55 miles downstream (into the Missouri River) — illustrating high individual variability in movement behavior within the same population.

8. **Seasonal habitat shift**: Trout exhibited a clear seasonal habitat preference shift from pools (spring–autumn) to riffle areas under surface ice (winter), likely driven by cover availability dynamics as streamside vegetation cover diminished in late autumn and increased with ice formation.
