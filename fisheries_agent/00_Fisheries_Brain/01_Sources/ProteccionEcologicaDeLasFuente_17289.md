# Ecological Protection of Drinking Water Sources: Restoration at the Ojo de Agua de Apodaca

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This report describes the first attempt in Mexico at ecological management of a spring ecosystem, carried out at the [[Ojo de Agua de Apodaca]] in [[Nuevo León]] between 1988 and 1989. The project was a joint effort of the [[Monterrey Water And Drainage Services]] (water utility), the municipal government of Apodaca, and the Fish Laboratory of the Faculty of Biological Sciences, UANL. Its central goal was to protect the endemic [[Platy Apodaca]] ([[Xiphophorus Couchianus]]), a species listed as endangered, together with the water quality on which it depends.

Over one year, the platy population rose from 700 to about 2,000 individuals, and the number of [[Nymphaea Multituberculata]] rose from 10 to 95. Surface cover of the water lily increased by roughly 50%. Water quality, however, improved little or not at all, and two problems remain unresolved: transplant failure of the water lily and the lack of a mechanical method for removing algal mats without killing fry.

## Background & Description

**Context.** The author notes that a large body of literature on spring restoration exists, mainly driven by the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (USA), which requires habitat conservation for listed species. No comparable ecological restoration of a spring for water quality and species survival was known in Mexico. This project was presented as the first such case in the country.

**Site.** The Ojo de Agua de Apodaca is a municipal recreation area. It consists of a group of springs, their outflow into a former stream now converted into a series of pools, and surrounding forest. In 1961 the spring area was a marsh with dense aquatic vegetation, averaging 1 m deep with a maximum of 2.5 m. Plants listed in order of abundance were *Nymphaea multituberculata*, *Elatine*, *Potamogeton*, *Polygonum*, *Equisetum*, *Najas*, *Hydrocotyle*, and several submerged grasses. The surrounding forest of *Pithecellobium*, *Quercus* and *Acacia* had been reduced.

**Fish fauna.** The endemic platy *Xiphophorus couchianus* was described by Contreras (1967) and listed as endangered (Miller 1977; Contreras 1975, 1978, 1987). The source notes that it may be a local form still under study (Obregón and Contreras 1988). Similar forms were known from six other springs in central Nuevo León, all now dried, with their populations extinct. Local populations of *Gambusia speciosa*, *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Poecilia mexicana* and *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatus* were also recorded at Apodaca (Miller 1977).

**Early water quality.** Water was once described as excellent: clear, transparent and rich in vegetation. The first environmental readings were not taken until 1987, when pH was 6.6 and chloride salinity was 212 ppm. Muddy areas existed but were not black muds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Restoration plan.** An agreement was drawn up with the water utility to restore environmental conditions and protect the platy. Its main points were:

1. **Population restoration** for the platy:
   - Moving individuals from the pools downstream back to the spring, and favouring breeding and feeding sites.
   - Controlling exotic competitors, notably the guppy (*Poecilia reticulata*).
   - Controlling predators of the platy, notably *Gambusia speciosa* (guayacón).
2. **Habitat restoration**:
   - Re-establishing aquatic cover, preferably *Nymphaea*, to provide clear water and shade that limits algae, especially near the water intake.
   - Re-establishing riparian trees such as willows and cypresses, managed by a forestry department.
   - Controlling surface scum (natas) by manual removal or a simple mechanical skimmer that would not remove fish.

**Water conditions.** Water temperature at the spring was 24 °C. Downstream it fell to 22 °C in winter and rose to 25 °C in summer. Maximum salinity was 2‰ at all observations. pH varied between 6.8 and 7.0 without a regular pattern, and conductivity ranged from about 2,700 to 3,000 for most readings. These conditions were maintained after the program ended. Table 1 summarizes parameters before, during and after the program. **Water quality.** Habitat improved clearly, and the restored areas gave better protection and breeding and feeding grounds to the platy. Scum production fell, but no reliable method was found to quantify water quality improvement.

## Key Findings

- **Platy recovery.** The [[Platy Apodaca]] population increased from 700 to about 2,000 individuals, including more than 300 from the downstream pools. Scum mats and aquatic vegetation were found to be the main refuge and feeding areas for platy fry. The author recommends keeping scum except around the water intake, because it offers better protection.
- **Guppy and predator control.** The exotic [[Poecilia Reticulata]] remained under control, but the author stresses that this work must be permanent. [[Gambusia Speciosa]] also stayed at low numbers, and a natural balance with other native species was expected.
- **Water lily restoration.** *Nymphaea* increased from 10 to 95 individuals, with about 50 surviving. Plants placed within 1 m of the intake on black mud failed through rotting. Plants in the minor canal had 50% success, and all plants in the major canal survived. Cover increased by about 50% overall. The author recommends more planting near the intake.
- **Riparian trees.** Tree planting had not yet been carried out, as responsibility lay with another government department. The author expects shade to reduce algae and scum, but the full effect would only be measurable in 5 years or more.
- **Scum removal problem.** Attempts at skimming removed large numbers of fry and some adult platies. This work was suspended until a method could remove scum without harming the platy. No mechanical solution had been found, so removal by hand (searching the mats for each fry) was the only option. The source considers this too slow and tedious for non-biologist workers.
- **Water quality.** Habitat quality improved, but water chemistry changed little. Reported values were broadly stable: temperature 22–26 °C, pH 6.6–7.0, and conductivity about 2,600–3,000.
- **Transplant limitation.** Because no other known population of *Nymphaea multituberculata* exists in Mexico, recovery of this species depends on natural reproduction. The transplant method was still being refined.
