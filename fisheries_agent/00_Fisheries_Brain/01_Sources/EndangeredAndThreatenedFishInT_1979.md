## Executive Summary

This 1976 study, conducted by the Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit, documents the status of four endemic and endangered/threatened fish species in the Yampa and Green Rivers within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]]. These species, once widespread throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], have declined precipitously due to river regulation—primarily the construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (completed 1963)—which altered flow regimes, temperature, and turbidity, and facilitated the establishment of competing non-native fish. The [[Yampa River]], remaining largely free-flowing and warmwater, is identified as a critical refuge that ameliorates the cold, regulated Green River downstream of their confluence, allowing continued (though diminished) persistence of these native fish. Evidence of successful reproduction was found to be sparse to absent for most species, with only large, aging adults commonly captured, raising serious concern about long-term population viability.

## Background & Description

- **Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** Largest native cyprinid of the basin; a top piscivorous predator. Fish as small as 50 mm total length (TL) had already begun eating fish; by 200 mm TL, fish dominate the diet almost exclusively. Sexual maturity occurs at a minimum TL of ~420 mm, at approximately age VII. Historically attained much larger body sizes than observed in the contemporary population, suggesting possible declines in longevity, growth rate, or both.
- **Humpback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Distinctive "humpbacked" body form. Known to hybridize with the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — 8 hybrid specimens collected during 1974–76 — and, in the lower basin, with the introduced [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*). Hybridization incidence appears to be increasing.
- **Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*):** A relatively recently described species (Miller 1946), never documented as historically abundant. Possible hybrids with other *Gila* species were noted among specimens collected during this study.
- **Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*):** Closely related to the more common [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*); historically more numerous than roundtail chub in the 1959–1961 year classes, but has since become nearly extirpated from the study area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Zonation within the study area** — four distinct habitat types were identified:
1. **Green River above Echo Park** – Coldwater environment artificially controlled by Flaming Gorge Dam.
2. **Yampa River** – Free-flowing warmwater river; primary spawning/refuge habitat for endemic fish.
3. **Green River below the Monument** – Warmwater, largely recovered from dam effects; extends to confluence with the Colorado River.
4. **Green River between Echo Park and lower Monument boundary** – Transitional gradient zone (~42 km) from cold- to warmwater conditions.

**Species-specific ecology:**
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]:** Adults migrate into the lower Yampa River in July–August once water temperatures reach 20–21°C, presumably for spawning; congregate in shallow eddies (males) while presumed spawning females occupy deeper water. High spring flows in the Yampa provide critical habitat and food; as the river recedes in late summer, fish return to the Green River. Some may overwinter in deep, ice-covered pools of the Yampa.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]]:** Found primarily in quiet water except during spring spawning, when they congregate over gravel bars in swift water. Key locations: mouth of the Yampa River (spring/fall) and a flooded gravel pit at the Walter Walker Wildlife Area near Grand Junction, CO. One tagged female traveled 21 km upstream (Island Park to Echo Park) in 3 weeks. Spawning bars comprised large cobble at 0.5–1 m depth with ~1 m/s water velocity.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]:** Typically found in eddies adjacent to fast currents and near deep pools/glides (4–5 m depth).
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]:** Historically abundant but now nearly absent from the Monument; occasional individuals persist in the lower basin (e.g., Lake Mohave, Arizona).

**Threats outlined:**
- **Stream alteration** — dams (flow stabilization, thermal and turbidity changes, physical migration barriers, reservoir habitat unsuitability), irrigation-induced salinization, dewatering, channelization, and unstable banks from grazing/development.
- **Biotic interactions** — competition and predation from introduced species (e.g., [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], [[Red Shiner]]).
- **Pollution/eutrophication** — industrial, agricultural, and municipal waste inputs affecting salinity and sedimentation.
- **Other factors** — introduced parasites, shifts in food organism composition, and heavy angling pressure on large, vulnerable adult squawfish and chubs.

## Key Findings

- Native fish comprise only ~34–40% of species recorded in the upper Colorado River system; introduced species now dominate (60–66%).
- All 10 native and 15 of 19 introduced species documented by Holden (1973) were reconfirmed in the lower Yampa River during 1974–76, **except** the bonytail chub, which was not recollected.
- Three new introduced species were documented in the Yampa River during this study: [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus kansae*), [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*), and [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*).
- The Green River below the Monument showed different species composition than the Yampa, with centrarchids, walleye, and [[Red Shiner]] more common — and juvenile Colorado squawfish (~75 mm TL) were captured at Jensen, Utah, suggesting successful reproduction occurs downstream of the Monument but not upstream.
- Colorado squawfish reproduction has declined steadily since the 1960s: strong year classes in 1959, 1961, 1963–64 (and possibly 1966) versus weak years in 1962 and 1965 (275 young-of-year in 1964, 42 in 1965, 560 in 1966 per Vanicek 1967). By the 1970s, juveniles were rare or absent in most survey years.
- No ripe female Colorado squawfish were definitively documented, though suspected spent females and one angler-reported ripe female were noted near the Little Snake River confluence.
- Captive spawning of Colorado squawfish was achieved at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (AZ) via hormone injection, with eggs deposited on gravel substrate — but natural spawning has never been directly observed in turbid river conditions.
- Humpback sucker spawning migrations documented up to 21 km upstream in the Yampa (Echo Park), with historical records up to 18 km further (Castle Park). No young-of-year or juvenile humpback suckers were captured despite repeated sampling, indicating a persistent lack of documented recruitment.
- Hybridization between humpback sucker and flannelmouth sucker appears to be increasing, likely due to relative abundance imbalance and shared spawning requirements — a genetic introgression concern for the rarer parent species.
- The bonytail chub is assessed as nearing extinction within the study area, with the roundtail chub (a close congener) remaining common by contrast.
- The paper emphasizes that defining "critical habitat" under the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] is complicated for river fish because upstream alterations (temperature, flow, dissolved oxygen) can affect populations far downstream, and specific streamflow requirements for these endemic species remain poorly understood.
- The Yampa River is identified as ecologically critical not only as direct habitat but for its role in ameliorating (warming, re-turbiding) the regulated Green River downstream of their confluence — any future alteration to the Yampa or its tributaries could undermine this buffering effect and jeopardize regional persistence of all four species.
