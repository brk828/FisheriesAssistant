# Adaptations of fishes in arid Australia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by C.J.M. Glover of the [[South Australian Museum]], summarizes a decade of work on the fishes of arid central Australia, covering the Western Plateau and [[Lake Eyre Drainage]] divisions. Fish occurrence in the region is governed mainly by water permanence, temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen. Most species survive through physiological and behavioural tolerance, and [[Floodwater Dispersal]] is the main means by which populations are connected. The paper uses [[Desert Goby]] as a detailed case study of a non-specialized, highly adaptable species.

## Background & Description

The study area, arid Australia, comprises the central drainage divisions where aridity is most extreme (see [[Australian Water Resources Council]] 1976 mapping). Surface waters range from ephemeral to permanent. Ephemeral waters include scattered waterholes and rivers that flow only after seasonal rain. Permanent waters include [[Artesian Springs]], artesian bores, deep waterholes, and man-made dams and tanks.

The fauna comprises 13 families, 19 genera and about thirty species, most of which also occur outside the central drainages. The most widespread and successful species are:

- [[Lake Eyre Hardyhead]] (*Craterocephalus eyresii*)
- [[Spangled Perch]] (*Leiopotherapon unicolor*)
- [[Bony Bream]] (*Nematolosa erebi*)
- [[Desert Goby]] (*Chlamydogobius eremius*), the only fish endemic to arid Australia that is studied in detail
- [[Mosquito Fish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), an introduced species
- *Neosilurus* catfishes

Families best represented include the Plotosidae (catfishes) and Terapontidae (grunters or perches). Of the species, at least five are considered adaptable forms, and four are not indigenous to Australia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and permanence.** Water temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen are the parameters most closely tied to fish occurrence. Most fresh water is of direct atmospheric origin and ephemeral. Permanent waters are more chemico-physically stable, although shallow or slow-flowing artesian waters are not. Most artesian waters are inherently saline and warm. The less diverse fauna of the Western Plateau is attributed largely to less permanent water.

**Temperature regimes.**
- Non-artesian surface waters: winter 12–19°C (shallows sometimes below 10°C overnight); summer 25–30°C (shallows near 40°C in daylight).
- Artesian outflows: often about 30°C, sometimes approaching 100°C, and sometimes cooler. Outflow temperature stays constant.
- Artesian streams: winter 12–30°C; summer 25–40°C. One monitored spring had a mean winter–summer range of 21°C.
- Lateral gradients reached up to 25°C over seven metres in summer. Vertical gradients between warm surface and cooler sub-bed silt approached 10°C.

**Salinity.**
- Artesian waters: commonly 3–6 ‰, occasionally above 8 ‰.
- Large non-artesian waters: usually below 1 ‰.
- Ephemeral shallows of partly dried waterholes: higher, up to 100 ‰ or more.
- Salt-lakes such as Lake Eyre are invariably hypersaline.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Levels range from 0.6 to more than 15.0 mg O₂/L. Artesian waters typically show 6.0–9.0 mg O₂/L (100–140% saturation). Non-artesian waters are usually below 7.6 mg O₂/L.

**Barriers and habitat use.** Salinity and thermal barriers restrict some species to certain waters or sections of water, at least occasionally. Partial selective action may keep certain species apart, reducing competition and predation. Most species show a preference for one habitat type, even where they occupy several.

## Key Findings

**1. Tolerance ranges.** About a quarter of the listed species have been tested for tolerance to temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen.
- All examined species have an upper thermal limit above 30°C, and some survive brief exposure above 40°C.
- Nearly all tolerate temperatures below 15°C, and at least half have a lower limit below 10°C.
- All tolerate salinities of at least 5 ‰. The Lake Eyre Hardyhead survives 110 ‰.
- No species required more than 6 mg O₂/L as a minimum. Most tolerated about 1 mg O₂/L or less.

**2. Dispersal.** Floodwaters are the principal means of fish dispersal. Aerial dispersal is considered doubtful. Lake Eyre is probably the essential transfer point between basins, because species unable to tolerate its high-salinity floods may be excluded from connected basins.

**3. Salinity-driven fish kills.** Fish kills in Lake Eyre correlate with rising salinity, and salinity therefore acts as a temporary, selective barrier to movement. Field observations at Madigan Gulf (Price Peninsula) show the pattern:

| Date | Total salinity (‰) | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Sept 1974 | 19.0 | Live fish netted in abundance |
| 10 Dec 1974 | 30.5 | Live fish netted; some stranded dead fish |
| 25 Mar 1975 | 52.0 | No fish netted; many dead fish on shoreline |
| 1 Dec 1975 | 80.0 | No live fish netted or sighted; many dead fish |

**4. Thermal restriction of endemics.** [[Dalhousie Hardyhead]] and [[Dalhousie Catfish]] (*Neosilurus* sp. nov.) are endemic to warm artesian waters at [[Dalhousie Springs]]. They appear unable to survive temperatures much below 20°C, which dispersing floodwaters can reach. Such lower thermal barriers are considered exceptional. No upper thermal barrier was found to limit the overall distribution of any fish.

**5. Desert goby case study.** The desert goby (*Chlamydogobius eremius*) is the best-studied native species in the region. Reported adaptations include:
- Survival of up to 27 days in distilled water, and more than 60 days in seawater above 37 ‰.
- Temperature tolerance of at least 5–40°C, with rapid acclimation to sudden changes.
- Thermal refuges in cooler vegetated shallows or buried in cooler silt.
- Emergence from water as a last resort for evaporative cooling.
- Tolerance of dissolved oxygen below 5 mg O₂/L, and temporarily below 1 mg O₂/L.
- Use of algal mats as a respiratory refuge, and aerial respiration as a last resort.
- An omnivorous, catholic diet of flora and microfauna present in artesian waters.

**6. Non-specialized ecology.** The desert goby's success is attributed to a non-specialized ecology. Its morphological uniformity across the range suggests recent initial dispersal and/or sufficient gene flow. Establishment in artesian bores and other artificial waters since European settlement suggests an efficient floodwater dispersal mechanism.

**7. Conclusions.** Much of the fauna is highly adaptable, which suits the region's variable aquatic environment. The morphological uniformity of other widespread species also suggests that their success depends on gene flow carried by floodwaters. The two endemic Dalhousie species, by contrast, occupy an exceptionally stable and permanent environment.
