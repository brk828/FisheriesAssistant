# Adaptations of Fishes in Arid Australia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by C.J.M. Glover, of the South Australian Museum, Adelaide, reviews how fishes survive in the inland waters of arid Australia. Arid Australia's surface waters are ephemeral or semi-permanent and vary widely in temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen. The paper argues that the region's fish fauna is composed of tolerant, non-specialized species, with the [[Desert Goby]] (*Chlamydogobius eremius*) serving as the main case study. Dispersal by [[Floodwaters]] is presented as the key process that maintains gene flow and distribution across the region. The paper also notes that [[Salinity]] and thermal barriers appear to restrict some endemic species but not most widespread ones.

## Background & Description

- **Study region:** Arid Australia, with emphasis on the [[Lake Eyre Basin]] and the central drainage divisions (Lake Eyre, Diamantina, Georgina, Todd River and Finke River systems, among others).
- **Water types:** Surface waters are largely scattered waterholes and creeks that fill after seasonal rains. Permanent habitats include waterholes associated with [[Artesian Springs]], artesian bores, reservoirs and tanks. Artesian sources are especially important because they are stable and host the most specialised fauna.
- **Data basis:** The paper summarises the author's own field studies and unpublished data, alongside earlier work by other authors. Most fish-environment studies in the region were conducted in the decades before publication.
- **Fish fauna:** The region's fish fauna is described as relatively small and dominated by a few families. The text reports 13 families and 19 genera, with some species still undescribed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physicochemical regimes**

- **Salinity:** Permanent waters are often more saline, with artesian waters commonly in the range of 6.0 to 9.0 and sometimes higher in some sources. Ephemeral floodwaters are generally fresh, while some lakes and shallow sources are strongly saline.
- **Temperature:** Surface waters in winter are commonly recorded at 12 to 19°C. In summer, shallow waters may rise sharply in temperature, while thermal artesian springs remain much warmer and nearly constant (about 25 to 30°C). Winter temperatures are lower in non-artesian waters.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Oxygen levels are often low in artesian waters, and many fishes tolerate periods of hypoxia.
- **Hydrological instability:** Shallow, ephemeral waters can dry out and then refill. This instability shapes the temporal patterns of fish occurrence and dispersal.

**Community patterns**

The paper highlights that species' distributions are shaped by environmental gradients, competition and predation. Few fish species are strictly confined to a single habitat type. Most species show a preference for either artesian, floodwater or lake habitats, but many inhabit all types. Temperature is a limiting factor for only some species, such as [[Ambassis]] and [[Mosquitofish]], which occupy the warmer sections of springs and bores.

**Dispersal and barriers**

Fish dispersal in this region relies heavily on floodwaters. The Lake Eyre drainage is described as having a restricted connection to other basins. Periodic fish kills in Lake Eyre, linked to rises in salinity, demonstrate that salinity can act as a temporary and selective barrier to movement.

## Key Findings

1. **Tolerance is the central adaptive strategy.** [[Chlamydogobius eremius]] tolerates a very wide range of conditions, including:
   - up to 27 days of immersion in distilled water;
   - periods exceeding a number of days in seawater above 37‰ salinity (the exact figure is garbled in the source text);
   - temperatures from about 5°C to 40°C, at least temporarily;
   - dissolved oxygen below 5 mg O₂/l, with short-term tolerance of much lower levels.

2. **Behavioural and physiological adaptations include:**
   - using thermal refuges created by steep lateral and vertical gradients, especially in summer;
   - aggregating in cooler vegetation or shallows, or burying in cooler substrate;
   - emerging from water as a last resort to reduce temperature through evaporative cooling;
   - using photosynthesising algal mats as a local oxygen source;
   - using aerial respiration as a last resort.

3. **Diet and ecology:** The desert goby is omnivorous and opportunistic, feeding on flora and microfauna common in arid Australian surface waters. The paper argues that its non-specialised ecology allows survival in habitats outside artesian waters, including floodwaters.

4. **Dispersal drives genetic uniformity.** The morphological uniformity of the desert goby across its range suggests either recent dispersal or sufficient gene flow to prevent divergence. Successful colonisation of artesian bores and other artificial waters since European settlement indicates that efficient dispersal by floodwaters operates across much of arid Australia.

5. **Thermal barriers are limited.** Two endemic species, [[Craterocephalus Dalhousiensis]] and [[Neosilurus]] sp. nov., are restricted to warm artesian waters at [[Dalhousie Springs]], apparently because they cannot tolerate temperatures much below 20°C. Other species probably tolerate temperatures down to about 10°C, and no evidence shows that upper thermal limits restrict their overall distributions. Chemical regimes are also similar across the region, so they do not appear to impose a strong barrier.

6. **Salinity acts as a selective barrier.** Periodic fish kills in [[Lake Eyre]] show that salinity rises can temporarily exclude some species. Species absent from or restricted to basins not draining into Lake Eyre may also be denied access to it.

7. **Sampling record.** Records from September 1974 to December 1975 at Lake Eyre show a sequence of mass strandings, die-offs and live catches of species including [[Bony Bream]], [[Glassfish]] and [[Smelt]]. Water salinity was measured at each sampling date, with values ranging up to about 52 in some instances (as presented in the source table).

8. **Research gaps.** Detailed ecological studies of arid central Australian fishes began only recently. The author notes that many species remain undescribed, and that the taxonomy and distribution of the region's fauna are incomplete.
