# Vertical Distribution of Fishes Relative to Physical, Chemical and Biological Features in Two Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #reservoir-fisheries #centrarchidae #clupeidae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis investigated the vertical distribution of fishes in [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]], two contiguous reservoirs on the [[Salt River]] in central Arizona, during 1970-1971. Using vertical gill nets at multiple transects, the study examined fish depth distributions relative to physical-chemical parameters (temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, pH, turbidity, light penetration) and biological factors (chlorophyll a, net plankton, species interactions). Despite high variability and often small sample sizes, multiple linear regression analysis identified dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll a, net plankton, and to a lesser extent temperature as important factors influencing fish dispersion patterns. The majority of all fishes (>60%) were captured within the upper 10 meters of the water column in both reservoirs, regardless of thermocline depth. Vertical light penetration (depth of euphotic zone) appeared to act indirectly through its influence on phytoplankton and zooplankton distributions, which in turn affected forage fish ([[Threadfin Shad]]) and their predators. The study documented predator-prey relationships and food-chain dynamics across multiple trophic levels.

## Background & Description

The [[Salt River]] system in central Arizona contains four contiguous reservoirs built in the early 1900s for irrigation, industrial, and domestic water supply. [[Roosevelt Lake]] (completed 1911) is the largest and uppermost, impounding a relatively broad valley at the confluence of the Salt River and Tonto Creek. [[Apache Lake]] (completed 1927) lies immediately downstream in a narrow, steep-walled canyon. The reservoirs differ markedly in morphology: Roosevelt has mean width of 2.14 km versus 0.4 km for Apache; Roosevelt volume is nearly six times that of Apache (170.4 × 10⁷ m³ vs. 30.2 × 10⁷ m³); basin slope is 7.0% in Roosevelt versus 32.0% in Apache (Table 1).

Both reservoirs are warm monomictic lakes—temperatures never fall below 4.0°C at any depth, with circulation during winter and direct stratification in summer. Maximum depths at full pool are 62.5 m (Roosevelt) and 76.2 m (Apache), though actual depths varied during the study (Table 2). The reservoirs support important commercial and game fisheries and are heavily utilized for recreation.

Sampling was conducted during five periods (runs): August 1970, November 1970, March-April 1971, August 1971, and November 1971. Four transects were established on Roosevelt (R-1 through R-4) with 3-4 net sites each; five transects on Apache (A-1 through A-5) with 1-3 net sites each. Apache Lake was essentially drained during run #5 for dam maintenance work.

**Sampling Methodology**: White nylon vertical gill nets of three mesh sizes (1.27 cm, 3.81 cm, 7.62 cm bar measure) were deployed as "gangs" at each site. Nets ranged from 10-60 m in length depending on water depth, were dyed at meter intervals for depth identification, and were typically fished for a minimum of three nights per site. A [[Hydrolab II-B]] measured temperature, dissolved oxygen, specific conductivity, and pH at meter intervals (0-20 m) and 10-m intervals thereafter. Submarine photometer measured vertical light penetration. [[Chlorophyll A]] was extracted from filtered water samples using 95% acetone. Net plankton was collected with a [[Clarke-Bumpus Plankton Sampler]] at surface, 3.5, 7.0, and 14.0 m depths.

**Fish Species**: Thirteen species were captured (Table 4), dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) which comprised over two-thirds of all fishes netted (1,490 from Roosevelt, 802 from Apache). Other important species included [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), and [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal Regime**: Both reservoirs exhibited typical warm monomictic thermal profiles with summer stratification and winter circulation (Figure 3). The metalimnion formed at 10-15 m depth in Roosevelt versus 5-7 m in Apache, reflecting differences in basin morphology and wind exposure. Roosevelt's broader, more open configuration subjected it to greater wind mixing from summer thunderstorms, deepening the mixed layer. Metalimnion temperatures ranged 21.0-23.4°C in both lakes. During run #4, Apache displayed isothermal conditions due to hypolimnetic drainage through deep penstocks, removing cooler bottom waters and leaving only warm epilimnion.

**Dissolved Oxygen**: Oxygen profiles closely followed seasonal temperature patterns (Figure 4). Hypolimnetic waters became severely oxygen-deficient (<1.0 mg/L) during summer stratification in both reservoirs. During winter homothermy, oxygen concentrations remained elevated throughout the water column. The depth of rapid oxygen decline was more similar between lakes than thermocline depths. The study adopted 2.0 mg/L as the lower limit for fish distribution based on [[Eley Et Al 1967]] work on similar reservoir species, contrasting with [[Ellis 1937]]'s 5.0 mg/L threshold for riverine habitats.

**Light Penetration & Turbidity**: The reservoirs differed markedly in water clarity. Apache had mean euphotic zone depth of 9.2 m versus 5.4 m in Roosevelt. Roosevelt's greater turbidity resulted from direct inflow from Salt River and Tonto Creek, functioning as a settling basin, plus greater wind-driven resuspension due to larger surface area. Turbidity values were consistently higher in Roosevelt, measured in Jackson Turbidity Units (JTU).

**Water Chemistry**: Specific conductance ranged 1,031-3,380 micromhos/cm, equivalent to 500-1,800 mg/L total dissolved solids, varying spatially and temporally. pH ranged 6.7-9.4, within the 5.0-9.0 range considered suitable for adult freshwater fishes ([[Doudoroff 1957]]). These parameters showed greater horizontal than vertical variation and were not considered limiting factors for fish distribution in the upper 10 m.

**Biological Productivity**: [[Chlorophyll A]] concentrations indicated phytoplankton abundance and nutrient availability. Net plankton (primarily zooplankton) wet weights were obtained from split samples. Both parameters showed highest concentrations in upper water column, directly related to euphotic zone depth. [[Haskell 1959]] and [[Baker And Schmitz 1971]] documented threadfin shad as pelagic plankton feeders utilizing diatoms, establishing the trophic link between primary production and forage fish abundance.

## Key Findings

**Overall Depth Distribution**: The majority of all fishes netted (>60% in Roosevelt, >68% in Apache) occurred within the upper 10 meters of the water column across all sampling periods, regardless of season or thermocline depth. This pattern was remarkably consistent between the morphologically distinct reservoirs. Correlation coefficients for numbers of fishes by depth between reservoirs increased from r=0.36 (run #1) to r=0.88 (run #4), indicating increasingly similar vertical distribution patterns as the study progressed.

**Species-Specific Distributions**:

*Threadfin Shad* (n=2,292 total): Mean capture depth 4.2 m (Roosevelt), 5.2 m (Apache). During run #1 in Roosevelt, shad displayed bimodal distribution with peaks at 3 m and 7-9 m; 99% were above thermocline. In Apache, single peak at 7-9 m with only 30% above thermocline. During summer de-oxygenation, <1% of Roosevelt shad and ~16% of Apache shad were captured in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen. Approximately 73% (Apache) and 61% (Roosevelt) of shad were netted within the euphotic zone. Multiple linear regression analysis (MLRP) indicated temperature and dissolved oxygen as most frequently correlated factors, though variance explained was low (6-36%). Biological MLRP showed significant correlations with predator species (bass, catfish) and food sources (chlorophyll a, net plankton), suggesting food-chain relationships.

*Largemouth Bass* (n=259 total): Mean capture depth 5.8 m (Roosevelt), 6.7 m (Apache). Ranked second in abundance. During all runs, 98-100% of Roosevelt bass were above thermocline; approximately half of Apache bass were below thermocline despite both populations occupying similar absolute depths (<10 m). Ten percent of Roosevelt bass and 20% of Apache bass were captured in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen during summer stratification. Approximately 58.5% (Roosevelt) and 54.1% (Apache) were above euphotic zone depth. MLRP analysis showed temperature most consistently correlated (though variance explained remained low, 8-11%). Biological comparisons showed close positive association with shad (run #4, Roosevelt) and with carp and catfish (runs #3-4).

*Carp* (n=138 total): Mean capture depth 7.7 m (Roosevelt), 11.6 m (Apache)—the deepest average of any species. Carp was the only species occurring in greater numbers below the euphotic zone limit in both reservoirs (27.9% within euphotic zone in Roosevelt, 41.6% in Apache), reflecting bottom-feeding habits. Stomach contents included clams (*Corbicula maniliensis*). Approximately 30% of Roosevelt carp and 21% of Apache carp were captured in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen. Carp displayed widest depth distribution of all species, particularly in Apache, consistent with [[Dendy 1945]] findings for Norris Reservoir. Non-biological MLRP yielded very low variance explained; biological comparisons showed relatively high values (48-63%) with significant correlations to black crappie, bass, and sunfish.

*Channel Catfish* (n=81 total): Mean capture depth 7.7 m (Roosevelt), 6.7 m (Apache). All Roosevelt catfish during summer runs were above thermocline. Approximately 20% of Roosevelt catfish were captured in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen; none from Apache in such conditions. About 60.9% (Roosevelt) and 57.1% (Apache) were netted in euphotic zone. MLRP showed high variance explained (run #4, Apache) with temperature as primary correlate. Biological comparisons indicated high positive correlation with shad (runs #2, #4 in Apache), suggesting predator-prey relationship.

*Sunfish (Lepomis spp.)* (n=96 total): Mean capture depth 4.8 m (Roosevelt), 5.4 m (Apache). [[Bluegill]] dominated this group. Nineteen of 20 specimens from Roosevelt during summer runs were above thermocline. Only four specimens were taken in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen (all from Roosevelt, run #1). Approximately 64% (Roosevelt) and 56.2% (Apache) were captured within euphotic zone. MLRP showed highest variance explained during run #4 in both reservoirs, with positive correlation to water temperature in Apache. Biological analysis suggested correlation with shad (run #4, Apache), likely reflecting shared habitat preference for upper water column rather than direct trophic interaction.

*Black Crappie* (n=112 total): Abundant in Roosevelt (third-ranked species), virtually absent from Apache (n=2). Mean capture depth 3.4 m—shallowest of all species. All specimens captured above thermocline during summer stratification. Only 4 of 12 crappie taken during summer runs were in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen, consistent with [[Dendy 1945]] observation that black crappie avoid concentrations <1.5 mg/L. Approximately 77% were collected within euphotic zone. Biological MLRP showed significant correlation with threadfin shad (run #3, Roosevelt), indicating predator-prey relationship.

*Bigmouth Buffalo* (n=41 total): Mean capture depth 6.2 m (Roosevelt), 7.8 m (Apache). Second-deepest average depth after carp, reflecting bottom-feeding habits ([[Minckley Et Al 1970]]). All Roosevelt specimens above thermocline during summer. During run #4, 50% (Roosevelt) and 20% (Apache) were in water <2.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen. Approximately 33% (Roosevelt) and 64.3% (Apache) captured within euphotic zone. Insufficient data precluded statistical analysis. Absence of smallmouth and black buffalo from Roosevelt likely relates to past drought and reservoir desiccation ([[Johnson And Minckley 1972]]).

**Environmental Correlates—Multiple Linear Regression Results**:

*Non-biological parameters*: Despite statistical significance in some cases, variance explained by temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, pH, and turbidity was generally low (typically <20%). Temperature showed inconsistent correlations—negative with shad in Roosevelt run #1, positive in run #3. Dissolved oxygen appeared more consistently important but still explained limited variance. These parameters appeared to set outer limits on distribution rather than determine specific depth preferences.

*Biological parameters*: MLRP comparisons yielded higher variance explained (up to 96% for catfish-shad correlation in Apache run #4). In 9 of 21 cases where correlation was established at 90% or 95% confidence level, food-chain relationships were indicated. Significant correlations included: (1) shad with predators (bass, catfish, black crappie); (2) shad with food sources (chlorophyll a, net plankton); (3) bass with shad; (4) catfish with shad; (5) black crappie with shad and chlorophyll a.

**Thermocline vs. Fish Distribution**: The study revealed a surprising disconnect between thermocline depth and fish distribution. In Roosevelt, virtually all species occurred above the thermocline (10-15 m depth). In Apache, the majority of fish species were found below the thermocline (5-7 m depth). However, both populations occupied the same absolute depth range (upper 10 m), demonstrating that thermocline position per se was not the primary determinant of vertical distribution. During winter homothermy, all species dispersed to greater depths, attributable to increased dissolved oxygen at previously anoxic depths, though the entire oxygenated water column was not fully utilized.

**Euphotic Zone as Indirect Limiting Factor**: The depth of the euphotic zone emerged as the most consistent correlate with fish distribution, with ~60% (Roosevelt) and ~68% (Apache) of all fishes captured within this zone. Since most fish were caught at night, light could not have acted directly. The proposed mechanism operates through trophic cascades: (1) euphotic zone depth determines vertical extent of net primary production (phytoplankton); (2) zooplankton distributions track phytoplankton; (3) planktivorous threadfin shad concentrate where plankton is abundant; (4) predatory species (bass, catfish, crappie) concentrate where forage fish occur. This indirect effect of light penetration through food web structure appeared more important than direct effects of temperature or dissolved oxygen within the habitable zone.

**Reservoir Comparison—Convergent Patterns**: Despite marked morphological differences (Roosevelt: broad, open, shallow thermocline; Apache: narrow, canyon-like, deep thermocline), fish distributions converged on similar patterns. Correlation coefficients for fish numbers by depth increased from r=0.36 (run #1) to r=0.88 (run #4). The low initial correlation resulted from different shad distribution patterns (bimodal in Roosevelt vs. unimodal in Apache), but both peaks in Roosevelt were still within the upper 10 m. Average depths of capture were remarkably similar between reservoirs for all species except carp. This convergence suggests that absolute depth and associated biological factors (food availability) were more important than reservoir-specific physical features.

**Seasonal Patterns**: Winter circulation periods (runs #2, #5) showed deeper fish distributions than summer stratification periods (runs #1, #4), attributable to increased dissolved oxygen at depth. However, even during circulation, the upper 10 m contained the majority of fishes, indicating factors beyond oxygen availability were limiting. Spring sampling (run #3) showed intermediate patterns. The consistency of upper-water-column occupancy across seasons reinforces the importance of biological factors (food web structure) over purely physical-chemical parameters.

**Predator-Prey Relationships**: Multiple lines of evidence documented trophic interactions: (1) MLRP analyses showing significant correlations between predators and prey (bass-shad, catfish-shad, crappie-shad); (2) similar depth distributions of predators and forage species; (3) stomach content data (carp with clams, indicating benthic feeding; shad as plankton feeders per [[Haskell 1959]]); (4) temporal and spatial co-occurrence patterns. These relationships appeared more important in determining fish distributions than physical-chemical factors alone.
