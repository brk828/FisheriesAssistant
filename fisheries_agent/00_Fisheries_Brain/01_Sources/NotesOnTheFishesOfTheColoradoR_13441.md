# Notes on the Fishes of the Colorado River Near Moab, Utah

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This two-year limnological survey (1962–1964) of the [[Colorado River]] near [[Moab, Utah]], extending 25 miles downstream to [[Dead Horse Point]], documented **2,785 fish specimens** representing **5 families, 13 genera, and 16 species**. The study, funded by the Texas Gulf Sulphur Company (Potash Division), provides baseline distributional and dietary data for both native Colorado River basin fishes (e.g., [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]]) and introduced species (e.g., [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]]). Notably, the paper reports the **first published Utah record of the [[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*), which comprised ~45% of the total catch, and the **first record of the crayfish [[Orconectes virilis]]** in this river section, an important food item for larger predatory fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning**: Most native suckers ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]]) and cyprinids favored quiet backwaters, tributary wash mouths, and gravel-bottomed shallows, while the swift-water-adapted [[Bonytail]] was difficult to sample effectively with seines, suggesting a main-channel/riffle affinity underrepresented in the catch.
- **Turbidity tolerance**: [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] appear well-adapted to the highly turbid conditions of the Colorado River, both spawning abundantly in quiet backwaters and tributary washes during late spring through summer (June–July); both species survived >18 months in laboratory aquaria.
- **Diet spectrum**:
  - Algae/detritus feeders: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Mountain Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]] (algae + bottom ooze), [[Carp]] (amorphous organic matter), [[Fathead Minnow]] (bottom ooze + algae).
  - Invertivores: [[Speckled Dace]] (green algae *Cladophora* + tendipedid larvae), [[Rio Grande Killifish]] (aquatic insects), [[Red Shiner]] (algae, protozoans, insects, crustaceans), [[Largemouth Bass]] (aquatic insects).
  - Piscivores/omnivores: [[Bonytail]] (small [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]]), [[Green Sunfish]] (strong preference for minnows), [[Black Bullhead]] (killifish, [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], insects, detritus, seeds), [[Channel Catfish]] (37 stomachs analyzed: 8% minnows, 45% organic detritus, 41% [[Orconectes virilis]] crayfish).
  - Juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] ingested organic detritus; largest individuals sampled had empty stomachs.
- **Predator-prey link**: [[Rio Grande Killifish]] frequently found in stomachs of catfish, sunfish, and other piscivores, indicating a key forage role.
- **Invertebrate community**: The crayfish [[Orconectes virilis]], native to the Great Lakes/Mississippi drainages and preferring lotic (riverine) over lentic habitats, was verified as a first record for this Colorado River section and constitutes a major dietary component for larger fishes near Moab.

## Key Findings

- Total of **2,785 specimens** across **16 species, 13 genera, 5 families** collected over the 1962–1964 study period.
- **First published Utah record** of the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), a bait-fish introduction to the lower Colorado River (per La Rivers 1962; Miller 1952) that became the dominant species in the collection (~45% of total catch, 1,251 individuals).
- **First record** of the crayfish [[Orconectes virilis]] in this stretch of the Colorado River, verified by H. H. Hobbs Jr. (U.S. National Museum).
- [[Fathead Minnow]] first reported for Utah via a 1958 Glen Canyon survey (Smith 1959); found abundant and reproductively active in the Moab reach.
- Native basin fishes ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Mountain Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Speckled Dace]]) co-occur with several long-established introduced species ([[Carp]] since 1881, [[Black Bullhead]] since 1914 via Utah Lake, [[Largemouth Bass]] since 1890).
- [[Bluegill]] appears poorly suited to the turbid Colorado River habitat, with only a single specimen collected and an empty stomach.
- Sampling limitations (seining) likely undersampled swift-water species such as adult [[Bonytail]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Findings collectively document an early-1960s baseline of native/introduced species coexistence in the Moab reach, useful for tracking subsequent declines of endemic big-river fishes (e.g., [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]]) later listed under conservation concern.
