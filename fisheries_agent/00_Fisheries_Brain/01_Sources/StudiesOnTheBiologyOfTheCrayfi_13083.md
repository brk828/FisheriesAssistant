# Life History and Biology of the Crayfish *Cambarus propinquus* in Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1937 monograph by William Carl Van Deventer, published in the *Illinois Biological Monographs* (Vol. XV, No. 3), presents a two-year field study (June 1932 to June 1934) of the life cycle of [[Cambarus Propinquus]], a crayfish common in small streams of eastern Illinois. Because laboratory studies were judged unrepresentative, the work relied on repeated field collections, graded by cephalothorax length and tracked as size-group modes through time. The study documents a warm-water-type life cycle with spring egg laying, a short brood-attachment period, six to ten molts in the first season, and widespread natural death of yearling adults after reproduction. It also reports that most individuals produce a single brood, and that the species is diurnally active under sunlight, contrary to prevailing textbook accounts.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** [[Cambarus Propinquus]] Girard, a freshwater decapod of the family Cambaridae (crayfish). It is named from specimens described from Lake Ontario and western New York in 1852.
- **Morphology used for measurement:** The cephalothorax length (tip of rostrum to anterior end of abdomen) was chosen as the index of size. It equals about 49% of total body length after the young become free-swimming, and the author treated it as one-half of total length for comparison with older literature.
- **Sexual forms:** Adult males exhibit a "first form" (hard, pointed copulatory appendages) and a "second form" (softened, blunt appendages) tied to molt cycles. The author argues that juvenile and adult second forms are only partially equivalent.
- **Taxonomic context:** Ortmann's (1905, 1906) hypothesis places [[Cambarus Propinquus]] with *C. propinquus sanbornii* and *C. obscurus* as a closely related group, derived from three tributaries of a pre-glacial river (the "Erigan").
- **Historical background:** The review traces earlier crayfish life-history work by [[Andrews]] (C. affinis), [[Ortmann]] (C. obscurus), [[Steele]] (C. virilis), [[Smith]] (unpublished Urbana and Michigan records), and [[Creaser]] (Michigan population), and notes that textbook generalizations were often drawn from a single species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The species is most abundant in the Great Lakes drainage and has spread into the Ohio and Mississippi systems, and into the Missouri system in Nebraska. In Illinois it occurs in both the Mississippi and Ohio drainages.

**Habitat.** The main study site was a roughly 150-yard reach of the [[Saline Drainage Ditch]] near Urbana, a slow-flowing stream with two small rapids, silt-sand bottom, stones and gravel, and depths from a few inches to about three feet. Supplementary work was done at [[Stony Creek]] in Vermilion County, a swifter, rock-bottomed stream with rapids and pools. Flow depended on surface run-off, so high water and drought periods disrupted collecting. The streams were not ice-bound for long during the two winters.

**Niche and behavior.**
- The species does not construct burrows, so it is confined to permanent water bodies.
- Juveniles inhabit the surface layer among weedy vegetation during summer. Adults stay on the bottom, usually under stones in shallow areas.
- From mid-September, mature individuals moved to deeper pools (partial segregation). Over winter, most animals concealed themselves in leaf drifts or under stones, or buried in mud.
- Activity in winter was restricted to midday on sunny days (roughly 11:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m.).

**Predation.** [[Fishes]] are the most important natural enemies. The author did not quantify predation and considered its toll roughly uniform across age classes.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling.** Field collections used dip nets (summer and early fall), seines (late fall to early spring), and "still-hunting" on clear winter days. Marking individuals failed because of molting and regeneration, so collections were analysed statistically using size-frequency modes.

**Reproduction and seasonal cycle.**
- The species belongs to Ortmann's "warm water type," with a fall mating season, a spring egg-laying season, and molt-linked changes in male form.
- Eggs are laid in late March or early April at Urbana. Egg-carrying lasted 4 to 6 weeks, depending on temperature.
- Young of the season appeared in quantitative collections in mid-May (1934) or June 7 (1933). The difference was attributed to a cool, wet 1933 spring that prolonged hatching; egg-carrying spanned 43 days in 1933 and 29 days in 1934.
- Brood attachment was about one to two weeks; the author concluded young probably undergo two molts before becoming free-swimming at about 5 mm cephalothorax length.
- Copulation was recorded in September through November and March; the author argues the mating season is latitude-dependent and may persist through mild winters.

**Growth.**
- Juvenile growth produced five distinct size groups by late summer. Average increment per molt was 2.42 mm cephalothorax length, close to the 2.45 mm derived from Andrews' *C. affinis* data.
- Number of molts in the first season was 6 to 10. By mid-September, cephalothorax lengths ranged from 12 to 27 mm (about 24 to 54 mm total length).
- Growth ceased in late September or early October. The latest recorded soft, freshly molted individual was a female of 17.1 mm on October 6.
- Growth-rate differences were attributed mainly to inherent genetic factors, with environment setting the upper size limit. The author rejected food shortage as a cause in the natural population.

**Sexual maturity.**
- Maturity boundary in both sexes was about 20 mm cephalothorax length. First-form males below 18 mm were exceedingly rare.
- Females of 20 to 25 mm were as likely to be copulating as larger animals.
- Egg-bearing females were rarely found below 20 mm (smallest: 16.1 mm, with 40 eggs). Clutches ranged from 5 to 250 eggs, roughly proportional to size; the largest female (36.1 mm) carried an estimated 175 eggs.
- Michigan populations (studied by [[Creaser]]) matured at smaller sizes, which the author attributed to a shorter growing season and a smaller race.

**Spring molts and adult death.**
- Adult yearling males undergo two molts (first adult molt in April or May; second in summer, or in 1934 an early "second spring molt"). Adult females molt once, immediately after the young are shed.
- Yearling growth is marked at the second molt, while the first spring molt shows no growth, which the author links to low winter physiological activity.
- Maximum adult size was 25 to 35 mm. The largest individual recorded was a 40 mm male taken June 29, 1932.
- Most yearling adults that reproduced died in June, disappearing wholly from collections and not found in bank burrows. The cause was judged natural death, linked to the coincidence of maximum size and parenthood, not disease or catastrophe.

**Lifespan and brood production.**
- Most individuals die as yearlings after one brood. Those maturing later survive into a second year, and a very few survive into a third.
- Of the 34 individuals of 35–40 mm, 21 were female and 13 male, implying higher male mortality in the older class.
- The author concludes that most or all individuals produce only a single brood. This contrasts with earlier estimates of 3 to 4 years, and agrees with [[Creaser]]'s Michigan study.

**Conclusions (summary).**
- Juveniles remain in surface water in summer; adults stay on the bottom; both seek deeper water in winter.
- Individuals are positive to sunlight at all seasons, with no definite nocturnal rhythm, contrary to common textbook claims.
- Principal life-history determinants are the rate of growth (which sets the time to maturity) and reproductive activity itself.
