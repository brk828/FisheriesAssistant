# More Facts—More Fish: Wisconsin Sport Fishery Research and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae #Centrarchidae #Percidae #Moronidae #Sciaenidae #Acipenseridae #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This article by Edwin L. Cooper, Chief Fishery Biologist, presents the rationale and active research program of the Wisconsin fishery research department. Its central argument is that effective [[Fish Management]] depends on learning the biological facts of fish populations and then acting on them, rather than relying on artificial propagation or habitual [[Maintenance Stocking]]. The article reviews ongoing projects on [[Muskellunge]], [[Northern Pike]], panfish and [[Walleye]] at [[Five Lakes Project]] and [[Murphy Flowage]], [[Freshwater Drum]] and [[Lake Sturgeon]] at [[Lake Winnebago]], [[Brook Trout]] at [[Lawrence Creek]], and [[Common Carp]] control. It also describes collaboration with the [[University of Wisconsin]] and the use of [[Echo-Sounder]] technology. The author concludes that more questions than answers remain, and that research is a tool for better fishing rather than an end in itself.

## Background & Description

The document is a magazine-style article, numbered pages 19 through 24 of a larger publication. A fragment on the first page notes that state fish and wildlife programs are funded under the [[Pittman-Robertson Act]] and [[Dingell-Johnson Act]], which draw on taxes on hunting and fishing equipment. It also describes land acquisition as a conservation investment that remains in indefinite public use and increases in value.

The article's author, Edwin L. Cooper, is identified as Chief Fishery Biologist. The text does not give a publication date.

The research program is described as based on practical problems of fish management. Some projects are short-term, while others are long-term studies carried out with University of Wisconsin personnel and facilities. Department research sites are widely scattered across the state, and all focus on game fishes popular with anglers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native productivity and population regulation:** The author argues that good fishing is associated with populations that are expanding. Overpopulated, slow-growing populations usually produce poor angling. The natural capacity of fish populations to replenish under heavy harvest "usually has been grossly underestimated," according to the Five Lakes results.
- **Predator–prey dynamics:** Research at Murphy Flowage examined how northern pike and introduced muskellunge affect panfish populations. Over-exploitation of predators had been suspected for years, but the author states there was no proof that it caused depletion.
- **Lake Winnebago:** This "inland sea," of more than 200,000 acres, is described as "fabulously productive" of warm-water fishes. Its dominant species, the freshwater drum, is considered inferior to sport species such as walleye, sauger, white bass, and lake sturgeon. The deeper, more turbid water of the lake is credited with protecting sturgeon from over-exploitation.
- **Sturgeon life history:** Lake sturgeon are described as slow to mature. The text notes that five or more generations of trout, walleye, bass, or panfish can pass in the time a female sturgeon takes to reach first spawning.
- **Trout streams:** Most trout caught by anglers were originally hatched in streams from wild parents. The author notes that unstocked, better streams can offer more satisfactory angling even with fewer fish caught. The value of feeder-stream refuges is under study, and the text notes little evidence of downstream movement of brook trout at Lawrence Creek.
- **Carp dominance:** Carp were introduced in 1883. The author reports that warm, fertile southern Wisconsin waters are ideal for them, and that they can quickly dominate an environment without active control.

## Key Findings

**Management philosophy**
- The primary challenges of sport fishery research are recognizing the basic characteristics of fish populations that produce good fishing and developing new management tools to assure those populations.
- Maintenance stocking of many warm-water species is described as being used "more as a placebo than a fish management tool."
- Selective harvest or eradication of undesirable fishes is accepted as an effective method, but accurately counting fish in a lake is difficult, which limits its use.
- Improving the environment for fish is a promising tool, but the author states that better procedures depend on understanding what fish need to prosper.

**Muskellunge ([[Muskellunge]])**
- Muskellunge is the official state fish of Wisconsin and is under intensive study.
- Research questions include factors limiting abundance in lakes, improved rearing methods, the most efficient stocking size per dollar spent, and whether lakes invaded by [[Northern Pike]] can be maintained or reclaimed as productive musky waters.
- Hatcheries and rearing ponds at Spooner and Woodruff are used to study musky culture. Muskies were reported at 15 inches and nearly ½ pound at five and a half months of age.
- Production is stocked experimentally in High Lake (Vilas County) and Murphy Flowage (Rusk County) to determine optimal planting size and expected angler catch.
- Adult muskellunge are being tagged to study total life span, number of spawnings, and movement. Anglers are asked to report marked fish.

**Five Lakes Project (Vilas County)**
- The project is beginning its second 10-year period to determine angling restrictions needed to maintain satisfactory fishing for walleye and panfish.
- Results of the first 10 years suggest many conservative restrictions on game and panfish "have no worthwhile function" and only limit recreational potential.

**Murphy Flowage (Rusk County)**
- In the first year of unrestricted fishing, anglers took about 10,000 bluegills.
- More than 100,000 catchable-size bluegills remained when anglers quit.
- The author states that bluegill fishing here faces more danger from over-population and stunting than from over-fishing.
- Rock bass, black crappies, yellow perch, and bullheads are also under-exploited.
- Largemouth bass, bluegills, and northern pike depletion by anglers is under study.

**Lake Winnebago and Freshwater Drum**
- Freshwater drum (sheepshead) dominate the lake and are considered inferior in sporting quality and edibility.
- Department crews and commercial fishermen have increased drum removal to more than three million pounds per year without a noticeable decline in the drum population or significant change in fish condition.

**Lake Sturgeon**
- Sturgeon in Lakes Poygan, Winneconne, and Butte des Morts are described as over-fished due to extensive removal by winter spearers.
- Research indicates these populations are distinct from Lake Winnebago sturgeon, although they share spawning grounds in the Wolf River.
- A sturgeon speared in Lake Winnebago in 1954 hatched from an egg deposited in the Wolf River in 1871. The author notes that many speared fish are older than the people spearing them.
- The Lake Winnebago population is not currently considered at immediate risk.

**Trout and Lawrence Creek**
- The Lawrence Creek research area, near Westfield, is typical of Wisconsin streams with abundant native brook trout.
- Studies aim to determine how badly trout populations are depleted after the angling season, and what restrictions maintain optimum sustained fishing.
- Stream improvement and watershed stabilization projects, in cooperation with state and federal soil conservation agencies, are planned for trout streams.

**Carp control**
- Carp control began a few years after their 1883 introduction and remains a "never-ending problem."
- At Delafield, studies found carp out-survive largemouth bass by a wide margin.
- Seining and fish toxins are the two main control methods, but neither is a complete answer. Toxins cannot be used effectively in large drainage systems without destroying desirable game fishes.
- Research is underway on carp behavior around nets and on electro-mechanical seines or trawls.

**Technology and Research Tools**
- Echo-sounder studies on [[Lake Mendota]] have located and identified major fish population segments by season.
- Yellow perch density in Lake Mendota is described as "almost unbelievable," substantiated by skin-diver observations and brief gill net sets.
- The author suggests echo-sounders could help anglers locate under-harvested species.
- Disease and nutrition problems in trout hatcheries are under continuing study.

**Conclusion**
- The author states that substantial future progress depends on wisely applying available research and on a public that understands fish management.
- The closing message is that "Fishing in Wisconsin can be as good as you want it to be."
