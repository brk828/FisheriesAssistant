# Factors Affecting Growth of Razorback Sucker in Captivity: A Review and Knowledge Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department report (Ward, Childs and Persons, 2007) reviews published literature, agency reports, hatchery site visits and surveys of hatchery personnel to examine what controls growth of captive-reared [[Razorback Sucker]]. The few remaining wild populations depend on captive rearing and stocking, but stocked fish suffer high predation, so target stocking sizes have risen and hatcheries must rear fish larger. Growth is highly variable and influenced by size, age, sex, density, living space, water quality, food quantity and quality, genetics and temperature, which makes single-factor evaluation difficult. Reported growth rates range from 0.2 to 1.8 mm/day, with the highest rates typically in natural or semi-natural ponds. The authors call for replicated, detailed studies to compare rearing practices, arguing these would save time and cost in reaching stocking size.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Conservation status:** Populations are sustained largely by [[Captive Rearing]] and [[Stocking Programs]] because wild recruitment is limited. Genetic resources depend on these programs until permanent solutions are found.
- **Rearing history:** State and federal agencies have reared the species since the 1970s to supplement declining populations. Unlike commercial species, rearing methods have received little published study.
- **Stocking size trend:** Low survival of stocked fish (Brooks 1986; Marsh and Brooks 1989; Marsh and Facey 2005) has driven target stocking sizes steadily upward, from 300 mm TL toward 400–500 mm TL.
- **Facility scope:** More than 50 locations have been used to rear the species, including raceways, circular tanks, production ponds, golf-course ponds and natural floodplain wetlands. Six major producers supply most stocked fish: [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]], [[Grand Valley Native Fish Facility]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]], [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Uvalde National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Methods:** A questionnaire (Appendix 1), telephone follow-ups (Appendix 2), and site visits to five facilities were used. Interviews were conducted with personnel from additional hatcheries, some of which had formerly produced the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Growth drivers (general fish biology):** Growth varies with size and age, sex, season, activity level, density, living space, food quality and quantity, genetics and temperature. Water temperature is probably the most important variable, since feeding, digestion and metabolism are all temperature-dependent. Density affects growth through territorial behavior, dominance hierarchies, and physical interference in crowded conditions. Its effects are often confounded with food availability and space. Food amount, quality, particle size and feeding frequency all influence growth.

**Razorback-specific growth patterns:**
- Growth is typically very rapid in the first year, then declines with age. First-year growth ranged from about 50 mm to 350 mm across studies.
- Wild adults in Lake Mohave showed growth too slow to measure accurately over 1987–1997 (Marsh and Facey 1998).
- Variable growth may be adaptive: fast-growing fish may survive high-discharge years, while slow-growing fish may survive droughts (Minckley 1983). Because cohorts do not reach target size together, production is harder to manage.

**Temperature:** In laboratory shuttle-box trials, adults preferred 23–24 °C and avoided water below 11.8 °C or above 28.6 °C (Bulkley and Pimentel 1983). Detailed seasonal temperature profiles are unavailable for many grow-out sites.

**Pond culture:** Pond culture promotes rapid juvenile growth (Kaeding and Osmundson 1989). Marsh (1994) reported growth in golf-course ponds exceeding the best rates in intensive federal hatchery culture, especially in early years. Growth in semi-natural ponds was comparable to estimated wild juvenile growth (McCarthy and Minckley 1987).

**Handling stress:** Repeated capture and handling reduced growth in bonytail chub by 26% compared with controls (Pfeifer et al. 2005, as reported in the source). Razorback Suckers handled at Willow Beach often do not eat for two weeks after handling.

**Food and feeding:** Razorback Suckers feed on plankton and benthic organisms. Larvae reared in ponds with natural foods and no predators show high survival. Artificial fertilization may increase pond production.

## Key Findings

**Hatchery practices and constraints**

- **[[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona):** Larvae from Dexter or Achii Hanyo are stocked into a 0.6-acre unfertilized pond in spring and harvested in September. Fish average about 0.6 mm/day and take one to two years to reach 300 mm TL. Target production is 12,000 fish per year. The main problem is Ich and bacterial infections from an open spring source with mosquitofish.
- **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico):** Keeps two separate broodstocks. Larvae are stocked at about 20,000 per 0.1-acre pond (50–100 thousand per acre) after fertilization with alfalfa pellets and superphosphate. Fish average 100–200 mm TL after the first season and reach 300 mm in 12–18 months for most. Production is limited by pond space because 16 other species are maintained on site.
- **[[Grand Valley Native Fish Facility]] (Colorado):** Uses indoor recirculating systems at the 24-Road facility plus eight broodstock ponds at Horsethief Basin. Fish are fed to about 2.5–7.0% body weight and take 12–18 months to reach 300 mm. Key constraints are space and water flow/oxygen. Disease (Ich, Lernaea) and low dissolved oxygen challenge pond grow-out.
- **[[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (Utah):** Stocks 0.2-acre outdoor ponds at 10,000–20,000 larvae per pond, fed a slow-sinking salmon diet. Fish reach 300 mm in 12–15 months. Ponds were troublesome because of poor water quality, harvest difficulty and non-native fish. Key constraints are winter space and high iron and manganese levels.
- **[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona):** Rears wild-caught larvae from Lake Mohave in warm, solar-heated recirculating systems at 22–25 °C. Feeds 1.0–7.0% body weight per day. The 2004 target was 325 mm TL or greater, with 6,000 fish per year, usually requiring two growing seasons. Space limits production toward 400–500 mm TL.
- **[[Uvalde National Fish Hatchery]] (Texas):** Receives 35,000–60,000 fry annually from Dexter. Fish are stocked into one-acre ponds, with about 4,000–5,000 fish per pond. Juveniles reach 300 mm in about six months. In 2006/2007 the hatchery produced 6,000 fish for the [[San Juan River]]. Cormorant predation and bacterial problems are managed through netting, harvest timing and oxytetracycline-medicated feed.

**Comparative findings**

- Reported growth rates range from 0.2 to 1.8 mm/day, with the highest reported in natural or semi-natural ponds.
- Stocking densities were largely set by trial and error. Space, water quality and disease were the main constraints across facilities.
- Feed types varied from [[Commercial Catfish Feed]] enriched with spirulina and krill to [[Salmon Diet]] and [[Razorback Sucker Diet]] formulated by researchers.
- Weight is the traditional growth measure, but length and individual-based measures give better estimates of confidence and variance. Length-frequency analysis or recapture of marked fish is considered most useful.
- Sex may matter only where target size exceeds 450 mm TL (Mueller 2006, 86 PIT-tagged fish in High Levee Pond).

**Research recommendations**

- Use replicated studies to establish optimum stocking densities for ponds and tanks.
- Test whether sorting or grading improves overall growth in ponds and intensive culture.
- Investigate artificial fertilization to improve growth of larvae and juveniles in ponds.
- Determine whether razorback diets give better growth than catfish or salmon feeds.
- Evaluate growth and production potential of large circular tanks and other intensive methods.
- Compare long-term survival of fish reared in raceways and tanks with fish reared in ponds.
- Evaluate effective treatments for fish diseases.
- Assess factors besides size that may increase post-stocking survival, such as exercise conditioning and predator-recognition training.

**Conclusion:** Survival of stocked fish is strongly tied to stocking size, so research must identify ways to raise growth rates in captivity. Replicated studies with consistent data on location, temperature, initial size, density and final size are needed. Such work would reduce time to stocking size and improve production efficiency.
