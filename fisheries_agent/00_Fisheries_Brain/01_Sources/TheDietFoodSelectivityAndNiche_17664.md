# Diet, Food Selectivity and Niche of Rhabdosargus sarba in Natal Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sparidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 study by S.J.M. Blaber (Department of Zoology, University of Natal, Pietermaritzburg) examines the feeding ecology of juvenile and subadult [[Rhabdosargus Sarba]], a common benthic fish in [[Natal Estuaries]]. The diet is dominated by aquatic macrophytes and filamentous algae, with a substantial share of bivalves and the amphipod [[Grandidierella Lignorum]]. Bivalves and amphipods are positively selected when little plant material is eaten. The species feeds during daylight, peaking in the early afternoon. The paper also compares *R. sarba* with the sympatric *R. holubi* and *R. auriventris*, and assesses resource partitioning with the [[Gerreidae]] and [[Pomadasys Commersonii]].

## Background & Description

*Rhabdosargus sarba* is a sparid bream common in subtropical and tropical inshore waters and estuaries across the [[Indo-Pacific]]. In Natal it is abundant, and juveniles and subadults are conspicuous members of the estuarine fish fauna.

The study formed part of a wider investigation into the community structure and functioning of Natal estuarine fishes. Earlier work covered iliophagous, planktivorous and piscivorous species, but common benthic feeders such as *R. sarba* had received little attention. A concurrent benthic invertebrate survey, published separately, allowed prey selectivity to be estimated.

**Sampling and methods**
- **Sites:** Kosi system, South Lake St Lucia, [[Mlalazi Estuary]] and [[Durban Bay]].
- **Period and gear:** Roughly three-monthly collections from October 1981 to December 1982, using seine and gill nets.
- **Preservation:** Stomachs removed and preserved in 10% formalin.
- **Analyses:** Percentage frequency of occurrence; the volumetric [[Ricker Points Method]]; and percentage dry weight (plant material excluded, molluscs weighed shell-free after decalcification).
- **Selectivity:** Calculated with the [[Ivlev Electivity Index]]. Selectivity for plant material could not be computed because its environmental abundance was not quantified.
- **Feeding periodicity:** Derived from volumetric "points" data.

Sample sizes reported in the tables were n = 52 (Durban Bay), n = 52 (Mlalazi), n = 273 (South Lake St Lucia) and n = 353 (Kosi system). For the periodicity analysis, 380 fish from Kosi and 286 from St Lucia were used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diet variation by locality.** Diet composition varied considerably among estuaries, though the main food categories stayed broadly consistent.
- **Kosi system:** Plant material ranged from 87% by volume at the estuary to 29% at the entrance to Ist Lake (47% in Ist Lake, 73% in 2nd Lake). Bivalves ([[Brachidontes Virgiliae]]) and [[Chironomid Larvae]] were the main invertebrate prey. At the estuary, where plant intake was highest, penaeid prawns and fish fry were the chief animal foods.
- **South Lake, St Lucia:** Plant material (a mix of [[Ruppia]] and *Enteromorpha*) ranged from 14% at Nkazana to roughly 77–83% at the most plant-rich sites (Makakatana and Off Charters Creek). Plant proportions varied seasonally at Game Guard Camp (26% in Nov/Dec 1981, 77% in March 1982, nil in June 1982). In June 1982 the siphons of *Solen cylindraceus* made up 68% of volume.
- **Mlalazi Estuary:** Filamentous algae (mainly *Chaetomorpha*) and *Ruppia* were eaten in almost equal proportions (29% points each). Bivalves and crustaceans were minor.
- **Durban Bay:** Littorinid gastropods dominated (85% frequency, 60% points, 99% dry weight). These are likely taken from harbour walls and pilings, the only hard substrata in the bay. Filamentous algae and *G. lignorum* were also present. Day & Morgans (1956) had recorded much less plant material in the 1950s, when industrial and harbour development were less extensive.

**Diel feeding.** Feeding occurs during daylight, with maximum stomach fullness in the early afternoon and the lowest fullness early in the morning. No night samples were taken, but empty stomachs in dawn catches suggest feeding ceases in darkness. Kosi and St Lucia showed similar patterns.

**Interspecific comparison.** Three *Rhabdosargus* species occur sympatrically in mixed shoals but differ in feeding ecology:
- *R. sarba* is partly herbivorous, macerating plants with molariform teeth and gaining nutrition from plants and their epiphytes.
- *R. holubi* ingests large quantities of plant material but digests mainly the epiphytic diatoms, and takes few bivalves.
- *R. auriventris* is almost entirely carnivorous, with bivalves (chiefly *Brachidontes virgiliae*) as the main prey. It was sampled at Kosi (n = 77), where little plant material was found.

**Resource partitioning.** Overlap with the Gerreidae and *P. commersonii* is limited. The Gerreidae depend on bivalve siphons in the Kosi system, where competitive exclusion by *P. commersonii* has been reported. Overlap with *R. sarba* there is considered insignificant, since few siphons are eaten by *R. sarba* in Kosi. In St Lucia, where Gerreidae are scarce, siphons are a common part of the *R. sarba* diet. At St Lucia, *P. commersonii* consumes large amounts of the tanaid *Apseudes digitalis* and few *G. lignorum*, whereas *R. sarba* strongly selects *G. lignorum* and largely ignores *A. digitalis*. Their feeding times also differ: *P. commersonii* feeds mainly at dawn and dusk, when *R. sarba* shows its lowest fullness.

**Size and life stage.** Juveniles measured 30–265 mm standard length. No change in feeding preference with size was detected.

## Key Findings

1. **Dominant diet:** Filamentous algae and aquatic macrophytes (e.g., *Ruppia*, *Potamogeton*) form the bulk of the diet and are well digested after maceration by the molariform teeth.
2. **Important animal prey:** Bivalves are the most important animal food, followed by *Grandidierella lignorum* and, in Kosi, chironomid larvae.
3. **Selectivity:** The [[Ivlev Electivity Index]] showed strong positive selection for *G. lignorum* at almost all times and strong negative selection for *Apseudes digitalis*. Bivalves such as *Eumarcia* and *Solen* were selected variably, with greater positive selection when plant intake was lowest.
4. **Local variation:** Diet composition differed strongly among sites, reflecting local food availability. Plant proportions ranged from 14% to about 83% across St Lucia sites alone.
5. **Diel pattern:** Feeding is diurnal, peaking in early afternoon, with a likely cessation at night.
6. **Niche:** *R. sarba* is partially herbivorous and occupies a unique niche among common Natal benthic estuarine fishes.
7. **Divergence in Rhabdosargus:** Juvenile *R. sarba* is considered a true omnivore and possibly ancestral to the more specialized *R. holubi* (epiphytic diatom feeder) and *R. auriventris* (bivalve specialist). *R. holubi* and *R. auriventris* are African endemics, whereas *R. sarba* is Indo-Pacific.
8. **Resource partitioning:** Dietary overlap with the Gerreidae and *P. commersonii* is limited, and the species partition food and feeding times in St Lucia.
9. **Conservation/management relevance:** The author notes that adult diets in Natal seas are poorly known. Bivalves are an important diet component for all three *Rhabdosargus* species at all life stages (Van der Elst 1981), but the study does not address management directly.
