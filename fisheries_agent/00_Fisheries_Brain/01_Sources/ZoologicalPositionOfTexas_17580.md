# On the Zoological Position of Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetology

## Executive Summary

This 1880 monograph by [[Edward D. Cope]] examines where Texas belongs in the zoogeographic scheme of North and Central America. Cope argues that the state's vertebrate fauna, including mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fishes, places it within the [[Nearctic Realm]]. The fauna is a mixture of Nearctic and [[Neotropical Realm]] elements, and it is divided into regional units that track climate, rainfall, geology and topography. The paper proposes a revised eastern boundary for the [[Sonoran Region]] and restricts a distinct "Texan district" to the southwest of the state. It is based largely on field collections and local reports from correspondents such as [[Jacob Boll]] and [[Gabriel W. Marnock]].

## Background & Description

The paper was published by the [[Smithsonian Institution]] as Bulletin No. 17 of the United States National Museum. It is the twentieth in a series illustrating the museum's natural history and ethnology collections. Cope's stated aim is to clarify the zoogeographic boundary between the Nearctic and Neotropical realms, which is poorly defined where North America narrows into the Mexican isthmus. Because no physical barrier separates the two realms there, Cope treats climate and rainfall as the main limits on animal ranges.

Cope bases his scheme on Mexican zoogeography, which divides the country into three altitudinal belts:

- [[Tierra Caliente]]: the hot coastal lowlands, linked to the neotropical realm.
- [[Tierra Templada]]: the intermediate, well-watered mountain zone, described as a "garden region."
- [[Tierra Alpina]]: the highest land.

Amphibians and reptiles are used as the main indicators because they cannot migrate far, which makes their distributions useful for mapping boundaries.

Cope also describes Texas's geology and topography. Northeast-southwest strikes divide the state much as they divide the eastern states. A series of limestone "steppes" rises to a high plateau, and the Staked Plains lie above that. Rainfall falls from roughly 50 inches on the eastern border to about 15 inches on the high Pecos plains. Vegetation changes accordingly, from cypress swamps and pine forest in the east to prairie, brush and cactus in the west.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and rainfall gradient.** Cope gives the following annual rainfall figures:

| Location | Annual rainfall |
|---|---|
| Eastern border of Texas | 50 in |
| Gulf coast meridian | 35 in |
| Longitude 98°30′ (border of first plateau) | 25 in |
| Fort Concho | 20 in |
| High plains of the Pecos | 15 in |

He notes that the 97th meridian divides the wet eastern third of the state from the drier west.

**Regional units.**

- **[[Austroriparian Region]]:** humid, forested and stream-rich. It has many salamanders and water turtles, and some amphibian families not found elsewhere. Eastern and southeastern Texas belong here.
- **Sonoran Region:** dry, sparsely vegetated and spring-poor. It supports desert toads and many lizards and snakes. Its eastern limit is the key boundary Cope re-examines.
- **[[Texas Plateau Region]]:** a limestone "first plateau" of rugged hills, canyons and brush. It sits between the Sonoran and Austroriparian faunas and contains a mix of Sonoran, Mexican and Central elements.
- **Southwestern "Texan district":** Cope restricts this unit to southwestern Texas.

**Faunal relationships.** Cope divides the fauna into Nearctic, mixed and Neotropical genera. Nearctic genera include *Antilocapra*, *Bos*, *Lynx*, *Mephitis*, *Cynomys*, *Holbrookia*, *Crotaphytus*, *Cnemidophorus*-type forms, *Storeria* and *Ophibolus*. Mixed genera include *Felis*, *Procyon*, *Spermophilus* and *Anolis*. Neotropical genera include *Dicotyles*, *Dasypus*, *Sceloporus* (shared with the north) and *Smilisca*.

**Plateau fauna.** Of the forty species Cope lists as characteristic of the plateau, 19 are Sonoran, 11 are peculiar to the plateau, 5 are Mexican and 7 are Central. No genus is peculiar to the plateau, so Cope treats it as a subdivision of the Sonoran Region rather than a new primary region.

**Southwestern species.** Of 22 species in the extreme southwest, 9 occur in Mexico and 6 are Austroriparian.

**Habitat notes.** Cope records several habitat associations:

- The horned toad (*Phrynosoma cornutum*) is abundant across the cretaceous plateau.
- The ringneck-type lizard *Sceloporus spinosus* is arboreal, while *S. poinsettii* lives on limestone cliffs.
- The peccary (*Dicotyles torquatus*) favours brushy highlands and retreats to limestone caves.
- *Bassaris astuta* and the rock squirrel *Spermophilus grammurus* inhabit rocky cliffs and fissures of the plateau.

## Key Findings

**Mammals.** Cope reports the jaguar (*Uncia onca*) as uncommon on the Nueces River. A pair was reportedly killed on the Medina River at about latitude 30°. He notes that the ocelot (*Felis pardalis*) is more abundant and ranges further east, that the peccary is common on the plateau and in the lower Nueces chaparral, and that the prairie dog (*Cynomys ludovicianus*) reaches the San Saba River as its southern limit. He records the white-tailed deer as the common deer of western Texas and the pronghorn (*Antilocapra americana*) as reaching Laredo. The bison comes into northwestern Texas in winter.

**Birds.** Cope gives only brief notes. He cites the scissor-tail (*Milvulus forficatus*) as a diagnostic species absent from the eastern forest, and he describes the chaparral cock (*Geococcyx viaticus*) in detail, noting that its range appears limited by its lizard prey.

**Reptiles.** The largest group, Cope describes several new species and subspecies:

- *Holbrookia lacerata* (new species)
- *Eumeces brevilineatus* and *E. pachyurus* (new species)
- *Eumeces epipleurotus* (new species, from Smithsonian specimens)
- *Coluber bairdi* (described by H. C. Yarrow from Fort Davis)
- *Eutenia cyrtopsis* subsp. *ocellata* (from near Helotes)

He also gives a dichotomous key to the *Holbrookia* species, which includes *H. propinqua*, *H. maculata*, *H. lacerata*, *H. texana* and *H. elegans*. He treats *Coleonyx variegatus* as the only gecko found in Texas.

**Amphibians.** New forms include *Lithodytes latrans*, *Syrrhophus marnochii* (named for Marnock) and *Diemyctylus miniatus* subsp. *meridionalis*. Cope notes the spread of the Mexican toad *Bufo debilis* and the desert toad *Bufo punctatus* as Sonoran indicators.

**Fishes.** Cope describes several new species from Texan rivers, including *Boleosoma phlox*, *Zygonectes brachypterus*, *Amiurus bolli*, *A. brachyacanthus*, *Hybognathus flavipinnis*, *H. nigrotaeniata* and *Cochlognathus biguttata*. He calls *Xenotis megalotis* (the bluegill-type sunfish) "the most abundant freshwater fish in Texas." He also reviews the taxonomy of the black bass *Micropterus* and proposes retaining the name *Micropterus* provisionally.

**Boundary conclusions.** Cope's main conclusions are:

1. Texas belongs to the Nearctic realm. Neotropical genera are fewer, and Nearctic genera are numerous and largely exclusive.
2. Eastern and southeastern Texas belong to the Austroriparian Region.
3. The plateau is part of the Sonoran Region. Its eastern and southern boundary runs south to within about 20 miles of San Antonio, northeast across the Colorado River just northwest of Austin, and on to the Brazos near Weatherford.
4. The southwestern "Texan district" is characterized by *Testudo berlandieri*, *Holbrookia propinqua*, *Eumeces tetragrammus*, *Rhadinea imperialis* and *Diemyctylus meridionalis*, plus Mexican admixtures such as *Uncia onca*, *Felis pardalis*, *Dasypus peba*, *Dicotyles torquatus* and *Smilisca baudinii*.

Cope also notes that his earlier *Check-List* (1875) had underestimated the extent of the Sonoran fauna in northwestern Texas, and this paper corrects that.
