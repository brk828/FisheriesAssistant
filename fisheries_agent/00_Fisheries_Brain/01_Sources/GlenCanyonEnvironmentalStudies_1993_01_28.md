## Executive Summary

This document is a **Bureau of Reclamation Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES)** memorandum summarizing a January 26, 1993 coordination meeting among agencies (Arizona Game and Fish Department [AGF], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [USFWS], Arizona State University [ASU], and the Navajo Nation) regarding 1993 research permitting for endangered fish studies in the **Little Colorado River (LCR)**. Rather than presenting primary morphological or ecological survey data, the memo addresses **research protocol, permitting, and methodological coordination** concerns surrounding population studies of the **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*), an endangered cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River system. Six items of concern raised by the Navajo Fish and Wildlife Department are discussed, covering sampling overlap, spawning aggregation disturbance, adult fish/gamete removal for propagation, field flagging impacts on recreation, and lack of habitat data analysis.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, body plan, coloration) for *Gila cypha* or associated taxa are presented in this administrative document. The paper references only the species' conservation status (federally listed **[[Endangered Species]]**) and its **life stages relevant to sampling**: larvae, young-of-year (YOY), and adults — each subject to distinct collection and handling protocols.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The Little Colorado River (LCR) and its **backwater habitats** serve as critical habitat for the humpback chub, particularly for **[[Spawning Aggregations]]** and larval/YOY rearing.
- **Spatial context**: Research and habitat sampling were concentrated in the LCR mainstem, including areas within **Grand Canyon National Park** boundaries (e.g., "Railroad Tie Rock" reach) and Navajo Nation lands.
- **Life-stage habitat use**: Distinct sampling approaches targeted **larval and YOY fish** (via seining and minnow trapping) versus **adult fish and gametic material** (via direct capture for propagation), implying differential habitat use across life stages — a pattern consistent with the species' known reliance on warm, turbid backwaters for early life stages and mainstem/tributary confluence habitat for adults.
- **Interagency research footprint**: Multiple entities (USFWS, AGF, ASU) operated concurrently in the LCR, raising concerns about **cumulative sampling stress**, particularly the risk of desiccation, thermal stress, and water quality degradation to YOY during seining in summer months.
- **Propagation ecology**: Discussion of the **[[Bubbling Ponds]]** hatchery facility (selected as an ex-situ propagation and thermal stress experiment site) versus the **Dexter National Fish Hatchery** (Dexter, NM) highlights logistical and physiological concerns (transport-related mortality/stress) in captive rearing of a wild-collected endangered cyprinid.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling overlap risk**: AGF and USFWS field efforts (seining, minnow trapping) showed only "marginal" temporal/spatial overlap, but **repeated seining at the same sites risked biasing compositional and distributional data**.
- **Differing research objectives**: USFWS focused on **habitat characterization** (two fixed 1000-meter sample reaches), while AGF prioritized **large sample sizes** for compositional/distributional analysis of LCR fish populations — a methodological divergence requiring coordination to avoid duplicative disturbance.
- **YOY vulnerability**: Seining-based sampling in summer was flagged as a potential stressor to YOY humpback chub via direct handling, desiccation, thermal stress, and water quality disturbance.
- **Propagation challenges**: Historical attempts at **artificial propagation of humpback chub** were characterized as largely unsuccessful ("dismal failures"), even among experienced teams, underscoring the difficulty of captive breeding for this species. **[[Bubbling Ponds]]** was selected by the Aquatic Coordination Team (ACT) two years prior as the designated facility for propagation and thermal stress experiments, partly to avoid mortality risk associated with larval transport from Dexter, NM.
- **Gamete and adult removal protocols**: A shift in permitting philosophy was proposed — requiring **ACT/GCES protocol review prior to removal** of adult fish or gametic material, with adult removal for propagation designated a "last resort" only after non-lethal gamete collection methods were exhausted.
- **Field disturbance (flagging)**: Excessive and conspicuous (fluorescent) flagging used for transect marking was identified as a recreational and habitat disturbance issue; resolution included switching to camouflage-pattern flagging within the National Park and plain red flagging elsewhere, with efforts to minimize the total number of flags per transect.
- **Personnel density**: Despite Navajo Nation interest in limiting the number of researchers in the LCR at one time, agencies reported already operating with minimal crews (2–3 personnel per camp), and no restriction was ultimately imposed.
- **Habitat data analysis gap**: USFWS had not yet analyzed collected habitat data as of the meeting date, raising Navajo Nation concern that ASU's sampling effort might be missing portions of the "full array of habitat utilized by the fish" — highlighting a broader need for iterative data analysis to inform ongoing field sampling design.
- **Adaptive management structure**: The memo illustrates an early 1990s **multi-agency, permit-based adaptive management framework** for endangered fish research in the Colorado River Basin, coordinated through the **[[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]]** program and the Navajo Nation's environmental permitting authority.
