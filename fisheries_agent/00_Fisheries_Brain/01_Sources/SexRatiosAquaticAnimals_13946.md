# Sex ratios: Aquatic animals

## Executive Summary

This 1962 reference compilation by Rezneat M. Darnell (Marquette University), reproduced from the *Handbook on Growth* (Federation of American Societies for Experimental Biology), presents a comparative dataset of **sex ratios (expressed as % females)** across more than 50 aquatic taxa spanning fish, crustaceans, molluscs, echinoderms, cnidarians, and other invertebrates from marine, estuarine, freshwater, brackish, and subterranean habitats. The table categorizes species by the *pattern* of sex-ratio variability observed in natural populations: **highly variable ratios** (linked to locality, season, or habitat segregation), **ratios that change gradually with age/life stage**, **relatively constant/near-equal ratios**, and **populations where males are rare or functionally absent** (parthenogenesis, gynogenesis, hermaphroditism). Although not a single-species study, the source is a foundational cross-taxon synthesis useful for understanding the ecological, physiological, and behavioral drivers of sex-ratio bias in aquatic systems.

## Background & Description

The data derive from Table 121 (pp. 439–442) of the *Handbook on Growth*, compiling primary literature (references spanning 1919–1959) on natural sex ratios in aquatic populations. Sex ratio is reported uniformly as **percent females** in sampled populations, allowing cross-taxon comparison. Species are drawn from:

- **Fishes (teleosts, elasmobranchs, chondrosteans, cyclostomes):** [[Micropogon undulatus]] (Atlantic croaker), [[Morone chrysops]] (white bass), [[Osmerus mordax]] (rainbow smelt), [[Perca flavescens]] (yellow perch), [[Gambusia affinis]] and [[Gambusia holbrooki]] (mosquitofish), [[Leucichthys artedi]] (cisco), [[Lepibema chrysops]] (white perch), [[Acipenser fulvescens]] (lake sturgeon), [[Lepomis]] hybrids (sunfishes), [[Lampetra planeri]] (brook lamprey), [[Lebistes reticulatus]] (guppy), [[Carcharhinus milberti]] (sandbar shark), and the unisexual [[Mollienisia formosa]] (Amazon molly).
- **Crustaceans:** [[Uca pugnax]] (mud fiddler crab), [[Callinectes sapidus]] (blue crab), [[Cancer magister]] (Dungeness crab), [[Panulirus interruptus]] and [[Panulirus guttatus]] (spiny lobsters), [[Mysis relicta]], [[Artemia salina]] (brine shrimp), and various ostracods, anostracans, and amphipods.
- **Molluscs:** [[Ostrea virginica]] (Eastern oyster) and [[Ostrea edulis]] (European flat oyster), gastropods including [[Lioplax subcarinata]], [[Pomatiopsis cincinnatiensis]], [[Campeloma rufum]], and [[Urosalpinx cinerea]].
- **Other invertebrates:** freshwater hydromedusa [[Craspedacusta sowerbii]], echinoderm [[Asterias rubens]], copepods [[Pseudocalanus minutus]] and [[Oithona similis]], rotifers, and gastrotrichs.
- **A marine mammal:** [[Mirounga angustirostris]] (northern elephant seal).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The compiled data reveal several recurring ecological mechanisms driving sex-ratio deviation from 1:1 in aquatic populations:

- **Habitat segregation by sex:** Adult females of [[Callinectes sapidus]] and [[Perca flavescens]] occupy different salinity zones or depths than males; female [[Atya scabra]] preferentially use marginal habitats.
- **Differential migration timing:** In [[Micropogon undulatus]] and [[Callinectes sapidus]], male and female spawning migrations are temporally or spatially offset, producing seasonally skewed estuarine sex ratios.
- **Differential mortality/longevity:** Many species (e.g., [[Perca flavescens]], [[Leucichthys artedi]], [[Gambusia holbrooki]], [[Lacuna pallidula]]) show sex ratios that shift progressively with age due to higher male mortality (often attributed to fishing pressure, predation, or intrinsic viability differences), producing female-biased older cohorts.
- **Temperature-dependent sex determination:** [[Gammarus deubeni]] exhibits a striking pattern where spring-generation young are predominantly male and summer-generation young predominantly female.
- **Reproductive strategy diversity:** Numerous taxa (e.g., [[Craspedacusta sowerbii]], [[Triops longicaudatus]], [[Trichoniscus biformatus]]) show geographically variable reproductive modes—dioecious, parthenogenetic, hermaphroditic—depending on habitat favorability, contributing to extreme sex-ratio variance.
- **Social/behavioral effects:** In [[Mirounga angustirostris]], sex ratio is skewed toward males in outlying (non-breeding) populations due to male belligerence and exclusion from breeding rookeries.
- **Interspecific hybridization (Haldane's Rule exception):** [[Lepomis]] hybrid crosses show near-total absence of females, presented as an apparent exception to Haldane's Rule regarding heterogametic sex sterility.
- **Unisexual/gynogenetic reproduction:** [[Mollienisia formosa]] females are inseminated by males of congeneric species but reproduce gynogenetically, resulting in male-absent populations.

## Key Findings

**Category 1 — Highly Variable Sex Ratios (locality/season dependent):**
- [[Uca pugnax]]: sex ratio increases in higher intertidal zones.
- [[Mirounga angustirostris]]: 1–71% females; high in breeding rookeries, low in outlying groups.
- [[Micropogon undulatus]]: 43–78% females; male spawning migration precedes female migration.
- [[Morone chrysops]]: ~0–100% females; sexes migrate in unisexual schools to spawning grounds.
- [[Osmerus mordax]]: 0–66% females; males dominate streams around spawning runs.
- [[Perca flavescens]]: 0–100% females; male schools occupy deeper water than female schools.
- [[Callinectes sapidus]]: 4–85% (and separately 18–50%) females; strong salinity-based habitat segregation and seasonal migration effects.
- [[Gambusia affinis]]: apparent sex ratio (via secondary sexual characters) increases through summer.
- [[Gammarus deubeni]]: 45–71% females; temperature-controlled sex determination (spring = male, summer = female).
- [[Urosalpinx cinerea]] and [[Lacuna pallidula]] (marine gastropods): sex ratio rises during/after breeding season, partly due to differential male mortality.
- [[Ostrea edulis]]: 1–56% females; ratio highest in early summer, declines due to sex change in individuals.

**Category 2 — Sex Ratio Changes Gradually with Age/Life Stage:**
- [[Perca flavescens]]: ratio increases with age due to differential fishing-related mortality of males.
- [[Gambusia holbrooki]]: 19–~90% females; males less viable, ratio increases with age.
- [[Leucichthys artedi]] (cisco): ~10%+ females rising with age due to greater female viability.
- [[Lepibema chrysops]] (white perch): 0–69% females; ratio *decreases* in upper age groups (males outlive females — reverse pattern).
- [[Lioplax subcarinata]] (freshwater gastropod): 63–81% females in winter; males live 1 year, females 2 years.

**Category 3 — Relatively Constant Sex Ratios:**
- [[Lepomis]] hybrids: males predominate; females nearly absent in interspecific crosses (Haldane's Rule exception).
- [[Lampetra planeri]]: 35–71% females in spawning populations, with local/annual variation.
- [[Acipenser fulvescens]]: 56–93% females; near-equal up to ~29 years, then female-biased due to greater longevity.
- [[Cancer magister]]: near-equal sex ratio across larval, postlarval, and adult stages; slight commercial-catch segregation.
- [[Panulirus interruptus]]: 21–71% females; near-equal overall despite catch-method variation.
- [[Mysis relicta]]: 51–93% females; nearly equal through most of life.
- [[Lebistes reticulatus]] (guppy): ~67% females; artificial populations stabilize at a consistent ratio regardless of starting composition.

**Category 4 — Males Rare or Absent:**
- [[Carcharhinus milberti]] (sandbar shark): 95% females; males rare in catches and presumed rare in breeding populations.
- [[Mollienisia formosa]] (Amazon molly): unisexual, gynogenetic — females inseminated by congeneric males but produce all-female offspring.
- [[Potamocypris smaragdina]], [[Pleuroxus hammulatus]], and several freshwater ostracods/cladocerans: near-exclusively female populations via parthenogenesis.
- [[Stygobromus spinosus]] (subterranean amphipod), [[Candona caudata]], [[Caenestheriella gynecia]], [[Campeloma rufum]], bdelloid rotifers, and gastrotrichs: 100% female populations, presumed obligate parthenogenesis.

**Cross-cutting observation:** Across nearly all categories, **differential mortality between sexes** — driven by fishing pressure, predation, habitat-linked exposure, or intrinsic longevity differences — emerges as the single most common proximate explanation for departures from a 1:1 sex ratio in aquatic populations.
