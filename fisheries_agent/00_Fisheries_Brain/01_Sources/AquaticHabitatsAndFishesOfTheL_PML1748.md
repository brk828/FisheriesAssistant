# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Lower Colorado River: Yuma Division

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-assemblage

## Executive Summary

This appendix describes the aquatic habitats and fish assemblage of the Yuma Division of the lower [[Colorado River]], a reach of about 34 km of channel with backwaters, drains, and canals, including the inflow of the [[Gila River]]. Fish sampling by seine, hoop net, and gill net was combined with benthos, aquatic plant, pigment, and physical-chemical measurements. The fauna is dominated by introduced and tropical species, with [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Sailfin Molly]] most abundant. Of 29 species recorded historically in the reach, only a subset was obtained during the survey, and no native fish were collected. The authors link the decline in diversity and standing crop to periodic drying, possible pesticide kills, and changes in irrigation operations.

## Background & Description

The Yuma Division is used mainly as a delivery route for irrigation water to the Yuma valley and to [[Morelos Dam]], from which water flows into Mexico. During low irrigation demand, mainstream flow supports domestic and irrigation use, partly routed through the Gila River channel. Water-use patterns and the proximity of [[Imperial Dam]] produce bottoms with a high percentage of silt and organic material. Banks are largely modified or constrained by dense streamside stands of introduced [[Tamarisk]].

Sampling included:
- **Fishes:** seines in the mainstream, backwaters, and near Morelos Dam; small- and large-mesh hoop nets; gill nets, by day and night; creel observations.
- **Benthos:** ten [[Ekman Dredge]] samples.
- **Plants and algae:** field observations of marshes, riparian vegetation, and submergent taxa.
- **Pigments:** chlorophyll and carotenoid analyses, including 99 samples in June.
- **Physical-chemical:** temperature, dissolved oxygen, specific conductance, pH, and orthophosphate (PO4-P).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish assemblage and seasonality.** Mainstream seines yielded 10 species. Backwater seines during the sampling period were less diverse and averaged larger catches, but only four species were taken. The authors attribute the depauperate backwater fauna to winter temperatures. Tropical species, including Threadfin Shad, Sailfin Molly, Mexican Molly, and Mossambique Mouthbrooder, appeared to avoid cold nights, consistent with lower thermal tolerances near 10 °C. Flowing mainstream water, sustained by upstream habitats, stayed warmer and more thermally stable than shallow peripheral backwaters.

**Spatial concentration.** Seine catches near Morelos Dam far exceeded those upstream, driven by a concentration of Threadfin Shad in the small semi-impounded area above the dam.

**Predation and gear effects.** [[Soft-Shelled Turtle]] preyed heavily on fish in traps and nets. The authors note that consistently small catches may also indicate small populations of larger species.

**Recreational and commercial use.** Sport fishing was restricted to the reach upstream of the Gila inflow and near Morelos Dam. Striped Mullet supported a substantial fishery in the reach just downstream of Yuma and upstream of Morelos Dam during 1965–71, with Mexican nationals using throw nets. None was recorded in 1974–75.

**Benthos and vegetation.** Stable, organic-rich bottoms are dominated by [[Asian Clam]] biomass and by chironomid and oligochaete numbers. Backwaters support [[Cattail]] and [[Sedge]] marshes, submergent [[Spiny Naiad]], and dense films of [[Blue-Green Algae]] in still, fine-grained areas.

**Water chemistry.** The Gila River inflow markedly raised specific conductance and phosphate levels. Daytime phosphate was lower than nighttime, suggesting uptake during photosynthesis.

## Key Findings

- **Fish catch (mainstream seines):** 10 species, up to 76,000 individuals per ha, weighing 52.4 kg (per ha, as reported).
- **Dominant species (mainstream seines):** Threadfin Shad made up about 40% of fish taken and more than 50% of estimated weight. Sailfin Molly followed with 36% of numbers and about 19% of weight (OCR ambiguous), then Red Shiner at about 12.5% of numbers and 9.3% of weight. Mossambique Mouthbrooder was about 5.5% of numbers.
- **Remaining mainstream species:** Mosquitofish, Bluegill, Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish, Mexican Molly, and Yellow Bullhead, in decreasing order of numbers.
- **Backwater seines (winter):** catches averaged about 101 kg/ha (OCR ambiguous) and about 20,000 individuals per ha, from four species: Red Shiner, Mosquitofish, Goldfish, and Channel Catfish. Goldfish made up about 17% of weight, followed by Channel Catfish at 3%.
- **Morelos Dam:** Threadfin Shad exceeded 80% of both estimated numbers (more than 240,000/ha) and weight.
- **Hoop nets:** large-mesh nets caught no animals. Small-mesh nets averaged 1.0 animal per day (including 0.33 turtle) and a mean of 2.5 fishes per night.
- **Gill nets:** 0.67 fish per set by day (not weighed because of turtle depredation) and 3.0 fish per set at night, the latter weighing 2.74 kg per set.
- **Species richness trend:** of 29 species recorded in the Yuma Division (Table Y-1, compiled from earlier surveys including Baily et al., 1970, and records from 1973), only a subset (reported as 14, OCR ambiguous) was obtained or observed in 1974–76. Species not taken include [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Desert Pupfish]], which are native or of conservation interest. No native fish were obtained in the Yuma Division.
- **Causes of decline:** the authors cite periodic drying, possible pesticide kills (notably a large 1974 kill in the Limitrophe Division that also affected fishes above Morelos Dam), and operational changes in the Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District.
- **Benthic biomass:** Asian clams made up 83.3–98% of total biomass in 10 Ekman dredge samples. Total biomass was 14.2–22.3 kg/ha with clams and 0.16–0.46 kg/ha without them. Chironomids and oligochaetes made up 86–96% of numbers. Culicid larvae and snails were rare.
- **Pigments (June):** chlorophyll a was consistent (only 2 of 99 samples below 1.0 mg/m³, both from a drain inflow; most between 4 and 6 mg/m³). Chlorophyll b was mostly at magnitude I (56 of 99 samples). Chlorophyll c was mostly at magnitude II (61 of 99), undetected in 16, magnitude I in 21, and magnitude III in one. Carotenoids were higher in daytime samples, probably from drift of organic matter. Diatom values tracked chlorophyll a and peaked during periods of greatest insolation.
- **Temperature and oxygen:** summer mean temperatures were reported at roughly 25–26 °C (OCR garbled), with dissolved oxygen of 7.0 to 10.5 mg/l, lowest at the downstream end below Yuma.
- **Specific conductance:** rose from about 1,316 mmhos/cm at RM 662.7 to about 1,630 at RM 663.0, then jumped below the Gila inflow to an average of 2,766 mmhos/cm (25 samples from the Gila River).
- **pH:** 7.0 to 8.1 across 19 samples, usually 7.9 to 8.1.
- **Phosphate (PO4-P):** about 0.1 mg/l upstream of the Gila (range 0.03–0.19, 32 samples), 0.05–0.65 mg/l in the Gila inflow (22 samples), and 0.05–0.39 mg/l downstream (47 samples), averaging about 0.15 mg/l below the inflow.
