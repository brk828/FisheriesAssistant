# Gila elegans (Bonytail Chub) Species Abstract

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Heritage Data Management species abstract covers the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila elegans]]*), a cyprinid once widespread across the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is now restricted to a few isolated populations, and it is federally listed as Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The main causes of decline are habitat modification by dams, altered hydrology, cold tailwater releases, and competition and predation by [[Nonnative Fish]]. The document summarizes taxonomy, morphology, reproduction, diet, habitat, status, and the recovery and management actions in place as of its revision history.

## Background & Description

**Nomenclature and type locality.** The species was described by Baird and Girard (1853). The type locality is given as the Zuni River (now dry), New Mexico, though the text notes it was probably the Little Colorado River below Grand Falls, Coconino County, Arizona. Type specimen records are listed under USNM catalog numbers. The taxonomic status is unsettled: the species has been treated as a full species, as a subspecies, and as an ecotype of *Gila robusta*. The scan does not reliably give the total number of species in the genus.

**Morphology.** Adults generally reach 300 to 350 mm (12 to 14 in) total length, though larger individuals have been taken from [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Key features include:
- A laterally compressed, streamlined body that rises smoothly into a predorsal hump.
- 47 to 50 total vertebrae.
- A narrow caudal peduncle.
- Scales that are sometimes incomplete, absent, or deeply embedded on the predorsal area and caudal peduncle.
- Large, falcate fins; dorsal fin origin is set well behind the pelvic fins.
- Dorsal fin rays usually 10 or more.
- A terminal, somewhat oblique mouth.
- Pharyngeal arch ray counts reported as 2, 5, 4, 2.

**Coloration.** The fish is dark above and light below. It is very dark (almost black) in clear water and paler in turbid streams. Fins are often dusky, with yellow pigment, especially on the paired fins. Mid-orange lateral slashes appear between the paired fins. Small tubercles occur on the head and anterior body. Breeding colors are more subdued, and tubercles are less developed in females.

**Identification.** The bonytail is separated from [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] by a combination of characters:
- Gill rakers on the anterior row of the second arch: usually 15 (range 15 to 21) in bonytail, 15 (13 to 17) in humpback, and 13 (12 to 15) in roundtail.
- Dorsal and anal fin-ray counts, which differ among the three species.
- Caudal peduncle, which is much narrower in bonytail than in roundtail. The text gives a head length to caudal peduncle depth ratio that separates the species, though the scanned values are partly garbled.
- The nuchal hump: in bonytail it rises smoothly from the snout. In humpback chub it rises more abruptly from the skull.

**Record history.** The abstract was first revised in 1994 and last revised on 2001-10-10. The entry is compiled by the Heritage Data Management System, with major contributors listed as James E. Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) and Paul C. Marsh.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range.** The species was once widely distributed throughout the Colorado River and its major tributaries, including the Green River in Utah and Wyoming and the Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers in Arizona. It now survives only in isolated populations in the Yampa, Green, and Colorado rivers, including the confluence of the Green and Colorado rivers and the Colorado/Utah border. In the lower basin it is found only in [[Lake Mohave]], with possible individuals between Parker Dam and Davis Dam. In Arizona, records range from 235 to 1,960 ft (72 to 598 m) in elevation.

**Biology and adaptation.** The reduced, embedded scales and relatively small eyes may be adaptations to the high silt loads of the formerly erosive, turbid Colorado River system before dams were built (Minckley 1973). Bonytail in the lower Colorado reservoirs keep a streamlined body and apparently occupy an active [[Limnetic Niche]] in open water. Many fish identified as bonytail from the upper basin show evidence of [[Hybridization]] with roundtail or humpback chub.

**Reproduction.** In [[Lake Mohave]], spawning has been observed in May. In the upper Green River, spawning occurs in June and July at water temperatures near 18 °C (64 °F). Eggs are scattered over the bottom, and no parental care or nest is reported. Cold water released below dams prevents successful egg hatching (Bagley 1989).

**Diet.** In rivers, adults eat mainly terrestrial insects, plant debris, and algae, and young bonytail eat aquatic insects. In lakes, the fish apparently feed on algae and plankton.

**Habitat.** Available information indicates that bonytail used the main channel of mid-sized to large rivers, in both strong current and pools, usually over mud or rock. During spring flooding, the fish used backwaters and inundated tamarisk habitats. In reservoirs, bonytail occupy a variety of habitat types but appear to prefer open water.

**Population trends.** Populations are declining. Remaining populations are small and consist of only a few adults, many over 40 years old. The decline is attributed to [[Dam Regulation]] and habitat modification. The text notes that current conditions differ greatly from those in which the species evolved.

## Key Findings

**Conservation status**
- Federally listed as Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (USDI, FWS 1980).
- [[Critical Habitat]] was designated in March 1994 for:
  - Designated portions of the Colorado, Green, and Yampa rivers in the upper basin.
  - The Colorado River from Hoover Dam to Parker Dam, including Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu.
- Other designations:
  - Arizona Wildlife of Special Concern (WSC).
  - AGFD Endangered status.
  - USDA Forest Service Region 3 Sensitive species.
  - Group I, Nevada Department of Wildlife (NNDFW) status.
  - Listed as Endangered in Mexico (Secretaría de Desarrollo Social 1994; Secretaría de Medio Ambiente 2000).

**Threats**
- Altered hydrology and cold tailwater releases from reservoirs.
- Predation by and competition with [[Nonnative Fish]].

**Recovery planning** ([[Recovery Plan]], approved September 4, 1990)
- Prevent extinction of bonytail in the wild.
- Protect remaining populations and their habitats.
- Reintroduce hatchery-reared bonytail into the wild.
- Obtain essential information on life history and habitat requirements.
- Resolve taxonomic problems in the Colorado River basin.
- Promote communication and information dissemination.
- Develop quantitative recovery goals and a long-term habitat protection strategy.
- Bagley (1989) calls for habitat requirements to be determined and for water release modification from dams to mimic the conditions needed for successful spawning and development.

**Management needs**
- Reduce the effects of nonnative fish in chub habitat.
- Monitor chub population status.
- Establish [[Refugium Population]]s in Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu, and Lake Mead.

**Existing refugium**
- A refugium population is held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico.

**Suggested projects**
- Continue survey work on Lake Mohave.
- Continue hatchery propagation at Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
- Continue work on grow-out facilities at Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu.
- Evaluate additional refugium sites, including:
  - Arizona Game and Fish Page Springs Hatchery.
  - Fish ponds at Ouray National Wildlife Refuge, Utah.
  - Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona.
  - Palm Lake at The Nature Conservancy's Hassayampa River Preserve, Wickenburg, Arizona.
  - Niland Native Fish Ponds, California.

**Land management**
- Bureau of Reclamation (BOR).
- U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), including Cibola and Havasu National Wildlife Refuges.
- National Park Service (NPS), Lake Mead National Recreation Area.
- Lake Havasu State Park and La Paz County Park.
- The Nature Conservancy (TNC), Hassayampa River Preserve.
- Private landowners.
