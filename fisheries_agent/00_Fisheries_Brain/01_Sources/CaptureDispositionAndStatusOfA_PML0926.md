# Razorback Sucker Capture, Disposition, and Status from Lake Mohave, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 progress report from the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] at Arizona State University, submitted to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Albuquerque), documents the March–April 1975 effort to recover the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona. The goals were to establish a brood stock at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and to gather information on population size, distribution, behavior, and health. The team captured, spawned, and hatched razorbacks, but high mortality from bacterial disease and possible hybridization with the introduced [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) threatened the population. The authors call for immediate action on research and disease control.

## Background & Description

The report is a field and hatchery progress account rather than a formal study. It covers:

- **Target species:** The razorback sucker, a large catostomid of the Colorado River system that is listed as threatened and is a recovery priority. Its spawning behavior follows [[Douglas 1952]], which the authors cite.
- **Team and affiliation:** Eric S. Gustafson (Research Assistant) and Dr. W. L. Minckley (Associate Professor and Director), Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory, Arizona State University, Tempe.
- **Reference collaborators:** Biologist Bruce D. Rosenlund (Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery, Whiteriver, Arizona) performed necropsies and a pathology report, attached as an addendum.
- **Companion publications:** A detailed account of embryo and larval development was reserved for the quarterly report. Literature cited includes [[Minckley 1973]] (*Fishes of Arizona*), [[Hubbs and Miller 1953]] (natural hybridization of *Catostomus* and *Xyrauchen*), and fish-disease references by Bullock, Wood, and Snieszko.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning aggregations at Lake Mohave.** In late March 1975, the team observed spawning aggregations about one mile south of Hoover Dam along a lotic-like shoreline with hot springs. Using scuba, divers counted about 200 razorbacks in shallow water and deeper water. About one-third were in "nesting" posture. Spawning took place among boulders near a hot spring outflow in water about 1 m deep. Spawning behavior matched [[Douglas 1952]]: one female was flanked by two males.

**Cottonwood Cove collections.** In early April, the team netted a small cove on the northwest perimeter of Cottonwood Cove, about 20 miles north of Davis Dam. Two gill nets were set: one perpendicular to the shore at the southern point, and one parallel to shore in shallow water. From these sets the team captured 91 suckers:

- 33 males and 21 females were transported to the hatchery.
- 12 ripe, spawned fish were returned to the lake.
- Fish were marked with fin clips and treated with an antifungal or antiseptic to reduce infection.

Four bonytail chubs (*Gila elegans*) and a nonnative cestode-infected specimen were also taken. The bonytails were transported to the hatchery; two died within one week, and all four were gravid.

**Hatchery holding and spawning.** Over four weeks the team netted 101 adult razorbacks. Of these, 29 females and a number of males were placed in three raceways at the Willow Beach facility. Analysis showed that a large share of the females and males had already spawned (exact percentages are garbled in the source scan). Ripe females received injections of [[Human Chorionic Gonadotropin]] to induce ovulation. Eggs from one female were fertilized with the milt of two males, yielding about several thousand fertilized eggs by 28 April 1975.

**Disease and mortality.** Bacterial infections were the main obstacle. Necropsies of nine dead fish showed lethal infections of *Pseudomonas* and *Aeromonas* in internal organs. Infections were contracted before the fish reached the hatchery. Almost all embryos and larvae died: all but 18 of the developing embryos and larvae perished from these infections. Hatchery staff also noted gonads that were rudimentary or absent in several necropsied fish, which may reflect pathogens or backcrossing between hybrids and pure *X. texanus*.

**Eye lesions.** Many razorbacks from the Cottonwood Cove collection had degenerated eyes. Investigators found [[Myxosoma]] spores in the eye and cranial tissues, but the primary cause of the lesions remains debatable. Bacterial and viral cultures of kidney and spleen were negative. Some blinded fish had good body condition and were feeding normally.

**Hybridization risk.** A preliminary inspection of a specimen suggested it was the White Sucker. This species is native to the eastern United States and had not been officially recorded in the Colorado River basin. Hybridization between *Catostomus* and *Xyrauchen* occurs naturally in the Colorado River drainage and has happened at low frequency without apparent harm to parent stocks (Hubbs and Miller 1953). The long-term effect of these introduced suckers on the confined razorback population is unknown.

## Key Findings

- **Spawning population:** About 200 razorbacks were observed in the Hoover Dam shoreline aggregation. Collections at Cottonwood Cove totaled 91 individuals.
- **Hatchery brood stock:** The team established a brood stock of 101 adult razorbacks at Willow Beach, including 29 females, and produced several thousand fertilized eggs in April 1975.
- **Disease:** Systemic *Pseudomonas* and *Aeromonas* infections killed most embryos and larvae. Necropsy showed lethal bacterial conditions in the internal organs of nine dead fish.
- **Eye degeneration:** Eye lesions with *Myxosoma* spores were common in the collected fish. The primary cause was unresolved.
- **Hybridization:** Four hybrid razorback-sucker fish were found, and the White Sucker was identified as a likely new introduced species in the Lake Mohave population. Long-term genetic and ecological effects were unknown.
- **Recommendations:** The authors state that immediate action is needed to:
  - Study population size, hybridization frequency, ecology, and reproductive strategy.
  - Develop a disease detection and treatment system for infected razorbacks.
  - Monitor introduced species that interact with the razorback sucker.
