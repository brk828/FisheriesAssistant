# Interdisciplinary Research on Runoff and Erosion Processes in an Arid Area, Sde Boker Experimental Site, Northern Negev, Israel

#geomorphology #arid-ecosystems #hydrology #soil-erosion #isopoda #interdisciplinary-research

## Executive Summary

This methodological paper by [[Aaron Yair]] and an interdisciplinary team describes a research programme on slope runoff and erosion at an experimental watershed of about 11,325 m² in the arid [[Northern Negev]] of Israel. The team, which includes climatologists, zoologists, botanists, pedologists and geomorphologists, instrumented the site with more than 100 devices measuring rainfall, wind, evaporation, soil moisture, runoff, sediment (mineral and dissolved), biotic sediment, isopod population dynamics and dust input. The motivation was a strikingly non-uniform sediment delivery from adjacent plots that rainfall, runoff and slope geometry could not explain. Field observations pointed to burrowing by [[Isopods (Woodlice)]] and digging by [[Porcupines]], which break the soil crust and supply easily eroded loose sediment. The paper focuses on describing approach and instrumentation rather than presenting final results.

## Background & Description

The study originated from field observations that intense burrowing by desert animals (porcupines and isopods) breaks up the mechanically and biologically stabilised soil crust, making fine particles available to shallow flows. Isopod pellets were found to be of the same order of magnitude as the sediment removed from the site during a single rainy season, and their spatial pattern matched the pattern of eroded material. Both patterns were linked to the microenvironments of different [[Limestone Lithological Units]].

The site lies in the Northern Negev, where mean annual precipitation over a 27-year record is 95 mm (range 34–167 mm). Rain-days number 15 to 42 per year, and few storms exceed 20 mm per day. The drained area is one half of a first-order basin, with a relative relief of about 30 m, slope lengths of 55–76 m and mean gradients of 11.5–29.5%. Strata are Turonian, comprising three limestone formations with distinct environments:

- **[[Drorim Formation]]**: thin, densely jointed beds that weather to cobbles and boulders; bare rock about 20%; stony desert brown lithosol; sparse vegetation (5–10%) of the Artemisia herba alba – Gymnocarpos decander association.
- **[[Shivta Formation]]**: massive limestone forming rock terraces; bedrock exposed over about 50%; shallow loessial serozem in strips, plus deeper red, organic-rich soil in joints; richer lichen and moss cover; vegetation (5–10%) of the Varthemia montana – Origanum dayi association, indicating better soil moisture.
- **[[Netser Formation]]**: limestone with flint concretions, very densely jointed; shattered rock over about 30% of the surface; very shallow soil in patches; Hammada scoparia association, indicating the poorest moisture regime.

The paper argues that overland flow dominates runoff in arid areas and is essential to flood initiation, so attention was directed to slope rather than channel processes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rainfall.** Rainfall controls runoff and erosion directly and, through soil moisture, the distribution of burrowing animals and plant associations. Conventional horizontal raingauges underestimate the rainfall actually intercepted by slopes. Because rain falls obliquely (inclinations of about 40° assumed for the site), windward slopes receive roughly twice the rainfall of adjacent leeward slopes at 20° gradient, and about 1:1.5 at 15°.

**Runoff generation.** Following the [[Partial Area Contribution Concept]] from humid-zone studies, the authors assume a similar pattern operates here, but driven by rainfall regime and surface properties rather than soil moisture. Short showers generate very shallow flows with velocities of about 3–6 cm/s (rarely above 10 cm/s), so upslope runoff often infiltrates before reaching the channel. Infiltrated water improves moisture in the upper and middle slope, creating microhabitats for biological activity.

**Biotic sediment.** Isopod families (40–70 members, annual life cycle) survive the hot season mainly where soil depth exceeds 30 cm and moisture stays above 6% by weight. Population size varies greatly between years; after a drought year, active burrows may fall by 50–90%. Overexploitation of geophytic bulbs by porcupines can also suppress their activity for several years.

**Chemical denudation.** Soil is 30–50% CaCO₃ and 80–95% wind-deposited silt and sand, so solution of limestone and block detachment may matter more than removal of allochthonous soil for long-term slope recession.

**Dust.** Ganor (1975) reported dustfall of 250–300 g/m²/yr for the Northern Negev, two to three times higher than known runoff erosion rates in the same area, suggesting possible aggradation.

## Key Findings

The paper is a methodological description, and its stated conclusions and planned work are as follows.

- **Instrumentation:** over 100 instruments are installed in about 11,325 m². Laboratory analyses exceed 1,000 per year. Instrument placement follows working hypotheses, not random sampling.
- **Rainfall network:** 21 stations, each with twin raingauges (tilted and horizontal, 30 cm above ground), a pit gauge and a Hellman-type recorder. A [[Woelfle Recorder]] measures wind at 3.5 m. The goal is a predictive model of effective rainfall, after which the number of raingauges could be substantially reduced.
- **Runoff plots:** initially 10 contiguous plots, later 16 runoff plots, with plots II, IV and IX subdivided into three subplots each. Stage recorders and volumetric water meters measure runoff, and soil moisture is sampled at nine stations.
- **Sediment sampling:** automatic suspended-sediment samplers (24 bottles of 500 cc each) sample at 2.5 or 5-minute intervals.
- **Chemical denudation:** over 500 water samples collected in two years. Ca, Mg, Na, K, Cl and HCO₃ are analysed. Most Ca and about half of Mg derive from rock–soil, while most chloride comes from rainwater.
- **Biotic sediment mapping:** a 204-square grid is surveyed annually at the end of summer, with porcupine digging points and isopod burrows counted and converted to sediment weight.
- **Isopod population:** five consecutive seasonal surveys since 1973 show burrow density consistently higher on Shivta outcrops than on Drorim or Netser, with less temporal fluctuation there.
- **Dustfall:** 20 water-filled buckets (3 at 1.8 m, 17 at 50 cm) measure dust monthly or after storms, starting April 1976.
- **Hypothesis of cyclic erosion:** temporal variation in slope sediment delivery may follow cycles of available-sediment production, rather than being driven mainly by slope length and gradient. The authors note this applies only where biotic sediment supply is quantitatively significant.
- **Methodological benefit:** interdisciplinary discussion led to tailored methods, for example measuring rainfall as intercepted by slopes rather than with conventional meteorological gauges, and placing isopod studies on a microenvironment-based network rather than a random one.
- **Caveat:** the authors state that the biotic sediment hypothesis is not proven, since isopod pellets and porcupine aggregates disintegrate quickly, and crusted soil may supply far more sediment during high-magnitude events.
