# Mosquito Larviciding, New Pesticides, and Fish Toxicity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

Clarence M. Tarzwell of the [[Public Health Service]] Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center argues that [[Mosquito Control]], [[Wildlife Management]] and fisheries management share more common goals than their practitioners usually recognize. He contends that chemical larviciding, especially with [[DDT]] and the newer organic insecticides, can severely harm aquatic life, and that [[Biological Control]] and habitat manipulation are the more durable approach. The paper reviews historical larvicide use, summarizes laboratory and field toxicity data for fishes, and recommends minimal dosages, species-by-species testing, and closer cooperation between agencies.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review and position statement, not a new field study. Tarzwell frames the conflict between control goals as a result of specialization, and states that effective control is "applied ecology" in which one must "work with nature; not against her."

Historical larvicides discussed:

- **Oil**: The most widely used larvicide for years, applied at 15 gallons per acre. Investigations in the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] area in 1939 and 1940 found that oiling greatly reduced fish food organisms, especially those in marginal vegetation, and could harm waterfowl.
- **[[Paris Green]]**: Applied as dust by airplane. Possible harm to aquatic biota was suspected but not confirmed by TVA investigations.
- **DDT**: Introduced as a larvicide and adulticide in 1944. Its residual action and broad toxicity "completely changed" insect control and the pollution problem.

Scale of change: In 1943 only one synthetic organic insecticide was on the market; by 1955 over 100 materials were commercially used in thousands of formulations. Insecticide use tripled from 1940 to 1950, doubled again from 1950 to 1955, and has since nearly doubled again.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Extremes matter**: Short lethal exposures can destroy a population as effectively as longer ones, since "a fish can be killed only once." Survival of individuals is not the same as the persistence of a species, which requires conditions suitable for growth, reproduction and survival.
- **Habitat manipulation**: Biological approaches include controlled burning of coppice in reservoir fluctuation zones, maintaining high constant pool levels in spring and early summer (which benefits spawning and fry survival of black bass and other centrarchids), draining potholes to prevent fish stranding, and controlling lotus, water hyacinth and alligator weed. Reflooded diked areas can be managed for waterfowl without losing mosquito-control value.
- **Drift and runoff**: Organic pesticides applied to land must be kept from drifting onto water, and direct water applications must use very small dosages.
- **Soil binding**: DDT is absorbed or inactivated by soil particles and organic debris. Clay turbidity and organic matter reduced its effect in the Savannah ponds, but this did not clearly apply to Toxaphene and Dieldrin.

## Key Findings

**Species-specific toxicity**
- Toxicity varies widely among species and compounds. Organic phosphates are generally less toxic to fishes than chlorinated hydrocarbons, with exceptions. Malathion is over 130 times more toxic to bluegills than to fathead minnows, and over 500 times more toxic to Chinook salmon than to fathead minnows.
- Sensitivity to the same material may vary over 500-fold among fish species. Single-species bioassays are therefore unreliable for setting safe levels.
- Table 1 (96-hour TLm, ppm, soft water, 25°C) gives bluegill and fathead minnow values such as:

| Compound | Fathead Minnow | Bluegill |
|---|---|---|
| Endrin | 0.0010 | 0.0006 |
| Toxaphene | 0.0075 | 0.0035 |
| Dieldrin | 0.016 | 0.0079 |
| DDT | 0.032 | 0.016 |
| Malathion | 12.5 | 0.095 |

- Endrin is more toxic to fish than any other substance tested at the Cincinnati laboratory, at a fraction of a part per billion for bluegills.

**Laboratory thresholds**
- For many compounds the 96-hour TLm is below 0.1 ppm, including Endrin, Toxaphene, Dieldrin, DDT, Aldrin, Chlordane, Lindane, Methoxychlor, Heptachlor, Guthion, Di-syston, Malathion and Diazinon.
- Per the cited laboratory work, 50 percent or more of certain fishes would die within 96 hours in 3-foot water areas receiving 0.5 lb/acre of many of these materials.
- Table 2 gives the chlorinated hydrocarbon amounts that produce 50 percent fish mortality when applied to a 3-foot-deep surface, such as Endrin at 0.005 lb/acre (TL50 of 0.60 ppb), Toxaphene at 0.03 lb/acre, DDT at 0.13 lb/acre, and BHC at 6.4 lb/acre.
- The author cautions that laboratory TLm values are not safe field dosages. Even DDT, the most readily inactivated, killed fish at 0.1 lb/acre when applied routinely.

**Savannah, Georgia field studies (1945 to 1949)**
- Routine DDT applications as in malaria control were studied. Hand applications were recommended not to exceed 0.05 lb/acre. Airplane applications are larger because only part of the release reaches the water.
- In a pond with little organic matter, a significant kill followed 2 treatments at 0.05 lb/acre.
- In a clay-turbid pond, there was no fish kill after 14 treatments at 0.1 lb/acre. Kills were generally noted after the tenth treatment at 0.1 lb/acre.

**Recommendations**
- Use the minimum dosage that gives adequate mosquito control, and limit field applications to that dosage.
- Test each material for dosage, formulation, method and frequency of treatment, with a range of species, before routine use.
- Prefer materials less toxic to non-target aquatic organisms, even if somewhat less effective against the pest.
- Direct research toward mosquito-specific toxicants, and toward biological and environmental control methods where they work.
- Wildlife, fisheries and mosquito control agencies should cooperate to resolve conflicts, since many management measures benefit all three.
