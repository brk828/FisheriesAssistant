# Population and Survival Estimates of Flannelmouth Sucker in Northern Grand Canyon, with Distribution and Abundance of Hybrids with Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined population size, movement, survival and hybridization of two indigenous [[Colorado River Basin]] catostomids, the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*, a species of concern) and the federally endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Fieldwork ran for four years (1 July 1991 to 27 June 1995) at the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and its confluence with the mainstem river in [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors recorded 2,619 unique individuals over 49 trips, comprising 2,578 flannelmouth suckers and 41 putative hybrids. Flannelmouth Sucker population estimates were stable across the four years, while survival in larger size classes declined in 1994. Putative hybrids were rare, and genetic analysis showed that most morphologically identified hybrids were of hybrid origin and backcrossed. The authors conclude that the razorback sucker is a transient member of the Grand Canyon fauna and that the species is being genetically swamped under current dam-regulated conditions.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] covers nearly 650,000 km² of arid terrain in western North America. Dam construction, dewatering for agriculture and urban development have fragmented about 2,400 km of riverine habitat formerly used by large-river fishes into 965 km (Miller, 1982). [[Minckley]] et al. (1986) grouped endemic Colorado River fishes into "big-river forms," seven species in all, including both study species.

**Flannelmouth Sucker.** Historically widespread in moderate-to-large rivers across the basin, this species is essentially extirpated from the lower basin and does poorly in impoundments. It typically occupies pools and deeper runs, and adults spawn at the upstream ends of cobble bars. Individuals are considered adult at 300–400 mm TL. The maximum size recorded in this study was 661 mm TL.

**Razorback Sucker.** Historically common in the lower basin and in reservoirs, this species was uncommon and declining in the upper basin by 1950. Smith (1959) recorded only two immature specimens from Glen Canyon. Many researchers contend it was never abundant in Grand Canyon. Genetic variability across remnant populations follows a north-south cline, with the highest diversity in [[Lake Mohave]].

**Hybridization.** Hybrids between the two species have been recognized since Jordan's (1891) original description of *X. uncompahgre* from a hybrid specimen. Hybrids share intermediate morphology, including lateral-line scale counts and a reduced but distinct nuchal keel. The nuchal keel served as the field criterion for hybrid identification in this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Catostomids are primarily benthic. The authors frame the two species as members of an indigenous but declining Colorado River fish community. Several factors shape their status in Grand Canyon:

- **Dam regulation.** Cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] are considered unsuitable for mainstem razorback spawning, and the dam blocks upstream movement to spawning areas. [[Lake Powell]] inundated the former upstream habitat.
- **Tributary use.** Flannelmouth suckers congregate at tributary outflows, and the LCR and [[Paria River]] are key aggregation sites. Spawning and nursery habitat depend on backwaters and tributaries that respond to flow and temperature.
- **Longevity and recruitment.** Long adult lifespans allow populations to persist through years of poor recruitment. The authors warn that stable adult numbers can mask a lack of recruitment, as seen in Lake Mohave razorback populations.
- **Age estimation.** Scales, opercular bones and otoliths have all been used to estimate age, with conflicting results. The authors grouped fish by body length rather than age because of these limitations.

## Key Findings

### Study design and sampling
- Sampling occurred at three base camps 0.6, 3.1 and 10.8 river km upstream from the confluence, across 49 six- to eleven-day trips at roughly monthly intervals.
- Fish were captured with hoop and trammel nets. Total effort was 401,367 net-hours.
- Adults over 150 mm TL were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] and released at capture sites.
- Population estimates used the [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber Model]] with [[POPAN-4]] for open-population analysis. Survival estimates were calculated by size class using capture-history matrices.

### Flannelmouth Sucker population
- 2,578 unique flannelmouth suckers were recorded, with 2,578 entries after pooling. Individuals were recaptured up to 12 times.
- Cormack-Jolly-Seber estimates by trip ranged from 1,591 (winter 1992/spring 1993) to 5,214 (June 1994).
- Seasonal estimates showed peak movements in spring and autumn, especially spring.
- Population size varied significantly across the 16 seasons (F = 2.44, df = 16). Pairwise contrasts showed significant differences between summer and autumn (F = 4.41, p < 0.04) and between autumn and spring (F = 4.86, p < 0.03). No significant differences appeared among years.
- Population estimates remained stable across the four years. The authors note that it is unknown whether the population is at carrying capacity.

### Survival
- From 1991 to 1993, survival was relatively uniform across size classes 1–4 (150–350 mm TL). It rose through size class 3 and was generally positive across size classes 5–9 (351–600+ mm TL).
- Survival in 1994 was lowest of the study for size classes 4 and 6–8, indicating a decline in larger adults.
- The authors argue predation is an unlikely cause because the decline affected larger fish. They propose natural variability in long-term movement or senescence as more plausible explanations.

### Hybrids
- 41 putative hybrids were identified by nuchal keel and recaptured 60 times in total. Of these, 35 (85%) were male and 5 (12%) were female.
- Hybrid population estimates ranged from 8 to 136 (mean 30) across 26 of 49 trips.
- Hybrids were captured most often in spring (46%) and autumn (32%).
- Of 12 individuals tested for mitochondrial DNA, 7 (58%) carried *X. texanus* mtDNA.
- Of 9 individuals analysed both electrophoretically and by RFLP, 8 (89%) were of hybrid origin and one was pure flannelmouth sucker. None were F₁ hybrids; the eight were backcrossed to *C. latipinnis* at 60–90%.
- Field identification was inconsistent. The seven individuals with razorback mtDNA were recorded as flannelmouth sucker 53% of the time, as hybrid 40%, and as razorback 7%.
- 56% of hybrid captures (53/94) occurred about 3 km or more above the confluence, with an average capture distance of 2.2 km.

### Interpretation and recommendations
- The authors conclude that razorback suckers are transient in Grand Canyon. Historical and recent evidence, including the absence of razorbacks from Stanton's Cave remains, suggests the species used the canyon as a corridor rather than as resident habitat.
- A very small hybrid subpopulation exists in the Marble Canyon reach. Its members frequent the LCR in spring.
- Recommended research includes a canyon-wide genetic study of flannelmouth sucker, genetic assessment of hybridization and backcrossing, and long-term monitoring at tributary confluences such as the Paria and Little Colorado Rivers, as well as Bright Angel, Shinumo, Tapeats, Kanab and Havasu creeks.
- The authors recommend adaptive management of flannelmouth suckers, using recruitment and senescence benchmarks from local year classes, before the species is pushed into endangerment.
