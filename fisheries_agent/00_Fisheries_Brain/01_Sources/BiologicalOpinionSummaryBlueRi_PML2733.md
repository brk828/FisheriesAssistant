# Blue River Fish Hatchery NPDES Permit Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinion, dated December 21, 1998, evaluates the proposed issuance of a [[NPDES Permit]] by the [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA) to the privately owned [[Blue River Fish Hatchery]] in Greenlee County, Arizona. The consultation covers two listed species: the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The Service concludes that the permit is **not likely to jeopardize** either species, but only if the terms, conditions and an [[Incidental Take Statement]] are followed. The opinion finds that hatchery discharges, water diversion, sediment, chemical use, petroleum pollutants and escaped nonnative fish all add adverse effects to a river the Service describes as seriously degraded.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** EPA requested formal [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] on April 2, 1997. EPA had found the project "not likely to adversely affect" loach minnow and no effect on razorback sucker, but the Service disagreed for both species. The 135-day consultation period ended August 20, 1997, and the opinion was delayed by workload. A draft opinion was issued August 28, 1998, and a revised permit followed on November 18, 1998.

**Facility.** The hatchery, in operation since 1989, is the first facility to seek an NPDES permit. It has 14 ponds and 4 raceways, sits about 12 miles south of Alpine, and lies at the confluence of the Blue River and [[Bush Creek]].
- Production: about 60,000 rainbow trout per year. Licensed species also include Kamloops trout and brown trout.
- Water right: 365 acre-feet per year (about 0.5 cfs), drawn mainly from an infiltration gallery.
- Discharge: about 432,000 gallons per day (about 0.7 cfs) through two settling ponds into the Blue River.

**Species status.**
- Loach minnow: listed threatened in 1986. Its range has declined about 85 percent. It is a small, benthic fish that feeds on aquatic insects and has a lifespan of about 2 years. Critical habitat designated in 1994 was later revoked by the Service in 1998.
- Razorback sucker: listed endangered in 1991, with critical habitat designated in 1994. It can exceed two feet in length and may live up to about 50 years. Reintroduction into the Blue River occurred between 1986 and 1989, and no razorbacks were found in recent surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The opinion describes the [[Blue River]] as a seriously degraded ecosystem. Overgrazing, timber harvest, beaver trapping, road building, channelization and floodplain development from the late 1800s onward have caused channel erosion, a wide braided channel, depleted riparian vegetation and reduced base flow.

- **Flow:** A single USGS gauge (1969–1991 continuous, later reinstated) shows a median yearly mean of 43 cfs and a 50 percent exceedance flow of 12 cfs, with flashy summer storms. The project area is estimated at a median of about 4–11 cfs. Surface flow is sometimes lost entirely in early summer.
- **Fish community:** Historically up to nine native species, about 65 percent of the native fauna, have been extirpated. Four native species remain alongside loach minnow: speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker.
- **Nonnative fish:** Rainbow trout, brown trout, fathead minnow, western mosquitofish, red shiner and channel catfish have been recorded. Rainbow trout were stocked by the Arizona Game and Fish Department until about five years before the opinion.
- **Consultation history:** Eight formal consultations and nine informal concurrences on Federal actions affecting these species in the basin are summarized in the opinion.

## Key Findings

**Effects identified by the Service**
1. **Water quality:** The only hatchery data (January 1995, total Kjeldahl nitrogen, total phosphorus and BOD) were within acceptable limits, but no data exist for warm, low-flow periods. Chemical use for disease and vegetation control is undocumented.
2. **Petroleum products:** Equipment lubricants may enter the river. The opinion cites lethal oil concentrations of 0.5 mg/l and higher for some fish.
3. **Settling pond materials:** Disposal is unspecified. Settling ponds lie within the 100-year floodplain, so flood events could move accumulated materials into the river.
4. **Temperature:** Hatchery water is held in unshaded ponds and may significantly raise river temperature. Larval loach minnow and razorback sucker are most vulnerable to thermal shock.
5. **Dewatering:** Water diversion can completely dewater the reach between diversion and discharge, killing individuals and stranding larvae.
6. **Sediment and channel disturbance:** Infiltration gallery and ditch maintenance adds sediment in short, intense pulses, which smother loach minnow eggs and fill razorback sucker pools.
7. **Escapement and predation:** Escaped rainbow trout are known to prey on native fish and compete for invertebrate food. Brown and Kamloops trout would add further impacts if cultured.
8. **Cumulative effects:** The hatchery is described as an incremental factor in long-term decline. Mitigating this facility alone would not address the wider watershed alterations.

**Incidental take and terms**
- Take is anticipated through direct mortality (toxics, water-quality exceedances, predation by escapees, dewatering) and indirect habitat loss. Exact take levels cannot be quantified because data are limited.
- Take is exceeded if permit limits are breached, toxic releases occur, or a fish kill of 20 or more dead fish occurs within one-half mile of the hatchery in one day.
- Required terms include:
  - A [[Sand Filter]] on all discharge to prevent escape of nonnative species, completed within one year of the permit effective date.
  - Oil and grease at undetectable levels on a daily basis.
  - Hatchery discharge limited to 50 percent of instantaneous river flow above the confluence, with proportional reductions at low river flow, and reevaluation after two years.
  - Continuous flow monitoring on the discharge, and upstream and downstream monitoring of dissolved oxygen, turbidity, temperature and pH.
  - Monthly compliance reports to EPA and the Service.

**Permit provisions noted**
- Revised permit limits include E. coli, total nitrogen, oil and grease, temperature and dissolved oxygen, with total phosphorus replacing total phosphates.
- A construction schedule for the sand filter runs from design (3 months) through routine use (12 months).
- Chemical usage must be reported annually, and solid wastes must be disposed of without impacting the stream.

**Conclusions**
- Conservation recommendations are discretionary. The Service provides none within EPA's authority.
- Reinitiation of consultation is required if take is exceeded, new information arises, the action is modified, or a new species is listed.
