# Gray Whale Censuses by Airplane in Mexico

#marine-biology #cetology #eschrichtiidae #gray-whale #population-census

## Executive Summary

This 1967 paper by Carl L. Hubbs and Laura C. Hubbs (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) reports thirteen aerial censuses of the eastern Pacific [[Gray Whale]] (*Eschrichtius gibbosus*) population conducted between 1952 and 1964 along the wintering and calving grounds of Baja California, Mexico, in collaboration with pilot Gifford C. Ewing. The censuses covered the coastline from San Diego to the Cape region of Baja California and, from 1954–1957, the eastern shore of the [[Gulf of California]]. Although this is not a fish study, it is included in *California Fish and Game* as a marine vertebrate population assessment relevant to broader [[Marine Megafauna Census Methods]]. Counts ranged from a low of 631 (1957, flagged as an undercount) to a high of 1,581 (1964). The authors conclude that the population increased after 1952–1953, leveled off from 1954–1964, and totaled roughly 3,000 individuals (assuming aerial surveys observed about half the population). The data also refute the hypothesis that odd-year and even-year migratory runs differ substantially in abundance, and the authors argue aerial census methods are more reliable and efficient than traditional shore-based counts.

## Background & Description

While this paper does not describe morphology in a taxonomic sense, it provides count-based demographic structuring relevant to population biology:

- **Species**: *Eschrichtius gibbosus* (Erxleben), synonymized in later literature with *Eschrichtius robustus*; historically also referenced as *Rhachianectes glaucus* (Cope) in Berdegué (1956).
- **Age-class differentiation**: Observers distinguished **calves** from **adults** during aerial counts (though in the 1962 flight, calves were not distinguished from adults in all areas).
- **Behavioral/observational traits affecting detectability**:
  - Gray whales exhale underwater when disturbed, exposing only the blowhole for inhalation, which reduces visibility and detectability from shore or air ([[Underwater Exhalation Behavior]]; Hubbs, 1965).
  - Migratory groups ("gams") complicate individual counting; some shore observers reportedly miscounted by tallying spouts rather than individuals.
  - Migration reportedly continues under bright moonlight but is suspended during the dark of the moon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use**: The eastern Pacific gray whale population winters in coastal lagoons along the western coast of Baja California, Mexico, which serve as the primary calving grounds ("all of the calves are born" there). A smaller subpopulation winters along the eastern coast of the [[Golfo De California]].
- **Migratory corridor**: The census area spanned from near San Diego, California, southward to the Cape region of Baja California (Cabo San Lucas), tracking the southbound and northbound migration of the population.
- **Key breeding/aggregation site**: [[Scammons Lagoon]] (Laguna Ojo de Liebre) is identified as the location of the greatest concentration of whales, requiring early-morning counts to minimize visibility-related bias from wind and haze.
- **Additional aggregation site**: Laguna San Ignacio (referenced as the "Vizcaino breeding area") was another key lagoon; its omission in certain survey years (e.g., 1954 Gilmore flight) caused anomalously low counts.
- **Temporal window of peak occupancy**: The population appears to peak in the lagoons between mid-February and February 27; counts outside this window are considered unreliable due to either incomplete arrival or the onset of northward exodus.
- **Interspecies/anthropogenic interactions**: Increased small-boat traffic and whale-watching/chasing behavior along the migratory coastal route is suggested to have altered migration routes (more offshore) and increased evasive behavior over time, biasing shore-based counts downward in later years.

## Key Findings

1. **Census counts (1952–1964)**, by year (calves / adults / total; parenthetical values considered likely undercounts):
   - 1952: 79 / 748 / **827**
   - 1953 (Rechnitzer): 190 / 722 / **912**
   - 1953 (Gilmore): 118 / 613 / **731**
   - 1954 (Gilmore): 59 / 217 / **(276)**
   - 1954 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 227 / 1,088 / **1,315**
   - 1955 (Gilmore): 148 / 436 / **(584)**
   - 1956 (Gilmore): 138 / 822 / **(960)**
   - 1957 (Gilmore): 98 / 533 / **(631)**
   - 1959 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 286 / 1,223 / **1,509**
   - 1960 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 244 / 1,241 / **1,455**
   - 1961 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 169 / 780 / **(959)**
   - 1962 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 141 / 1,052 / **1,193**
   - 1964 (Hubbs & Hubbs): 209 / 1,372 / **1,581** (highest count recorded in the series)

2. **Population trend interpretation**: Higher counts after the initial 1952–1953 surveys suggest population growth; data from 1954–1964 indicate a **leveling off** of that growth (consistent with earlier suggestion by Hubbs, 1959).

3. **Total population estimate**: Assuming aerial surveys detected approximately half of the true population, the total eastern Pacific gray whale population was estimated at roughly **3,000** individuals — substantially lower than contemporaneous estimates of 5,000–9,000 (Gilmore 1960a,b, 1961; Rice, 1961) and far below unsubstantiated Mexican press estimates of 15,000.

4. **Odd-year vs. even-year run hypothesis refuted**: Near-identical low counts in 1952 and 1953, and near-identical high counts in 1959 and 1960, contradict the hypothesis (held by Gilmore, 1958) that odd- and even-year migratory runs differ markedly in abundance.

5. **Methodological comparison — aerial vs. shore census**:
   - Aerial censuses were judged superior to shore-based counts (from Cabrillo National Monument, Point Loma, San Diego, ongoing since 1946–47) and far more time-efficient (5 days vs. 2 months).
   - Shore counts are subject to major uncertainty from: (a) fog occlusion for days at a time, (b) inability to observe nighttime migration and uncertain effects of moonlight/darkness on migratory activity, and (c) difficulty distinguishing individual whales within a migrating "gam," with some observers erroneously counting spouts rather than animals.

6. **Aircraft and methodology evolution**: Survey aircraft included a Cessna 180 (1954), a Grumman Super Widgeon G44 amphibian (1959–1960), and an Aero Commander 500A (from 1961 onward); a second smaller plane (piloted by Lawrence C. Kuebler) was added in later years (1960, 1962) to widen observational coverage without excessive zig-zagging.

7. **Sources of count bias**: Additional trained personnel, improved pilot skill (notably Gifford Ewing's zig-zag survey technique), and use of a second aircraft in later years may have contributed to somewhat higher counts over time, though the authors judge this bias to be moderate given consistent methodology and personnel across most of the highest-count years.

8. **1963–64 migration anomaly**: Unusually low whale counts observed passing San Diego during the 1963–64 southward migration prompted concern about possible population depletion (e.g., disease, Arctic or western Pacific exploitation), motivating the resumption of the aerial census in February 1964 — which yielded the highest count of the entire series (1,581), alleviating those concerns.

9. **Related species/behavioral note**: The tendency of gray whales (and other whale species, e.g., [[Humpback Whale]]) to exhale underwater when disturbed, exposing only the blowhole, is cited as a general source of undercounting in cetacean censuses (Hubbs, 1965).
