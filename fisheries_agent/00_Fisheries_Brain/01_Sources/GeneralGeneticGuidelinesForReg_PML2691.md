# Region 2 Fish Hatchery General Genetic Guidelines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #hatchery-genetics

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of general genetic guidelines for federal fish hatcheries in U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region 2, written by [[Joe N. Fries]] of the [[San Marcos National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] in September 1993. It was circulated with an October 1993 cover memo to all Region 2 National Fish Hatcheries (NFHs) and Fish Resource Offices (FROs) as part of the 1993–1994 Work Activity Guidance. Its core argument is that hatchery-produced fish can harm wild stocks unless their genetics are managed. The guidelines set out principles for matching hatchery stock to wild stock, minimum broodstock sizes, and methods for limiting inbreeding. They also require each hatchery to produce a genetics plan by 30 September 1994.

## Background & Description

Federal hatcheries produce fish for four purposes: restoring extirpated populations, augmenting declining wild stocks, serving as artificial genetic refuges for endangered species, and developing recreational fisheries. The document notes that critics argue hatchery fish are "mongrels" poorly adapted to local waters, and that some consider hatcheries the greatest single threat to wild fishes. The authors respond that these concerns make genetic planning necessary.

Two general genetic management categories are defined:

- **Limited-geographical management** applies to native populations with distinct genetics, including endangered and threatened species and strains adapted to narrow ranges, sometimes as small as a single drainage. Examples given are the fountain darter, Yaqui catfish, and Rio Grande cutthroat trout. Cultured fish should be nearly genetically identical to the corresponding wild stock.
- **Wide-geographical management** applies to populations without distinct genetics, often due to natural selection, straying, or past stock manipulation. Examples given are many populations of channel catfish, largemouth bass, rainbow trout, striped bass, and paddlefish. Cultured fish should represent the genetic variation of stocks in all receiving waters.

The document also notes that some fish fit neither category. The [[Florida Largemouth Bass]], originally of limited distribution but now scattered across the country, is one example. If the goal is to maintain it where introduced, it should be cultured to preserve within-strain diversity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidelines rest on the idea that genetic diversity lets a population persist in a changing environment. Because the best-suited gene combination for target waters is unknown, the authors treat diversity as critical in most hatchery programs. Several hatchery-related genetic problems are described:

- **Bottlenecking**: small hatchery populations lose genetic variation, and wild populations cannot be fully represented in the hatchery.
- **[[Inbreeding]]**: matings between close relatives can reduce vigor and fertility and cause physical deformities.
- **Artificial selection**: selection for traits such as large size or tame behavior reduces variation and may bring along hidden harms, such as reduced disease resistance.

The authors also say that where self-sustaining native populations exist, hatcheries should supplement them rather than dominate them. Managers need to know the genetics of both wild fish in receiving waters and their own hatchery stock. Where this is unknown, production should follow general principles intended to preserve the presumed diversity of wild populations.

## Key Findings

**Broodstock development**
- Broodstock for enhancing wild stocks should include at least 50 successfully spawning pairs of non-closely related fish per genetically distinct group.
- Broodstock should be collected across the whole range of the wild population, on the assumption that geographic distance loosely tracks genetic distance.
- Collection should span fish of various sizes and shapes over the entire spawning period, and healthy fish should not be culled.
- Where 50 pairs is impractical, wild fish may be used directly (examples given: paddlefish, striped bass), with gametes still collected across the spawning period from as many pairs as possible.

**Broodstock maintenance**
- Without periodic replacement with wild fish, hatchery broodstock become self-perpetuating, and random mating quickly raises inbreeding.
- The least complex remedy is very large, randomly mating populations, but most hatcheries lack space for this.
- The practical remedy is **rotational line crossing** (adapted from [[Kincaid 1977]]): keep at least three breeding lines per distinct group and cross lines each year class. Figure 1 shows this scheme, with each line ideally containing at least 50 pairs.
- Occasional crossing with unrelated (preferably wild) fish is an effective, less labor-intensive alternative.
- Breeding different year classes from the same broodstock does not effectively reduce inbreeding.

**Production for stocking**
- Broodfish should be paired and isolated. An unequal sex ratio or non-isolated pairs allows a few individuals to dominate matings.
- The 50-pair minimum assumes all pairs contribute equally. Several hundred fish are needed if sex ratios are unequal or pairs are not isolated. If no pairing is done, at least 1,000 fish may be sufficient.
- Wild brooders used directly should also be paired and their gametes kept separate until after fertilization.

**Needs and responsibilities**
- Each federal hatchery in Region 2 must develop a production plan that maintains genetic integrity for each cultured stock by **30 September 1994**.
- Details should be developed at each hatchery, since facilities differ in schedules and resources. The Regional Office is to communicate the requirement to hatchery managers.
- [[San Marcos National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] may provide technical help, and final plans are to be reviewed by [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]].
- Hatchery managers are responsible for producing fit, genetically appropriate fish, even though stocking plans are not their responsibility. Adequate lead times and a clear understanding of the intended products are needed.
