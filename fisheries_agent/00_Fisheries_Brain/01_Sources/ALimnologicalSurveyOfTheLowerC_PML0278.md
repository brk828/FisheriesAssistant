# Limnological Survey of the Lower Colorado River (1976-1977) and Related Agency Correspondence

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #limnology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This report by [[James E. Broadway]] and Perry L. Bergersen, Ph.D., for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], describes a one-year limnological survey of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from [[Needles California]] to [[Morales Dam]] near the U.S.-Mexico border. Nineteen stations, covering main-channel, backwater and lake habitats, were sampled quarterly from July 1976 to April 1977. The study measured physical and chemical conditions, [[Phytoplankton]] [[Primary Productivity]] and standing crop ([[Chlorophyll A]]), and used trophic indices to characterize the river. The authors conclude that the river is largely oligotrophic and heavily affected by human water management, so fish populations may be limited by food-chain production. An accompanying August 1978 letter from the Department's water quality biologist answers the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]'s questions about filtration, incubation light and sampling depth methods.

## Background & Description

The lower Colorado River has been extensively altered for municipal, industrial, irrigation, hydroelectric and recreational use. Regulated reservoirs, diversion dams and interconnecting channels have replaced its historic seasonal flood regime, and flows no longer reach the ocean. Earlier work focused mainly on salinity, public health, and fish surveys, so the plankton and benthos were known only qualitatively. This study was designed to:

1. Measure basic physical-chemical parameters relevant to phytoplankton primary production.
2. Determine phytoplankton primary productivity and standing crop at selected river and backwater sites.
3. Describe the trophic status of the river and its relation to system dynamics.

**Methods (summary):** Sampling used a Kemmerer bottle within the euphotic zone, defined by [[Secchi Depth]]. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the Azide modification of the Winkler method, and pH, [[Total Dissolved Solids]], turbidity and alkalinity were measured in the field. Nutrients (total phosphorus, nitrate, nitrite, ammonia and organic nitrogen) were analyzed in the laboratory using standard methods. Primary productivity used the [[Carbon-14 Method]] with light-dark bottles under fluorescent light, and chlorophyll was measured spectrophotometrically. Statistical analyses (correlation and ANOVA) were run on California State Water Resources Control Board programs. The study was funded by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation under contract 14-00-300-2621 (as read from the scan).

**Agency correspondence:** The Bureau of Reclamation's regional environmental officer asked about methods. The Department's reply states:
- **Filtration:** Samples were filtered through 0.80 µm rather than 0.45 µm filters. The larger pore size was used because turbid samples clogged the finer filters. Nannoplankton probably contributed little in this river, but this could not be quantified.
- **Incubation light:** Bottles were incubated under constant fluorescent light in ambient-temperature river-water baths to standardize conditions. Light intensity and spectral quality were not investigated.
- **Depth:** Productivity samples came from the euphotic zone. Aphotic production (e.g., bacteria) may have added substantially to estimates, and composite surface and bottom samples were used in some cases because of sample-bottle and time limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting.** Across the study, water temperature ranged from 9 to 30 °C. Transparency varied from 15.24 to 609.60 cm, and turbidity from 1 to 52 Formazin Turbidity Units (FTU). pH ranged from 7.2 to 8.4, and total dissolved solids from 380 to 1100 mg/L. Dissolved oxygen was 5.8 to 12.0 mg/L, with an overall average of 8.8 mg/L. Nitrate ranged from 0.05 to 0.40 mg/L, and total phosphorus was generally low, with a maximum of 0.34 mg/L at Morales Dam in October 1976.

**Lotic versus lentic environments.** Lentic (backwater) stations were warmer, clearer (mean Secchi depth about twice that of lotic sites) and more variable from site to site. Lotic stations showed clear downstream trends in temperature, [[Total Dissolved Solids]], alkalinity and productivity. The [[Palo Verde Drain]] carried high TDS and nitrate, and its alkalinity was roughly three times that of other stations.

**Human influence.** The authors attribute much of the longitudinal pattern to reservoirs ([[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Mohave]]), upstream hypolimnetic releases, agricultural return flows, and the [[Imperial Diversion]]. Reduced flows below the diversion allow more standing water, higher temperature and less dilution of nutrient-rich wastewater. Pesticides and herbicides from agricultural drains may further suppress phytoplankton.

**Fish context.** Three native species are federally classified as endangered: the Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Sucker and Bonytail. The Desert Pupfish is a remnant native species still present in limited areas. Earlier surveys of backwaters found a variety of fish, but densities were judged by Department biologists to be lower than expected for a river of this type. The study did not measure fish populations directly.

## Key Findings

- **Study design:** 19 stations, sampled every three months for five-day periods from July 1976 to April 1977 (four sampling periods).
- **Primary productivity:** Mean 0.1394 mgC/m³/hr. The highest single estimate, 1.3501 mgC/m³/hr, occurred at Station 4 (Lake Havasu, Bill Williams Arm). The lowest station mean (0.0092 mgC/m³/hr) was at Senator Wash Reservoir, and the lowest single estimate (0.0034 mgC/m³/hr) was in the main channel above Needles (Station 1).
- **Standing crop:** Chlorophyll a never exceeded 0.0250 mg/L. The highest station means were in the Bill Williams Arm and Palo Verde Drain. Senator Wash Reservoir had the lowest mean.
- **Trophic state:** The [[Carlson Trophic State Index]] (Carlson 1977) gave a system-wide mean TSI of 41, which is just above the mesotrophic threshold (40). Most main-channel stations were oligotrophic. Lake Washington (TSI 65, with sewage effluent in 1957) was given as a comparison for a more eutrophic system.
- **Most eutrophic sites:** Main-channel stations 17, 18 and 19 below the Imperial Diversion, and station 5 below Parker Dam, were the most eutrophic lotic sites, linked to reduced flow and nutrient inputs from wastewater.
- **Seasonal patterns:** Productivity was highest in summer and lowest in winter. Chlorophyll patterns did not closely follow the productivity pattern.
- **Storm effect:** A tropical storm in October 1976 raised turbidity and TDS and produced the highest systemwide productivity average of the study, mainly in the upstream reaches and Lake Havasu.
- **Lentic heterogeneity:** Lentic stations showed no consistent longitudinal pattern, so one backwater's values were poor predictors of another's.
- **Conclusion:** Human activity heavily affects the lower Colorado River. Low primary production limits the food base for higher aquatic forms, so [[Fish Populations]] may be constrained by food-chain production. The authors call for more intensive work to document the effects of toxicants such as herbicides, insecticides and chlordane.
- **Limitations (from the letter):** Filter pore size, artificial incubation light, and euphotic-only sampling probably affected productivity estimates, and aphotic production may be underestimated.
