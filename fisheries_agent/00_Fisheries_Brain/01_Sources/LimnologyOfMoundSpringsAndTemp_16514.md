# Limnology and Fauna of Mound Springs and Temporary Pools South and West of Lake Eyre

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gobiidae #freshwater-ecology #invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This compilation of 1978 surveys by B.D. Mitchell (aquatic limnology) and Penelope Greenslade (terrestrial invertebrates) describes the physico-chemistry and fauna of [[Mound Springs]] in the arid north of South Australia, compared with [[Temporary Pools]], [[Artesian Bores]] and creek beds. Mound springs are permanent, slowly flowing, clear and moderately saline waters with a distinctive fauna, including the [[Desert Goby]] (*Chlamydogobius eremius*) and the isopod [[Phreatomerus Latipes]]. The authors conclude that grazing by stock degrades these habitats, and that fencing, and caution over capping bores, are key management considerations.

## Background & Description

Mound springs in the arid north of South Australia are a unique aquatic habitat. They are permanent, slowly flowing, clear and moderately saline (conductivities up to 9000 millimhos/cm), differing markedly from the ephemeral, highly saline lakes and turbid, seasonally inundated freshwater pools of the surrounding area. The fauna of springs was therefore expected to differ from adjacent fresh or slightly saline environments.

The limnological survey covered a group of springs from Horse Springs to Strangways Springs, from 30 September to 3 October 1978, with temporary freshwater localities sampled for comparison. Methods included:

- Field measurement of temperature and dissolved oxygen (YSI oxygen meter); laboratory conductivity at 25 °C (Radiometer meter), converted to field values after Bayly & Williams (1973).
- Invertebrate sampling by dip-nets, plankton nets, washing vegetation, sorting sediment and turning rocks.
- Fish sampling by dip nets and small cylindrical traps.

Terrestrial work (Greenslade) used vegetation beating and sweeping, suction sampling, pitfall traps, Tullgren funnel extraction of soil and litter cores, and hand collection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry**
- Spring water temperatures ranged from 14 to 30 °C, mostly 25–28 °C; seeps could be markedly warmer than main pools.
- Dissolved oxygen was above 15 ppm (supersaturated) in the main pools at Horse Springs and [[Blanche Cup]], while the pool at Hamilton Hill Homestead was 75% saturated, probably from night-time respiration of filamentous algae. The Bubbler was very low (23% saturated at 30 °C), suggesting oxygen-depleted underlying water.
- Conductivities of spring waters ranged from 5,600 to 8,970 millimhos (upper TDS about 6 °/oo); shallow seep pools reached 14,700 millimhos (about 10 °/oo). Conductivity was apparently unrelated to discharge or geographic position.
- Dissolved phosphate was very low, but nitrate was very high (up to 14 mg/l), consistent with organic pollution from stock defaecation.
- Major ions: Na⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻. Travertine (CaCO₃) deposition from pH/carbonate/bicarbonate equilibria forms the 'mounds'. Cation order Na⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺; anion order Cl⁻ > CO₃²⁻ > SO₄²⁻, with carbonate more prominent than in springs farther west.

**Stock impacts**
Stock have degraded many springs. Grazing removes macrophytes, which are the main primary producers and important for oxygenation and invertebrate habitat. Falling flow rates shrink pools, trampling fouls margins, and filamentous algal masses can cause oxygen depletion. The most degraded example, an unnamed spring near the Margaret River, held only chironomids.

**Faunal components**
The Mound Spring fauna has two parts. Widely dispersing taxa (Odonata, Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Diptera) are probably not endemic. A second group with poor dispersal and no resistant stages (hydrobiid gastropods, *Phreatomerus latipes*, an amphipod, *Chlamydogobius eremius*, and endemic ostracods) forms a unique mound spring assemblage absent from temporary pools. Temporary pools instead host desiccation-resistant cladocerans, conchostracans, anostracans, copepods and ostracods.

**Terrestrial context**
The survey area is extremely arid. Spring and bore faunas were compared with creek beds, Stuart, Anna and Margaret creeks, and nearby arid habitats such as gibber plains and dunes at [[Muloorina]].

## Key Findings

**Fish**
- Thirteen fish species are recorded from the Lake Frome Basin; about seven occur in natural artesian springs, including catfish (*Neosilurus* spp.), [[Gambusia Affinis]], [[Craterocephalus Stercusmuscarum]], [[Craterocephalus Eyresii]], [[Terapon Unicolor]], [[Mogurnda Mogurnda]] and the desert goby.
- The desert goby and, less often, the Lake Eyre hardyhead were found in springs. Both are common in springs and bores and often occur together.
- The desert goby tolerates salinity from 0.2 to 12.5 °/oo, direct exposure to 37 °/oo, and water up to 110 °/oo. It has been recorded at 9–40 °C and tolerates oxygen down to 0.8 mg/l, possibly by aerial respiration. It is omnivorous, feeds at night, and breeds November to March. Spawning was observed at Coward Springs on 3 October 1978.
- The Lake Eyre hardyhead tolerates oxygen only to about 6 mg/l and schools in open water.
- Floodwater is probably the most important dispersal agent in this low-relief landscape, with artesian bores also aiding dispersal.

**Aquatic invertebrates**
- Three cypridid ostracod species were found. An undescribed genus, *Heterocypris* sp., was the most widespread and benthic.
- [[Phreatomerus Latipes]] was widespread, sometimes at densities up to 800/m², with about 60% of individuals buried in sediment. Mating was observed during the survey, and brooding females carry up to 100 embryos.
- A small gammarid-type amphipod extends the inland northern limit of freshwater amphipods in Australia.
- The copepod *Nitocra lacustris* was recorded for the first time in Australia, an atyid prawn *Caridinia* sp. represents new records for the genus, and four or five undescribed hydrobiid gastropods were found.
- Microcrustacea were restricted to springs with deep pools and long retention times, such as Blanche Cup.

**Terrestrial invertebrates**
- 28 collembolan species were collected. Seven species typical of humid climates were restricted to springs, bores or creek lines, most probably introduced. Two isotomid species appear new, and *Stenognathellus* is a new South Australian record.
- Isotomidae are salt-tolerant, and many springs' species occur in European saline habitats.
- 26 ant species were recorded, with diversity highest in tree-lined creek beds. None nested near springs.
- Of 15 Heteroptera species, eight were found only at springs, including notonectids and corixids. A new Miridae species and genus were found, along with a second South Australian record of Meenoplidae.
- Tiger beetle *Megacephala australis* occurred near springs. A new species of *Buddelundia* isopod was found at a spring and a creek bed.
- Soil arthropod density at Welcome and Emerald springs was 814/m², much lower than arid-Australia values cited (2,720/m² and 2,200/m²).

**Habitat comparison and conclusions**
- Habitat richness ranked creek beds > springs > other habitats > bores. Bore faunas were low in diversity, partly derived from creek beds, contained an exotic element, and lacked waterside specialists.
- Mound springs are islands of moist habitat supporting waterside and mesic species absent from surrounding regions, rather than novel endemics.
- Stock grazing and trampling cause the main damage, and fencing is recommended to restore macrophytes and reduce pollution.
- Capping bores may raise flow and pool depth, aiding recolonisation, but very high flows could flush out planktonic populations in pools such as Blanche Cup.
- Isolation of goby populations following bore capping could cause divergence and reduce physiological tolerance.
- The authors recommend focused, quantitative sampling of single taxonomic groups, with general collections deposited in major museums.
