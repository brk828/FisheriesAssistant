# Turbulent Diffusion in Lake Huron

#limnology #fluid-dynamics #turbulent-diffusion

## Executive Summary

This paper by G. T. Csanady (University of Windsor) reports the summer 1962 field programme on [[Turbulent Diffusion]] at Douglas Point, [[Lake Huron]], run by the [[Great Lakes Institute]] (University of Toronto). Diffusion was tracked with floating objects (drift bottles, paper sheets and surface drogues) and with a continuous [[Rhodamine B]] dye plume. The main conclusions are that horizontal diffusion in the lake is much slower than in the lower atmosphere or the ocean, vertical diffusion is very slow, longitudinal diffusion is faster than lateral diffusion because of shear, and [[Meandering]] of the plume dominates large-scale dispersal. Surface [[Confluence]]s such as [[Slicks]] and [[Windrows]] can temporarily reverse diffusion of floating objects.

## Background & Description

**Study site.** Douglas Point, on the eastern shore of Lake Huron, was the location of the first Canadian nuclear reactor for commercial operation then under construction. Preliminary work was done in 1961, and further studies were proposed for 1963. The work was funded under contract for [[Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd]], with direction by Dr R. E. Deane.

**Floating-object experiments.** Two object types were used:
- Drift bottles: cylindrical jars 5 cm in diameter and 8 cm high, weighted to float with lids (painted fluorescent yellow) about level with the surface. Usually 60 were released per trial, with 1 to 3 lost per trial. They averaged velocity over the top 8 cm.
- Paper sheets (foolscap, mimeograph): floated within the top 1 cm. They drifted about 20% faster than the bottles but diffused identically within statistical scatter.
- Surface drogues: two metal sheets (60 cm deep by 90 cm long) at 90°, averaging velocity between about 25 and 85 cm depth. Eleven drogues were used in run no. 9 (10 July).

Group size was defined as the distance between the first and last objects along the longest diameter. For diffusion constants, group size was taken as four standard deviations, a check confirmed for drogues (usually within 10%).

**Positioning.** Two shore [[Transit Survey]] stations located objects. Accuracy was within 0.5 m under good conditions, with a probable error of 3 to 5 m in rough weather. Groups smaller than about 15 m were sized by eye from the boat.

**Dye experiments.** A constant-head siphon apparatus discharged dye at the surface from an instrument tower 800 m offshore, at about 1 to 5 gal/h (roughly 2 g/sec). The dye was Rhodamine B mixed with water and alcohol to specific gravity 1.0. Plumes were sampled from a 14 ft boat with a [[Fluorometer]] (Turner, constant-flow) and strip-chart recorder, and boat speed was measured with an Ott current meter. Calibration accuracy was within ±5%. Peak concentration was about 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ g/cc at 500 m from the source. Neutral density filters were needed close to the source, where the fluorescent signal could be up to 100 times stronger.

**Aerial photography.** Dye patches were photographed in 1961, and plumes on 15 and 20 August 1962, with the Royal Canadian Air Force. Visibility of dye in photographs was only qualitatively related to concentration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current regimes.** Plumes formed in steady, uniform currents, which were mostly wind-driven and usually parallel to the wind. Changing winds produced erratic, vertically sheared currents, and these were the cases where quantitative data could not be collected. Surface current reversed within minutes of a wind change, but momentum took longer to diffuse downward.

**Lake structure.** Depth in the study area ranged from 5 to 20 m. The thermocline averaged about 15 m in July and August, and developed during June. Douglas Point and the shore created some streamline crowding, and frictional shore effects extended 250 to 800 m offshore. The authors judged these effects unlikely to have changed the diffusion results appreciably.

**Surface features.** [[Slicks]] formed where surface flow converged toward the shore, roughly 2 to 3 m wide and possibly many miles long, and were especially common before the thermocline developed. [[Windrows]] formed under moderately strong winds, parallel to the wind, with spacing of about 2 to 3 m. The authors attribute windrows to helical vortices (see [[Langmuir Circulation]]), which may help maintain the thermocline. Patches of smooth and ruffled water moved at speeds consistent with internal waves, but no direct evidence was found and any waves were less than 0.5 m in amplitude.

**Theoretical framework.** Diffusion was interpreted using the [[Batchelor Theory]] of [[Relative Diffusion]] for homogeneous turbulence, which gives three growth regimes (initial, intermediate, asymptotic). The intermediate regime follows [[Richardson Law]] of accelerating growth with cloud size. Shear effects on longitudinal spread were interpreted using [[Taylor Dispersion]] in pipes and the [[Elder Formula]] for open channels. Key parameters are [[Gustiness]] (u′/U) and the turbulence length scale L.

## Key Findings

**1. Relative diffusion of floating objects.** In about half of the experiments (regular dispersal, free of confluences), groups grew with an accelerating rate, consistent with relative turbulent diffusion. Diffusion constants (Table 2 in the paper, eight runs) gave:
- Asymptotic longitudinal diffusivity estimate: about 400 cm²/s (order-of-magnitude).
- Turbulence length scale L: consistently about 10 m.
- Gustiness: about 6 to 7% (order-of-magnitude).
- Individual asymptotic diffusivities ranged from 115 to 1200 cm²/s. Their variation was attributed mostly to statistical scatter, with no clear link to measured wind or meteorological variables. A weak negative correlation between current speed and gustiness was noted.

**2. Confluences can reverse diffusion.** [[Slicks]] (run no. 2, 28 May) captured objects and reversed dispersal. Transitory slicks (run no. 6, 21 June) produced large fluctuations in group size. Windrows dominated run no. 9 (10 July), where the 11 drogues showed no net diffusion over the experiment, although their distribution approached Gaussian (100 readings) when averaged over time.

**3. Lateral dye diffusion is slow.** The lateral diffusivity of dye was low, close to the values from floating objects. Diffusivity in the horizontal was about 10⁵ times... (the authors state horizontal rates were only of order 10 times molecular diffusivity in the vertical, and much less than atmospheric or oceanic values horizontally). A typical plume at 600 m had a horizontal diameter of about 60 m.

**4. Vertical diffusion is very slow.** Effective vertical diffusivities were 0.1 to 1.5 cm²/s, not far above the molecular value of about 0.01 cm²/s. At 600 m from the source the plume was about 60 m wide but only about 1.5 m deep. Vertical turbulent diffusivity is expected to increase with depth, following K_z = m·u*·z.

**5. Longitudinal diffusion is faster than lateral.** Shear-enhanced spreading along the current, estimated from Elder's formula with u* = 0.5 m/s at a 5 m/s wind and an effective depth of about 10 m, gives a longitudinal diffusivity of about 3000 cm²/s. This is somewhat below an order of magnitude greater than the observed lateral value. Aerial patch analysis from 1961 suggested about 2000 cm²/s, about five times the lateral value.

**6. Meandering dominates large-scale dispersal.** [[Meandering]] (bodily displacement of the plume centre) was measured from aerial photos. Its standard deviation grew nearly linearly with distance to 2100 m with no sign of levelling off, implying very large horizontal eddies (plume-wavelength scale of about 1000 m). Because of this, a fixed-point instrument would record strong concentration peaks but a 4 to 5 h average would be low. The authors conclude that meandering is far more efficient than relative diffusion for dispersal in the lake, and that relative diffusion can often be neglected in first approximation.

**7. Sampling considerations.** Meandering variance increases strongly with sampling time, so comparisons required a fixed averaging period (about 3 h, with 12 photos at roughly 15 min intervals, at minimum). Fixed-point diffusion measurements therefore carry sampling-time errors that relative diffusion avoids.

**8. Methodological limits.** Some group sizes were underestimated late in bottle trials because stragglers were hard to find, and plume widths may have been overestimated by up to 10% from current-meter distance readings. Lack of time and suitable weather limited depth profiles to about 3 m.

**Conclusion.** Turbulent diffusion in Lake Huron is slower horizontally and much slower vertically than in the ocean or atmosphere. Dispersal of a surface release is controlled mostly by meandering and shear-driven longitudinal spreading, with surface confluences able to reverse or suppress dispersal on the timescale of hours.
