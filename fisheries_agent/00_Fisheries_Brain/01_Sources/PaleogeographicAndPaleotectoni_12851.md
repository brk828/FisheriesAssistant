# Paleogeographic and Paleotectonic Setting of Laramide Sedimentary Basins in the Central Rocky Mountain Region

#paleolimnology #sedimentary-geology #laramide-orogeny

## Executive Summary

This Geological Society of America Bulletin paper (Dickinson and others, 1988) synthesizes the sedimentary history of Laramide basins between central Montana and central New Mexico. It groups the basins into three paleogeographic classes (perimeter, axial, and ponded), applies twelve stratigraphic criteria to date the onset and end of [[Laramide Orogeny]] deformation, and reconstructs Paleogene [[Paleodrainage]] patterns. The authors conclude that Laramide deformation began synchronously in the Maastrichtian, ended diachronously from north to south during the Eocene, and that large lakes in the ponded basins may have drained westward to the Pacific Northwest. The document is a research paper rather than a fish study. Its only aquatic-biological content is a brief reference to shared fish and mollusc faunas in two Eocene lakes.

## Background & Description

The Laramide orogeny produced basement-cored uplifts and intervening sediment-filled basins across a broad area east of the [[Sevier Overthrust Belt]]. Before deformation, a broad Late Cretaceous retroarc [[Cretaceous Interior Seaway]] foreland basin had spread marine and deltaic facies laterally across the region. Onset of Laramide deformation fragmented this foreland into sedimentologically isolated nonmarine basins. Structural relief between basin floors and adjacent uplift crests was commonly 5 to 10 km.

The authors classify the basins by paleogeography rather than by the morphostructural scheme of Chapin and Cather (1981, 1983):

- **Perimeter basins** are broad, gently relieved bowls on the eastern cratonic fringe, drained toward the Great Plains. Fills are about 1,000 m thick in the southern basins, more than 2,000 m in the [[Powder River Basin]], and locally near 5,000 m in the [[Crazy Mountains Basin]].
- **Axial basins** are small, elongate, and en echelon, with high structural relief and conglomeratic facies. They lie along a north-south intramontane trend later occupied by the [[Rio Grande Rift]]. Depocenters received 1,200 to 2,000 m of Paleogene stream deposits, except the [[Hanna Basin]], which holds perhaps 5,000 m.
- **Ponded basins** lie farther west, near the overthrust belt. Their fluvial drainages were blocked at times, forming large freshwater or saline lakes. Fills are generally 3,000 to 5,000 m thick and include organic-rich lacustrine shale, with lacustrine facies mainly accumulating between mid-Paleocene and mid-Eocene time.

Chronology relies on the authors' twelve deformational criteria (Figure 6), applied uniformly across the province. They include the first appearance of isopachs defining a local depocenter, locally derived clasts, lacustrine facies, coarse alluvial fans, unconformities, and the start of migratory arc volcanism. Geohistory plots could not resolve discrete Laramide subsidence pulses, so the timing rests on these stratigraphic criteria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Ponded basins hosted some of the largest Paleogene lakes in the region. [[Lake Gosiute]] occupied large parts of the [[Green River Basin]] and [[Washakie Basin]], and [[Lake Uinta]] occupied the [[Uinta Basin]] and [[Piceance Creek Basin]]. Lacustrine interpretation remains contested. One model proposes deep stratified lakes with anaerobic bottoms that preserved organic-rich laminae. The other proposes shallow playa lakes with organic production on algal mudflats. The authors favor broad perennial lakes with marginal shoals and mudflats, with lake level fluctuating between deep-lake and playa-salt-pan end members.

Paleoclimate was warm. Mammalian faunas indicate warm temperate to subtropical conditions, which the authors use to infer elevations below about 300 m for the Eocene lakes. Fish and mollusc faunas shared by Lakes Gosiute and Uinta (Hansen, 1985) confirm connections between the two lake systems. The fish faunas show affinities mainly with the Mississippi River drainage, with some genera also recorded in Pacific Northwest Paleogene lacustrine deposits.

Water balance was a key question. The volume of evaporites in the lacustrine facies seems too small to account for all dissolved salts delivered over millions of years, which suggests that waters periodically left the lakes during high stands.

## Key Findings

1. **Basin classification:** The three-group paleogeographic scheme (perimeter, axial, ponded) differs from earlier morphostructural classifications.
2. **Onset of deformation:** Laramide deformation began approximately synchronously across the central province during the Maastrichtian (65 to 75 Ma). The authors find no systematic areal diachroneity at inception, and the same conclusion appears in a parallel analysis by [[Cross]] (1986).
3. **Termination of deformation:** Termination was systematically diachronous from north to south. Where the post-Laramide hiatus is short, the tectonic transition is dated to the early-middle Eocene boundary (50 to 55 Ma) in the north and to the end of the Eocene (35 to 40 Ma) in the south.
4. **Volcanic markers:** Middle Eocene [[Absaroka Volcanic Supergroup]] (mainly 46 to 49 Ma, overall 43 to 52 Ma) caps Laramide successions in northwest Wyoming. Oligocene volcanics cap them in Colorado and grade into Oligocene volcaniclastic strata in New Mexico.
5. **Paleodrainage:** Paleocene drainage was generally eastward toward the continental interior. By mid-Eocene time, several ponded basins drained into [[Lake Uinta]], and the Uinta basin probably acted as a lacustrine sump.
6. **Pacific outlet hypothesis:** Lake Uinta's outlet was probably a westward river through the extinct overthrust belt, possibly the "Tyee" paleoriver that built a deltaic complex in coastal Oregon (Heller and others, 1987). The authors consider this conjectural but the only feasible drainage route, given the blocking topographic barriers to the east, north, south, and southeast.
7. **Subduction control:** Diachronous termination from north to south supports the argument that subduction of a linear aseismic ridge controlled Laramide events, linked to the [[Subducted Slab]] geometry.
8. **Plateau rotation:** Clockwise rotation of the [[Colorado Plateau]] may have contributed to strain, but the authors argue that structures formed within a generally synchronous, complex strain field rather than in distinct phases.
9. **Post-Laramide elevation:** Much of the present elevation of the province is attributed to Neogene mantle-driven processes, not to Laramide thickening alone.

The authors also note that Laramide basin chronology rests on criteria that are individually interpretive, with margins of error that cannot be determined. Conclusions are considered valid only where grouped criteria agree.
