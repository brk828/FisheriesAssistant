# Mineral Return via the Litter of Artemisia Tridentata

#botany #plant-ecology #artemisia

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies litter production and mineral return by [[Artemisia Tridentata]] (big sagebrush) in the arid shrub-steppe of S-central Washington. Using litter traps placed around individual shrubs, the author measured annual litter fall, estimated its nutrient content, and followed nutrient release from decomposing leaves in the field. Sagebrush shed about 117 kg/ha of leaf and inflorescence litter per year, returning only about 1.2 to 1.4 kg/ha of key elements annually. Its litter is a small nutrient flux compared with the large soil pool, but it may still help recycle nutrients into the surface soil. The paper also reports that the leaf litter loses potassium fastest and calcium slowest during the first year of decay.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in [[Mineral Cycling]] research. Most cycling studies in terrestrial systems have focused on trees, and shrubs in arid regions had been little quantified. The author notes that about 600,000 km² of the intermountain USA supports *A. tridentata*, the most widespread North American sagebrush, yet no mineral cycling studies existed for this species.

Earlier litter studies usually collected litter from the whole shrub community. That approach gives total nutrient flux but cannot separate the contribution of each species and is prone to sampling error. The author instead followed the [[Calluna Vulgaris]] litter-trap approach and built traps around individual shrubs. This allowed known individuals to be sampled and changes in the proportions of plant parts in litter to be tracked through time.

The study area was the [[Arid Lands Ecology Preserve]] on the [[Hanford Reservation]], at about 430 m elevation on Rattlesnake Hill. Public access had been prohibited for about 30 years, and the site had been little grazed and reported free of fire since AEC control, so it was considered relatively undisturbed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The site lies in [[Shrub-Steppe]] vegetation. Precipitation averages about 16 cm per year, falling mainly between October and May. Summer dry spells of more than a month are common, temperatures of 40 °C or more occur, and frost can occur in at least six months of the year. The main habitat types were the *A. tridentata*-[[Agropyron Spicatum]] habitat type, where litter production was measured, and the nearby *A. tridentata*-[[Poa Secunda]] habitat type, where decomposition was followed.

Sagebrush litter also affects other plants. The paper cites evidence that [[Bromus Tectorum]] grows more vigorously and has higher nutrient content beneath sagebrush canopies, partly because of nutrients added in sagebrush litter. The author argues that wholesale eradication of sagebrush to improve rangeland could remove this benefit.

Soil data came from two auger sites sampled in August 1973. The soil is [[Ritzville Silt Loam]], with an average bulk density of 1.2 g/cc in the top metre. Available nutrients to 1 m depth were large: Ca 21,936 kg/ha, Mg 4,450 kg/ha, P 127.2 kg/ha, and K 3,588 kg/ha.

## Key Findings

**Sampling design**
- In March 1972, nylon net-canvas [[Litter Trap]]s were installed around 20 individual shrubs. Wind tore 10 traps, so data come from 10 shrubs.
- Each trap had a 1.2 × 1.2 m canvas base, 15 cm sides, and netting to 1.2 m height. The author reports that the netting did not create a measurable microclimate difference.
- Litter was collected weekly during peak leaf drop and irregularly afterward, hand-sorted, oven-dried at 105 °C, and weighed.
- In July 1973, the 10 shrubs were harvested. Ages (7 to 26 years, from xylem ring counts), [[Canopy Volume]] (0.8 to 4.6 m³ in Table 1), and phytomass were recorded.

**Litter production**
- Annual leaf litter of individual shrubs (March 1972 to March 1973) was closely predicted by canopy volume. The regression was y = 18.657 + 282.154X (r² = 0.7582), with X in m³.
- Extrapolated to the 70 shrubs in an adjacent 10 × 20 m plot, annual leaf litter was 107.7 kg/ha/yr.
- Inflorescence litter was estimated separately because production was highly variable, ranging from 0 to 23% of individual litter totals. The 10 enclosed shrubs produced 96.4 g/yr of inflorescence litter, giving an estimate of about 10 kg/ha/yr. Combined litter is therefore about 117 kg/ha/yr.
- Litter was about 91% vegetative and 9% reproductive by weight.
- Major leaf fall occurs in late June to early July. Summer leaves are large (>30 mm), while autumn and winter litter is under 10% of the summer total by weight and consists of smaller leaves.
- Inflorescences fall in winter, often driven by wind and snow. Stem litter is a very small part of annual fall.
- Age did not correlate directly with canopy volume, partly because neighbours suppressed growth.

**Nutrient content and return**
- Summer leaf litter had nutrient concentrations similar to winter litter. Reported July versus March values were Ca 1.1 vs 0.8%, Mg 0.32 vs 0.16%, P 0.13 vs 0.15%, and K 1.3 vs 0.52%. The author attributes some differences to leaching while leaves sat in traps.
- Annual return from leaves plus inflorescences, in kg/ha (Table 2), was 1.198, 0.342, 0.164, and 1.366 for the four elements. Leaves contributed most of the total, and the table columns are ordered differently from the text.
- Based on phytomass nutrient content, the proportion of each element returned annually via leaf litter ranged from 10–42% (Ca), 5–18% (P), 5–19% (K), and 10–39% (Mg).

**Decomposition**
- Leaf litter placed in [[Litter-Bag Technique]] mesh bags lost about 50% of its dry weight in the first year.
- All four elements were released faster than dry weight. K loss was the most rapid, and it peaked in autumn, coinciding with the resumption of rain.
- The mobility order was K > Mg > P = Ca. The author attributes slow Ca loss to calcium pectinate in cell walls and P loss to resorption into the plant before abscission.
- Inflorescences decayed almost completely within six months of abscission.
- The author identifies [[Eleodes Hispilabris]] (Tenebrionidae) as the main litter pulverizer in the first year. The mesh may hinder beetle foraging, so the 50% first-year decay value is treated as approximate. The litter bags became unreliable after one year because weathered litter was lost through the mesh during handling.

**Comparisons and conclusions**
- Sagebrush produces far less litter per hectare than comparable Australian shrubs and trees: [[Eremophila Gilesii]] about 500 kg/ha, *Acacia aneura* 260 kg/ha, and *Atriplex vesicaria* 1,094 kg/ha.
- Total ash elements returned by sagebrush litter are small relative to the soil nutrient pool. The author suggests sagebrush may still increase the depth of soil from which nutrients are recycled to the surface, which benefits shallow-rooted associates.
- Soil surface nutrient status under sagebrush did not differ significantly from adjacent open areas, in agreement with earlier work by Fireman and Hayward (1952).
- The author concludes that canopy volume is a better predictor of community litter fall than basal area or canopy cover, and that extrapolation works for leaf litter but not reliably for inflorescences.
