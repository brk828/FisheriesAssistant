# Influence of Physico-Chemical Factors on Habitat Selection by Red Shiners (*Notropis lutrensis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how [[Physico-Chemical Factors]] shape [[Habitat Selection]] in the [[Red Shiner]] (*[[Notropis Lutrensis]]*), a small cyprinid of [[Great Plains]] streams. The authors combined [[Field Study]] distribution data with [[Laboratory Preference]] tests in [[Gradient Chamber]] apparatus, covering [[Temperature]], [[Dissolved Oxygen]], pH, [[Total Dissolved Solids]] (TDS), and [[Turbidity]], plus [[Current Speed]], depth, [[Shelter]], [[Shade]], and [[Substrate]]. Field work ran over five seasonal periods (February 1976 to February 1977) in the [[South Canadian River]] and [[Pond Creek]]. The authors conclude that temperature, current speed, and water depth had the greatest influence on habitat selection, while dissolved oxygen, turbidity, shelter, shade, and substrate were of lesser importance.

## Background & Description

The Red Shiner is the most abundant cyprinid in [[Oklahoma]] and is common across the central United States. It is most abundant in streams where conditions are too harsh for many other fish species (Cross, 1967). The species has wide physico-chemical tolerances (Matthews and Hill, 1977), but the authors argue that the ability to select favorable parts of available habitat would be advantageous for a species that thrives in harsh environments.

Earlier work had shown that fish respond to temperature, oxygen, pH, and salinity in laboratory gradients, and that physico-chemical factors influence habitat selection in the field. Few studies, however, had combined quantitative field distribution data with laboratory preference results across several variables. Many of those that did examined only temperature.

The study used the following terms consistently:
- **Preference** and **avoidance** indicate only that more or fewer fish than expected occurred in a given range of conditions.
- Fish were tested as **field-acclimatized** animals, following the [[Preferendum]] approach rather than a final preferendum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**
- **South Canadian River:** A 600 m reach near Norman. It is a typical shallow Great Plains stream with drastic seasonal fluctuations. It exceeded 150 m in width in winter and spring but shrank to a series of pools in summer. The main channel was no deeper than 0.9 m during sampling, and the sand riverbed was unstable.
- **Pond Creek:** A tributary (McClain County) whose lowest 200 m were sampled. It was more permanent, with dirt banks 1–2 m high, bordered by trees and tall grasses. The creek was about 10 m wide and 0.5 m deep for most of the year, but it fragmented into small pools in late summer and autumn.

No known point-source pollution was present, and the site was relatively inaccessible to the public. Sharp physico-chemical gradients arose from uneven thermal effects, photosynthesis, decomposition, and variable current speed.

**Water chemistry (river/creek, 1 February 1976):** bicarbonate 240/260 ppm; carbonate 0/0 ppm; sulfate 27/61 ppm; chloride 16/0 ppm; silica 10/5 ppm. Laboratory test water was non-chlorinated deep-well water with bicarbonate of about 354 ppm.

**Predators:** Little Blue Herons were common at the site, and shallow water was avoided partly because of predation risk.

**Co-occurring species:** The Arkansas River Shiner (*Notropis girardi*) and other cyprinids shared backwater pools with Red Shiners.

## Key Findings

### Field methods
- Study periods: February–March 1976, May 1976, August–September 1976, October–November 1976, and December 1976–February 1977.
- Sampling points per period: 100, 100, 89, 97, and 98 (484 total). Adjacent points were at least 15 m apart.
- Fish were collected by a "best effort" downstream seine sweep of 5 m, using a 1.22 × 3.05 m seine with 0.32 cm mesh.
- Habitat variables were scored as follows:
  - **Shelter:** none, minimal, or substantial.
  - **Shade:** none, up to 50%, or 50% or more.
  - **Substrate:** no sand, mixed, or all sand.
- Temperature, oxygen, and pH were split into low, medium-low, medium-high, and high subranges. Observed counts were compared with expected counts using goodness-of-fit tests, and results were expressed as percent deviation from expected.

### Laboratory methods
- Test fish were held in aerated styrofoam boxes and used within 48 hours. No individual was used more than once.
- Gradient chambers were divided into four sections, with each section supplied from a separate reservoir.
- Each fish's position was photographed at 35 exposures per test.
- A control with normal flow but no gradient showed a significant departure from uniform distribution (P < 0.005), so the uniform distribution was used as the expected pattern.

### Principal results
- **Temperature:** Red Shiners consistently avoided thermal extremes. In August field samples they selected high-temperature locations, and in the laboratory they selected 26.5–35.1 °C while avoiding water warmer than 36 °C. In October–November they showed a field preference for 11.9–13.1 °C, which matched the laboratory preference. Temperature had the greatest influence on habitat selection in August–September. Cold torpor below 6 °C may have reduced responses to external stimuli in winter.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Fish avoided supersaturated oxygen in both field and laboratory tests (laboratory gradients up to about 20 ppm). Oxygen had little effect on distribution in the field except in certain periods, and its influence was weak overall.
- **pH:** Field selection of pH 8.2–9.5 in October was strong, but laboratory preference was for pH 7.1–7.4 and the species avoided pH values above and below this range in tests. Overall, pH had a weak but possibly important influence, and gradients were not large enough to test fully.
- **Total dissolved solids:** Field TDS ranges (about 500–700 ppm) were too narrow to influence habitat selection in most periods. Laboratory tests, however, showed preference for TDS concentrations similar to those where fish were collected.
- **Turbidity:** Field and laboratory results conflicted. Fish strongly selected turbid water (98–158 JTU) in laboratory tests and in some field periods, but the authors judged the effect to be negligible overall and considered that turbid-water selection in the laboratory could be a shelter-seeking behavior.
- **Current speed:** The most consistent factor. Fish selected low current speeds (0–7 cm/s) in all periods and avoided turbulent flow.
- **Depth:** Shallow water (under about 15–21 cm) was avoided in most periods, while depths of about 5–33 cm were selected in some periods.
- **Shelter, shade, and substrate:** Red Shiners selected locations with minimal to moderate shelter but did not seek substantial shelter. They preferred shaded locations in August and October and unshaded locations in December–January, largely because shade was correlated with shelter and temperature. Sand bottoms were avoided in favor of mixed or sand-free substrates.
- **Congregation:** About 90.7% of fish collected in October were crowded into backwater pools, where temperatures fluctuated only 1–2 °C daily.

### Interpretation
- The authors ranked the influence of variables as follows: temperature, current speed, and water depth had the greatest impact on red shiner habitat selection, followed by pH and TDS when they occurred over substantial gradients. Dissolved oxygen, turbidity, shelter, shade, and substrate were of lesser importance.
- Habitat selection depends on the full set of conditions at a given time. Organisms seldom respond to single factors.
- The authors reanalyzed field data using only stations with depth above 10 cm and current speed under 15 cm/s. Results did not change, which suggests the main trends were robust.
- Field and laboratory results agreed on temperature, current speed, and TDS in some periods, but turbidity and pH results were mixed, so the authors described these as inconclusive.

### Conclusions
- Red Shiners tolerate harsh conditions but still discriminate among habitats, and their selection is strongest for temperature and current speed.
- Thermal stability, backwater refuge, and access to low-velocity water appear to be important for persistence in fluctuating Great Plains streams.
- Combining field distribution data with laboratory preference tests strengthens inferences about habitat requirements, though contradictory results (notably for turbidity) show the limits of either method alone.
