# Redescription of Tryonia Stimpson, 1865 and Pyrgulopsis Call and Pilsbry, 1886 (Hydrobiidae)

#malacology #freshwater-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This systematic paper by [[Robert Hershler]] and [[Fred G. Thompson]] redescribes two freshwater snail genera of the family [[Hydrobiidae]], [[Tryonia]] and [[Pyrgulopsis]], using anatomical data from the type species of each and of related taxa. The authors reassign several genera and species to these two genera, resolving long-standing uncertainty about the group's relationships in southwestern North America. The paper is a taxonomic revision rather than an ecological study, so ecological content is limited to habitat and distribution notes.

## Background & Description

North American Hydrobiidae were described as poorly understood, with about 28 genera and 148 species recorded by [[Burch 1982]]. Anatomical work had been done mainly on southeastern taxa by [[Frederick G. Thompson]]. The arid Southwest was largely unstudied: of the generic-group taxa there, only [[Fluminicola]] had received enough morphological study to clarify its relationships. [[Tryonia]] and [[Fontelicella]] together comprise more than 30 species in the Southwest, mostly undescribed.

Prior work placed Tryonia in the [[Littoridininae]] on the basis of its turreted shell and glandular penial lobes. Fontelicella and its subgenera [[Natricola]] and [[Microamnicola]] had been placed in the [[Nymphophilinae]]. The penis of the type species of Pyrgulopsis, P. nevadensis, had not been studied anatomically before this paper.

**Methods.** Specimens were dissected in dilute Bouin's solution at 50x under a dissecting microscope. Ciliation patterns on tentacles and penes were examined by scanning electron microscopy after dehydration in ethanol and critical-point drying. Shells and radulae were cleaned and photographed by SEM. Most material was fully relaxed alcohol-preserved material. The P. nevadensis material was rehydrated dried bodies, and P. archimedis was unrelaxed.

**Tryonia diagnosis (summary):**
- Shell colorless and transparent, elongate-conic to turreted, 1.7-7.0 mm tall, with 4.0-8.0 whorls.
- Pronounced sexual dimorphism, with males often about half the shell height of females.
- Central radular tooth broader than tall, with 1-3 pairs of basal cusps.
- Penis flattened, elongate and slender, with one enlarged mammiform basal lobe and 1-4 smaller glandular lobes.
- Females ovoviviparous, brooding 3-15 embryos in an enlarged capsule gland.

**Pyrgulopsis diagnosis (summary):**
- Shell globose to elongate-conic, 1.2-8.0 mm tall, with 3.0-6.0 whorls.
- Protoconch partly or totally covered with wrinkled pits.
- Penis with a small distal lobe and narrow, elongate filament; one to fifteen glandular ridges, sometimes on stalked crests.
- Females oviparous, with a capsule gland having two tissue sections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species discussed live in freshwater habitats. Tryonia occurs in much of Florida and in the arid Southwest, including parts of California, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and northern Mexico. Study material came from springs such as Moapa Springs (Clark County, Nevada), Alexander Springs and Lake Dora (Lake County, Florida), and a small spring at Tierra Blanca near Cuatro Cienegas, Coahuila, Mexico. Pyrgulopsis is distributed across much of eastern North America, throughout western North America, and in parts of northern Mexico. Sampled localities include Pyramid Lake (Nevada), Upper Klamath Lake (Oregon), Campo Creek (California), a creek near Crenshaw Lake (Michigan), and the Meramec River (Missouri).

The authors note that Pyrgulopsis nevadensis has long been considered endangered (Taylor, 1970). It is known from a single locality, [[Pyramid Lake]] in Nevada, where a limnological survey by [[Galat et al. 1981]] found no live individuals. Neither did a more recent visit by Thompson, so the species may now be extinct. The authors also suggest the minute size of P. manantiali likely explains the stunted appearance of its bursa copulatrix complex.

## Key Findings

1. **Generic reassignments.** The paper makes the following changes:
   - *Hyalopyrgus* Thompson, 1968 (endemic to Florida) is assigned to *Tryonia*. Its type species is *Bythinella aequicostata*.
   - *Fontelicella*, *Microamnicola*, *Natricola*, *Marstonia* (widespread in eastern North America), and *Mexistiobia* (northern Mexico) are assigned to *Pyrgulopsis*.

2. **Relationships.** Tryonia is closely related to a group of North and Central American littoridinine genera with elongate-conic shells and mammiform glandular penial lobes. These include *Aphaostracon*, *Littoridinops*, *Mexipyrgus*, and *Pyrgophorus*. Tryonia is separated from them by its turreted shell and the unique position of its penial lobes. Pyrgulopsis is a nymphophiline and is most similar to [[Cincinnatia]] from eastern North America, which has a larger, broader shell and a more complex penis.

3. **Diagnostic character.** The only character that distinguishes the species assigned to Pyrgulopsis and the other taxa treated as congeneric is a peripheral carina on the shell. Pyrgulopsis is otherwise highly diverse in shell and penial morphology, and its species intergrade across regions.

4. **Species list.** Tryonia includes about nine names, among them *T. clathrata* (type species), *T. aequicostata*, and *T. brevissima*. Pyrgulopsis includes about twenty names, among them *P. nevadensis* (type species), *P. archimedis*, *P. lustrica*, *P. micrococcus*, and *P. manantiali*. Fossil species assigned to Marstonia and Fontelicella by earlier authors were excluded.

5. **Reproductive differences.** Tryonia is ovoviviparous, while Pyrgulopsis is oviparous. Both genera share a similar bursa copulatrix complex in the Florida and Southwestern species of Tryonia, which supports placing them in one genus.

6. **Unresolved issues.** The identity of the Central and South American taxa previously assigned to Tryonia (Taylor, 1966) remains uncertain because they have not been studied anatomically. Anatomical data for P. nevadensis are limited to dried material. The authors note that the penis of P. nevadensis could be described only partly.
