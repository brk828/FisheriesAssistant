# Movement Ecology of Imperilled Fish in a Novel Ecosystem: River-Reservoir Movements by Razorback Sucker and Translocations to Aid Conservation

#ichthyology #Catostomidae #endangered_species #movement_ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endangered, endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is long-lived and exhibits a periodic life-history strategy, hypothesized to have evolved bet-hedging strategies to capitalize on environmental variation across broad spatial and temporal extents. Fish in this study averaged 485 ± 57 mm TL (mean ± SD). The species experiences a recruitment bottleneck in early life stages. Age, maturity parameters, fecundity, and specific growth rates are not reported in this document. Razorback sucker are sexually mature and ripe during late winter–early spring when temperatures and flows approach spawning conditions.

**Habitat Associations**: Razorback sucker use a variety of habitats including mainstem rivers, smaller tributaries, floodplain wetlands, and reservoirs. The species occupies novel ecosystems created by damming, including [[Lake Powell]] and associated river fragments. Fish utilize lacustrine-transition zones (inflow areas) of reservoirs extensively. Spawning habitat occurs in upstream river reaches, including areas with rapids and cobble bars approximately 30–40 km upstream of the [[Piute Farms Waterfall]] in the [[San Juan River]]. The species has access to spatially and temporally disjunct habitats, historically including the Colorado River Delta, volcanically impounded reaches, and lentic environments created by high water events. Contemporary habitat includes river-reservoir complexes with variable connectivity: the [[Colorado River]] arm offers unimpeded access to upstream habitat (~299 km of inundation), while the San Juan River arm is blocked by a 6 m waterfall approximately 30 km upstream of the reservoir transition zone.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Extensive long-distance movements occurred within and across river and reservoir habitats. Of 722 fish captured in the [[Colorado River Arm]] of Lake Powell (2014–2016), 36% (n = 107 of 295 re-encountered fish) moved outside the capture area, including upstream into the Colorado or [[Green River]] systems or across the reservoir to the San Juan River arm. Fourteen fish moved more than 600 km. The distribution of movement distances showed significant leptokurtosis (kurtosis = 3.86, P = 0.01), indicating most individuals moved relatively short distances while a smaller contingent made longer movements. Fifty-six fish were detected at [[Tusher Diversion]] weir (~300 km upstream), and 14 individuals were detected approximately 600 km upstream in the upper Green River. Seventeen acoustically tagged fish moved through lacustrine habitat from the Colorado River arm to the San Juan River arm (≥170 km). Fish at large for longer periods were more likely to be encountered outside the capture area (GLM: likelihood ratio = 57.8, P < 0.001), but fish total length was not a significant predictor (likelihood ratio = 3.2, P = 0.08). Distance moved was not correlated with days at large (r = −0.08, df = 105, P = 0.39) or fish total length (r = 0.04, df = 105, P = 0.67).

In the [[San Juan River Arm]] of Lake Powell, annual movements between reservoir and river habitats were documented. In 2017, 29% (95% CI [21–36%]) of 147 reservoir-captured fish moved upstream 30–40 km to the waterfall within one year. In 2018, 20% (95% CI [12–30%]) of 74 fish made similar movements. These proportions represent minimum estimates given high detection probabilities (0.6–0.9) at the waterfall PIT-tag antenna.

**Translocation Outcomes**: Between 2016 and 2017, 303 razorback sucker were translocated upstream of the waterfall barrier (152 in 2016, 151 in 2017; 492 ± 39 mm TL). In 2016, nine of 152 fish (6%) were re-encountered in the river; eight moved at least 99 km upstream, with one fish moving 307 km to the [[PNM Diversion]]. Minimum distance moved upstream averaged 218 km (range: 99–307 km), and minimum residence time ranged from 17 to 536 days. In 2017, 20 of 151 fish (13%) were re-encountered in the river, with minimum distances of 17–186 km and residence times of 13–132 days. Fish were not detected as far upstream in 2017 compared with 2016. Critically, 80% of translocated fish from 2016 (123 of 152) and 79% from 2017 (119 of 151) returned downstream of the waterfall within one year. The same number of fish (n = 27) in both years were never re-encountered after translocation.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Not reported.

**Environmental Drivers**: Movements were associated with spawning behavior, occurring during periods when temperatures and flows approached spawning conditions (late winter–early spring). Fish aggregated below the waterfall in spawning condition (expressing eggs or milt). The waterfall has presented a complete barrier to upstream movement since 2001 (except for a 2-week inundation in July–August 2011) and periodically formed between 1992 and 2001. Mean annual discharge from the Colorado and Green rivers combined was approximately eight times that of the San Juan River (2014–2018). Access to upstream habitat varies by river system due to eight relatively large diversion structures with varying fish passage capabilities.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: All razorback sucker in Lake Powell were stocked from upstream rivers and moved downstream into the reservoir. Between 2002 and 2015, 627 of 722 fish captured in the Colorado River arm had stocking records, with total basin-wide stocking ranging from 11,941 (2002) to 59,970 (2010) fish annually. Most fish were stocked in the Green River (n = 574) and Colorado River (n = 37), with 33 from the Gunnison River. Recruitment to adulthood is rarely documented in areas with most access to river habitat, suggesting connectivity alone does not ensure recovery. The species experiences a recruitment bottleneck in early life stages. Untagged fish captured in the reservoir either match proportions from upstream rivers (tag loss) or overlap with year classes stocked without PIT-tags in 2006–2007.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Primary threats include habitat fragmentation from dams and diversion structures, altered flow and temperature regimes, decreased floodplain connectivity, and restricted movement. The [[Piute Farms Waterfall]] emerged after reservoir elevations dropped, creating a complete barrier that prevents access to approximately 30 km of upstream spawning habitat. Eight large diversion structures further fragment upstream habitat, with varying fish passage capabilities (no passage, non-selective, or selective). Non-native species pose additional threats; preventing access to upstream habitat by species such as [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) is a management objective. Habitat degradation includes loss of floodplain connectivity and complex habitat structure. The species must cope with novel ecosystems featuring altered abiotic conditions and species assemblages without shared evolutionary history.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Annual pre-spawn translocations of razorback sucker upstream of the waterfall are necessary for fish to gain access to spawning habitat, given that 80% return downstream within a year. Translocation timing should coincide with late winter–early spring when fish are in spawning condition and environmental conditions (temperature, flow) are favorable. Maintain and expand PIT-tag antenna arrays and acoustic receiver networks to improve detection of fish movements across the basin. Promote data sharing among multiple agencies through centralized databases (e.g., [[STReaMS Database]]). Manage river-reservoir systems holistically rather than as independent units, recognizing that mobile fish functionally connect these habitats. Maintain connectivity pathways to allow access to spatiotemporally dynamic habitats that may provide favorable recruitment conditions. Evaluate fish passage efficiency at diversion structures quantitatively. Consider selective fragmentation strategies that maintain native fish connectivity while preventing non-native fish access. Acknowledge that connectivity alone may not override other limiting factors (temperature, rearing habitat) necessary for successful recruitment.

**Biological Reference Points**: Not reported. No specific population targets, harvest rates, or spawning stock biomass thresholds are provided.
