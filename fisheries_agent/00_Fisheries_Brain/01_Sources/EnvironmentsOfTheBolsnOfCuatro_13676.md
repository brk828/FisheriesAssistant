# Environments of the Bolsón of Cuatro Ciénegas, Coahuila, México: With Special Reference to the Aquatic Biota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, published 1969 by Texas Western Press as Science Series No. 2) describes the physical environment, climate, vegetation, and aquatic habitats of the Cuatro Ciénegas basin in arid northern [[Coahuila]], México, with emphasis on its aquatic and semiaquatic fauna. The basin is a small, isolated desert valley whose springs, lagunas, posos, marshes and canals support a highly endemic biota, including fishes, molluscs, crustaceans and scorpions. The author stresses that these spring-fed systems are being rapidly altered by canalization, water-table lowering and other human activity, and calls for urgent study, salvage and protection.

## Background & Description

- **Location:** About 26°59′N, 102°04′W, on the eastern edge of the Mesa del Norte (Ridge and Basin Province), roughly 270 airline km south-southeast of the Big Bend of the [[Rio Grande]]. The basin floor averages about 740 m above sea level; basin dimensions are roughly 40 km east–west by 30 km north–south.
- **Study history:** Collections began with E. G. Marsh, Jr. in 1939. [[Carl L. Hubbs]] recognized the high endemism in the fishes. Intensive work began in 1960, and the paper draws on more than twenty expeditions from 1958 to 1967, plus some 1968 data.
- **Physiography:** Surrounded by mountains (e.g., Sierra de la Madera rising above 3,000 m; Sierra de San Marcos approaching 2,500 m). The basin floor is a "barrial" with sinkholes, travertine ridges, dunes and playas. Three gaps in the northeast (Puerto Salado, Puerto Sacramento, Puerto Nadadores) carry an aquatic connection to the Rio Grande system.
- **Climate:** Arid, with mean annual precipitation below 200 mm; no month averaged over 20 mm. Air temperatures reach above 44.0 °C in summer and may fall below 0 °C in winter. Occasional intense storms (over 50 mm overnight in 1958 and 1960).
- **Vegetation:** Heterogeneous desert flora dominated on bajadas by mesquite (*Prosopis*), creosote bush (*Larrea divaricata*), and tar bush (*Flourensia cernua*). Near water, willow, acacia, seep-willow (*Baccharis glutinosa*) and ash occur. Wetland vegetation includes sedges and cattail.
- **Aquatic habitat types:** Rivers and creeks ([[Rio Mesquites]], [[Rio Puente Chiquito]], [[Rio Cañón]]); posos, lagunas and marshes; barrial lakes ([[Laguna Grande]]); temporary playa pools; and canals (e.g., the "New Canal," Canal de La Angostura).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Water emerges from fracture zones at the mountain fronts. Discharge in the Rio Mesquites is remarkably stable (less than 10 cm level fluctuation over more than 30 visits; estimated 1.3–1.5 m³/s at Puente Orosco). In contrast, the Rio Puente Chiquito dries and refills, reaching 58 cm depth on 23 August 1960 and running dry at its downstream end for at least 400 m in several years.
- **Temperature and chemistry:** Rio Mesquites water ranges from 31.0 °C at the Tierra Blanca thermal source to 16.3 °C (April 1963). Rio Cañón water ranges from 18.0 to 25.0 °C in summer. Laguna Grande holds 4–5 g/L total dissolved solids, while cut-off pools exceed 400 g/L and are azoic. Waters are generally sulfate-rich, but Cañón del Agua waters are richest in carbonates.
- **Geomorphic succession:** Posos form by foundering of subterranean channels and may develop into lagunas, then marshes or roofed-over pits (Fig. 3). Salt and travertine deposition progressively mineralizes these systems.
- **Human impact:** Canalization lowers water levels, drains marshes, and removes shallow downflow habitat. Periodic burning, clearing of canals, and the 1960s–1968 canal construction (including drying of segments that eliminated hybrid Cyprinodon populations) are key threats. Salt works at Laguna Salada and planned commercial exploitation of dunes and spring deposits are also noted.
- **Geology:** Mesozoic strata overlie Permian marine sediments. The Cuatro Ciénegas area lay near the eastern margin of the old Coahuilan Peninsula and was relatively unaffected by Tertiary orogeny. Intermittent isolation (periodic closure and spillage) is proposed as a driver of biotic differentiation.
- **Biogeographic context:** Basin fishes show about 50% similarity to fauna outside the basin (Rio Salado de los Nadadores system). Molluscs of the endemic genera *Mexipyrgus* and others may date to the early Tertiary or Mesozoic, though the author notes this is an inference.

## Key Findings

**Fishes (main focus):**
- Nine families, 14 genera and 20 species of fishes (plus one unconfirmed possible introduction) are present in or adjacent to the valley; 9 of the 18 kinds inside the basin are endemic.
- Comparison: Bonneville (5 families, 11 genera, 21 species; 62% endemic), Lahontan (5, 10, 13; 69% endemic), Death Valley (3, 5, 10; 90% endemic).
- **Mexican tetra** ([[Astyanax]] *fasciatus mexicanus*): abundant mid-water fish in deep lagunas and rivers.
- **Gambusia marshi:** the most abundant fish in the basin; dark (springs) and spotted (canals, rivers, saline lakes) color phases show homotypic mate selection.
- **Gambusia longispinis:** a small, secretive endemic restricted to dense vegetation.
- **Xiphophorus gordoni:** endemic to the southeast lobe, studied for histocompatibility.
- **Cyprinodon atrorus** and **Cyprinodon bifasciatus:** two endemic pupfishes that are ecologically allopatric yet sympatric in parts of the basin. Hybridization in canals produced mass hybrid swarms, and the hybrid populations were destroyed in 1967.
- **Lucania interioris:** a secretive endemic that buries itself in the substrate.
- **Cichlids:** three undescribed endemic *Cichlasoma* species show trophic specialization (snail-crushing, detritivorous, piscivorous) comparable to African Great Lakes cichlids, alongside the Rio Grande cichlid *C. cyanoguttatum*.
- Introduced and other species: carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) in ponds; largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) as top carnivore; long-ear sunfish (*Lepomis megalotis*); a rare darter (*Etheostoma*); channel catfish (*Ictalurus lupus*); rare flathead catfish (*Pilodictis olivaris*, fewer than five specimens seen).

**Crustaceans (seven kinds from permanent waters):**
- Four endemic subterranean isopods, including *Speocirolana thermydronis*, the most inland species of its genus, and a new asellid of the subfamily Stenasellinae (new to the Western Hemisphere).
- *Hyalella azteca* (widespread amphipod) and *Palaemonetes suttkusi* (shrimp).
- Temporary waters: an unidentified anostracan and *Cletocamptus albuquerquensis* (found at nearly 400 g/L TDS in Laguna Salada).

**Molluscs:**
- D. W. Taylor (1966) reported about 18 forms, 13 identified to species. Twelve new endemic species were classified in five new endemic genera, some with new subfamilies. Six additional endemic aquatic snails were later found.
- The fauna shows scarcely any resemblance to known molluscs of eastern Mexico.

**Other fauna:**
- Scorpions studied by S. C. Williams show considerable endemism. Birds and mammals were documented from the state of Coahuila rather than the basin specifically. Reptiles and amphibians were treated separately, including the endemic Coahuilan box turtle, *Terrapene coahuila*.

**Conservation and research priorities:**
- Spring-fed habitats are disappearing across the desert Southwest; the loss of an isolated spring may eliminate a species' entire gene pool.
- The author identifies major gaps: taxonomic revision of *Dionda episcopa* and darters, unstudied invertebrates, and detailed sediment cores (one radiocarbon date of 2,070 ± 250 years BP from Posos de la Becerra).
- The basin is described as an unusually accessible site for comparative desert-spring ecology.
