# Idaho Statewide Fishing Harvest Survey 1966: Salmon and Steelhead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fishery-management #anadromous-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1966 Federal Aid completion report (F-18-R-13, Job 1) by Ted C. Bjornn evaluates two methods for estimating [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]] harvest in Idaho waters: postal card questionnaires and salmon/steelhead permits. The study found that questionnaire-based estimates contained substantial positive bias, approximately doubling the true catch. Based on a random sample of 6,651 returned permits (81.9% return rate from 8,123 sampled), the estimated 1966 harvest was 8,500 chinook salmon and 20,400 steelhead trout. The permit-based method proved far more accurate, as validated by check station counts on the [[South Fork Salmon River]] where permit estimates closely matched actual tallies while questionnaire estimates were 3-4 times higher.

## Background & Description

### Survey Context
Idaho initiated a mandatory permit system in 1962 for steelhead anglers, expanded in 1965 to include chinook salmon. Permits were issued at no charge, requiring anglers to record date and location of each fish caught. This report compares two harvest estimation methods for the 1966 fishing season across Idaho's major anadromous fish drainages.

### Study Species
- **[[Steelhead Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): Ocean-run rainbow trout over 20 inches long
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]** (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*): Spring-run and summer-run populations (summer-run season closed in 1966)

### Geographic Coverage
Primary fishing areas included:
- [[Salmon River]] drainage (including [[Middle Fork]], [[South Fork]], [[East Fork]], [[Lemhi River]])
- [[Clearwater River]] drainage (including [[North Fork]], [[Selway River]], [[Lochsa River]])
- [[Snake River]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Fishery Characteristics
The Idaho anadromous fishery targets two distinct seasonal runs. Steelhead caught in spring entered the [[Columbia River]] the previous summer; fall-caught fish entered during the current year's summer. The 1965 run produced a total catch of 20,300 steelhead (13,900 in fall 1965 + 6,400 in spring 1966).

### Angler Demographics and Behavior
- Only 14% of licensed anglers fished for salmon and/or steelhead in 1966
- 5.6% fished for salmon; 11.2% fished for steelhead
- Success rates: 36% of salmon anglers caught fish; 34% of steelhead anglers caught fish
- Salmon and steelhead anglers were largely distinct groups: only 18.7% fished for both species
- Non-resident anglers comprised 41% of steelhead anglers but caught only 34% of fish; 35% of salmon anglers but caught only 22% of fish

### Temporal Distribution
**Steelhead**: Peak catches occurred March-April (spring run) and October-November (fall run), with continued activity through winter months.

**Chinook Salmon**: Concentrated harvest during June-July for spring-run fish. Summer-run fishing was closed in 1966 for conservation.

### Long-term Trends (1960-1966)
Both angler participation and catch rates showed gradual decline:
- Steelhead anglers decreased from 28,900 (1960) to 21,700 (1966)
- Salmon anglers decreased from 26,900 (1960) to 16,600 (1966)
- Mean annual catch per successful steelhead angler: 4.79 (1960) to 3.36 (1966)
- Mean annual catch per successful salmon angler: 3.46 (1960) to 2.79 (1966)

## Key Findings

### Harvest Estimates: Permit-Based Method (Recommended)

**1966 Statewide Harvest** (based on random sample of 6,651 permits):
- **Steelhead**: 20,400 fish (confidence interval ±7% of estimate)
  - Before July 1: 6,374 fish
  - After July 1: 13,648 fish
  - Mean catch per successful angler: 2.83 fish
  - Successful anglers: 7,212

- **Chinook Salmon**: 8,500 fish (confidence interval ±8% of estimate)
  - Mean catch per successful angler: 2.28 fish
  - Successful anglers: 3,743
  - Catch taken almost entirely from [[Salmon River]] drainage (7,977 fish)

**Drainage-Specific Steelhead Harvest**:
- [[Salmon River]] drainage: 10,209 fish (50% of statewide catch, ±8% CI)
- [[Clearwater River]] drainage: 6,339 fish (31% of statewide catch)
- [[Snake River]]: 3,421 fish (17% of statewide catch, ±11% CI)
- [[South Fork Salmon River]]: 242 fish

### Harvest Estimates: Questionnaire Method (Biased, Not Recommended)

Questionnaires mailed to 15,295 anglers; 8,626 returned (56.3% return rate).

**1966 Estimates** (containing substantial positive bias):
- **Steelhead**: 37,800 fish (1.85 times permit estimate)
- **Chinook Salmon**: 16,400 fish (1.92 times permit estimate)

### Method Validation

**[[South Fork Salmon River]] Check Station Comparison**:
- 1966: Permit estimate = 242 fish; Check station count < 200 fish (168 counted April 8-May 5); Questionnaire estimate = 722 fish
- 1965: Permit estimate = 75 fish; Check station count < 100 fish
- 1964: Permit estimate = 726 fish; Check station count = 737 fish

The permit-based estimates consistently matched check station tallies across three years, while questionnaire estimates were 3-4 times higher, confirming substantial positive bias in questionnaire methodology.

### Voluntary Permit Returns (Biased Sample)

Voluntary returns comprised 6.4% of permits issued (3,124 of 48,865 in 1966). These showed strong positive bias:
- Mean catch for voluntary returns: 3.25 steelhead vs. 2.61 for random sample
- Voluntary return estimate: 58,000 steelhead (2.8 times random sample estimate)
- 36.5% of voluntary returns were from successful anglers vs. 14.8% in random sample

### Statistical Considerations

**Sample Size Analysis**:
With N=6,651 returned permits, confidence intervals were:
- Statewide steelhead: ±7%
- [[Salmon River]] drainage (50% of catch): ±8%
- [[Snake River]] (17% of catch): ±11%
- [[Clearwater River]] before July 1 (6% of catch): ±20%

**Recommendation**: Sample size could be reduced to one-fourth (approximately 2,125 permits) while maintaining acceptable precision for statewide and major drainage estimates. This would reduce postage costs from $1,200-$1,300 to approximately $300-$325 (at 1968 rates) while providing confidence intervals of ±14% for statewide estimates and ±22% for major drainages.

### Methodological Biases Identified

**Questionnaire Method**:
- Non-response bias: Successful anglers return questionnaires more readily
- Memory bias: Anglers rely on recall rather than contemporaneous records
- Positive reporting bias: Tendency to overreport catch
- Result: Estimates approximately double true harvest

**Permit Method Advantages**:
- Contemporaneous recording reduces memory errors
- Random sampling eliminates non-response bias
- Anglers less likely to alter recorded catch
- Validated against independent check station counts

**Potential Permit Method Concerns**:
- 58% of returned permits in 1966 were "sample permits" (filled out retrospectively when original lost)
- High proportion of sample permits could reintroduce questionnaire-type biases
- Requires monitoring to ensure data quality

### Management Recommendations

1. **Adopt permit-based random sampling** as the sole method for harvest estimation; discontinue postal card questionnaire surveys due to large positive biases

2. **Optimize sample size** based on desired confidence intervals; consider reducing to 2,000-2,500 permits for cost efficiency while maintaining adequate precision for management decisions

3. **Monitor sample permit proportion**: If percentage of sample permits (vs. original permits) increases substantially, investigate potential bias reintroduction

4. **Maintain check stations** on selected streams (e.g., [[South Fork Salmon River]]) for periodic validation of permit-based estimates

5. **Statistical protocols**: Use normal approximation methods for confidence intervals only when proportions ≥0.05 for steelhead and ≥0.10 for salmon with current sample sizes

### Historical Context

The 1966 chinook salmon harvest (8,500 fish) was the smallest in 13 years except for 1965 when the entire season was closed. Historical high was 78,000 fish in 1957 during a year with large salmon returns. Steelhead harvest (20,400 fish) was down from a 1959 high of 62,000 fish, continuing a multi-year declining trend in both angler participation and catch rates.
