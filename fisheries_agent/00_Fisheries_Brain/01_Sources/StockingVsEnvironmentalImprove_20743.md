# Stocking vs. Environmental Improvement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

Albert S. Hazzard of the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], argues in this 1948 article (reprinted from *Michigan Conservation*, based on a paper given to the [[Izaak Walton League of America]]) that stocking and habitat improvement are both valid management tools, but that the public overrates stocking. He contends that most warm-water fish populations sustain themselves through natural reproduction, so systematic restocking is unnecessary or harmful for them. Stocking is justified mainly to introduce species, re-establish fish after [[Winterkill]], or provide trout in waters with suitable habitat. He argues that pollution control, [[Watershed Management]], and in-stream habitat work should receive priority, because they build lasting productivity.

## Background & Description

The article opens by questioning the popular belief that fish planting is essential every year. Hazzard notes that the public has been swayed by spectacular introductions, such as [[Brown Trout]] in many waters, [[Striped Bass]] in California, and the [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] in Lake Pend Oreille, Idaho. He calls the comparison of fish planting to planting corn "not a proper comparison," although it is widely credited.

Two points underpin his argument:

- **Hook and line is inefficient.** Fish are a three- or four-year crop in northern states, and most anglers catch fish that have already spawned, often several times. Southern workers found that even with heavy fishing and no closed seasons or limits, only about half of adult [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] could be removed by [[Hook and Line Angling]]. Similar studies in Indiana, Ohio, and Michigan reached the same conclusion for mid-western waters.
- **Fish are highly fecund.** The average bluegill produces more than 16,000 young each year. The largemouth bass produces about 6,000 fry, and a sizeable [[Walleye]] may deposit as many as half a million eggs. Bluegill spawning extends over several months, and one female may produce several batches in a year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hazzard argues that natural reproduction is usually sufficient. Several years are needed to reach catchable size, so spawning need not succeed every year to maintain a maximum population. He concludes that in most waters there are too many small panfish rather than too few, and that better [[Population Control]] could improve fishing. Dead fish that are not eaten by predators waste food and space that could support fish reaching the creel.

Some situations do call for stocking:

- Isolated high-mountain lakes where trout introduction would create new fishing.
- Salmon runs blocked by barriers, where planting [[Salmon]] fingerlings restores runs.
- New farm ponds and reservoirs, for roughly the first year, unless suitable game fish are already present or are outnumbered by [[Carp]] or other rough fish.
- Shallow, muddy-bottomed lakes subject to winterkill, where bass and bluegill are re-introduced because kills occur only once or twice a decade.

Hazzard warns that introductions should be preceded by careful study. He cites the example that planting [[Northern Pike]] in a lake with stunted perch or sunfish might help local fishing but could harm trout fishing in connected waters above or below.

Trout differ from warm-water species. Many lakes suitable for trout lack spawning grounds, and smaller lakes with stunted warm-water fish must first be poisoned. Cold, well-oxygenated water in deeper lakes, used little by bass and bluegill, forms "vacant rooms" that trout can fill. Only [[Suckers]], [[Ciscoes]], and less commonly [[Whitefish]] and [[Smelt]] occupy these areas.

## Key Findings

**Stocking: where it fits**
- Systematic restocking is unnecessary or harmful for maintaining warm-water species (bass, bluegills, perch, walleyes) where habitat is suitable and species are present.
- Stocking is justified to introduce or re-establish species, particularly in isolated waters, in barrier-blocked salmon streams, and after winterkill.
- Brook, rainbow, and possibly brown trout (and cutthroat trout in the west) fingerlings planted in lakes with unused cold water provide economical, effective fishing. They grow on natural food and are "wild" when they enter the catch, usually about two years later.
- Larger trout (6–8 inches) planted in lakes with "vacant rooms" supplement warm-water fishing. Trout up to six and eight pounds have been taken a few years after planting. Returns are estimated at a minimum of 25 percent of the number stocked. Most catch comes from the previous fall's planting, except [[Lake Trout]], which appear in numbers about two years later. About a third of the total are older, larger fish. No adverse effect on warm-water catch was evident.

**Put-and-take trout programs**
- Michigan developed special spring-fed trout ponds during wartime travel restrictions. These ponds, once used for bass and bluegill rearing, are restricted to daylight fishing, no boats, artificial flies only, and a daily limit of two trout. Three new ponds had recently been built, with more planned.
- Legal-sized stream plantings are caught quickly, usually within days, and very few are recovered after the third or fourth week. Their quality is inferior to wild trout, and little skill is needed to catch them.
- Hazzard views put-and-take programs as justified only where no natural trout streams exist. Where good trout waters are present, restoring the environment should take priority. The major share of trout-state license funds went to "put and take" fishing, which he criticized.

**Habitat improvement priorities**
- Pollution control is the first priority, as filth and poisons destroy or impair fish production.
- [[Watershed Management]], starting at headwaters, is the logical approach, though no fish and game department had adopted it. Upstream erosion fills pools and chokes riffles with sand and silt, so deflectors and dams alone do little good. Practices such as contour plowing and strip-cropping are recommended, with Hazzard suggesting that anglers may eventually demand license money go to [[Soil Conservation]].
- [[Livestock Grazing]] along streams should be controlled, with stream-bottom fencing and protected water access. Cattle widen channels, cave banks, and eliminate tree and shrub reproduction. Bank planting is hopeless on heavily pastured streams.
- Forest cover should be preserved, including ungrazed woodland strips along streams.
- [[Stream Deflectors]] and shelters narrow channels, create pools, and expose gravel for spawning. Research showed trout yield could be greatly increased, and similar work could benefit bass and catfish. However, well-built structures are expensive, and their cost per extra trout may equal that of legal-size planting.
- Lake improvement remains less developed. Experiments test fertilization, weed reduction, liberalized fishing, and population control. Brush or stone shelters on barren shoals concentrate fish for anglers and are considered sound.

**Conclusion**
- Stocking and habitat improvement are both valuable tools, but should be used only where needed and where results are consistent with sound conservation. Hazzard calls for a balance between short-term demands (pheasants for the gun, trout for the creel) and long-term goals of clean, clear waters and naturally productive fish and game habitat.
