# A Case of Mistaken Identity: Genetic and Morphological Evidence for the Presence of Redeye Bass in the Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #centrarchidae #invasive_species #genetic_identification

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Redeye Bass]] (*Micropterus coosae* sensu stricto) is native to the Coosa River system of Alabama, Georgia, and Tennessee. The species was originally described in 1940 and has since undergone genetic revision separating it into several species within the Redeye Bass complex, each restricted to disjunct upland river systems in the Southeastern United States. Related species include [[Cahaba Bass]] (*M. cahabae*), [[Chattahoochee Bass]] (*M. chattahoochae*), [[Tallapoosa Bass]] (*M. tallapoosae*), and [[Warrior Bass]] (*M. warriorensis*). Age, maturity, fecundity, and growth parameters: Not reported. The species was introduced to several states outside its native range in the 1900s as a game species for warm water streams not ideal for larger bass species but too warm for trout.

**Habitat Associations**: [[Redeye Bass]] are primarily upland and small stream fish in their native range. In the [[Verde River]] system of Arizona, they were found at the Upper Verde River Wildlife Area (34.867749°N, 112.401625°W) and detected across approximately 120 river kilometers. The Verde River is one of few perennial streams in Arizona that flows largely unimpeded. Previous studies of purported [[Smallmouth Bass]] (later identified as Redeye Bass) in Wet Beaver Creek showed preference for shallower habitat than typical for true Smallmouth Bass. Environmental DNA (eDNA) detections occurred at three sites on the Verde River, with additional citizen science observations from Wet Beaver Creek and near Cottonwood, Arizona.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: A migration study from Alabama indicated that [[Redeye Bass]] were routinely able to scale a 6-ft (1.83-m) vertical rock dam on a small stream, presumably during flash flood events, which has implications for fish barrier effectiveness. Unassisted upstream migration in the Salt River is prevented by a series of dams constructed from 1911–1936. Reservoirs on the lower Verde River (existing since at least 1939) may impact downstream migration to the Salt River system, though this is unclear.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Dietary analysis of fish previously identified as [[Smallmouth Bass]] from the Upper Verde River Wildlife Area showed lower tendency toward piscivory (<5% of diet by weight) and instead favored invertebrate prey, consistent with known dietary preferences of [[Redeye Bass]] in their native range. However, gut contents included several species of native fishes. The presence of black bass (historically thought to be [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Spotted Bass]]) has caused detrimental effects on native fishes, including three ESA-listed species: [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*), and [[Razorback Chub]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Large portions of the Verde River and tributaries are Designated Critical Habitat for these endangered species.

**Environmental Drivers**: Not reported beyond general habitat preferences for warm water streams.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: [[Redeye Bass]] were reportedly introduced to the Verde River in 1942 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, though historical records indicated [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Spotted Bass]] introductions. [[Spotted Bass]] were considered rare or extirpated by 1973. In 2019 surveys, Redeye Bass was the most abundant nonnative fish in the stretch from Granite Creek to Sycamore Creek, and at many sites was the most abundant fish overall. AZGFD encountered 1,275 individuals identified as Redeye Bass and no individuals identified as Smallmouth Bass. The species has proliferated across approximately 120 river kilometers of the Verde River system. Possible spread to other major tributaries (Oak Creek, West Clear Creek, Fossil Creek, East Verde River) and the Salt River system remains to be determined.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: As an introduced species, [[Redeye Bass]] directly compete with and prey upon native species. Their presence poses ongoing threats to ESA-listed native fishes in Designated Critical Habitat. The misidentification as [[Smallmouth Bass]] has implications for monitoring and management, as eDNA detection methods designed explicitly for Smallmouth Bass (qPCR) would fail to detect Redeye Bass. Hybridization with other black bass species is possible but not definitively documented; SNP analysis of five individuals showed Q-values >0.90 for Redeye Bass assignment, with two individuals showing potential minor genomic contributions from [[Spotted Bass]] (Q-values 0.081 and 0.096) and one showing potential contribution from [[Cahaba Bass]] (Q-value 0.087).

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) Conduct comprehensive surveys to determine the full extent of [[Redeye Bass]] distribution in Arizona, including all Verde River tributaries and potential spread to the Salt River system. (2) Revise existing literature on native-nonnative fish interactions in the Verde River to account for the misidentification of Redeye Bass as Smallmouth Bass. (3) Update eDNA monitoring protocols to target Redeye Bass rather than Smallmouth Bass, as existing qPCR methods designed for Smallmouth Bass detection will not detect Redeye Bass. (4) Assess whether [[Smallmouth Bass]] are present elsewhere in the Verde River system. (5) Investigate potential hybridization between Redeye Bass and other black bass species in different reaches. (6) Re-evaluate fish barrier designs given the documented ability of Redeye Bass to scale vertical obstacles during flood events. (7) Consider behavioral differences between Redeye Bass and other black bass when developing management strategies, particularly their preference for upland/small stream habitats and lower piscivory rates.

**Biological Reference Points**: Not reported. The study focused on species identification rather than population assessment or harvest management.
