# Dexter National Fish Hatchery 1980 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 annual report from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter NFH) describes the station's shift from warmwater sport fish production to the holding, propagation and rearing of rare, threatened and endangered southwestern fishes. In 1980 the station held 14 species and two hybrid chubs. Three new endangered species and the hybrid chubs were added during the year. The report covers stocking and growth results for [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[San Marcos Gambusia]] and [[Big Bend Gambusia]], pond renovation by a Youth Conservation labor crew, staff training, and planning for a visitor contact center.

## Background & Description

Dexter NFH lies one mile east of Dexter and 20 miles southeast of [[Roswell]] in [[Chaves County]], New Mexico. The station was established in 1931 and was historically a warmwater sport fish facility, producing channel catfish, largemouth bass, crappie and sunfish. As other stations took over game fish production, Dexter NFH moved into rare fish work.

**Primary goal:** prevent the extinction of endangered and threatened fishes and ensure their continued existence.

**Stated objectives:**
1. Provide a protected gene pool for rare, threatened and endangered Southwestern fishes.
2. Develop methods for propagating and rearing these species.
3. Supply fish to authorized agencies for reintroduction into historic habitats, in line with recovery and management plans.
4. Provide live specimens and small population samples for scientific study of taxonomy, physiology and habitat requirements.
5. Devise methods for managing these species and their habitat.
6. Exchange expertise, ideas, data and personnel with federal and state agencies, academic organizations and other nations.
7. Provide public information on the plight and recovery of these fishes.
8. Develop information for [[Biological Opinions]] in response to requests for [[Section 7 Consultation]].

**Species status and planned additions** (summarized from Table I, which lists status under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973):

- **Currently held (14 species):** includes [[Pecos Gambusia]], [[Big Bend Gambusia]], [[Goodenough Springs Gambusia]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Commanche Springs Pupfish]], [[San Marcos Gambusia]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Fountain Darter]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Woundfin]], [[Chihuahuan Chub]] (two populations) and [[Yaqui Sucker]]. Several are listed as endangered (a), proposed (b), or under work initiated by the station (c).
- **Planned for 1982 (9 species):** including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Clear Creek Gambusia]], [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]], Yaqui catfish, Yaqui topminnow and Devils River minnow.
- **Planned for 1985 (12 species):** including [[Leopard Darter]], loach minnow, spikedace, Yaqui shiner, Mexican stoneroller, Little Colorado River spinedace, Zuni sucker, Virgin River spinedace, [[Guadalupe Bass]], Sonoran chub, desert chub and bluntnose shiner.

The report states that plans call for 21 additional species by 1985. It also notes that the scope may later expand to other Regions and Mexico. Table I's Region 2 species are the only ones considered at present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most work took place in small hatchery ponds, from 0.1 to 2 surface acres in size, used to hold forage and stocked fish. Forage communities were managed so that predation did not reduce forage biomass below its reproductive rate. Predators recorded at the pond were [[Osprey]] and [[Great Blue Heron]]. The report also records that a sub-adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] growth study was run in cultured ponds, and that chemical toxicity trials were conducted for aquatic chemicals used in fish culture.

## Key Findings

**1. Colorado squawfish stocking and growth (Willow Beach transfer)**
- 271 fish, about 6 years old, were transferred from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on March 27, 1980 (averaging 293 g and 333 mm TL), and arrived in excellent condition.
- They were stocked in a 2-surface-acre pond holding about 700 lb of minnows and juvenile goldfish.
- Fish readily accepted trout pellets offered five days a week.
- In September, a sample of 140 fish showed average gains of 78 mm in length and 269 g in weight, nearly doubling body weight in six months.
- Losses were limited to predation. One osprey was seen carrying a squawfish away from the pond, and a great blue heron peck killed one fish by damaging the kidney. About 10% of the sampled fish bore wounds from these two predators.

**2. San Marcos gambusia**
- 225 fish were collected in Texas and air-shipped on August 27, 1980, after about 20 hours in transit.
- About 10% were dead when removed from the shipping containers. The survivors were stocked in a 0.1-acre pond.

**3. Big Bend gambusia**
- Two staff members collected a new stock from [[Big Bend National Park]] on August 20, 1980.
- The 1979 stock arrived in very poor condition and was lost entirely. In 1980, fish were trapped rather than seined, which gave much better quality fish.
- About 250 were stocked in a 0.8-acre pond. By October, many fry were seen, indicating heavy reproduction.

**4. Hybrid chubs**
- Two hybrid chub fry types were obtained from Willow Beach NFH: *Gila robusta* x *G. elegans* and *G. alpha* x *G. elegans*.
- They will serve as comparative specimens for taxonomists studying the parent species' distinctness. Samples were preserved through the season, and length–weight data were recorded.
- After 90 days, one hybrid reached 120 mm and 16 g.

**5. Reproduction and maintenance**
- Reproduction was heavy in nine of the species held, light in three species, and absent in two species.
- Cyclic maintenance funding was taken early in the fiscal year, which eliminated one facet of operations. Funds were returned later in the year for a replacement well.

**6. Pond renovation (Youth Conservation Corps labor)**
- The [[Youth Conservation Corps]] (YACC) crew, led by Terry Dupree and Mitchell Daubert, began renovating ponds 5A and 6A into 0.1-acre ponds.
- By fiscal year-end, eight ponds were complete, drainlines were installed for the remaining ponds, and work had started on the remaining 12 kettles and water control structures.
- The total cost for the 20 ponds was estimated at about $55,000. The report states that YACC labor produced significant savings without sacrificing quality, which the report judged comparable or superior to commercial contract work.
- YACC also assisted with general hatchery maintenance and stream habitat completion.

**7. Visitation, training and research**
- Estimated visitor load for 1980 was 2,750.
- Buddy Jensen attended the [[Fish Hatchery Managers Long Course]] at [[Leetown]].
- Late-year projects included bioassays of approved aquatic chemicals at recommended treatment levels, photographs of endangered and threatened fishes, trap effectiveness for various species, and growth of sub-adult Colorado squawfish in culture ponds.

**8. Future plans**
- Basic planning and engineering were completed for a proposed visitor contact center. About $15,000 was spent on materials in anticipation of YACC construction in 1981.
