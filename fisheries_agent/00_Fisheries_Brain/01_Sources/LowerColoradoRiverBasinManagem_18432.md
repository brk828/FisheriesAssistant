# Lower Colorado River Basin Management Plan for Indigenous "Big-River" Fishes

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This 1994 management plan from the [[Parker Fishery Resource Office]] addresses recovery of four federally endangered "big-river" fish species in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The plan proposes a comprehensive, ecosystem-based approach to prevent extinction and establish self-sustaining populations through habitat development, genetic management, and circumvention of recruitment failure. The lower basin encompasses ~650,000 km² draining portions of seven U.S. states plus northwestern Mexico, divided into five bioregions. Unlike upper basin recovery efforts, this plan addresses a system with fewer free-flowing reaches, greater habitat modification, and no dedicated funding mechanism. The strategy emphasizes creating isolated habitats on [[National Wildlife Refuges]] where non-indigenous predators can be excluded, allowing indigenous fish to survive critical early life stages.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] basin comprises approximately one-twelfth of the contiguous United States. The lower basin is separated from the upper basin near [[Glen Canyon Dam]], Arizona, and includes the Colorado mainstem plus the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila river sub-basins. Elevations range from below sea level in the [[Salton Sea]] and [[Laguna Salada]] basins to >4,000 m in Rocky Mountain headwaters.

Prior to impoundments, the mainstem featured slow-moving, turbid reaches in valleys alternating with violent whitewater rapids in canyons. Discharge reflected Rocky Mountain snowmelt, running high and cold in spring then decreasing and warming in summer. Under these conditions evolved four of the largest and most unusual freshwater fishes in the world, collectively termed "big-river" fishes.

All four species are federally listed as endangered. Three have designated critical habitat downstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (USFWS 1994a). [[Colorado Squawfish]] was extirpated from the lower basin in the 1960s and has no designated critical habitat there. [[Bonytail]] are caught infrequently in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. [[Humpback Chub]] persist as a viable population in the [[Grand Canyon]] area, mostly in the lowermost [[Little Colorado River]]. The largest remaining [[Razorback Sucker]] population persists in [[Lake Mohave]], with wild individuals sporadically captured in the Grand Canyon region and downstream from [[Davis Dam]].

These taxa form a natural management unit, collectively representing a unique and geographically sympatric fauna with shared life-history patterns indicating a coevolved assemblage driven by predation dynamics. All four attain large sizes and great ages. [[Colorado Squawfish]] grows slowly but to large size, with special adaptations (large head, raptorial pharyngeal teeth, solitary habitat) for near-shore ambush predation. Prey species display high reproductive potentials, rapid growth, hydrodynamic shapes, and falcate fins—adaptations to avoid predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Declines of these taxa have been attributed to habitat loss/modification and negative interactions with introduced non-indigenous species. Habitat loss is illustrated by long, dry reaches along the present middle and lower [[Gila River]] resulting from dam closures for large lower-basin impoundments. Where streams continued flowing after damming, structures blocked historic movement corridors, released water too cold for spawning requirements, and removed innate variance from the system. Torrential floods alternating with prolonged drought were hydrologic conditions to which indigenous taxa were adapted.

Predation on young (eggs/fry/juveniles) by non-indigenous fishes has been identified as a major depletion factor. As more non-indigenous taxa establish, fewer indigenous taxa are found. Artificial reservoirs, cattle tanks, canals, and agency stocking programs provide constant sources of alien predators and competitors.

The lower basin differs substantially from the upper basin in several ways: (1) substantially longer time-frame for modifications has resulted in stabilization—most damsites are already used, major alterations completed, few new projects anticipated; (2) population sizes of bonytail, humpback chub, and razorback sucker are larger in the lower basin; and (3) existence of extensive site-specific research information allows fact-based recovery actions with confidence.

The plan divides the lower basin into five bioregions based on fish distribution and watershed relations: (I) Mainstem Colorado between Glen Canyon and Hoover dams including tributary confluences; (II) Middle Colorado River system, Little Colorado River above Grand Falls, and Virgin River; (III) Mainstem Colorado from Hoover Dam to U.S.-Mexican boundary including Bill Williams River; (IV) Gila River from confluence upstream; (V) Mexican drainages (not included in present plan).

## Key Findings

**Management Goals and Objectives:**

The ultimate goal is to stabilize and maintain self-perpetuating populations of all four species in the lower Colorado River basin, characterized by genetic variability reflective of existing wild populations and occupying portions of historic habitats throughout the region. Six primary objectives are defined in hierarchical levels:

- **Level I**: Prevent extinction
- **Level II**: Understand and acquire maximum existing genetic variability
- **Level III**: Stabilize sufficient numbers, population structure, and genetic variability
- **Level IV**: Re-introduction and re-establishment of additional stocks
- **Level V**: Promote self-sustaining status
- **Level VI**: Down-listing to threatened status or de-listing

Different taxa enter the program at different levels based on endangerment status and knowledge state. [[Bonytail]] enters at Level I (prevention of extinction) but can simultaneously pursue Level III (population stabilization). [[Humpback Chub]] faces slight extinction danger compared to bonytail, with considerable genetic information available and steps underway for stabilization and expansion. [[Razorback Sucker]] shows concern regarding potential population collapse of the [[Lake Mohave]] stock, but genetic data are available and stabilization/expansion efforts are underway. [[Colorado Squawfish]] has no remaining wild lower-basin populations but has been re-introduced as "experimental, nonessential" in the Salt and Verde rivers.

**Implementation Strategy - Preventing Extinction:**

The plan focuses on circumventing recruitment failure through dedication of isolated habitats from which non-indigenous fishes are removed and excluded. Methods build on experience from ongoing Lake Mohave razorback stock efforts:

1. Identify existing natural or semi-natural habitats that are: (a) naturally or artificially isolated from other waters; (b) amenable to piscicide eradication of non-indigenous species; (c) suitable for wild-caught and artificially cultured endangered taxa. Such habitats are generally <5.0 ha surface area.

2. Remove non-indigenous fishes and stock one or more endangered taxa within three years, beginning on [[National Wildlife Refuges]].

3. When indigenous species reach sizes minimizing predation pressure (~300 mm TL), use them for: (a) augmentation of existing populations; (b) re-introductions elsewhere; (c) buffer against catastrophic events.

4. Additional re-introduction sites depend on taxon requirements. Expansion to other critical habitats, public lands, and elsewhere contingent on cooperative agreements. Ultimate objective: release and re-establish appropriate taxa throughout former ranges.

**Hatchery Operations:**

[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] currently holds brood stocks of all "big-river" fishes except humpback chub. The facility's propagation, rearing, and distribution capabilities are pivotal to the plan. Other facilities including [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]'s [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] are coming online. Primary roles: (1) maintain brood stocks supplying genetically representative progeny and serve as refugia; (2) perpetuate capabilities to produce sufficient quantities and qualities of fish for recovery requirements.

**Fish Health:**

Natural and semi-natural habitats must be examined for health-related problems by examining non-indigenous fishes prior to renovation and stocking. Health of indigenous fish in hatcheries and stocked habitats should be monitored annually at minimum and prior to site-to-site transfers to maximize fish health and minimize disease transfer probability.

**Genetic Management:**

Maintenance of genetic variability is of primary concern in long-term recovery. The plan ensures perpetuation of existing genetic variability through maximization and conservation of variability in founder stocks and offspring. Approaches in reducing order of resemblance to natural conditions:

1. Maximize numbers of individuals contributing to recruitment. For razorback suckers, wild-caught larvae collection is possible due to positive phototactic response. Collections made at different times during extended breeding season at various Lake Mohave locations to preserve maximum genetic diversity.

2. Experimental alternative: isolate embayments, remove non-indigenous fishes, stock wild-caught breeding fish for natural reproduction, then tag and release progeny into Lake Mohave after achieving predation-immune sizes. Genetic variability limited by breeding adult numbers, which is limited by available habitat.

3. If wild-caught larvae or sufficient embayment space unavailable, employ artificial propagation based on strict pedigree with: (a) measured gamete contributions from individuals with known genetic attributes; or (b) statistical breeding regimen based on probabilities that each brood stock allele is present in progeny.

4. Brood stock management should include: (a) maximal use of wild individuals; (b) infusion and mixing of wild individuals with "domestic" brood fish (F₁ individuals retained as brood fish) to minimize inbreeding; (c) application of strict pedigree if necessary to form or use domestic brood stock.

Genetic management will be closely integrated with parallel upper-basin efforts. Information from upper-basin researchers and protocols from [[USFWS Region VI Recovery Implementation Program]] will be extensively used in developing lower-basin genetic management plans.

**Population Stabilization and Expansion - Habitat Development:**

Development of new, larger habitats (~80 ha or larger) isolated from other waters and dedicated for long-term management will proceed as follows:

1. Plan and implement within five years the creation and stocking of large, semi-natural, isolated habitats, initially on NWRs as compatible with refuge management. Includes renovation of large isolated water bodies and construction of new waterbodies, managed under ecosystem concept including waterfowl, riparian, and neotropical bird habitat enhancement.

2. Modify existing or newly constructed isolated habitats to encourage natural reproduction and self-sustaining populations, which may be utilized as sources for re-introduction and available for movement among facilities as part of long-term genetic management.

3. Expand efforts within 10 years to include habitats on other federal lands and elsewhere targeted as recovery areas, especially on tributaries in Bio-regions I (humpback chub) and IV (Gila basin). Ultimate objective: expand stocks precluding loss of critical population size or genetics components to single catastrophe.

4. Take advantage of major reservoir drainage for maintenance, drought, or other reasons. Stock adult and larval indigenous fish coincident with reservoir refilling to exploit: (a) absence of predators allowing adults to recruit young naturally plus artificially produced larvae to grow and establish; (b) rapid growth potential and longevity resulting in long-lived population even if later recruitment suppressed; (c) dramatically expanded population size allowing selection to operate on abundant gene pool.

**Habitat Maintenance:**

Success requires perpetual maintenance of facilities and habitats:

1. Periodic renovations needed to control non-indigenous fishes appearing even in dedicated habitats. Repeated stockings necessary with attendant genetic, population, and production planning. Exchange fish among sites, into brood stocks, and for reintroductions/augmentations.

2. Determine interactions of non-indigenous and indigenous fishes, particularly regarding larger non-indigenous predators—an area for encouraged research effort.

3. Since natural floods formerly creating oxbows, backwaters, and isolated aquatic habitats no longer exist due to river control, human intervention involves not only creation but maintenance of desired conditions. Anticipate and plan periodic dredging, vegetation control, and other manipulations. Alternatively, changes through natural succession reducing habitat value to indigenous fishes will form habitat for other vegetative and wildlife communities, incorporating fish habitats into NWR management as open-water component of wider ecosystem-level development.

**Program Coordination:**

A lower-basin Coordinator is necessary to: form a lower-basin Recovery Team, coordinate overall program, consult with upper-basin representatives, and advise Regional Director on implementation, progress, and achievements. The position is necessary to maintain order in site-specific management plan development and deal with diverse participating agencies (Appendix B).

The Recovery Team would consist of biologists responsible for development, revision, or modification of existing plans to fit lower-basin needs. All four species would be addressed by one Recovery Team rather than individual teams, consistent with ecosystem emphasis. Team-generated information will assist Regional Director in recovery action decisions. No more than half the group should be employees of agencies actually or potentially affecting the taxa.

Annual working sessions should be convened by the Coordinator to explore biology knowledge state of subject taxa, emphasizing quality of fish for stabilization, maintenance, and recovery efforts. Principal goals: (1) ascertain best information and produce documented decisions to be applied and tested by consensus; (2) settle upon means of brood stock and progeny acquisition/production for augmentation and re-introduction; (3) consolidate best information on stabilizing and perpetuating populations of each taxon.

**Rationale:**

The plan spells out actions based on current scientific information on distribution, status, and biology of the four species, emphasizing management scenarios most likely to achieve recovery. It is a research-based effort centered in large scientific literature. The plan must be flexible and adaptable to conditions dictated by species and available or developable habitats. For instance, [[Colorado Squawfish]] are relatively slow growing even in isolated habitats and must be treated differently than fast-growing [[Razorback Sucker]] regarding stocking protocols or population maintenance.

The plan advocates ecosystem-level habitat improvement and development plus circumvention of recruitment failure as principal recovery promotion means. This focus is justified by: immediate availability of fish from wild stocks or [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], availability of natural or semi-natural habitats or land for newly built habitats amenable for re-introduction, and immediately available concentration of expertise and personnel.
