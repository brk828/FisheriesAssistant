# Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan for Endangered Colorado River Fishes (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 plan, prepared under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin, is a joint U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, state and Bureau of Reclamation effort. It establishes the need for captive-reared [[Endangered Colorado River Fishes]] (the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]), identifies presumptive stocks and their priorities, sets genetics management principles, quantifies space and water requirements, evaluates existing and potential hatchery facilities, and recommends disposition rules for surplus fish. The plan is a reference guide and was revised from an April 1993 draft. It explicitly notes that the San Juan River stocks were excluded from the status analysis because a separate recovery effort exists for that river.

## Background & Description

The four species are large-river fishes native to the Colorado River system, listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Their wild recruitment has been impaired by flow regulation, altered water temperatures and habitat, backwater loss, and predation and competition by non-native fishes. Captive propagation is positioned as a temporary management tool, not a panacea. The plan lists the following Program-recognized purposes for captive fish: refugia, research and development, information and education, and augmentation or restoration stocking.

Presumptive stocks were delineated using spawning sites, homing or fidelity, geographic separation, and interchange (Table 1). Genetics surveys (allozyme, mitochondrial and nuclear DNA, and systematic work on the [[Gila Genus]]) were ongoing, with interim reports planned for 1995 to 1997. Stock priorities were assigned with Priority 1 as highest:

- Razorback sucker: four presumptive stocks, all Priority 1 (populations declining rapidly, very low numbers).
- Bonytail: Upper Basin stock Priority 2 (nearly extirpated); Lake Mohave origin broodstock is the practical source.
- Humpback chub: Priority 3, with Westwater Canyon and Black Rocks stocks noted as at catastrophic risk.
- Colorado squawfish: Priority 4 overall; the upper Colorado River upstream of Westwater Canyon was Priority 1 at stock level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hybridization is a stated concern. Introgressive hybridization between the Colorado River fishes and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] has been documented, with the Etter Pond "razorback" stock found to be hybrid. Exchange rates between some stocks are described as low (0–5%). Historical flow regimes are altered; high flows in 1983 and 1993 were associated with recruitment questions. Survival of young fish is limited by predation, competition and the early-life "bottleneck."

Water quality requirements for propagation include:

- Growth occurs between about 60 and 70 °F (16–21 °C); growth ceases below 50 °F (10 °C).
- Minimum inflow dissolved oxygen of 4.0 ppm.
- Unionized ammonia must stay below 0.0125 ppm, requiring about 6 gpm per pound of daily feed at pH 7.5.
- Nitrogen supersaturation must be avoided to prevent gas bubble disease.

Outdoor ponds were judged more productive than raceways; field experience showed ponds yielded about twice the growth rate of raceway-reared fish. Ponds of 0.25 to 0.33 acre were judged optimal, with 0.5 acre an absolute maximum. Carrying capacity was set at 500 lb/acre in the first growing season, 1,000 lb/acre in the second, and 1,500 lb/acre thereafter. Evaporation was estimated at 4 acre-feet per surface acre annually.

## Key Findings

**Genetics and broodstock design**

- A 5 × 5 di-allele (factorial) breeding matrix is the minimum recommended strategy, yielding 25 family lots from five males and five females.
- The target effective population size is 50; the maximum acceptable inbreeding rate is 1% per generation.
- Approximately 30 adults of each sex per family lot are needed, in both primary and backup refuges.
- Attrition assumptions: fertilized eggs 90% loss; 50% between first and second growing seasons; 20% in subsequent years; 5% annually after maturity.
- Fish needed at the end of each growing season for one family lot: 500, 250, 200, 160, 152, 144 (for razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish, which mature after six seasons).

**Space and water requirements**

- Broodstock development for four stocks (100 family lots) requires about 16.0 active acres and roughly 112 acre-feet of water annually.
- Immediate and short-term needs (Tables 13–15) total about 27.3 acres of pond space.
- Existing dedicated propagation pond space was 6 acres in 1994, expanding to about 7.2 acres in 1995, producing a deficit of about 20.1 acres.
- Proposed augmentation and restoration stocking experiments would add about 43.25 acres of ponds and 606 to 806 acre-feet of water.

**Existing facilities**

- [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (Utah): primary long-term refuge; six existing ponds, with six additional 0.2-acre ponds completed in 1993 and a funded expansion.
- [[Horsethief State Wildlife Area]] (Colorado): refuge ponds of 3.5 acres total, capable of holding about 6,180 lb of large fish; no room for additional ponds.
- [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] (Utah): one 0.4-acre pond; potential expansion to about 5 acres.
- Temporary facilities include the Bellvue research hatchery (Colorado) and Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico).

**Potential new facilities**

- Five potential long-range sites in Colorado were identified (Cline Trout Farm, Craig Municipal, Crystal Properties, Silver Springs Trout Farm, Rainbow Springs Trout Ranch).
- Utah sites included Gandy Warm Springs (4,500 gpm of 27 °C water) and Wahweap.
- Colorado Division of Wildlife proposed a Native Aquatic Wildlife Propagation Facility at about $4.95 million in construction cost. The Utah Division of Wildlife proposed a Gandy facility estimated at $6.32 million and a Wahweap facility at $870,000.

**Stocking and disposition**

- All augmentation or restoration stocking must be experimental, approved through a stocking plan, Section 7 consultation and a Federal permit.
- Proposed experiments include razorback sucker survival trials of 4-, 8- and 12-inch fish (300,000, 75,000 and 18,000 fish respectively) and bonytail restoration in three reaches.
- Surplus fish disposition follows a priority list, with euthanasia following AVMA and American Fisheries Society guidance when fish are not needed.

**Specific recommendations** (ten total, abbreviated):

1. Adopt a policy and guidelines for stocking captive-reared fish.
2. Finalize the Genetic Management Plan.
3. Establish a central program office policy for reporting captive fish quarterly.
4. Revisit endangered fish and hatchery needs annually.
5. Give the Propagation Subcommittee program responsibility for culture recommendations.
6. Maintain broodstocks in two refuges as insurance.
7. Shorten genetics and systematics contract timeframes.
8. Focus initially on developing refuges and broodstocks for priority species.
9. Evaluate cost sharing for state propagation facilities.
10. Review and adopt a protocol for disposition of surplus fish.
