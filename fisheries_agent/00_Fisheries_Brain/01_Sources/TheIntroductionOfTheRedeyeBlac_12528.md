# The Introduction of the Redeye Black Bass and the Threadfin Shad into California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Centrarchidae #Dorosomatidae #[[Fish Introduction]] #[[Forage Fish]]

## Executive Summary

This 1954 report by J.B. Kimsey documents the experimental introduction of two fish species into California waters by the California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG), in cooperation with the Tennessee Game and Fish Department. The effort targeted a persistent management problem: California's "marginal waters" — streams and reservoirs poorly suited to trout or existing warmwater game/forage species. Two species were airlifted from Tennessee in November 1953: the [[Redeye Black Bass]] (*Micropterus coosae*), intended as a new game fish for small, swift, warm upland streams; and the [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Signalosa petenensis atchafalayae*), intended as a forage fish for fluctuating reservoirs where existing forage species (e.g., [[Gizzard Shad]]) failed due to littoral zone instability during drawdown.

## Background & Description

### Redeye Black Bass (*Micropterus coosae* Hubbs and Bailey)
- Member of the [[Micropterus]] (black bass) genus, closely related to other game basses.
- Described qualitatively by source-range anglers as "scrappy, colorful, and highly palatable."
- No detailed morphometric data (length/weight) provided in this source; 40 individuals collected, 39 survived transport.

### Threadfin Shad (*Signalosa petenensis atchafalayae* Evermann & Kendall)
- Fork length (FL) of 161 specimens (collected November 6, 1953): mean = **2.7 inches**; range = **2.2–3.2 inches**.
- Conversion factor: Fork Length = 0.851 × Total Length.
- Maximum size noted: does not exceed approximately **7 inches**.
- Described as "extremely fragile and difficult to transport," a key logistical constraint distinguishing it from hardier forage species like the [[Gizzard Shad]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Redeye Black Bass
- Native range: Georgia, Alabama, and parts of Tennessee.
- Primary habitat: small upland streams that are poor producers of other warmwater game species and trout — an ecological niche largely vacant in California's stream systems.
- Feeding/capture behavior: readily taken by artificial lures; live bait most effective in smaller, less turbid streams (per Tennessee angler experience).
- Targeted California niche: streams unsuitable for trout (due to high summer temperatures) yet also unsuitable for typical warmwater fishes (due to small size, low average temperature, and swift current) — a management gap the species was expected to fill.

### Threadfin Shad
- Trophic role: planktivorous forage fish, feeding on plankton — supporting higher trophic levels (game fish) in reservoir systems.
- Reproductive ecology: requires **no substrate for spawning**, making it well-suited to fluctuating reservoir water levels.
- Life cycle independence from the littoral zone — a critical adaptive advantage over other forage fishes whose spawning/food production is disrupted by reservoir drawdown.
- Comparative advantage over [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*): lacks its "undesirable characteristics" (e.g., excessive size, poor forage value), while remaining highly acceptable as prey for game species.

## Key Findings

- **Rationale for introduction**: CDFG research identified the lack of a suitable forage species as the primary limiting factor for warmwater sport fishing success in California's fluctuating reservoirs, due to drawdown effects on littoral zone spawning and food production.
- **Transport event**: Both species were flown from Tennessee to California in November 1953 via cooperation with the Tennessee Game and Fish Department.
- **Redeye Black Bass outcome**: 39 of 40 individuals (from Sheeds Creek, tributary to the Conasauga River, southeastern Tennessee) survived transport (arrived November 11, 1953); held in spawning ponds at [[Central Valleys Fish Hatchery]], Elk Grove, pending experimental stream stocking planned for 1954.
- **Threadfin Shad outcome**: Of ~3,150 shad seined from the Tennessee River (~24 miles below Watts Bar Dam), only 314 survived air transport to California (~10% survival), underscoring the species' fragility.
- **Threadfin Shad stocking sites** (all San Diego County, November 1953):
  - Circle R Ranch Pond (Dougherty Ranch, tributary to [[Moosa Creek]]) — 135 fish
  - Bates Pond (Bates Bros. Ranch, tributary to Moosa Creek) — 87 fish
  - Wire Mountain Pond (Camp Pendleton Marine Base, tributary to [[Santa Margarita River]]) — 50 fish
  - Valley Center Game Farm Holding Pond (tributary to Moosa Creek) — 42 fish
- **Management significance**: This represents an early, deliberate biological introduction program aimed at filling specific ecological niches (game fish for marginal streams; forage fish for reservoirs) rather than general stocking — a notable case study in mid-20th-century fisheries management strategy in California.
- **Species identity note**: The threadfin shad is classified here under the now-revised taxonomy as *Signalosa petenensis atchafalayae*; modern nomenclature places this species as *[[Dorosoma petenense]]*.
