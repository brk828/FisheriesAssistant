# Submersed Aquatic Plants and Water Temperature Gradients in Shallow Water Bodies

#aquatic-botany #limnology #macrophytes

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Dale and Gillespie]] (Department of Botany and Genetics and Department of Land Resource Science, University of Guelph) examines how dense populations of [[Submersed Aquatic Plants]] and [[Phytoplankton Blooms]] create vertical [[Temperature Gradients]] in shallow water. Part I analyses field data from [[Lake Opinicon]], Ontario, plus two Scottish lakes. Part II reports controlled greenhouse and outdoor tub experiments. The central result is that the steepness of the midday temperature gradient rises with plant biomass, because plant tissue intercepts incoming [[Solar Radiation]] near the surface and shades the bottom. The gradient also depends on the type of producer (macrophyte versus alga), on incoming radiation, and, outdoors, on wind speed above a threshold.

## Background & Description

Submersed macrophytes and phytoplankton are a recognised nuisance in Canadian lakes, including [[Lake Okanagan]], [[Lake Wabamun]], [[Lake Chemung]] and [[Pidgeon Lake]]. They affect boating, swimming and beaches, and they alter dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, ammonia, pH, light and temperature in the water column (Buscemi 1958; Ganf 1974; Idso and Foster 1974; Moss 1969).

Earlier work by the same authors showed that floating macrophytes strongly affect near-surface temperatures. At noon on clear days, water surface temperatures under floating mats of *Lemna* were 4–11 C° higher than beneath open water (Dale and Gillespie 1976). A companion study examined temperatures near the bottom of shallow water (Dale and Gillespie 1977).

The study had two parts:
- **Part I (Field Studies):** natural macrophyte stands at Lake Opinicon (44°35′ N, 76°20′ W) and phytoplankton blooms in two Scottish lakes.
- **Part II (Experimental Studies):** cultured plants and algae in insulated tubs at the University of Guelph (43°35′ N, 80°14′ W), in a heated greenhouse and outdoors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Energy distribution.** Submersed plants convert radiant energy to heat in the surrounding water. Plant structure determines which part of the water column heats up. Clear weather concentrates light as a near-point source, and cloudy weather scatters it from a wide arc (Wetzel 1975). Dense stands therefore produce steep gradients, while sparse stands distribute energy evenly.

**Analogy to canopies.** The authors compare the process to heat exchange in air canopies, which depends on [[Leaf Area Density]] (Uchijima 1966). Heat exchange in water depends similarly on biomass per unit volume.

**Seasonal succession.** Growth type changes through the year:
- Early-season species such as [[Wild Rice]] (*Zizania aquatica*) build underwater structure after germination.
- *Ceratophyllum demersum* and *Potamogeton pectinatus* show large early-season biomass increases.
- Evergreen stands such as [[Najas Guadalupensis]], [[Chara]] spp. and *Potamogeton robbinsii* have large spring biomass that is partly replaced during the year.

**Stratification.** Where gradients exceeded 2 C°, the soil lay at or below the minimum diurnal temperature of open water (24°C). These columns stayed semipermanently stratified because shading and night-time re-radiation prevented mixing. The soil temperature at 50 cm averaged 17.3°C (range 16.0–19.3°C) at these sites.

**Thermal pollution.** At Lake Wabamun, warm discharge from power-generating plants raised lake temperature enough for *Elodea canadensis*, previously rare in Alberta, to become a severe pest (Allen and Gorham 1973; Gallup and Hickman 1973). The authors describe this as a paradox: heated inflow can remove some fauna and flora through cold soils while warm-water species invade the upper layers.

## Key Findings

### Part I: Field Studies (Lake Opinicon and Scottish Lakes)

- **Survey design.** Data from 2 clear days, August 7 and 10, 1973, came from a survey by Bristow and Crowder. Biomass was sampled at **37 sites** using 25 × 25 cm quadrats collected by SCUBA divers. Biomass was reported as g dry weight per m².
- **Temperature sampling.** Readings were taken at five depths: 10 cm, 50 cm, the centre of the vegetation (usually 1–2 m), the soil surface, and 30 cm below the apparent surface.
- **Diurnal pattern (August 6–10, 1973).** Temperatures at 12 cm and in a patch of *Vallisneria americana* were consistently at or above those at 140 cm. The maximum gradient of **1.0 C°** occurred at 1400 hours and fell to **0.2–0.1 C°** by 1615 hours. The water became isothermal for part of each day.
- **Biomass–gradient relationship.** Temperature difference between 2 cm and the soil surface correlated with **log biomass per m³**, following **AT = 4.02(log biomass/m³) − 5.33, r² = 0.952** (Fig. 1). The fit against biomass per unit area was weaker.
- **Stand contrast.** Dense stands produced about **1 C° per 10 cm** of water. Sparse stands gave less than **1 C° over 2–3 m**, confined to the water near the substrate.
- **Scottish phytoplankton blooms (1972).**
  - *Loch Leven* (cloudy, air 17°C, midday): 0.6 C° in the upper 1 m; 1.5 C° in the upper 1.5 m.
  - *Lake of Menteith* (still and clear, 31 August, 1400 hours, air 20.5°C): 2.1 C° in the upper 1 m; 2.3 C° in the upper 1.5 m.

### Part II: Greenhouse Experiments (January–February 1976)

- **Setup.** Three insulated fibre-glass tubs (70 cm deep, 120 cm diameter, 1 m³ capacity) held *Potamogeton richardsonii* (rhizomes from Green Lake), *Elodea canadensis* (sprigs from the same lake), and an algal bloom. Supplemental lighting extended the photoperiod to 16 h from January 6. Greenhouse air was held at 15°C in the dark and at least 20°C in light hours.
- **Algal bloom.** The February bloom was **95% *Chlorella* sp.**, with *Ankistrodesmus* sp. present, at roughly **3.18 × 10⁴ cells/ml**. The cells were under 2.5 µm.
- **Plant biomass (end of February).**
  - *E. canadensis*: 38.4 g dry weight, 94 m total linear length, under 20% cover.
  - *P. richardsonii*: floating part 73.0 g, upright stems and leaves 49.5 g, rhizomes 23.1 g (100 m length), 100% cover, with shoots at the surface.
- **Light penetration (February 23).** Irradiance at 10 cm was **10%** of incoming in *Chlorella* and *Elodea*, and 7% in *P. richardsonii*. At 50 cm it was 1% (algae), 0.3% (*P. richardsonii*) and 10% (*Elodea*).
- **Gradients (2 cm minus 60 cm, 1430 hours, Table 3).**
  - 12 January (5.81 MJ/m²): algae 1.4 C°, *P. richardsonii* 1.4 C°, *Elodea* 0.9 C°.
  - 10 February (5.73 MJ/m²): algae 4.3 C°, *P. richardsonii* 3.9 C°, *Elodea* 2.3 C°.
  - 12 February (6.52 MJ/m²): algae 5.0 C°, *P. richardsonii* 4.9 C°, *Elodea* 3.0 C°.
  - Gradients in later readings were about three times those in January. The gradient was 0.3 C° larger for a roughly 10% larger solar input.
- **Upper-layer gradients (2 cm minus 10 cm, Table 4).** On 10 February, algae 2.5 C°, *P. richardsonii* 2.5 C°, *Elodea* 1.3 C°. On 27 February (hazy sun, larger energy input), algae 2.2 C°, *P. richardsonii* 3.1 C° at 1140 hours and 4.0 C° at 1340 hours.
- **Key result.** Gradient magnitude tracked incoming radiation and biomass, but also depended on plant type. Light interception alone did not explain the late-February profiles, so conduction and forced convection were implicated.

### Part II: Outdoor Experiments (June 1975)

- **Setup.** One tub held a pure population of *P. richardsonii* (planted May 26), another held an algal community, and a third held well water. Probes were at 2, 10, 20, 40 and 60 cm.
- **Algal population (1 July).** 90% *Scenedesmus acuminatus* with *Euglena* sp., *Cryptomonas* sp., and other *Scenedesmus*, *Golenkinia* and *Pandorina* species.
- **Pondweed depth.** Shoot tips of *P. richardsonii* lay 10–30 cm below the surface, averaging **17 cm**.
- **Reflectivity.** Lowest for *P. richardsonii* (0.046), then algae (0.058), then clear water (0.076).
- **Radiation and wind.** Air temperatures ranged from 21.3°C (27 June, 0730 hours) to 34.2°C (29 June, 1330 hours), and showed no correlation with gradient. Average wind speed ranged from 1.9 to 4.9 m/s. Summed hourly radiation ranged from 11.6 to 22.1 MJ/m².
- **Gradient values.**
  - Algal tub: gradients of 8 C°/m (cumulative radiation 22 MJ/m²) and 4.6 C°/m (12 MJ/m²).
  - Clear-water tub: no significant gradient at 1330 hours.
  - Algal tub: 3.9–4.2 C° under high radiation, and 2.1 C° with 45% less.
  - *P. richardsonii* tub: 5.2 to 3.4 C°/m, correlated with radiation below a threshold wind speed.
- **Wind threshold.** The *P. richardsonii* gradient dropped sharply between **4 and 5 m/s**, resembling a laminar-to-turbulent transition. On 26 June, winds of 4.9 m/s produced a gradient similar to that of 27 June, when incoming energy was much lower.
- **Algal response to wind.** Algal gradients showed little response to wind between 26 June and 1 July. The authors suggest that water currents only redistributed mobile algae.

### Comparisons with Other Systems

- **Lake George, Uganda** (equatorial, shallow): phytoplankton blooms produced gradients of **5.8 C°** in the upper metre (1400–1530 hours) and **10.0 C°** across a 2 m profile in very calm weather. Water was isothermal from 1700 to 1000 hours. Turbulence spread algae through the column, and algae sank to deeper water at midday (Ganf 1974).
- **Phoenix, Arizona** (desert pond): algal blooms produced a **9–10 C°** difference between surface and deeper water (Idso and Foster 1974).

### Conclusions

1. Temperature gradients in shallow water scale with plant biomass, expressed per unit volume, more closely than per unit area.
2. Macrophytes and phytoplankton both create strong gradients by intercepting radiation near the surface and shading the bottom.
3. Gradients are reinforced where plants reduce water currents, and reduced where wind mixes the water above a threshold.
4. Plant growth through the season shifts the depth of energy absorption. This changes soil temperatures, gas solubility and the thermal environment for plant tissues. Lower leaves, rhizomes and associated fauna experience cooler, steadier temperatures than upper leaves.
5. The authors point to these effects as relevant to lake management and to the impact of thermal pollution on aquatic communities.
