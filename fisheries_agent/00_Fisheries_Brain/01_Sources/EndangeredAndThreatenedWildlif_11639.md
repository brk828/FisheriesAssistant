# Proposed Critical Habitat Designation for Four Colorado River Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #conservation-policy #critical-habitat

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register document (Vol. 58, No. 18, January 29, 1993) presents the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's proposed rule to designate critical habitat for four endemic [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). All four species are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act. The Service proposes a total of 3,370 kilometers (2,094 miles) of critical habitat across portions of seven western states (Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, California, and Wyoming), with considerable overlap among species. The proposal was issued under court order requiring designation for the razorback sucker by January 25, 1993, using presently available information. The designation represents 52% of historical range for razorback sucker, 29% for Colorado squawfish, 28% for humpback chub, and 15% for bonytail chub.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] drains approximately 627,000 square kilometers (242,000 square miles) within the United States plus 5,000 square kilometers in Mexico. Historically, the native fish fauna was dominated by cyprinids (minnows) and catostomids (suckers). The four endangered species addressed evolved in the Colorado River system and were adapted to fluctuating seasonal flows, wet and dry climatic periods, and natural flood regimes. 

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family Catostomidae): Once one of the most abundant and widely distributed fish in mainstream Colorado River habitats. The largest remaining stock occurs in [[Lake Mohave]], but recruitment is virtually nonexistent. In the Upper Basin, the species persists in the lower [[Yampa River|Yampa]] and [[Green River]], mainstream [[Colorado River]], and lower [[San Juan River]]. The extant riverine population in the upper Green River Basin consists of only about 1,000 fish. Spawning occurs in spring with rising water levels and increasing temperatures over rocky runs and gravel bars. Adults occupy diverse habitats including eddies, backwaters, gravel pits, flooded bottoms, slow runs, and sandy riffles. Larvae prefer shallow littoral zones initially, then disperse to deeper waters.

**Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, family Cyprinidae): The only living representative of genus *Ptychocheilus* in the Basin, with origins predating 6 million years ago. Native stocks survive only in the Upper Basin, with relatively high numbers only in the [[Green River]] basin of Utah and Colorado. Adults are known to migrate up to 320 kilometers (200 miles) to reach spawning areas. Winter habitat includes backwaters, runs, and eddies, particularly shallow ice-covered shoreline areas. Spawning occurs in white water canyons during declining flows in June-August at water temperatures of 22-25°C. Larval fish enter stream drift and are transported downstream for approximately 6 days, traveling average distances up to 160 kilometers to reach nursery areas in productive alongshore embayments.

**Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*, family Cyprinidae): Remains dated to approximately 4000 B.C., but not described as a species until recent times due to restricted distribution in remote white water canyons. The largest populations occur in the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], and in the Black Rocks area. Populations are found in river canyons utilizing pools, riffles, and eddies associated with boulder-strewn canyons, travertine dams, and shoreline eddies. Fish in reproductive condition are captured in May-July, spawning soon after highest spring flows when water temperatures approach 20°C.

**Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*, family Cyprinidae): The rarest native fish in the Colorado River. Formerly widespread and abundant, now represented in the wild by low numbers of old adult fish (ages 40+ years) in [[Lake Mohave]] and perhaps other lower basin reservoirs. Always considered adapted to mainstream rivers, occupying pools and eddies. Spawning never observed in nature, but reported to occur in June-July at approximately 18°C water temperatures. Wild bonytails are still capable of successful reproduction when placed in ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four endangered fishes evolved in and were adapted to the natural Colorado River environment prior to large-scale water development. Recent population declines and disappearances are associated with rapid anthropogenic changes including:

1. **Habitat Fragmentation**: Dams and diversions have fragmented former fish habitat, restricting fish movement and eliminating genetic interchange between populations.

2. **Hydrologic Alteration**: Large floods once provided food and nutrient exchange between river channels and shallow-water floodplain habitats. These floods are now controlled by numerous dams, resulting in major changes to water quality, quantity, temperature, sediment and nutrient transport.

3. **Nonnative Species**: Altered habitats are now more suitable for introduced nonnative fishes, which have flourished and compete with or prey upon native species.

4. **Recruitment Failure**: Low numbers or lack of young for some species suggest lack of reproduction and/or recruitment as a possible cause of endangerment. Remnant stocks may lose genetic diversity over time, making populations more susceptible to environmental change.

**Primary Constituent Elements** identified as essential to conservation include:

- **Water**: Sufficient quantity and quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, contaminants, nutrients, turbidity) delivered according to hydrologic regime required for each life stage
- **Physical Habitat**: River channels, bottomlands, side channels, secondary channels, oxbows, backwaters, and other areas in the 100-year floodplain providing spawning, nursery, feeding, and rearing habitats or corridors between them
- **Biological Environment**: Food supply (function of nutrient supply, productivity, availability), predation, and competition (potentially out of balance due to introduced species)

The Service emphasizes that reestablishing populations in areas within former range is important for survival and recovery. Providing geographically distinct areas with varying thermal, chemical, geological, and physical parameters will encourage maintenance of the current genetic pool.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Critical Habitat Designations:**

1. **Razorback Sucker**: 2,935 kilometers (1,824 miles) across 15 reaches, representing 52% of historical range. Includes portions of Green, Yampa, Duchesne, Colorado, White, Gunnison, and San Juan Rivers (Upper Basin); Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers (Lower Basin).

2. **Colorado Squawfish**: 1,843 kilometers (1,148 miles) across multiple reaches, representing 29% of historical range. Includes Yampa River from State Highway 394 bridge to Green River confluence; Green River from Yampa confluence to Colorado River confluence; White River from Rio Blanco Lake Dam to Green River; Gunnison River from Uncompahgre confluence to Colorado River; Colorado River from Interstate 70 bridge to North Wash/Lake Powell; San Juan River from State Route 371 Bridge to Lake Powell.

3. **Humpback Chub**: 610 kilometers (379 miles) across seven reaches, representing 28% of historical range. Includes Yampa River, Green River (including Desolation and Gray Canyons), Colorado River (Black Rocks to Fish Ford, Brown Betty Rapid to Imperial Canyon, Nautiloid Canyon to Granite Park), and Little Colorado River.

4. **Bonytail Chub**: 554 kilometers (344 miles) across five reaches, representing 15% of historical range. Includes Yampa River, Green River, Colorado River reaches (Black Rocks to Fish Ford, Brown Betty Rapid to Imperial Canyon), and Colorado River from Hoover Dam to Parker Dam including Lakes Mohave and Havasu.

**Selection Criteria for Razorback Sucker** (no recovery plan yet available):
- Known or suspected wild spawning populations
- Areas where juveniles collected or suitable nursery habitat exists
- Presently or historically occupied areas necessary for recovery with establishment potential
- Areas required to maintain rangewide distribution and diversity under varying conditions
- Areas needing special management or protection for survival and recovery

**Landownership**: Proposed critical habitat flows through variety of public and private lands, including National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Forest Service, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, state, tribal, and private lands.

**Regulatory Framework**: Designation triggers section 7 consultation requirements for Federal agencies to ensure actions do not destroy or adversely modify critical habitat. This is incremental to existing requirements to avoid jeopardy to listed species. The 100-year floodplain delineates lateral boundary of proposed critical habitat for razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish.

**Economic Analysis**: Not completed at time of proposal due to court-ordered deadline. Service committed to completing biological support document and economic analysis using Computable General Equilibrium Model (CGE Model) before finalizing designation. Analysis will evaluate costs and benefits, including impacts on hydropower production, recreation, water management, agriculture, municipal and industrial uses, mining, and oil and gas production across seven-state region at county level.

**Recovery Plans**: Approved for Colorado squawfish (1978, revised 1991), humpback chub (1979, revised 1984 and 1990), and bonytail chub (1984, revised 1990). Razorback sucker recovery plan in preparation by Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team.

**Public Process**: Service solicited comments through March 30, 1993, with public hearings planned for Phoenix (Arizona), Denver (Colorado), and southern California. Final rule will incorporate public comments on both the proposal and the forthcoming economic analysis and biological support document.
