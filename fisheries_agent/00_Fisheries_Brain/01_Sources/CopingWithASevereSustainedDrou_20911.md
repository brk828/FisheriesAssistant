# Coping With a Severe Sustained Drought on the Colorado River: Introduction and Overview

#freshwater-ecology #water-policy #drought

## Executive Summary

This introduction to a Water Resources Bulletin special issue (Vol. 31, No. 5, October 1995) by [[Robert A. Young]] presents the second phase of an interdisciplinary study of a potential [[Severe Sustained Drought]] (SSD) in the [[Colorado River]] Basin. The study asks three questions: what hydrologic impacts such a drought would cause, what economic, social and environmental impacts would follow in the southwestern U.S., and which alternative institutional arrangements could help cope with it. Two modeling approaches were used: a hydrologic-economic optimization model in which water allocation institutions are decision variables, and a real-time simulation-gaming exercise in which players representing each basin state responded to an unfolding drought. The paper is a framing overview; it does not itself report the study's final quantitative findings.

## Background & Description

Drought is a recurring risk in the arid southwest, and growth in water demand is expected to intensify conflicts among consumptive and nonconsumptive uses, environmental and economic goals, and states. Tree-ring evidence, first presented by [[Harold Fritts]] at a 1982 conference, suggests that droughts more extreme and longer than any in the instrumental record occurred in past centuries. Based on this evidence, the study's Phase I report ([[Gregg and Getches]], 1991) laid the groundwork for a two-phase approach supported by the [[Man and the Biosphere Program]].

The Colorado River rises in the Rocky Mountains and drains parts of Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. It supplies agriculture, cities, industry, hydropower, recreation, and fish and wildlife benefits. Key facts reported in the text:

- Mexico receives 1.5 million acre-feet by treaty, about one-tenth of the estimated average virgin flow.
- The river is fully used for offstream purposes; water reaches the Gulf of California only during occasional high flows.
- Systematic flow measurements began just over a century ago, while tree-ring studies extend the climate record back several centuries.
- The most serious tree-ring-identified low-precipitation period was a several-decade episode in the late 1500s.
- Federal storage (managed by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]) is roughly four times the annual flow, so drought is unimportant under normal variation but could become significant under extreme conditions.

The study team included engineers and hydrologists, tree-ring scientists, attorneys, environmental scientists, economists, sociologists and public administration specialists from universities across the basin and the consulting firm Hydrosphere, Inc. It was overseen by a consortium of basin Water Resources Research Institutes and funded mainly by the U.S. Interior Department and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.

The conceptual framework draws on three traditions:

- Interdisciplinary water systems analysis, rooted in the [[Harvard Water Program]] (Maass et al., 1962).
- Multiobjective planning under U.S. Water Resources Council principles, including [[National Economic Development]] accounts, a social well-being account, and [[NEPA]]-type environmental assessment, with [[Benefit-Cost Analysis]] as the economic core.
- [[Gilbert White]]'s natural hazards paradigm, which identifies three responses to hazards: spreading losses (e.g., insurance), modifying the hazard (e.g., storage and conveyance structures), and modifying human vulnerability (e.g., changing operating rules and laws).

Because the basin's dams already provide the second kind of response, the study assumes no new structures and concentrates on vulnerability modification. The authors argue the work is unusual in anticipating drought impacts through modeling, rather than only assessing them retrospectively, and in its scope of collaboration.

**Defining drought.** Disciplines define drought differently. The team chose a hydrologic indicator: annual river flow at [[Lees Ferry]], just below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], the point that divides the Upper and Lower Basins by convention and law. Because the tree-ring reconstructions provide the long-term record, this choice makes it possible to extend the record back in time. The authors acknowledge that the choice ignores broader precipitation effects, including ecological and social effects not tied to river flow.

**Representative drought.** The study uses a 38-year drought patterned on, but not identical to, the most severe dry period in the tree-ring record. It contains about two decades of unusually low flow followed by a wet period long enough to return mean inflow to its long-term average.

**General approach.** The federal storage network, including [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]] (each able to store more than two years of average native flow), operates under the [[Law of the River]]. This body of law combines interstate compacts, federal statutes, Supreme Court decisions, the Mexico treaty and Interior Department operating rules. The study projected socio-economic conditions and water availability assuming the drought began in 1990, then carried out three components: hydrologic and availability projections, a legal and institutional assessment, and estimates of economic, social and environmental damages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The text addresses aquatic and riparian resources mainly through flow-dependent environmental indicators, not through species-level data. [[Thomas B. Hardy]]'s environmental evaluation identified seven categories of flow-dependent resources:

- Threatened, endangered or sensitive fish species
- Wetland and riparian habitats
- National and state wildlife refuges
- Fish hatcheries and other flow-dependent facilities
- Cold-water and warm-water sport fisheries (valued economically, not modeled as environmental variables)

Four categories were simulated directly. Each was assigned one of four states at every time step: stable, threatened, endangered, or extirpated, based on reservoir levels or stream flows. The [[Tennant Method]], which relies on aggregated flow data and professional judgment about discharge adequacy for aquatic life, was used because no site-specific data could be collected within the study's resource limits.

Salinity is another important water quality concern. The Lower Basin demand functions incorporate salinity, and salinity damages were estimated from Bureau of Reclamation reports after correcting for suspected overestimation. Hydropower, recreation and salinity abatement are also treated as instream values affected by low flows.

## Key Findings

The paper is an introduction, so its "findings" are the study's structure, its key assumptions and its scenarios. Substantive results appear in the companion articles.

**Drought scenarios (Tarboton, 1995), with annual flows at Lees Ferry:**

1. **Colorado River Basin Severe Drought (1579–1600):** the most severe sustained drought in the tree-ring reconstruction back to 1520. It has a 22-year mean flow of 11.1 maf, with a 17-year mean of 10.5 maf for 1579–1595. Mean recorded native flow is 15.2 maf. Estimated return period: 400 to 700 years.
2. **Colorado Drought in Historic Record (1943–1964):** the most severe drought in the observed record since 1906. Its 22-year mean is 13.4 maf. Estimated return period: 50 to 100 years, considered likely to recur regardless of tree-ring uncertainty.
3. **Colorado Rearranged Severe Drought:** an artificial scenario in which scenario 1's flows are reordered so the lowest come last. Its 16-year mean is 9.6 maf, with an estimated return period of 2,000 to 10,000+ years. It was designed to test system response to a catastrophic drought and served as the **representative drought** for most subsequent analyses.

**Modeling tools:**

- The [[Colorado River Network Model]] (CRM), a monthly network flow model, represents 107 river reaches, 14 major reservoirs, 29 inflow points and 265 consumptive use points, and estimates hydropower and salinity.
- The [[Colorado River Institutional Model]] (CRIM), a nonlinear optimization model with annual time steps, represents 22 river nodes, seven major reservoirs and 32 offstream consumptive use locations, and tests policy scenarios.
- [[Gaming Simulation]] involved one player per basin state plus one for the U.S. Secretary of the Interior. Three rule sets were tested in three games (one run by electronic mail, two in person): the status quo under the Law of the River, a limited-power river basin commission requiring unanimity, and a system allowing water banking and interstate sale or lease, with the Secretary holding veto power over third-party and environmental costs.
- Mitigation simulations used the CRM with the representative drought and, for comparison, 1,000 years of synthetic normal and wet flows.

**Social and legal components:**

- [[Krannich et al.]] (1995) surveyed residents of the Grand Valley of western Colorado (around Grand Junction) and the San Joaquin Valley of California (Bakersfield and Kern County) on drought likelihood, financial vulnerability and acceptable responses.
- [[MacDonnell et al.]] (1995) found that the Law of the River creates implicit priorities that are uncertain and examined flexible responses such as water banking and marketing within the existing framework.
- [[Kenney]] (1995) identified seven types of interstate institutional options and recommended forums and processes for resolving complex, divisive issues rather than substantive fixes.

**Limitations (as stated by the authors):**

- Results are partial and tentative because of the basin's large scale, technical complexity and limited resources.
- The hydrologic drought measure ignores broader precipitation effects.
- Environmental coverage was not as extensive as desired.
- Economic demand measures were highly aggregated and based on a few local sites.
- Recreational losses, including rafting and fishing elsewhere in the basin, are underestimated because data were lacking.
- Secondary economic impacts were not analyzed.

The research team planned to continue and refine the study over several years.
