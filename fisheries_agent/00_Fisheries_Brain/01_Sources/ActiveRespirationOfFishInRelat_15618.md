# Active Respiration of Fish in Relation to Ambient Concentrations of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 experimental study by S. P. Basu (University of Toronto) measured the oxygen consumption of five freshwater fish species during sustained, electrically stimulated swimming, across combinations of [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and [[Carbon Dioxide]]. Oxygen uptake fell linearly on a log scale as carbon dioxide rose, with a slope characteristic of each species and temperature. The study combined these active-rate data with literature resting rates to estimate [[Scope For Activity]], and compared species by [[Respiratory Sensitivity]] using several asphyxiation and residual-oxygen standards. It concluded that blood oxygen-loading capacity bears little direct relation to whole-animal oxygen transport from water during activity.

## Background & Description

Dissolved respiratory gases shape the metabolic rate of aquatic animals. Oxygen limitation of fish had been studied extensively, but the effect of carbon dioxide during activity had received little attention. Basu addressed this gap with a systematic series of experiments.

**Species studied:**
- [[Speckled Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): cold, well-oxygenated water; low oxygen affinity and high carbon dioxide sensitivity of blood. Obtained from the Chatsworth, Ontario hatchery.
- [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*): cold, well-oxygenated water; blood characteristics similar to trout. Difficult to keep active, so it was dropped after the first set of tests.
- [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) and [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*): warm-water species with high oxygen affinity and low carbon dioxide sensitivity of blood. Goldfish were included because they are widely available as laboratory animals.

**Methods overview:**
- Fish were acclimated to 10, 15, 20 or 30°C for at least one month, with oxygen held near 92% air saturation.
- Active metabolism was induced in a [[Rotating Chamber]] (5 L capacity) using a 3-volt, 60-cycle electrical stimulus and rotation at 20 rpm. Four consecutive 15 to 20 minute determinations were made per fish, with rest periods between them.
- Water with controlled oxygen and carbon dioxide was supplied by a column system (Shepard-type gas displacement with nitrogen, plus CO2 and O2 equilibrating columns).
- Oxygen was measured by the sodium azide modification of the [[Winkler Method]]. Free carbon dioxide was measured by titration and checked against a graphic method. Biochemical oxygen demand was below 0.2 ppm, so it was disregarded.

**Water supply (Table I):** temperature 9°C, dissolved oxygen 11.33 ppm, carbon dioxide 2.0 ppm, pH 8.0, total hardness 269.2 ppm as CaCO3, total alkalinity 224 ppm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames dissolved gas concentrations as ecological limits. Below a [[Critical Oxygen Content]], oxygen uptake becomes dependent on the surrounding water, a condition called [[Respiratory Dependence]]. Basu notes that carbon dioxide concentrations tested (up to about 250 to 275 ppm) cover the range normally found in nature, except under anaerobic metabolism. Oxygen reductions were judged to have larger effects on scope for activity than the carbon dioxide increases that occur in nature.

Mechanistically, Basu argues that respiratory dependence reflects the balance between blood transport capacity and water flow over the gills. Because blood and water flow in a [[Counter-Current Exchange]] system, uptake depends on how much water passes the gill as much as on oxygen partial pressure. At air saturation, 15 to 20 volumes of water must pass the gill per volume of blood; at 25% saturation this rises to 60 to 80 volumes.

## Key Findings

1. **Linear log-response to carbon dioxide.** At a given oxygen level, the logarithm of active oxygen consumption falls linearly with carbon dioxide concentration. The slope is characteristic of each species and temperature. Regression equations are given for each case (Tables IV and V). The one exception was speckled trout at 44% air saturation, where rates at 70 and 80 ppm CO2 showed complete loss of activity.

2. **Temperature sensitivity.** Carbon dioxide sensitivity decreases with increasing [[Acclimation Temperature]]. For goldfish, the slope at 10°C was significantly steeper than at 20 and 30°C (F = 32.5, df 2 and 15).

3. **Oxygen and carbon dioxide interaction.** Oxygen concentration sets the active rate in the absence of carbon dioxide. Its influence on the carbon dioxide response becomes marked only near the lower lethal limit. Goldfish were least affected down to 50% air saturation. Bullhead slopes differed significantly only at 8.3% air saturation (F = 10.32).

4. **Active metabolic rates.** At 164 g and 10°C, speckled trout active rate was 342 mg/kg/hr and standard rate was 66 mg/kg/hr, giving a scope of 276 mg/kg/hr. Scopes at 15 and 20°C were 291 and 220 mg/kg/hr. Scope peaked near 15°C, consistent with [[Graham]] (1949).

5. **Species comparisons of active rates.** Results agreed with previous studies for speckled trout (and with [[Fry and Hart]] 1948 for goldfish, where the present values were higher per unit weight). Bullhead results conflicted with the earlier work of Paul and Fry. Bullhead scope peaked at 20°C rather than rising to 30°C, which the author could not resolve. The data for the sucker and carp were new.

6. **Scope for activity with carbon dioxide.** Scope decreased with increasing carbon dioxide for all species. Speckled trout scopes at 6 mm Hg CO2 were almost the same at all three temperatures.

7. **Respiratory sensitivity (Table VI).** Using the half-scope index as an area, the goldfish was at least three times as resistant as speckled trout at 10 and 20°C. At 30°C, carp was the most resistant, goldfish least, and bullhead intermediate.

8. **Residual-level discrepancies.** Differences between the estimated and experimentally determined residual oxygen levels may be explained by [[Plasma Transport]] of oxygen during death in sealed containers.

9. **Blood versus whole-animal oxygen transport.** Blood-loading curves and active respiration curves diverged by species: almost identical slopes in sucker, a factor of about 2:1 in speckled trout, 10:1 in carp, and nearly infinite in bullhead. Basu concluded that blood oxygen uptake in the presence of carbon dioxide shows no direct relation to whole-animal oxygen transport from water during activity.

10. **Gill efficiency.** Hazelhoff and Evenhuis (1952), cited in the paper, reported 51% oxygen utilization from water under normal counter-current flow in tench, falling to 9% when flow was reversed.
