# Fishes of the Colorado River Drainage, Lees Ferry to Surprise Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents native and introduced fishes of the Colorado River between [[Lees Ferry]] and [[Surprise Canyon]], Arizona, based on a July 7–21, 1975 ecological survey run by the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] and sponsored by the U.S. [[National Park Service]]. High water and unusually cold water hampered main-river collecting, but twenty-one stations were sampled and useful data on native and exotic species were obtained. The author, Robert B. Miller, also draws on his 1968 [[Grand Canyon]] survey (Lees Ferry to Diamond Creek) and on earlier records. The report focuses on the conservation status of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native species, and on the threat posed by introduced fishes and flow and temperature fluctuations.

## Background & Description

The survey was conducted in July 1975 and reported by Miller. The appended summary of the 1968 survey was co-authored with Gerald R. Smith (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan, January 1972).

**Native fishes** (collected or reliably recorded in the region over roughly 45 years):
- Suckers: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*). Both are common in the main river and tributaries.
- Minnows: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and the [[Gila Robusta Complex]], which includes *G. elegans* and *G. cypha*.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), with no confirmed recent records.

**Introduced fishes** (about ten species or taxa, summarized):
- Salmonids: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and a single [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) taken at RM 19.5 on 16 August 1971.
- Cyprinids: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). A juvenile red shiner × fathead minnow hybrid was also noted.
- Ictalurids: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*).
- Others: [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*, Surprise Canyon only), [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*, Surprise Canyon only), and [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water conditions:** Very high water and unusually cold river temperatures (the OCR range is garbled, with an upper limit of about 60°F) prevented native fish spawning in the main river in 1975. The report concludes that the best spawning sites are the lower reaches of tributaries, especially the lower [[Little Colorado River]].
- **Flow and temperature fluctuation:** The author identifies this as a major problem for native fishes because it can curtail or prevent successful reproduction.
- **Habitat:** The [[Little Colorado River]] mouth area is the most important habitat for [[Humpback Chub]] reproduction and for the maintenance of other native fishes.
- **Exotic species pressure:** Rainbow trout appear to have taken over Tapeats Creek. The author notes that exotic fish populations are poorly quantified and their ecological relationships to natives are unclear.
- **Plains killifish spread:** Since 1938 *Fundulus zebrinus* has moved down the Little Colorado River to its mouth (none taken there in 1968). It was also taken in 1975 at the mouth of Parashont Wash, about 198.5 river miles above Diamond Creek. The author cites [[Hubbs and Hauer (1973)]], who reported detrimental effects of this species on native fishes in Texas, and holds out little hope of eliminating it once established.
- **Hatchery programs:** Adult humpback chub can be collected and reared at the [[Willow Beach Hatchery]], which also cultures bonytail.

## Key Findings

1. **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*)** is endangered and rarely recorded. The 1975 survey collected and released a fish about 6 inches long near the mouth of the Little Colorado River, possibly a humpback chub. Records include Lees Ferry (1963; 1971), Vasey's Paradise (1968), near the Little Colorado River mouth, south of the Colorado River (17 specimens, 1968), three miles below the Little Colorado (3 specimens, 1968), and south of Bright Angel Creek (1 holotype, before 1962). The 1968 survey recorded 5 fish from the main river. Juveniles cannot be reliably identified because the diagnostic hump develops only near adult size, so juveniles from the Little Colorado mouth were assigned to the *Gila robusta* complex.
2. **Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** is probably extirpated from this reach. Its absence from records is attributed to lack of suitable habitat, although it occurs in both upper and lower basin reaches.
3. **Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)** has no reliable records since 1920. The only recent record is a specimen of about 320 mm standard length, caught near Havasu Creek in June 1973 and examined by Miller on 25 July 1975.
4. **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)** may be even rarer than the humpback chub in Grand Canyon and is in danger elsewhere. Bonytail are being cultured at the Willow Beach Hatchery.
5. **Speckled dace** were taken at many sites in 1975, except Tapeats Creek, where rainbow trout appear to have displaced them. No specimens were collected in 1975 despite repeated efforts, unlike the 1968 collection. Dace above the Paria River and in the Virgin River basin represent several distinctive forms, but no comparative study of Paria–Spencer Canyon populations has been done.
6. **Exotic fish trends:** The author believes exotic fishes have not increased significantly since 1968. Carp and channel catfish have been in the river since around 1900, while red shiner and fathead minnow invaded within the past 20 years.
7. **Historical records:** The humpback chub was first described from Grand Canyon (a specimen from south of Bright Angel Creek). It is also known from archaeological material at [[Stanton's Cave]] in Marble Canyon, 32 river miles below Lees Ferry.
8. **Management recommendations:**
   - Avoid environmental disturbance on the Little Colorado River, which provides critical humpback chub and native fish habitat.
   - Maintain a preserved series of humpback chub specimens, especially juveniles, to aid identification.
   - Consider restoration using hatchery-reared fish from Willow Beach.
