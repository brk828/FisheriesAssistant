# The Oxygen Balance of Streams

#limnology #stream-ecology #aquatic-macrophytes #benthic-invertebrates #oxygen-balance

## Executive Summary

This review by Edwards and Owens, from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] at Stevenage, argues that the classical [[Streeter-Phelps Equation]] is inadequate for describing dissolved oxygen in streams. The equation treats oxygen demand as bacterial [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (BOD) and supply as [[Atmospheric Reaeration]] only. The authors show that benthic respiration, planktonic and attached respiration, [[Photosynthesis]] by algae and macrophytes, and gas loss from plant beds are often of comparable importance. Their evidence comes mainly from shallow southern English streams, especially the [[River Ivel]] and [[River Lark]].

## Background & Description

The Streeter-Phelps approach (Equation 1) describes the deficit D through time as a balance between deoxygenation (Kd·L, where L is remaining oxygen demand) and reaeration (Kr·D). The reaeration rate is proportional to the saturation deficit. The authors note that this model has been widely used to calculate [[Reaeration Coefficient]] values and to predict the effects of pollution loads, including with analogue computers.

The general oxygen budget for a stretch of river without tributaries is Q = P + D − R, where Q is the rate of oxygen gain, P is photosynthetic production, R is utilization, and D is surface transfer. The authors stress that isolating components, using [[Light and Dark Bottle Method]] tests or bell-jar experiments, alters nutrients, surfaces and turbulence. Estimates from isolated components must therefore be interpreted cautiously.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Benthic respiration.** The importance of sediment depends on water depth. For a hypothetical river with water BOD of 0.08 ppm/h and mud consumption of 0.1 g/m²/h, mud accounts for more oxygen use than the overlying water where depth is below 1.25 m. [[Odum]] (1960) concluded that benthic respiration usually exceeds water-column respiration in systems shallower than 3 m. Mud respiration is independent of sediment depth below about 2 cm in the [[Edwards and Rolley]] cores. Oxygen consumption depends on overlying oxygen concentration, with a fitted exponent of about 0.45. Seasonal maxima are about twice winter rates, and the temperature coefficient is about 0.07/°C (Q10 ≈ 1.9).

Benthic fauna modify this respiration. Larvae of [[Chironomus Riparius]] increase aerobic layer depth, with effects rising up to about 40,000/m². In the Elbe estuary, aerobic layer depth was directly proportional to tubificid density up to about 50,000/m². Erosion of the top 0.5 cm of mud raised oxygen consumption from 0.2 to 1.2 g/m² h.

**Sewage-related growths.** Filamentous bacteria such as [[Sphaerotilus Natans]] can develop in streams polluted by soluble organic wastes. A summer biomass of about 10 g dry weight/m² would consume at least 7 g O₂/m² day at 20°C.

**Macrophytes.** Summer standing crops of macrophytes reached up to about 500 g dry weight/m². Respiration may reach 20 g O₂/m² day at 20°C. Emergent species such as [[Hippuris Vulgaris]] and [[Berula Erecta]] complicate measurement because gas moves through aerenchyma. Temperature coefficients were 2.7 (Callitriche, Ranunculus), 2.2 (Berula) and 1.5 (Hippuris) between 10 and 20°C.

**Surface area.** About 1 mg dry weight of plant corresponds to roughly 1 cm² of plant surface. The plant surface to river-bed ratio may exceed 50:1 in summer and averaged about 30:1 across four shallow streams from April to September. Only about 10% of bacteria in the water column appeared to be attached to plants in the Ivel study.

## Key Findings

**1. Planktonic and BOD measurements.** Standard 5-day BOD tests have several flaws. Dilution suppresses natural conditions, algal oxygen dependence is ignored, and light is excluded. Bottle surfaces also promote bacterial growth. Bottle rotation raised BOD by 50% in Thames Estuary samples. Kittrell and Kochtitzky found oxidation rates up to 7 times laboratory BOD values in a turbulent river. Longitudinal mixing may treble rates, according to Nejedly and Pelz (1964). Short-term bottle respiration may be more useful in deep streams. Across 24 stream sites, rates were mostly below 0.22 ppm/h, with a mean of about 0.1 ppm/h. One grossly polluted site reached 0.44 ppm/h.

**2. Photosynthesis.** Algal oxygen production was significant at 8 of 24 English rivers surveyed in summer 1963. Gross production reached 18.2 g O₂/m² day in a diatom bloom in a tidal creek. Epiphytic diatoms on Hippuris in the Ivel had estimated production of 0.09–0.94 g/m² h in April 1960 and 0.94–9.40 g/m² h in March 1961. Gross macrophyte production ranged from 3.2 to 35 g O₂/m² day. Light saturation in a chalk stream was reached near 50 cal/cm² h, with a maximum of 1.1 g/m² h at 13°C.

**3. Gas loss from plant beds.** Gas bubbles from weed beds in the Ivel totalled about 1000 ml/m² day. This loss equalled about 6.5% of production in June 1962 and about 4% in August 1963. Loss increased with light intensity and supersaturation. In Silver Springs, gas loss was under 1% of production.

**4. Atmospheric reaeration.** Reaeration in the Ivel and Lark ranged from a loss of 1 g/m² day under supersaturation to a gain of about 4 g/m² day. Diffusion was largely masked in shallow, fast streams. Reaeration coefficients from sag-curve methods are underestimated where benthic oxygen use is high and overestimated where daylight photosynthesis occurs. A predictive regression for clean water gave k₂₀ = 9.41V^0.87H^−1.85 (V in ft/s, H in ft). Pollutants reduce reaeration rates. Weirs also increase aeration: the mean deficit ratio on the Ivel was 2.03, and a 6.25 ft fall in the Lark raised oxygen by 4.7 ppm.

**5. Balance and conclusions.** The authors conclude that the Streeter-Phelps model is inadequate in most streams except deep rivers without abundant algae. Respiration in unpolluted Ivel reaches were usually no more than 13 g O₂/m² day. Downstream of a sewage outfall on the Lark, respiration was 18–30 g O₂/m² day. Summer photosynthesis produced about 10–15 g O₂/m² day in both rivers. Photosynthesis and its subsequent breakdown are of comparable importance except in grossly polluted rivers.
