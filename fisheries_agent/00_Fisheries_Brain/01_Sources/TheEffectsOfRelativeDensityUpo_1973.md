## Executive Summary

This study by E. W. Warren (1973) experimentally investigates how **relative population density** affects behaviour and reproduction in the [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*), a live-bearing freshwater poeciliid. Using eight controlled experiments manipulating either fish number or living volume, the author examined male courtship, aggression, activity, and female reproductive output (young produced, ovarian staging) across a density gradient. Key findings show that **courtship behaviour peaks at an intermediate density (12–16 fish/l)**, declining both below and above this optimum, while **aggression increases monotonically with density**. Activity levels showed no consistent density-dependent pattern. Reproductive output (number of young, ovarian developmental stages) was inversely related to density and peaked at a considerably *lower* density than the behavioural optimum, suggesting that behavioural and physiological/reproductive responses to crowding may be regulated by partially independent mechanisms. These findings contribute to understanding of **density-dependent population regulation** in fish, complementing earlier work on plateau population sizes in guppy colonies (see [[Population Density Regulation In Fish]]).

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a behavioural/ecological study rather than a morphometric one, the following body-condition measures were recorded as controls:

- **Length**: Measured snout to base of caudal fin; no significant differences found across density treatments for either sex, indicating length was not a density-sensitive trait under ad libitum feeding.
- **Weight**: Males showed no significant weight differences between high/low density groups (beginning vs. end of experiments). Females occasionally showed significant weight differences, attributed to **gravidity** (density-dependent) rather than nutritional limitation.
- **Secondary sexual courtship markers in males** (used as behavioural proxies, not fixed morphology):
  - Number of discrete **black spots** on body (excluding tail markings) — density-sensitive, significant differences in 2 of 5 experiments.
  - **Dorsal fin erection** frequency — significant in all 5 male-present experiments.
  - **Gonopodial swings** — significant in 3 of 5 experiments.
  - **Sigmoid body posture** (S-shaped courtship display per Baerends et al. 1955) — frequency significant in 4/5 experiments; duration significant in 2/5.

Ovarian histological staging followed [[Liley And Donaldson 1969]] classification: oocyte stages I–IV, corpora lutea stages α–δ; analysis focused on **stage III oocytes** and **developing young** due to their predominance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Laboratory aquaria (12.6 l tanks and smaller Perspex containers, 0.5–10.5 l), maintained at 24°C, 18h:6h light:dark cycle, pH 7.6–8.0, hardness 160–180 mg CaCO₃/l — simulating stable freshwater conditions.
- **Social structure**: Experiments included male-only, female-only, and mixed-sex groupings to isolate sex-specific density effects.
- **Density manipulation**: Achieved via (a) reducing living volume for constant fish number, or (b) increasing fish number in constant volume — both methods produced comparable behavioural trends, indicating **true density effects** rather than artifacts of volume or number alone.
- **Confounding factors controlled**: Food (fed to excess with 'Tetramin'), oxygen (continuous aeration, verified sufficient per [[Warren 1972]]), and metabolic waste (partial water mixing between high/low density groups in early experiments; full water changes in later ones) — none of these explained the observed behavioural differences, supporting **density per se** as the causal factor.
- **Population regulation context**: Builds on prior studies (Breder & Coates 1932; Shoemaker 1944; Silliman 1948, 1968; Silliman & Gutsell 1958) showing that guppy populations plateau at species/strain-specific asymptotic densities (0.87–9.3 fish/l) despite excess food, implicating behavioural and reproductive suppression—rather than resource limitation—as regulatory mechanisms. See [[Cannibalism In Guppies]] and [[Rose 1959 Population Control]].

## Key Findings

1. **Courtship behaviour peaks at intermediate density (12–16 fish/l)**: Male dorsal fin erections, gonopodial swings, sigmoid reactions, black spot number, and courtship chases all increased with density up to this optimum, then declined at higher densities — indicating a non-monotonic, "inverted-U" relationship.

2. **Aggression increases with density**: Agonistic interactions (biting, tail-beating/"sparring") rose consistently with increasing density across nearly all experiments (all significant except Experiment 4). No consistent difference emerged based on whether density was manipulated by fish number or volume reduction.

3. **Activity is largely density-independent**: Locomotor activity (crossings of horizontal/vertical grid lines) showed inconsistent, non-directional responses to density across experiments — some showed decreased activity at high density, others increased, with several showing no significant effect. This argues against a generalized "stress/hyperactivity" model and supports independent regulation of courtship vs. aggression (which moved in opposite directions despite both being "active" behaviours).

4. **Reproductive suppression at high density**: Both the number of young born and the number of ovarian developmental stages (particularly stage III oocytes and developing young) decreased with increasing density, achieving significance in several experiments (notably Experiments 1, 2, and 5).

5. **Reproductive peak occurs at lower density than behavioural peak**: The ovarian/reproductive optimum occurred at ~2 fish/compartment (~4 fish/l) in Experiment 8, substantially lower than the 6–8 fish/compartment (12–16 fish/l) optimum for courtship behaviour — suggesting **decoupled regulatory mechanisms** for behavioural courtship display versus physiological reproductive capacity.

6. **No time-dependent acclimation**: Correlations between behavioural measures and time within experiments were largely non-significant, suggesting the behavioural response to density is **rapid/immediate** rather than gradual, in contrast to the presumably slower-acting ovarian/reproductive response.

7. **Cannibalism corroborated as a density-dependent factor**: Casual observation confirmed higher rates of cannibalism on young in high-density groups, consistent with [[Rose 1959 Population Control]], though the study notes that **reduced birth rate**, not cannibalism alone, is a major contributor to population regulation at high density.

8. **Confounds ruled out**: Neither food availability, oxygen levels, nor water-borne metabolites explained the density effects — supporting density itself (likely via behavioural/social interaction frequency) as the primary regulatory mechanism, consistent with [[Crew And Mirskaia 1931]] findings in mice.

9. **Sex-specific vulnerability at extreme densities**: A related unpublished result ([[Warren 1972]]) found that 2 males confined to 300 ml resulted in successive lethal aggression (19 males killed), suggesting that at very low absolute density with no escape space, aggression may become pathologically elevated — a potential confound distinct from the density-per-se effect described in the main experiments.
