# Humpback Chub Translocation to Shinumo Creek, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The National Park Service (NPS), working with partners, planned a mid-June 2009 translocation of 300 juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] (*[[Gila Cypha]]*) from the [[Little Colorado River]] to [[Shinumo Creek]], a small clear tributary of the [[Colorado River]] inside [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The experiment tests whether the endangered fish can establish a satellite population outside the Little Colorado River. The document is a National Park Service news release that describes the rationale, the fish-handling pipeline, the pre-release habitat preparation, and a planned three-year monitoring program.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a minnow-family (Cyprinidae) fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Individuals can live up to 30 years and reach nearly 20 inches in length. Adults have large fins and a pronounced muscular hump behind the head. Like other Colorado River natives, the species is adapted to high turbidity and seasonally variable flows and temperatures.

The species was first listed as endangered in 1967 and is protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Its decline is attributed to human-caused changes in the basin. In Grand Canyon, the chub faces:

- A [[Dam-Altered Ecosystem]] downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]
- Predation and competition from non-native trout, including [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]
- Non-native parasites such as the Asian tapeworm
- The risk of a catastrophic [[Hazardous Materials Spill]] from a bridge crossing the Little Colorado River

Because water released from Glen Canyon Dam is too cold for successful reproduction, the Little Colorado River remains the species' main spawning site in Grand Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site: Shinumo Creek.** Shinumo Creek is a small, clear tributary that joins the Colorado River at a point rendered as "River Mile 09" in the scanned text (the figure is likely River Mile 109; verify against the original). It has dense shoreline vegetation and supports abundant aquatic and riparian invertebrates. Officials selected it because:

- Water quality, water temperature, and food base appear suitable for humpback chub.
- A [[Barrier Waterfall]] just above the confluence with the Colorado River should keep non-native predator fish out of the creek.

**Upstream precedent.** Between 2003 and a later year (the end date is garbled in the scan), humpback chub were successfully [[Translocation|translocated]] to above [[Chute Falls]] in the Little Colorado River. This demonstrated that translocation is feasible for the species in Grand Canyon.

**Flood risk.** Biologists noted that [[Monsoonal Flooding]] could wash fish from Shinumo Creek into the mainstem Colorado River, which makes individual tracking important.

## Key Findings

**Source population and timing**
- Juvenile fish, 2 to 4 inches (50 to 130 mm), were captured in July and October 2008 near the mouth of the Little Colorado River (year rendered as "200g" in the scan).
- Fish were flown out of the canyon by helicopter.
- The planned release is 300 juveniles in mid-June 2009.

**Husbandry and tagging pipeline**
1. Parasite treatment at the [[Bubbling Ponds Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department, Page Springs, AZ).
2. Overwintering at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Dexter, New Mexico) until fish reached a size suitable for tagging.
3. Implantation of [[PIT Tagging|PIT (passive integrated transponder) tags]] about one month before translocation. These allow biologists to identify individual fish and determine which fish stayed in the creek and which were swept into the mainstem.

**Pre-release preparation**
- Biologists survey the existing fish community in the translocation reach.
- They remove non-native fish using [[Electrofishing]], angling, and other techniques.
- Humpback chub are acclimated ("tempered") to Shinumo Creek water conditions before release into reaches with suitable habitat.

**Monitoring and next steps**
- A three-year monitoring program will follow the release.
- Additional translocations to augment the population will be considered after analysis of the first year's findings.

**Partners**
- [[National Park Service]] (lead)
- [[Bureau of Reclamation]]
- Grand Canyon Wildlands Council
- Arizona Game and Fish Department
- U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service

**Official statements**
- Martha Hahn, chief of science and resource management, said Shinumo Creek was chosen because its habitat appears suitable and its waterfall provides predator exclusion, and that PIT tags will allow the team to evaluate the experiment scientifically.
- Superintendent Steve Martin described the translocation as "an important step towards protecting humpback chub" that "could set the stage for future conservation measures for this species."

**Overall conclusion:** The experiment is intended to test whether a satellite population can be established in a smaller Grand Canyon tributary. Success would give the NPS a conservation tool for the species, while outcomes will determine whether further translocations proceed.
