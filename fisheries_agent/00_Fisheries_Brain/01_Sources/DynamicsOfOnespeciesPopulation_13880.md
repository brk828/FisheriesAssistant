# Dynamics of One-Species Populations of Fishes in Ponds Subjected to Cropping and Additional Stocking

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Percidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Buck & Thoits, Vol. 30, Art. 2, March 1970) reports roughly six years of work on the production and population dynamics of six warmwater fishes held as single-species populations in 1-acre ponds. The study compared three treatments per unit (cropped, add-stock and control ponds) to measure how removals and additional stocking affect standing crop, growth, condition, mortality and spawning, and to address [[Carrying Capacity]] in fish ponds. The work was carried out at the former [[McGraw Hydrobiological Laboratory]] between 1956 and 1963, though most data were collected from 1958 to 1963. The program was ended early, in November 1963, when the sponsoring foundation reorganized.

## Background & Description

- **Species studied:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieu*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*) and [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*).
- **Aims:** (1) increase knowledge of pond [[Carrying Capacity]] for warmwater fishes; (2) relate carrying capacity to standing crop and rate of production; (3) measure the effect of controlled population increases and decreases on fish production.
- **Carrying capacity framework:** The authors adopt Bennett's (1962) definition, "the maximum poundage of a given species or complex of species of fishes that a limited and specific aquatic habitat may support during a stated interval of time." They note that standing crop can be below, equal to, or above carrying capacity, and they treat reported values as "current" capacity at the time of census.
- **Study site:** Sixteen 1-acre ponds were built for the study (15 in five three-pond units plus pond Alpha), on a roughly 1,200-acre hunting and fishing club in Kane County, about 35 miles south of the Wisconsin line and 30 miles west of [[Lake Michigan]], within the Wisconsin glaciation. Ponds had a maximum depth of 8 feet and average depths of 3 to 5 feet.
- **Design:** Each unit held one species. One pond was cropped at a predetermined rate from April through September, with the removed fish marked and added to an "add-stock" pond. The third pond was a control, left unmanipulated. Fish were sampled every two weeks (every three weeks in 1963), mainly with a 185-foot seine, and censused by pond drainage through a weir. Condition was calculated with the Thompson & Bennett (1939) index, C = 10,000 W / L³.
- **Data limitations:** Petersen estimates were judged unreliable in most cases and were used only when supported by draining censuses. The authors found that Petersen estimates tended to be grossly low when checked against seine samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fertility and water chemistry:** Ponds in Unit 1 were the most productive for smallmouth, but productivity was in the order Beta > Gamma > Delta, which matched the direction of water flow from a spring-fed trout stream. Units 1 and 2 also differed in substrate, with Unit 1 basins of black dirt and clay and Unit 2 basins largely of sand under clay.
- **High pH:** Daytime pH in many ponds reached 10.2 or higher, which was linked to intense photosynthesis in poorly buffered water. High pH is associated with the deaths of an experimental chain pickerel population and is judged to have suppressed largemouth bass spawning. Pond Beta's spawning was delayed about six weeks in 1963 until endothal treatment reduced the pH.
- **Vegetation:** Submerged plants were most abundant in pond Alpha and Unit 1, and absent in Unit 5. Common species included curly-leaf pondweed (*Potamogeton crispus*) and sago pondweed (*P. pectinatus*).
- **Fertilization:** All ponds except Alpha received inorganic fertilizer, with extra applications to the least fertile units.
- **Invertebrates and predators:** Dense crayfish populations, mainly *Orconectes virilis*, and abundant bullfrog tadpoles developed where bass or other efficient predators were absent. Crayfish appear to have reduced vegetation and possibly predated eggs. Large burrowing mayfly nymphs (*Hexagenia limbata*) disappeared from pond Theta, probably through predation by dense perch.

## Key Findings

**Carrying capacity and standing crop**
- Maximum smallmouth standing crops of 169 to 180 pounds per acre (Unit 1 ponds Beta and Gamma, 1959–1960) were judged close to the carrying capacity of those ponds. Largemouth maxima were about 120 to 130 pounds per acre in Unit 1 (control pond Beta, 126.8 pounds in 1961) and up to 160.2 pounds per acre in the add-stock pond Delta (1963), where the "pounds lost" deficit showed the carrying capacity was exceeded.
- Yellow perch reached 191.9 pounds per acre in the cropped pond Alpha (1960), and 183.7 pounds in add-stock pond Theta (1963).
- Bluegill standing crops averaged 125.3 pounds per acre across the four populations, with a maximum of 173.3 pounds in add-stock pond Kappa (1963). Brown bullhead standing crops were the lowest of any species, at a maximum of 90.2 pounds per acre in the add-stock pond.
- Carrying capacity was generally higher in fall than spring. For largemouth in Delta, spring capacity was about 77 percent of fall capacity.

**Cropping and production**
- Surplus production ("pounds gained or lost," close to Ricker's surplus production) was typically highest in cropped ponds, intermediate in controls, and lowest in add-stock ponds. The highest individual gain for smallmouth was 137.2 pounds in cropped Gamma (1958–1959).
- In the largemouth Unit 2 ponds, cropped pond Theta produced surplus production 3.8 times that of the control in 1958–1959, and 2.1 times in 1959–1960.
- Overcropping sharply reduced production, as in smallmouth pond Tau (9.3 pounds per acre in 1963), where spawning failed.
- Perch in pond Alpha: cropping of 88.3 pounds in 1960 and a standing crop of about 192 pounds, with 220.8 pounds gained in cropped pond Theta (1960–1963), showed a near-complete turnover of the population.

**Population structure and growth**
- Efficient populations had a balanced mix of size classes. Largemouth standing crop in pond Beta fell as the population shifted to about 90 percent of its weight in fish over 8.5 inches.
- Smallmouth of intermediate size (Ages I–II) appeared to use invertebrate foods more efficiently than largemouth of similar size, which relied more on fish and large food items.
- Smallmouth growth in Age I–II fish was intermediate to other waters, but growth of older fish was poor where populations were overcrowded (Unit 4).
- Perch growth beyond the first year was poorer than in most other waters.

**Mortality**
- Transferred fish suffered higher mortality than resident fish. For perch, transferred fish died at 96.1 percent (1962 transfers) and 85.1 percent (1961 transfers), while original stock mortality was 74.4 percent over three years.
- Adult smallmouth in control pond Sigma had very low losses (5 unaccounted for in one year, and about 2 percent in a marked sample). The authors concluded that adult smallmouth are "a much tougher and superior experimental fish than the largemouth."
- Mortality rates were uniformly high in marking experiments, including about 51 percent in some bluegill groups and 48.9 percent for largemouth marked in April.

**Spawning success**
- Smallmouth spawning success declined with the flow order of the ponds, and sac-fry counts averaged 548.25 fry per nest.
- Largemouth in Unit 1 failed to spawn in pond Delta in 1961, and pond Beta spawning was delayed by high pH in 1963.
- Yellow perch failed to reproduce in the first spawning season (1961) in all Unit 2 ponds, which the authors linked to absent submerged vegetation. Tree limbs placed as spawning substrate worked in 1962 and 1963.
- Bluegill reproduction failed in all ponds in 1962, which the authors attributed to cool summer water temperatures.

**Overall conclusions**
- Single-species populations of [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] can sustain yearly production in 1-acre ponds, especially under cropping. The authors call smallmouth the more productive and tougher species.
- Cropping stimulated production in most populations, but only up to an optimum rate. Overcropping reduced standing crops and spawning.
- The study concluded that the replacement potential of smallmouth was not fully tested, due to population immaturity, overcropping and chance.
- The authors recommended that managers consider size distribution and pond fertility, and noted that single-species bass ponds can produce good fishing when reproduction and winterkill risks are managed.
