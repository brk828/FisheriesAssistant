# Razorback Sucker Historical Status and State Listing in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1996 correspondence package from the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] (NMGF) and a supporting expert letter from [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University. It concerns whether the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) occurred historically in New Mexico, and whether the species should be listed as endangered by the state. Marsh supports both the historic occurrence and the state listing, relying on circumstantial evidence. The draft biennial review recommends adopting the federal endangered listing for New Mexico, while acknowledging that no museum specimens confirm New Mexico occurrence in the San Juan or Gila rivers.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback sucker, *Xyrauchen texanus*, a large catostomid of big river systems in the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Regulatory process:** Under the [[New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act]], NMGF must conduct a biennial review of state-listed organisms, which may recommend adding species to the state list. Staff review, incorporate expert input, release the draft for public comment, and the Director then presents recommendations to the [[State Game Commission]].
- **Correspondence:**
  - Marsh's letter (20 February 1996) to [[David L. Propst]], Endangered Species Biologist, reviewed the draft, stated the fish historically occurred in New Mexico, and supported state listing.
  - Propst's letter (14 February 1996) requested professional opinion on native status in the Gila and San Juan rivers and noted that [[Fishes Of New Mexico]] (Sublette et al. 1990) incorrectly cited Propst et al. (1987) as authority for possible occurrence.
  - Copies were sent to several regional fish biologists, including [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[H. M. Tyus]].
- **Marsh's argument:** Razorback sucker is a powerful swimmer with documented long-distance movements. Historic presence is reported at least as far upstream as Fort Thomas on the Gila, the Utah portion of the San Juan, and the [[Animas River]], and no barriers would have prevented upstream movement into New Mexico when water was plentiful.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic distribution:** Formerly widespread in large rivers of the Colorado River drainage, from the Gulf of California upstream to Colorado, Wyoming, and Utah (Minckley et al. 1991).
- **Habitat use:** Occupies a variety of riverine habitats but is most commonly associated with main channel pools and associated backwaters, sloughs, and oxbow lakes. Spawning in rivers is poorly known; some evidence suggests shallow, swift riffles. Seasonally flooded shorelines and bottomlands may serve as nursery areas.
- **Current wild populations:**
  - Mainly [[Lake Mohave]] on the lower Colorado River, where the population is almost entirely older than 25 years with no documented natural recruitment (Marsh and Minckley 1989).
  - The upper Green-lower Yampa rivers complex, where the species remains relatively common but larval collections are few and recruitment appears limited.
  - Elsewhere in the basin, including [[Lake Powell]] and Lake Mead region areas, the species is rare or absent (Bestgen 1990).
- **Threats:** Habitat fragmentation by dams, reduced flows, altered natural flow regimes, loss of flooded bottomlands, and non-native predators and competitors. Contaminants may limit the species in the San Juan River.
- **Sampling constraints:** Sparse historic collecting and habitat alterations (dewatering and impoundments such as [[Navajo Reservoir]] and San Carlos on the Gila) limit certainty about historic range.

## Key Findings

1. **No museum evidence in New Mexico:** There are no museum specimens confirming historic razorback sucker occurrence in the New Mexico portions of the San Juan or Gila rivers.
2. **Circumstantial evidence for San Juan River:** Jordan (1891) and Koster (1960) support historic presence. The species was collected in Utah, and no barriers existed between Utah and New Mexico. In 1992, NMGF interviewed two long-time San Juan-Animas River anglers, who identified a fish they had caught in the Animas River during the 1940s from unlabeled specimens.
3. **Circumstantial evidence for Gila River:** Razorback sucker was collected at Fort Thomas, Arizona (Kirsch 1889) and reported near the Arizona/New Mexico border (Chamberlain 1904). No barriers were historically present.
4. **Survey gaps:** The first systematic San Juan survey (1961, near Navajo Reservoir) failed to find the species. Platania (1990) summarized San Juan surveys through 1989 and found none in the New Mexico portion. The first systematic Gila basin survey (Huntington 1955) also did not collect the species.
5. **Expert opinion:** Marsh concludes the species historically occurred in New Mexico, and that the Animas significance should be made explicit in the review.
6. **Conservation status:** Federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1991), with critical habitat designated (USFWS 1994). In New Mexico, critical habitat extends from the Hogback Diversion downstream to the New Mexico/Colorado border.
7. **Recommendation:** The draft biennial review recommends that New Mexico list the razorback sucker as endangered by adopting the federal listing. Marsh fully supports this and expects other biologists to endorse it.
8. **Literature correction:** Sublette et al. (1990) misattributed the authority for possible occurrence to Propst et al. (1987), a correction flagged by NMGF.
9. **Ongoing work:** Radio-implanted razorback suckers were stocked in the San Juan River in 1994 and 1995 to identify limiting factors and develop recovery strategies (Ryden and Pfeifer 1995).
