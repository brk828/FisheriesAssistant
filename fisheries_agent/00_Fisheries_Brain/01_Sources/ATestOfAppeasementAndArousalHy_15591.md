# Test of Appeasement and Arousal Hypotheses of Courtship Behavior in *Etroplus maculatus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 behavioral study by [[George W. Barlow]] tests two hypotheses for the function of courtship in the orange chromide ([[Etroplus Maculatus]]), a cichlid fish: the **arousal hypothesis** (courtship stimulates the mate's reproductive physiology) and the **appeasement hypothesis** (courtship reduces the mate's probability of attack). The approach manipulates the relative and absolute sizes of fish of both sexes in a multi-compartment laboratory arena, the [[Cichlitron]]. Results support a mixed picture: arousal explains some behavior (notably male quivering toward females), while appeasement explains much of the rest, depending on sex and context. The study also documents sexual differences, including why females form homosexual pairs while males do not.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Etroplus maculatus*, the orange chromide, a cichlid whose courtship involves a set of action patterns shared by both sexes (quivering, nipping, skimming, charging, tail beating, and others).
- **Reproductive cycle:** Pair formation (lasting a day or less, with much aggression), followed by courtship (about 7 to 10 days), spawning, and a parental phase. Quivering, nipping, and skimming disappear almost immediately after spawning.
- **Size and dominance:** In unmated groups, size usually determines dominance. Males are typically about 20% heavier than their mates in a pair.
- **Background hypotheses:**
  - *Arousal:* courtship stimulates the mate's reproductive physiology.
  - *Appeasement:* courtship lowers the probability of attack by the mate, as described by [[Tinbergen]] (1959).
- **Prior literature:** Work on [[Blackchin Mouthbreeder]] (*Tilapia macrocephala*) by [[Aronson]] (1948, 1949), [[Greenberg]] (1961) on the jewel fish, and [[Heinrich]] (1967) on homosexual female pairs in Tilapia.

### Methods Summary

- **Fish:** Second- to third-generation descendants of fish imported from Ceylon, aged one to two years, reproductively experienced, with proven sex. Sexes were held separately in 500-litre aquaria.
- **Water conditions:** 5 g marine salt/L, pH 7.5–8.0, 28 ± 1 °C by day (3 °C lower at night), and a 13 h light cycle.
- **Arena (Cichlitron):** A hexagonal aquarium with six peripheral "chamber" compartments held the chamber fish. A central dither compartment (Saran netting) suppressed fear behavior. A circular passage, the "toroid", let a single "rover" fish move between chambers.
- **Procedure:** Six chamber fish of one sex were chosen to span a size range in near-equal increments. One intermediate-sized rover of either sex was introduced per trial. Each trial had three 10-minute observation periods, and each experiment comprised 10 trials.
- **Sample:** Six experiments in total (three with males as chamber fish, three with females), giving 450 minutes of observation over 15 trials.
- **Statistics:** Friedman two-way analysis of variance across six size categories, with chi-square values combined across the three independent experiments. Spearman rank correlation was also used in one case.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dominance and size:** Larger fish usually win aggressive encounters. [[Nelson]] (1964) found that *Tilapia mossambica* contests were decided in favor of the larger fish when size differed by as little as 2 mm.
- **Appeasement mechanisms:** Behaviors that reduce attack include infantile behavior, feeding, and threat displays. Sexual behavior is also used to suppress attack.
- **Phase-specific appeasement:** In the orange chromide, tail beating is the main appeasement method during pair formation. Quivering becomes the main method as the pair bonds.
- **Species-typical size arrangement:** Males are normally larger than their mates, a pattern also seen in many vertebrates. Because fish have indeterminate growth, a wide range of sizes is usually available to both sexes.
- **Parental and nest-related behavior:** Digging of pits for nonswimming larvae occurs only during courtship and early parental phases. Chafing can occur at any time.
- **Artificial conditions:** The Cichlitron is described as "very unnatural", and the author notes that results apply to a controlled setting rather than to wild populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Heterosexual quivering by chamber males** rose with the size of the female they encountered, consistent with the arousal hypothesis. The peak occurred when the male was about 1.4 to 1.5 times the weight of the rover female.
2. **Chamber females** showed a left-skewed quivering curve, consistent with arousal, but the distribution only approached significance (p = .12).
3. **Rover males** (intruders) quivered more to larger chamber females, consistent with the appeasement hypothesis.
4. **Rover females** quivered more to larger chamber males, which the author interprets as appeasement. Results for chamber females were less clear.
5. **Sex differences:** Across all sizes and contexts, males quivered more than females. Females charged more, particularly when relatively large. Chamber males attacked more when relatively small.
6. **Quivering totals:** Females evoked more quivering than males in 11 of 12 comparisons. This was the basis for the conclusion that females are "more evocative" in courtship.
7. **Overall behavior:** Aggressive behavior (charging, tail beating) was more frequent in same-sex interactions than in opposite-sex interactions.
8. **Homosexual pairs:** Females form homosexual pairs and even threesomes, partly because their same-sex pairs evoke courtship without the strong aggression seen between males. Males do not form pairs, because they are more aggressive toward each other.
9. **Time-adjusted analysis:** Differences in observed behavior could not be explained simply by the time fish spent together.
10. **Absolute vs. relative size:** Relative size had a much larger effect than absolute size on courtship, according to the author's analysis.

### Conclusions

The author concludes that neither hypothesis explains all the data. Arousal and appeasement operate under different conditions, depending on the sex of the fish, their role (chamber vs. rover), and the size relationship. The data indicate that the species-typical size arrangement, with males larger than females, represents the most adaptive configuration for successful pairing and breeding.
