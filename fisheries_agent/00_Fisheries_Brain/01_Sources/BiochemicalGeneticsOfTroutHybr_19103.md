# Mitochondrial and Biochemical Genetics of Trout Hybridization in the Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1989 proposal from [[Thomas E. Dowling]] (Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]]) to the College of Liberal Arts and Sciences Summer Research Awards Program. It requests **$4,000** ($3,500 for supplies and materials; $500 for travel to eastern Arizona for specimen collection). The project studies how two native trout, [[Arizona Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]], hybridize with the introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] in the [[Gila River]] system. The aim is to quantify gene exchange and the strength of reproductive isolation using morphological, protein, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) characters. The applicant's signature is dated 1 March 1989. The summer school commitment section is blank.

## Background & Description

**Scientific question.** The proposal frames speciation as the process behind most biological diversity. It is poorly understood, especially the genetic changes that create reproductive isolation. Following [[Allopatric Speciation]] (Mayr, 1963), populations separated by a barrier diverge. Whether they become full species depends on whether genetic incompatibilities accumulate. Interbreeding species in nature are considered the best systems for testing these questions, because [[Reproductive Isolation]] mechanisms can be measured directly.

**Study taxa.** The trout belong to the genus *Oncorhynchus* (formerly *Salmo*):
- *O. apache* (Arizona trout), restricted to the upper Salt River. It is endemic to the Gila River system of Arizona and New Mexico.
- *O. gilae* (Gila trout), found only in the upper Gila River.
- *O. mykiss* (formerly *S. gairdneri*), the rainbow trout, native to the Pacific Coast from Alaska to northern Mexico and now widespread in the Southwest through human transplantation.

Arizona and Gila trout are morphologically similar, differing mainly in color pattern and body shape (Miller, 1972). Protein electrophoresis found no diagnostic differences between them (Loudenslager et al., 1986). Rainbow trout are distinct from both native forms in morphology and protein characters.

**Methods.** The proposal relies on two biochemical techniques:
- [[Protein Electrophoresis]] uses charge differences among enzyme alleles in starch gels. Diagnostic alleles identify species and hybrids.
- [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] of [[Mitochondrial DNA]] compares cleavage-site patterns. Restriction enzymes are bacterial enzymes that cleave specific sequences, usually 4–8 base pairs long.

mtDNA is favored because it is small and circular, strictly maternally inherited without recombination, and evolves faster than nuclear proteins. Together these traits make it a clear marker of maternal lineage and useful for distinguishing closely related species.

**Principal investigator.** Dowling is an Assistant Professor at Arizona State University (since September 1986). He earned a B.S. in Zoology from the University of Michigan (1980) and a Ph.D. in Biology from Wayne State University (1984). He was a Research Fellow at the University of Michigan from December 1984 to September 1988. His society memberships include the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] and the Society for the Study of Evolution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybridization patterns in the field.** Previous work (Loudenslager et al., 1986) documented hybridization in the Gila River:
- **Arizona trout × rainbow trout:** Where the two formerly co-occurred, most individuals are "pure" Arizona trout, with very few hybrids and no pure rainbow trout. At **Paddy Creek**, both species' alleles are equally frequent. All individuals sampled (n = 16) carry a mixture of Arizona and rainbow trout alleles.
- **Gila trout × rainbow trout:** Where the two formerly co-occurred, rainbow trout alleles predominate and no pure Gila trout were detected. At **Chitty Creek**, most individuals show typical Gila trout morphology, with some evidence of introgression from rainbow trout (personal communication from W. L. Minckley).

**Management relevance.** The proposal notes that these are endangered native species, so the work supports their identification and management. The Virgin River fish community project (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, pending) is a related perturbed-stream study.

**Comparative systems.** The broader program also covers *Notropis* minnows, including [[Notropis Cornutus]] and [[Notropis Chrysocephalus]], which hybridize widely in the Midwest, and [[Bottlenose Dolphin]] (*Tursiops truncatus*) population structure in the Gulf of Mexico.

## Key Findings

**Proposed design**
- Sample about **50 individuals** from each hybrid locality (Paddy Creek and Chitty Creek).
- Run restriction enzyme assays using **10–15 enzymes** on **ten individuals of each species** to identify mtDNA patterns that separate the three taxa. Samples come from localities where gene transfer was not possible, to ensure purity.
- Sort individuals into classes using protein and morphological characters: "pure" Arizona, Gila, or rainbow trout; first-generation hybrids; and backcrosses. This follows the approach used for minnow hybrids (Dowling et al., 1989).

**Hypotheses on reciprocity and directionality** (tested for each hybrid pairing)
- If reciprocal crosses occur equally and produce viable offspring, equal numbers of mtDNA types from both parental species should appear in first-generation hybrids.
- If one parental mtDNA type is significantly more common, reciprocal crosses are unequal or offspring viability differs.
- If backcross progeny survive equally and mating is not preferential, mtDNA types should match parental frequencies. Deviations would indicate mating bias or differential progeny viability.

**Expected outcomes**
- Identify the maternal species in hybrid production and test reciprocity and directionality of hybridization.
- Compare the Arizona × rainbow and Gila × rainbow pairings to locate the life-history stages where reproductive isolation operates.
- Provide a diagnostic mtDNA marker to tell Arizona and Gila trout apart, since they are not separable by protein electrophoresis.
- Produce publications and preliminary data for a larger, externally funded study.

**Prior findings from the applicant's work on minnows**
- Hybrids occur less often than expected under random production and survival, indicating partial reproductive isolation (Dowling and Moore, 1984).
- Adult hybrids die faster than parental species, indicating selection against hybrids (Dowling and Moore, 1985).
- Reproductive isolation varies among regions (Dowling et al., 1989).
