## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by Sam L. Spencer (Alabama Department of Conservation, in collaboration with Auburn University) investigated the use of electric current as a selective tool for thinning overcrowded populations of intermediate-size [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) in farm ponds without harming co-occurring [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Overcrowding of bluegill in bass-bluegill ponds is a well-documented management problem (see [[Swingle 1956]]) that degrades fishing quality and is otherwise addressed only through costly, non-selective chemical or mechanical methods. Using controlled electroshocking trials with varying current type, voltage, exposure time, and water resistivity, the study demonstrated that electricity can achieve high selectivity — killing up to 75% of exposed bluegill while causing negligible mortality (near 0%) in largemouth bass. This suggests electrofishing-based methods hold promise as a species-selective population control technique, pending further development of field-scale voltage/amperage delivery.

## Background & Description

This paper is management/behavioral in focus rather than morphological, but relevant size/life-stage distinctions are noted:

- **Bluegill test group:** Intermediate-size individuals, approximately 3–4 inches in length — the target size class responsible for pond overcrowding and stunted growth dynamics.
- **Largemouth Bass test group:** Larger size class, approximately 5–10 inches in length, representing the predator population meant to be protected from incidental electrical mortality.
- Size differential between the two species/size-classes is likely a key factor underlying the observed **species selectivity** of the electrical treatment (larger-bodied fish may experience different current density/voltage gradient effects than smaller fish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System context:** Classic farm-pond community pairing of [[Largemouth Bass]] (piscivorous predator) and [[Bluegill]] (prey/forage and sport fish) — a foundational combination in North American pond fisheries management (per [[Swingle 1956]]).
- **Problem being addressed:** Without adequate predation pressure or periodic removal, bluegill populations can become overcrowded, leading to stunted growth and poor angling ("overcrowding" dynamics well known in [[Farm Pond Management]]).
- **Experimental habitat:** Concrete ponds (24.2 ft × 9 ft × 2.5 ft) at the Auburn Farm Ponds Project, with fish confined in screened test enclosures (3 ft × 1 ft × 2.5 ft) to standardize exposure to the electrical field.
- **Water chemistry variable:** Water resistivity (ohms) was identified as an important abiotic factor modulating current flow (amperage) and thus treatment efficacy — resistivity was experimentally adjusted using sodium chloride to isolate its effect from other variables.
- Source populations: Bluegill were seined from an 8.5-acre pond (S-9) or hatchery-reared; largemouth bass were hook-and-line captured from a 2-acre pond (S-16).

## Key Findings

### 1. Current Type Comparison
- **230-volt A.C. (3-phase, 60-cycle)** produced the greatest amperage flow and the highest bluegill mortality compared to:
  - 115-volt A.C. (single-phase, 60-cycle)
  - 115-volt D.C.
- 230V A.C. was adopted for all subsequent experiments due to superior efficacy.
- However, short exposures (8–10 seconds) at 230V A.C. did **not** kill sufficient percentages of bluegill for practical field use.

### 2. Exposure Time Effects
- Mortality increased with longer exposure duration (tested 1–300 seconds).
- To achieve ~25% mortality, minimum exposure of **60 seconds** was required — likely impractical under normal field conditions.
- Majority of deaths among fish exposed ≥60 seconds occurred within **2–4 hours** post-shock.
- Even at 300 seconds exposure, 100% mortality was not achieved, attributed to individual fish variability and orientation relative to electrodes (consistent with findings of [[McMillan 1929]] on current pathway through fish body, and [[Haskell 1954]] on voltage gradient decay at electrode midpoints).

### 3. Water Resistivity Effects
- Lowering water resistivity from 10,000 ohms to 1,000 ohms increased amperage from 1.6A to 6.9A (at 230V A.C., 60-second exposure).
- Corresponding bluegill mortality rose from **4% to 56%**.
- Indicates that increasing conductivity (e.g., via electrolyte addition or larger electrode surface area) could substantially improve treatment efficacy.

### 4. Species Selectivity (Central Result)
- At 230V A.C., 3.1 amperes, across exposure times up to 300 seconds:
  - Bluegill mortality reached up to **69–75%**.
  - Largemouth bass mortality was **effectively 0%** across nearly all trials (only one bass death recorded total, possibly attributable to handling stress rather than shock).
- This stark differential supports the core hypothesis: electrical shock can **selectively target smaller bluegill while sparing larger largemouth bass**, offering a potentially efficient, low-cost alternative to chemical/mechanical thinning methods.

### 5. Electrode Design
- A custom electrode (braided shielding over rubber-covered wire with grounding clamp and weighted end) outperformed simple brass welding-rod electrodes and was used in primary trials.
- Electrode spacing of 12 inches (three electrodes, one per phase) was used, near the practical minimum given pond bottom obstructions and electrode flexibility.

## Management Implications

Findings support further research into higher voltage/amperage systems and improved field electrode designs to shorten required exposure time while preserving species selectivity — a promising direction for practical [[Bluegill Population Control]] in managed farm ponds.
