# Our Most Precious Resource: Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-resources

## Executive Summary

This 1980 National Geographic feature by Thomas Y. Canby, with photographs by Ted Spiegel, surveys water use, scarcity, pollution, and management across the United States. It argues that the nation is not running out of water in a general sense, since roughly four trillion gallons of precipitation fall daily and only a tenth is used, but that serious problems of regional shortage, groundwater depletion, contamination, flooding, and infrastructure persist. The article ties these problems to the [[Hydrologic Cycle]], [[Aquifer]] depletion (notably the [[Ogallala Aquifer]]), the [[Colorado River]] allocation, [[Acid Rain]] damage to Adirondack lakes, and the [[Clean Water Act]] era of pollution control, and it closes with a call for conservation and cooperative management.

## Background & Description

The feature opens inside [[City Tunnel No. 3]] in New York, a water tunnel under construction that cost many millions of dollars per mile and had killed 17 workers so far. The author notes that New York City residents draw about one and a half billion gallons per day, and that most U.S. cities provide safe drinking water, a condition the article contrasts with the 75 percent of the world where sanitation is lacking and an estimated ten million people die each year from waterborne disease.

Per-capita use is explained in layers:

- Direct household use averages about 87 gallons per person per day: 24 for flushing, 32 for bathing, laundry, and dishwashing, and 25 for swimming pools and lawns. Drinking and cooking account for only two gallons.
- Indirect (embedded) water raises the total to roughly 2,000 gallons per person per day. Examples given include 120 gallons for one egg, 3,500 gallons for a steak, and about 60,000 gallons for a ton of steel.

The article describes the [[Hydrologic Cycle]] as the solar engine that renews water, with about a trillion tons evaporated and returned daily. It also outlines extensive human manipulation of rivers and landscapes: about two million dams, sixty million irrigated acres, barge canals carrying a fifth of intercity freight, 50,000 public and private water utilities, a hundred million acres of drained wetlands, and millions of wells.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional imbalance.** The West holds about 60 percent of the nation's land but receives only 25 percent of its moisture. The 100th meridian divides wet East from arid West. Annual rainfall figures cited include about 40 inches in Maryland, 31 in Iowa, 27 in eastern Nebraska, 18 in western Nebraska, 14 in Wyoming, and nine in Nevada.

**Groundwater.** Beneath the surface lies about thirty times as much fresh water as in all rivers and lakes combined. The [[Ogallala Aquifer]] stretches about 800 miles from South Dakota to Texas, has almost no natural recharge, and supports roughly 150,000 wells irrigating more than ten million acres. Overdraft has caused land subsidence: Houston has sunk several feet, and parts of California's San Joaquin Valley have dropped as much as 30 feet over roughly 50 years. Saltwater intrusion threatens wells in Florida and Texas where aquifers are overdrawn.

**Surface waters.** The [[Colorado River]] carries about ten million tons of salt per year, with losses above fifty million dollars annually in lower reaches. Eutrophication from nitrogen and phosphorus runoff caused a fish die-off in Lake Erie; the Cuyahoga River famously caught fire in 1970. Lake Tahoe is cited as aging faster through erosion and air pollution.

**Atmospheric pollution.** In the Adirondacks, acid precipitation from Midwestern coal and oil-fired power plants has eliminated fish from about 200 lakes, with acidity reaching fifty times the norm.

**Floods.** Floods kill an average of about 100 Americans per year. Notable events include the 1889 Johnstown flood (2,200 dead), the 1927 Ohio and lower Mississippi flood, Big Thompson River (139 dead in 1976), and Rapid Creek (236 dead in 1972). About 8,000 dams protect populated areas, and the Army Corps of Engineers found a third of half the inspected dams unsafe.

## Key Findings

- **Quantity versus quality.** Water is not exhausted nationally, but regional shortage (Rio Grande, lower Colorado, Missouri Basin) and quality problems are serious.
- **Groundwater mining.** Pumping faster than recharge is termed "mining." The [[Ogallala Aquifer]] is the central example, with some Texas High Plains wells projected to fail within decades.
- **Colorado River overallocation.** Legal allocations exceed the river's flow. The [[Central Arizona Project]] is expected to create a shortfall within about a decade, and Indian water rights add uncertainty.
- **Rural water poverty.** At least four million Americans lack adequate water service, and about half a million Virginians reportedly drink polluted water or haul water. Half a million wells are sunk annually in rural America.
- **Pollution milestones.** Typhoid deaths fell from 27,000 in 1900 to about one-eighth of that level thirty years later, aided by filtration, chlorination (from 1913), and fluoridation. The 1972 Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments aimed for swimmable and fishable waters by 1984. About a third of cities had met cleanup requirements, and 90 percent of major industrial polluters had done so.
- **Emerging contaminants.** Toxic chemicals such as PCBs, DDT, mirex, and Kepone are found in water. Chlorination may produce cancer-causing byproducts, prompting EPA proposals for carbon filtration in cities over 75,000 people, costing roughly ten to more than a hundred dollars per family per year.
- **Acid rain.** Acid precipitation is degrading Adirondack lakes, accelerating rock erosion, and possibly affecting northeastern forests.
- **Flood policy.** Federal flood control since 1936 is estimated to have prevented about 40 billion dollars in losses, yet damages keep rising. Gilbert F. White argues the problem is "people," favoring land-use control and warning systems over structures.
- **Conservation potential.** Californians cut use by up to 63 percent in Marin County during drought. Denver is building water recycling, and older eastern systems can lose up to a third of water to leaks.
- **Management trends.** Experts expect increasing competition for water, shifting from agriculture toward industry, more recycling, and greater conservation. Joseph Cragwall concludes that "they're not making any more of it."
