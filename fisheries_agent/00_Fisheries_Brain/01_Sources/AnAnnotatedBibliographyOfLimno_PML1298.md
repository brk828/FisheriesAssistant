# Limnology and Ichthyology of the Colorado River in Marble and Grand Canyons: An Annotated Bibliography

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 annotated bibliography, prepared by [[Dennis Kubly]] of the Arizona State University Department of Zoology for [[Grand Canyon National Park]] under the U.S. National Park Service, reviews the limnological and fish literature for the [[Colorado River]] in [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]] and its tributaries. The author concludes that scientific knowledge of the regional aquatic ecosystem is very limited, especially for biology, and that the river's ecology is largely speculative. The bibliography of about 107 numbered references, supplemented by appendices on fish, plants, invertebrates and survey records, is intended as a basis for future management decisions.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River through Marble and Grand Canyons has attracted exploration for more than a century. Native peoples used the canyons mainly for hunting and seed gathering, with settlement concentrated in tributary valleys. Early Euro-American accounts were largely geological and observational. Lt. J. C. Ives (1857-58) described the river as destined to remain "forever unvisited," but [[John Wesley Powell]] navigated the canyons in 1869 and again in 1871. These expeditions established the framework for later regional knowledge.

Physicochemical data were first collected by the [[United States Geological Survey]] at gaging stations beginning in the early 1920s. These records initially covered only discharge and sediment load, but later included many more parameters at mainstem and tributary stations. Biological data were far sparser.

The report describes its bibliography as a partial basis for future studies, not a comprehensive one. It is arranged by reference number, with annotations, and is supported by four appendices:
- **Appendix A:** a list of U.S. Geological Survey water-supply and water-quality paper numbers for the Colorado River Basin (1943-1967 series).
- **Appendix B:** a fish species list, cross-referenced to bibliography entries and museum holdings.
- **Appendix C:** a list of aquatic and riparian plants and aquatic invertebrates.
- **Appendix D:** a note on nomenclature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author describes the Colorado River in this reach as a system recently removed from a quasi-equilibrium state by dam construction and flow regulation. Changes to the flow and sediment regime have altered the physical habitat, which in turn restructures biological communities. Fishes are subject to new selective pressures and to competition from species introduced by people. The author argues that any future system will be a new one, and that its study is useful for managing the area while preserving it.

Several specific environmental features recur through the bibliography:
- **Salinity:** One cited study attributes most salinity increases in the Grand Canyon reach to saline springs, with the Paria River mouth identified as a major source of salts.
- **Sediment and flow:** Historical suspended-sediment and discharge records, and studies of pre-dam hydrology using tree-ring data, are key sources for understanding baseline conditions.
- **Groundwater:** Karst systems on the [[Kaibab Plateau]] and the Tapeats and Deer Basin area discharge large volumes of water to the canyons through springs in the [[Muav Limestone]].
- **Tributaries:** [[Kanab Creek]] is subject to scouring floods that limit aquatic life, and [[Havasu Creek]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]] are noted as trout waters.
- **Water quality:** Bacterial and chemical quality of the river and its tributaries were studied in relation to recreation and runoff.

The author notes that the river's fauna has been shaped by stocking and introduction programs, which have affected native species and complicated interpretation of distributional records.

## Key Findings

- **Scope of the literature:** The bibliography contains about 107 numbered references, mostly from the 1920s to the early 1970s. Many are government reports, unpublished manuscripts or popular articles, and few are primary biological studies of the river itself.
- **Knowledge gap:** The author states that the biota of the river in this reach is poorly known. Competitive interactions, food habits and life histories of fishes are largely undocumented, and invertebrates and flora have hardly been surveyed.
- **Fish fauna:** Appendix B lists about 25 fish taxa, along with several hybrids, recorded in the literature or in museum collections. Native species include the [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha), [[Bonytail]] (Gila elegans), [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (Ptychocheilus lucius), [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus), [[Woundfin]] (Plagopterus argentissimus), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Catostomus latipinnis), [[Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus) and the Bluehead Sucker. Several of these are listed in the bibliography as threatened or endangered (for example, references 65, 69 and 96).
- **Introduced fishes:** The list includes species marked as introduced, such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]], Mosquitofish and various bullheads. [[Fish Stocking]] programs, mainly trout, are documented in several references (for example, references 3, 11, 12, 16 and 74).
- **Taxonomic issue:** A 1931 report of [[Gila cypha]] (as "Gila elegans" in one annotation) was reviewed and attributed to Gila robusta or Ptychocheilus lucius. A later personal communication stated that Gila cypha has never been taken from the Grand Canyon region, so the specimens were probably Rhinichthys osculus.
- **Invertebrates and flora:** Appendix C lists vascular plants, algae, rotifers, mollusks, crustaceans and aquatic insects. No quantitative data are provided, and the list is a presence-only record.
- **Key data sources:** The most useful sources for baseline conditions are the U.S. Geological Survey water-resources and water-quality series (references 97-101), the Survey's Colorado River Basin reports, and springs analyses (for example, references 59 and 88).
- **Management implication:** The author concludes that future decisions must be based on new scientific study. The bibliography is presented as a foundation for management that is both compatible with preservation and able to meet present-day demands.
