# Aquatic Macrophytes and the Oxygen Balance of Running Water

## Executive Summary
This 1961 study by Derek F. Westlake quantifies the standing biomass of submerged aquatic macrophytes (dominated by [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]]) in a polluted lowland stream in Hertfordshire, England, and models their contribution to the dissolved oxygen (DO) balance of the watercourse. The stream received purified sewage effluent and exhibited a transitional α-/β-mesosaprobic community state — a condition also relevant to the fish habitat quality studies conducted on the same stream system by [[Allen Et Al. 1958]] ("A field and laboratory investigation of fish in a sewage effluent"). While the paper itself is botanically and biogeochemically focused rather than ichthyological, its findings on oxygen production/consumption dynamics are directly applicable to assessing habitat suitability for fish in nutrient-enriched running waters, since dissolved oxygen concentration and diurnal fluctuation are primary determinants of fish survival in polluted streams.

## Background & Description
Not a fish-focused study; the "organism" characterized here is the macrophyte community rather than fish morphology. Key structural/biomass data:

- **Dominant species**: *Potamogeton pectinatus* L. (Fennel Pondweed), with minor occurrences of *Sparganium simplex* (Simple Bur-reed) and filamentous algae (*Cladophora* sp.)
- **Conversion factors (per 100 g fresh weight)**:
  - Volume: ~180 cm³ (extrapolated true value ~160 cm³ before handling losses)
  - Dry weight: 8.45 g
  - Ash (per 100 g dry weight): 22.4 g
  - Organic carbon (per 100 g dry weight): 34.9 g
- **Population density** (per m² stream surface):
  - Fresh weight: 1.46 kg
  - Volume: 2.81 litres
  - Dry weight: 123 g
  - Ash-free dry weight: 96 g
  - Organic carbon: 48 g
- **Estimated total biomass**: ~15.4 ± 1.8 metric tons of water weed per kilometre of stream

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: A straight, uniformly ~1 m deep, gravelly-bottomed lowland stream (Hertfordshire, England) receiving purified sewage effluent, situated between sampling stations 2 and 4 (~400 m reach) as defined by [[Allen Et Al. 1958]]. Mean current velocity ~8 cm/sec (per Wuhrmann discussion exchange).
- **Water quality classification**: Transitional α-mesosaprobic to β-mesosaprobic community, indicative of organic pollution gradient — directly relevant to fish tolerance zonation in polluted streams.
- **Trophic/production context**: Algal (phytoplanktonic) production was negligible; nearly all primary production was attributable to rooted submerged macrophytes, contrasting with [[Odum 1956]]'s hypothesis that organically polluted streams support the greatest primary production.
- **Comparative crop density**:
  - This polluted stream: 1.46–1.78 kg fresh weight/m²
  - Eutrophic lakes ([[Rickett 1922]], [[Rickett 1924]]): 1.5–1.8 kg/m²
  - Clear, unpolluted chalk stream ([[Edwards And Owens 1960]]): up to 7.8 kg/m² (September maximum), 1.65 kg/m² even in June
- **Seasonal dynamics**: Maximum macrophyte crop reached in July in the polluted stream (earlier than the unpolluted comparison stream's September peak), followed by die-back — suggesting pollution may suppress rather than enhance annual macrophyte production, contrary to prior assumptions.
- **Relevance to fish ecology**: Dense macrophyte stands influence stream hydraulics (reduced velocity, increased depth, siltation, flooding risk) and oxygen dynamics that structure habitat quality for fish populations in effluent-receiving streams, connecting to the broader fisheries investigation context of [[Allen Et Al. 1958]].

## Key Findings
- **Oxygen production estimate**: The measured macrophyte population could contribute ~0.72 g O₂/m² per hour at noon on a fine July day, translating to a theoretical DO increase of 2.6 p.p.m. per kilometre of stream (ignoring surface exchange losses).
- **Comparative unpolluted stream**: [[Edwards And Owens 1960]] (personal communication) observed a larger DO increase (~7 p.p.m./km) in a clear, unpolluted stream with a similar macrophyte population but lower flow — highlighting that flow rate and water clarity modulate the realized oxygen benefit of a given macrophyte biomass.
- **Net daily oxygen balance**: Average net gain over a July day (accounting for overnight respiration) was +1.8 g O₂/m² per day, equivalent to an average DO increase of 0.83 p.p.m./km. On heavily overcast days, plant respiration could exceed photosynthetic gain, making macrophytes a net oxygen sink — a critical risk factor for fish survival during low-light, high-pollution conditions.
- **Light/shading sensitivity**: Under ~25% of average daily light (heavy cloud), photosynthesis could not balance respiration, meaning plant growth — and its oxygenating benefit — would cease, with implications for fish kills during prolonged dull weather in weeded, polluted streams.
- **Decay/oxygen demand feedback loop**: Eventual decomposition of the macrophyte crop is expected to impose an oxygen demand theoretically equal to the oxygen gained during growth, meaning macrophyte stands represent a temporally deferred oxygen debt rather than a permanent net gain — relevant to seasonal fish kill risk during die-back periods.
- **Physical/hydrological side effects**: Dense weed growth reduces water velocity, increases depth, promotes silting and flooding, and prolongs retention of dissolved/suspended organic matter and benthic deposits — all factors that can indirectly and unfavorably affect the oxygen supply available to fish and other aquatic fauna.
- **Supersaturation dynamics** (from discussion with Pennak): Oxygen loss via diffusion/turbulence only occurs under supersaturation, which did not occur in this polluted stream but was noted elsewhere (up to 230% saturation) in streams with dense linear-leaved *Potamogeton* growth — plant species identity was considered less important than growth density/health in determining oxygenation rate.
- **Methodological note**: A companion analytical method (Westlake, in preparation) allows the oxygen balance contribution of macrophytes to be estimated from biomass, photosynthetic characteristics, and stream physical features — providing a template applicable to fish habitat assessment in other effluent-affected streams.
