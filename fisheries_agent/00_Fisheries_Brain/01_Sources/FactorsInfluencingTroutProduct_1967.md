# Factors Influencing Trout Production in Four Mountain Impoundments in Central Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #limnology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #plankton-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1967 University of Arizona thesis by Phillip A. Stewart investigates the ecological and chemical factors governing [[Trout]] production in four mountain reservoirs in central eastern Arizona: [[Hawley Lake]], [[Woods Canyon Lake]], [[Fool Hollow Lake]], and [[Becker Lake]]. Rather than focusing on fish morphology, the study is a limnological investigation linking primary production (phytoplankton and benthic photosynthesis), secondary production ([[Zooplankton]] standing crop), and nutrient chemistry of tributary inflows to overall [[Game Fish Production]]. The work was conducted between August 1965 and August 1966 and combines in situ photosynthesis measurements, chlorophyll standing crop assays, and water chemistry sampling from multiple tributary creeks feeding these impoundments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study frames trout production within a bottom-up trophic model for lentic mountain habitats:

- **Primary Producers**: Both [[Phytoplankton]] and [[Benthic Photosynthesis|benthic algae/macrophytes]] were measured in situ across all four lakes (Tables 2–8). Despite high benthic photosynthesis at two of the lakes, phytoplankton was identified as the **principal energy support for zooplankton**.
- **Secondary Producers**: [[Zooplankton]] standing crop (Table 9) was treated as the most reliable available index of secondary production and, by extension, of potential fish forage base.
- **Alternate Energy Pathway**: The authors raise the possibility that energy fixed by benthic primary producers may pass through **benthic fauna** rather than through the zooplankton-based pelagic food web, representing a parallel trophic pathway to fish production.
- **Nutrient Inputs**: Tributary streams supplying each lake (e.g., [[Porcupine Creek]], [[Trout Creek]], unnamed creeks "A" and "B" tributary to Hawley Lake, and [[Show Low Creek]] tributary to Fool Hollow Lake) were sampled for chemical and physical characteristics (Tables 10–14), framing tributary nutrient loading (biogenic salts) as a key external driver of lake productivity.
- **Habitat Stratification**: Temperature stratification and summer oxygen stratification were measured, indicating the study considered thermal/oxygen habitat structuring as relevant to fish habitat suitability within these impoundments.

## Key Findings
- **Zooplankton as productivity index**: Standing crop of zooplankton was considered the most reliable available proxy for secondary (and thus fish-supporting) production among the metrics used.
- **Phytoplankton dominance despite benthic photosynthesis**: Even where benthic gross photosynthesis was high (at two of the four lakes), phytoplankton appeared to be the main energetic base for the zooplankton community — suggesting decoupling between benthic productivity and pelagic secondary production.
- **Becker Lake anomaly**: High zooplankton standing crop relative to phytoplankton photosynthesis at [[Becker Lake]] was attributed to its shallow mean depth, implying that basin morphometry modulates the phytoplankton–zooplankton relationship.
- **Chemical drivers of phytoplankton photosynthesis**: Of all chemical parameters tested, only **alkalinity** and **phosphate** were significantly related to phytoplankton photosynthesis.
- **Physical vs. chemical control of benthic photosynthesis**: Physical factors — specifically **water clarity** and **average depth** — were more important than chemical factors in determining benthic gross photosynthesis rates.
- **Chlorophyll as a proxy metric**: The standing crop of chlorophyll was evaluated as an indirect index of phytoplankton photosynthesis; the author proposes an improved method relating the **assimilation number** to **euphotic depth** to strengthen this proxy relationship.
- **Multi-lake comparative design**: The inclusion of four structurally and geographically distinct impoundments ([[Hawley Lake]], [[Woods Canyon Lake]], [[Fool Hollow Lake]], [[Becker Lake]]) allowed cross-lake comparison of how morphometric, chemical, and biological factors jointly constrain trout production potential in Arizona mountain reservoirs.
