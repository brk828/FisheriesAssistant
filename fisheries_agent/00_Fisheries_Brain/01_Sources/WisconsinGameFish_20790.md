# Wisconsin Fish Management: Policy, Programs and Species Profiles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae #Percidae #Centrarchidae #Salmonidae #Acipenseridae #fish-management

## Executive Summary

This Wisconsin Conservation Department booklet sets out the state's freshwater fish management philosophy, the functions of its Fish Management Division, and profiles of the most important game, panfish, commercial and rough fish species. It argues that sound management depends on accurate biological research, that past decisions based on public whim caused serious errors, and that management must balance angling opportunity, habitat protection, and the commercial fishery. The second half profiles species with notes on identification, habitat, feeding, and spawning behavior. The document is undated in the portion provided, and it describes management practices rather than presenting a formal study with new data.

## Background & Description

Wisconsin's fishery resources are described as having great value. The document reports more than one million sportsmen and hundreds of commercial operators, and says a large resort and tourist business depends on the fishing offered. It also states that the state has about 8,600 lakes and 10,000 miles of fishing streams, with 149 distinct species and 174 recognized forms counting 14 species with two or more subspecies. Fish are grouped as game fish, pan and food fish, commercial fish, and rough fish.

### Fish Management Policy

The policy of the [[Fish Management Division]] is to conserve and perpetuate the state's fishery for all citizens "for all time." Its ten stated principles are:

1. Equal opportunity for all people to fish.
2. Fundamental research to improve fish and fishing.
3. Acquisition, protection and improvement of habitat.
4. Propagation, rescue and transfer of fish matched to the needs of each water.
5. Rough fish control when it benefits game fish and other interests.
6. Support for the commercial fishery of the [[Great Lakes]] and the Mississippi River.
7. Adequate, continuing funding for essential activities.
8. Complete accounting records, open to the public.
9. Cooperation with intra-state, interstate and federal agencies.
10. Public service, with the public kept informed of proposed activities.

### Operating Sections

- **[[Fishery Biology]]**: Plans and directs research on natural factors, productivity, species-to-water correlations, and control of competitive or destructive species. Contributory studies cover pollution, disease, parasitism, food resources, growth and stunting, exotic and overstocking effects, and erosion.
- **[[Fish Propagation]]**: Produces the species and sizes needed for stocking. Biological findings govern stocking, and program scale depends on total needs and allotted funds.
- **[[Rough Fish Control]]**: Removes rough fish where this does not harm game fish, hunting, trapping, or recreation. Rough fish taken are public property, and game fish caught in nets must be released uninjured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document presents several management concerns:

- **Predator–prey balance**: [[Muskellunge]] are large, voracious predators that need abundant food, so only relatively few can be supported in a given water. Where [[Northern Pike]] invaded native muskellunge waters, muskellunge declined. Regulations were therefore liberalized, allowing a daily bag limit of 25 northern pike with no size limit or closed season.
- **Competition among predators**: The [[Yellow Walleye]] may replace [[Largemouth Black Bass]] in small, soft-water lakes, which could harm bass fishing.
- **Carp and habitat damage**: Carp introduced into many waters are described as prolific and damaging to aquatic habitat valued by fish, waterfowl and other wildlife. A large removal program is considered necessary, and its proceeds have largely funded management.
- **Panfish stunting**: High reproductive rates of [[Bass]] and [[Panfish]] mean hook-and-line angling under liberal regulations is not considered enough to control populations. [[Stunted Panfish]] populations, often worsened by too few predators, are a major management challenge.
- **Watershed and land use**: The [[Watershed Stabilization Program]] pursues coordinated land use. Early work focused on erosion control through stream fencing, tree planting and contour plowing, with habitat improvements to follow.
- **Habitat-dependent species**: Brook trout declined as forest clearing and stream-side brush removal warmed waters. Stream improvement and tree planting have restored some streams. [[Brown Trout]] tolerate warmer water and were used to replace brook trout in such waters.

## Key Findings

### Management Practices and Stocking

- Stocking of legal-sized trout for immediate harvest is "highly effective" in increasing catch. Fingerling trout stocking gives fair returns where large predatory fish are largely absent.
- Walleye fingerlings are stocked to maintain fishable populations and offset years of poor natural reproduction. Walleye are described as the most extensively propagated fish in the state.
- The state is working to produce crayfish for stocking smallmouth bass waters, since crayfish are a favored smallmouth food.
- Rainbow trout distribution to waters not connected to large trout systems is discouraged because the species migrates. Its best use is seen as stocking trout lakes.

### Notable Observations

- Lake sturgeon were once destroyed in large numbers by commercial fishermen until about 1870. They later became a valued delicacy, with roe used for caviar and eggs for isinglass and oil.
- The muskellunge spawning operation involved netting spawners with fyke nets, holding them in tanks, and returning them unharmed after spawning.
- Largemouth bass may cannibalize their own young when they reach a fair size.
- The document notes that many anglers confuse yellow bass with white bass. Yellow bass have more yellow sides and about seven distinct, broken black stripes.

### Angler Conduct

The booklet closes with guidance for a "good sportsman": obey the letter and spirit of the regulations, release undersized fish gently, keep only what can be used, prevent forest fires, keep campsites clean, obtain landowner permission, report conditions that threaten fish life to the local conservation warden, hold a valid license, and respect other anglers.
