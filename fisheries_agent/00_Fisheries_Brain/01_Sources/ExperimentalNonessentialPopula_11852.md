# Experimental Nonessential Populations of Gila Topminnow and Desert Pupfish - Decision Memorandum

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This July 16, 1990 memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Field Supervisor to the Bureau of Land Management State Director announces the decision to halt designation of experimental nonessential populations of [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) on 44 BLM sites in Arizona. The FWS concluded that the experimental nonessential designation process under Section 10(j) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] was too cumbersome and that resources should instead focus on establishing fully protected populations at high-quality sites. The decision reflects lessons learned from seven years of applying the experimental population concept (added to the Act in 1982) and from limited success in existing reintroduction programs hampered by funding constraints and incomplete understanding of habitat requirements.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*, family [[Poeciliidae]]) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*, family [[Cyprinodontidae]]) are endangered fish species native to desert aquatic habitats in Arizona. The proposal, first discussed in 1983, sought to designate 44 sites on [[Bureau of Land Management]] lands in the Safford and Phoenix Districts as locations for experimental nonessential populations under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. This provision, added to the Act in 1982, was intended to accelerate species recovery by allowing reintroductions with reduced regulatory constraints.

Active reintroduction programs existed for fully protected populations of both species, though the [[Desert Pupfish]] program was described as small. However, these programs experienced limited success, with many reintroduced populations failing due to introduction of exotic fish, drying of water sources, human habitat modification, flooding, and unknown factors. The document notes that knowledge about habitat requirements for both species remained limited as of 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reintroduction sites were located on [[Bureau of Land Management]] lands in Arizona's desert regions, where aquatic habitats are naturally limited and vulnerable. The document identifies multiple threats to reintroduced populations including:

- Introduction of exotic fish species
- Desiccation of water sources
- Human-induced habitat modifications
- Flooding events
- Unknown factors indicating incomplete ecological understanding

The [[Gila Topminnow]] Recovery Team proposed that recovery be conducted on extant populations, with fish from these or parent stocks used to establish numerous other populations as needed. The recovery planning process emphasized establishing a hierarchical system for reintroduction sites, dividing them into high-quality biological sites and lesser-quality sites, with protection levels differing accordingly.

Ongoing land uses, particularly livestock grazing, were identified as activities requiring analysis for compatibility with species recovery. The document notes that most of the 44 BLM sites were identified for experimental nonessential designation regardless of habitat quality, which the FWS came to view as problematic.

## Key Findings

**Decision and Rationale:**
- FWS concluded no further action should be taken toward experimental nonessential population designation until recovery programs for both species were reanalyzed
- The experimental nonessential designation process proved cumbersome and lengthy, requiring publication of proposed and final rules in the Federal Register
- Expenditure of time and money on experimental populations was difficult to justify given limited funding, manpower, and success of existing fully protected reintroduction programs

**Problems with Experimental Designation Approach:**
- The provision was never intended to replace reintroduction of fully protected populations or draw resources from other recovery actions
- Under the Act, experimental populations must contribute to species conservation, which can only occur if designation accomplishes recovery purposes unattainable through other means while maintaining Section 7 protections
- Primary, high-quality reintroduction sites must be identified and afforded full protection rather than experimental status to prevent removing best habitats from species recovery
- No full inventory of potential reintroduction sites had been conducted to make sound biological determinations about which sites were most likely to succeed

**Revised Recovery Strategy:**
- FWS initiated revision of the [[Gila Topminnow]] recovery plan and preliminary drafting of a [[Desert Pupfish]] recovery plan
- New direction emphasizes quality over quantity of reintroductions
- Large proportion of future reintroduced populations should receive full protection
- Experimental nonessential populations counted toward recovery, creating risk of jeopardizing or delaying recovery by designating critical areas as experimental
- [[Gila Topminnow]] downlisting could be accomplished in the near term if collective efforts focused on establishing necessary number of viable self-sustaining populations

**Alternative Approach Recommended:**
- Comprehensive Section 7 consultation at time of reintroduction rather than experimental designation
- All ongoing and reasonably anticipated activities at a site would be analyzed for likelihood of jeopardizing species survival
- Approach eliminates need for cumbersome individual consultations
- Ongoing activities such as livestock grazing would be analyzed once, with no further consultation necessary unless changes not addressed in original consultation were proposed
- This approach considered more flexible and quicker than experimental designation

**Regional Concurrence:**
- June 6, 1990 memorandum from Regional Office concurred with Recovery Team concept
- Regional Office requested delay of further consideration of nonessential experimental population designations until recovery plans finalized
- Emphasized need to ensure recovery not jeopardized by designating critical recovery areas as experimental and nonessential
