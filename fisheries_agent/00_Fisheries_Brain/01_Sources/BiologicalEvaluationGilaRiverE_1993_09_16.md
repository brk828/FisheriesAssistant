# Biological Evaluation: Gila River Emergency Watershed Protection Program (AZ/NM Border to Geronimo, AZ)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #razorback-sucker #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1993 Soil Conservation Service (SCS) Biological Evaluation assesses emergency flood-repair projects (dikes, levees, jacks, tree plantings) along the Gila River following major floods (January–March 1993). Although the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) had proposed critical habitat designated in this river reach (January 1993), Arizona Game and Fish Department surveys confirmed **no razorback suckers have been detected in the Gila River since 1986**, and a 1981–82 reintroduction attempt failed for unknown/unconfirmed reasons. The evaluation concludes that proposed bank-stabilization projects will have **no adverse effect on the razorback sucker population** (since none are present) and will not adversely modify designated critical habitat. Concerns were also raised regarding the category-one candidate species, the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*), though it was not yet formally listed under the Endangered Species Act at the time of writing.

## Background & Description
The source document does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the [[Razorback Sucker]]. It is identified taxonomically as *Xyrauchen texanus*, family [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), a large-bodied freshwater fish native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. No meristic, coloration, or size data are reported in this evaluation; the focus is on life history, habitat use, and conservation status rather than anatomy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements (per Minckley 1973)
- Typically occupies water **>1 meter deep**, often **>15 meters** in lacustrine (lake) settings.
- All life activities occur over **mud, sand, or gravel substrates**.
- Critical microhabitats: **eddies and backwaters** lateral to strong currents, and areas **concentrated behind obstructions** or in **deep holes near cut banks or fallen trees** — especially important for large adults in the Gila River.
- Juveniles travel in **large schools along stream margins**.

### Habitat Use (per Rinne & Minckley 1991)
- Adults occupy **strong, uniform currents over sandy bottom**.
- Eddies and backwaters lateral to the main channel remain important.
- Functions as a **bottom feeder**, preferring [[Zooplankton]] and detritus.
- **No evidence of successful recruitment** of young razorbacks anywhere in Colorado Basin rivers — a key recruitment bottleneck.

### Spawning & Feeding
- Spawning occurs along **shorelines or in bays**, typically **one female accompanied by 2–12 males**, from **late winter to early spring**.
- Diet: **algae, dipteran larvae, zooplankton (crustaceans)**; feeding occurs in water up to **six meters deep**.
- In the [[Colorado River Basin]] (California Dept. of Fish and Game, 1985), **oxbows and backwaters** serve as spawning, nursery, and refuge habitat.

### Predation Pressure
- **Exotic/introduced fish predation** on eggs, larvae, and fingerlings is documented as a **significant depressive factor** on lower basin populations (CA Dept. Fish & Game, 1985).
- Minckley (1983): *"Predation by introduced fishes on early life-history stages of razorback sucker is considered the most important factor in their decline."*
- This predation pressure — rather than habitat loss alone — is proposed as a likely explanation for the **failure of the 1981–82 reintroduction effort** in the Gila River.

### Limiting Factors (per Tyus & Karp 1989)
1. Low winter flows
2. High spring flows
3. Seasonal changes in river temperature
4. Inundated shorelines and bottomlands

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** No razorback suckers detected in the Gila River (AZ/NM border to Coolidge Dam) in annual Arizona Game and Fish Department surveys **since 1986**; confirmed via personal communication with Kirk Young (AGFD).
- **Reintroduction failure:** 1981–82 reintroduction attempts were unsuccessful for unconfirmed reasons, plausibly linked to **predation by non-native fishes** rather than habitat quality alone.
- **No formal consultation required:** SCS determined no endangered species were present, so formal [[Section 7 Consultation]] was not initially triggered; however, informal consultation was held with USFWS due to concern over **proposed critical habitat** (published Federal Register, January 29, 1993) for the razorback sucker.
- **Project footprint on sensitive habitat:** Of 31,114 total linear feet of proposed dikes, only **2,186 feet (7%)** are located adjacent to cut banks with deep pools, and a further **2,186 feet (7%)** are in areas of strong current adjacent to cut banks — the two habitat types most critical to adult razorbacks. **86% of dikes are set back from the water**, minimizing direct habitat disturbance.
- **Net effect determination:** Project engineers/biologists concluded **beneficial effects will exceed detrimental effects**:
  - Tree plantings will moderate water temperature fluctuations and add allochthonous food inputs.
  - Structures (jacks, dikes, trees) will create new eddies and slower-velocity refugia during high spring flows.
  - Shoreline/shallow-water habitat for spawning and juvenile migration will be preserved or enhanced.
  - Effects on stream channel hydraulics assessed by SCS hydrologist/geologist as **immeasurably small** in both short and long term.
- **Willow Flycatcher effects:** No tree removal planned; new tree plantings are expected to reconnect fragmented riparian corridors, yielding a **beneficial effect** on habitat value for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and other neotropical riparian birds.
- **Final determination:** The EWP projects were found **not likely to adversely affect** razorback sucker populations (none present) or destroy/modify proposed critical habitat, and **not likely to adversely affect** willow flycatcher habitat — clearing the way for permit issuance under the emergency designation.
