# Forage Fish Introduction into Lake Powell: Comments, Responses and Management Position

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1991 Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR) correspondence and draft summary of public comments on a proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] as an additional forage fish. The proposal responds to a striped bass population, introduced in 1974, that reproduced unchecked in the reservoir, produced serious forage shortages, and degraded the sport fishery. The Division received comments from three federal agencies, six state agencies, six universities and several independent reviewers. Opposition was substantial and came mainly from native-fish and preservation interests. The Division answered each of 17 issue categories and concluded that the introduction is warranted and will not jeopardize endemic [[Colorado River Fishes]].

## Background & Description

- **Management history:** [[Striped Bass]] were introduced to Lake Powell in 1974. The [[Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council]] was alerted to a potential problem in 1979. The Council directed study of the forage problem, which considered fertilization as an alternative.
- **Species selection:** The State of Utah chose rainbow smelt as the best forage alternative in 1987 and submitted a formal proposal in 1988. In 1989 a majority of the Technical Committee recommended the introduction, and the Council deferred action pending a public impact assessment.
- **Review process:** A final draft "Assessment of a Forage Fish Introduction into Lake Powell" was circulated in March 1990, with public notice in newspapers in Utah, Arizona, Nevada and California. Comments came from 3 federal agencies, 6 state agencies and 16 independent reviewers (including 6 university reviewers).
- **Stated goal:** Lake Powell is managed as a basic yield fishery, with a target of minimum average-sized striped bass of 18 inches and a catch rate of 0.5 fish per hour. The introduction aims to make adult striped bass piscivorous and catchable rather than restore trophy fishing.
- **Comparative species:** [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) was introduced in 1968 and established reservoir-wide. Smelt have been introduced to several Colorado and Missouri River-basin reservoirs outside the Colorado system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal ecology:** Smelt prefer 6–16 °C and avoid warmer water, and they occupy the cold, oxygenated hypolimnion. Adult striped bass were recorded at 60–100 ft where temperatures are 9–15 °C.
- **Stratification:** From May to November the thermocline ranges from about 20 ft (May) to near 60 ft (September). About 20% of zooplankton remains below the thermocline. Zooplankton occur to depths over 300 ft.
- **Life-history parallels:** Evans and Loftus (1987) found smelt-associated declines in species with a pelagic larva, demersal metalimnetic juvenile and demersal hypolimnetic adult stage. Striped bass is the only established reproducing Colorado system fish with a similar life history.
- **Selenium:** Fish tissue from Lake Powell shows moderately high selenium, but no reproductive failure has been detected in game fish.
- **Endangered species:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are the primary concerns. Razorback larvae are virtually absent from the pelagic zone that adult smelt would occupy, and Lake Mohave has shown no or insignificant razorback recruitment since the 1950s. Predation by green sunfish and catfish is identified as the main recruitment limiter.
- **Dispersal risk:** Smelt could reach [[Bear Lake]] (Utah–Idaho) or other cold-water pelagic systems through illegal angler transfer. No known illegal transfers had occurred as of the document.

## Key Findings

- **Central problem:** Unrestricted striped bass reproduction caused forage shortages affecting the entire Lake Powell fish population.
- **Rationale:** Providing a cold-water forage fish would let adult striped bass feed on fish, reaching 18 inches, shifting the population from planktivorous juveniles to piscivorous adults, and increasing angler harvest.
- **Alternatives rejected:** Stripercides and sterile-fish releases were judged infeasible; a stripercide would need application along roughly 2,000 miles of shoreline within two to three weeks. Shad restocking was dismissed as unlikely to solve juvenile predation.
- **Endangered species position:** Under the Fish and Wildlife Service's March 3, 1986 jeopardy standard, the Division argued the introduction does not threaten survival or recovery of endemic fishes. Smelt migration upstream is considered impossible because of vertical drops of one foot or more.
- **Regulatory position:** The Division held that a state stocking action is not a federal action under NEPA, but it still complied with NEPA disclosure through the assessment.
- **Multi-species option:** A multi-species forage complex (threadfin shad, gizzard shad, alewives, blueback herring, rainbow smelt) was acknowledged as potentially more stable but was rated riskier for downstream native fishes.
- **Outcome:** As of August 8, 1991, the Division was still studying options for future introduction, and Director Timothy H. Provan's letter stated that opposing comments contained no new data invalidating the Division's analysis.
- **Concession:** The Division conceded that smelt might not establish in Lake Powell, given heavy predation, but judged the potential sport-fishery benefit sufficient to justify the modest cost.
