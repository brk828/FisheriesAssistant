# AGFD Comments on Proposed Razorback Sucker Endangered Species Listing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationPolicy #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) regarding the 1990 proposal to list the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as an endangered species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. While AGFD supports endangered status for the species, the letter argues that the listing proposal disproportionately emphasizes the Upper Colorado River Basin and fails to credit substantial Lower Basin recovery efforts, including large-scale hatchery stocking, reintroduction programs, and monitoring of wild populations in Arizona. The letter also draws a direct comparison to the conservation status of the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), arguing both species warrant full, undivided endangered protections without "nonessential" experimental population designations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use**: Razorback suckers occupy a range of habitats across the Lower Colorado River Basin, including:
  - Large reservoirs: [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Mead]]
  - Riverine reaches above and below these reservoirs
  - Tributary systems: [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], [[Gila River]] and their tributaries
  - Isolated backwater habitats and uppermost reservoirs of stocked drainages
  - Grand Canyon reach, including the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]]

- **Population status**: The largest known remaining **natural** population of razorback suckers is reported in [[Lake Mohave]], within the Lower Basin — a critical point emphasized by AGFD as under-represented in the federal listing proposal.

- **Co-occurring species**: Collaborative Lower Basin efforts included joint collection surveys of razorback suckers alongside [[Bonytail Chub]] and monitoring of introduced [[Striped Bass]] populations in Lake Mohave, indicating overlapping habitat use and potential predator/competitor interactions.

- **Reintroduction ecology**: Stocked razorbacks show evidence of establishing populations in isolated habitats and uppermost reservoir zones of stocked drainages, suggesting some degree of habitat suitability and survivorship beyond the immediate post-stocking period, despite low recapture rates.

## Key Findings

- **Large-scale stocking program**: Over the preceding 10 years (as of 1990), more than **13 million razorback suckers** were stocked across **57 sites** in Arizona, primarily in the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and [[Gila River]] systems and their tributaries.

- **Recapture and establishment evidence**: Recaptures of stocked razorbacks were relatively infrequent and generally occurred within one year of stocking; however, indirect evidence suggests population establishment in isolated habitats and upper reservoir reaches.

- **Hatchery production**: AGFD's [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] has increased both the quantity and size of razorback suckers produced annually, complementing production from the federal [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], forming a combined foundation for reintroduction programs basin-wide.

- **Wild population monitoring in Grand Canyon**: Seven individual razorback suckers were captured during the two most recent years of humpback chub monitoring efforts at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] in the Grand Canyon. Additional reliable sightings occurred in [[Bright Angel Creek]] over recent years, indicating a persistent, if small, wild presence in this reach.

- **Interagency collaboration**: Three major multi-agency collection efforts in the Lower Basin targeted razorback suckers and [[Bonytail Chub]], while concurrently gathering data on introduced [[Striped Bass]] populations in [[Lake Mohave]] — reflecting integrated multispecies management concerns.

- **Conservation status parity with Bonytail Chub**: AGFD asserts that razorback sucker status is "essentially the same" as that of the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]], and opposes "experimental, nonessential" population designations for either species, arguing no population of such critically imperiled fish can be considered biologically nonessential.

- **Policy/management implications**: AGFD warns that potential listing as merely "threatened" (rather than "endangered") could trigger critical habitat designations with significant impacts on sportfishing recreation and general fisheries management in Arizona, and requests full-partner involvement in future critical habitat determinations.

- **Institutional concern**: The letter highlights a recurring inter-basin governance issue — the Upper Basin's proposal process allegedly failed to credit or integrate Lower Basin recovery data, risking exclusion of Lower Basin agencies from future endangered species management decisions affecting shared species.
