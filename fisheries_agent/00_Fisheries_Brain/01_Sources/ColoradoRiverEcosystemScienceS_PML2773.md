# Colorado River Ecosystem Science Symposium 2005: Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #aquatic-ecology #adaptive-management

## Executive Summary

This document is the abstract volume for the Colorado River Ecosystem Science Symposium, held October 25–27, 2005, in Tempe, Arizona. The symposium was presented by the [[Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Program]] (GCDAMP) and organized by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]] of the U.S. Geological Survey. It reviews monitoring and research on the downstream effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on the regulated Colorado River through Grand Canyon. Coverage includes sediment and sandbars, hydrology and water quality, fishes (especially the endangered [[Humpback Chub]]), aquatic food webs, riparian vegetation, birds, cultural resources, recreation, hydropower economics, and monitoring technology. The volume contains roughly 60 oral and poster abstracts across three days, plus keynote and speaker biographies and an author index.

## Background & Description

The GCDAMP was largely created by the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992 (GCPA), which directs the Secretary of the Interior to operate Glen Canyon Dam to protect and improve the natural and cultural resources of Grand Canyon National Park and Glen Canyon National Recreation Area. Adaptive management was chosen so that dam-operation effects would be assessed and used to modify future operations. The program is administered by the Department of the Interior, facilitated by the [[Adaptive Management Work Group]] (a Federal Advisory Committee), and supported by the Technical Work Group, Independent Review Panels, and the USGS monitoring center.

The study area covers about 293 river miles: roughly 15 miles from the dam to Lees Ferry, plus the 277-mile corridor within Grand Canyon National Park. Limited investigation of tributaries such as the [[Little Colorado River]] and Paria River is also in scope.

The mission of the monitoring center is to provide credible, objective, ecosystem-scale science on dam effects. The 2005 symposium coincided with the tenth anniversary of the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) and the release of the first comprehensive report, *The State of the Colorado River Ecosystem in Grand Canyon* (SCORE).

The Vision and Mission statement commits the program to protecting native biodiversity, cultural and spiritual values, and recreational resources, consistent with federal trust responsibilities to tribes and the [[Law Of The River]], while giving due consideration to hydropower.

Twelve management goals were adopted. Those most relevant to aquatic biology include:
- Protect the aquatic food base to support viable higher-trophic populations.
- Maintain native fish, remove jeopardy from humpback chub and razorback sucker.
- Maintain a naturally reproducing rainbow trout population above the Paria River.
- Maintain Kanab ambersnail populations and riparian/spring communities.
- Establish water temperature, quality and flow dynamics supporting ecosystem goals.

Other goals cover sediment storage, recreation, power production, cultural resources and monitoring quality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and sandbars.** Sand is the central management concern. Closure of the dam in 1963 cut sand supply at the upstream boundary of the park by about 94%. The Paria River is the primary sand source for Marble Canyon, and sand is delivered in short, large floods, mainly in the warm season. Reported results include:
- Fine-sediment storage declined in the 1990s; the loss of eddy sand is uncertain but estimated at about 25% of area exposed at base flow in pre-dam photographs (range from 4% to –55%, depending on reach and method).
- The 1996 Beach/Habitat-Building Flow (45,000 ft³/s for 7 days) and the November 2004 High Experimental Flow (41,000 ft³/s for 60 hours) produced different bar responses. About two-thirds of upstream Marble Canyon bars were larger after 2004 than after 1996; only one-third in the downstream half were.
- Aeolian (wind) sand transport can redistribute flood-deposited sand, with transport rates above 9 kg/cm/day in the April–May windy season.

**Hydrology and climate.** A drought beginning in 2000 lowered Lake Powell about 142 feet by April 2005. Warm releases exceeded 15 °C (59 °F) by late August 2005, the warmest since 1970, and release dissolved oxygen reached unprecedented levels. Debris flows from Grand Canyon tributaries build debris fans and rapids; the 1996 controlled flood partly reworked these fans.

**Fishes and food webs.** Native humpback chub and nonnative trout are central. Black-fly larvae (*Simulium arcticum*) are a major food for both native and nonnative fishes. Trout diet near the [[Little Colorado River]] was dominated by aquatic invertebrates, especially black-fly larvae (60–80% by weight in rainbow trout). Piscivory by trout on small native fish was documented. Sample sizes in these studies were often too small to resolve predation rates.

**Riparian and terrestrial systems.** Riparian vegetation and bird abundance have declined. Arthropod abundances tracked the hydrograph in some habitats, while small-mammal and herpetofaunal abundance was highly variable and only loosely linked to vegetation.

## Key Findings

**Program and governance**
- The GCDAMP is an outgrowth of the GCPA and is built on adaptive ecosystem management.
- USGS is the non-regulatory science provider for the program.

**Sediment and geomorphology**
- Fine-sediment storage in eddies and channel margins decreased from pre-dam levels; the trend did not reverse under 1990s dam operations ([[Schmidt]] abstract).
- Flood deposits adjust over years to decades; the 1996 flood's deposits persisted about 5 years.
- A 2001 study found an earlier sand-transport paradigm was partly false: sand did not accumulate in the channel over multi-year timescales.
- The 2004 flood retained about 800,000 metric tons of new sand upstream, leading to more bar building in upper Marble Canyon.
- An ex post facto comparison found the EIS sand-accumulation method differed from measurements by 3–8 million metric tons, in the direction of accumulation, undermining stable rating-curve assumptions.

**Fishes**
- Humpback chub adult population declined by 30–60% since monitoring began in the late 1980s; more than 80% of the adult population was marked by the mid-1990s. The recovery criterion of stable abundance was not met.
- Mechanical removal of nonnative fishes (2003–2004) captured 22,261 fishes, of which 18,701 (84%) were nonnative, mostly rainbow trout (94%). Depletion estimates indicated roughly 12% removal per pass for rainbow trout.
- Warmer than normal releases from 2003 onward confound interpretation of native-fish responses.
- Fluctuating flows in 2003–2004 affected early life stages of rainbow trout and related recruitment.
- Otolith chemistry (Sr isotopes) classified individual humpback chub to source tributaries at 66% accuracy; combining with elemental ratios raised accuracy to 95%.
- Conservation genetics of humpback chub showed low mtDNA diversity, but surprisingly high microsatellite variability, with patterns reflecting a post-Pleistocene bottleneck.

**Aquatic food webs**
- Drift invertebrate biomass at the Little Colorado River averaged 1.2 mg/L, well below the Lees Ferry value of 6.8 mg/L.
- Drift was 92.5% aquatic in origin; terrestrial contribution rose to 23.5% in summer only.
- Experimental high flows increased drift and organic matter, with the source traced to tributary and reservoir-derived material rather than riparian inundation.

**Water quality and physical monitoring**
- Lake Powell dropped to hypoxic and warm conditions in 2005; decreased lake levels caused releases more than 5 °C above decadal norms.
- A new STARS-based model covers flows up to 200,000 cfs across about 226 river miles (Lees Ferry to Diamond Creek), with vertical accuracy of ±0.5 m at low and ±1.5 m at high discharge.

**Economics and power**
- Operational constraints on power releases were estimated through the Colorado River Storage Project simulation model.

**Monitoring methods and technology**
- Two-way satellite telemetry now allows real-time remote control of sediment instruments.
- LiDAR and oblique photogrammetry work well only in dry conditions, with exposed ground and minimal vegetation.
- Laser-acoustic methods can compute suspended-sand loads within about 5% of conventional values and generate far more data.

**Management implications**
- Several presenters argued that adaptive management should use more imaginative experimental designs, since unplanned treatments (such as the 2000 Low Summer Steady Flow) produced unexpected learning.
- A presentation of 10 years of adaptive management concluded that monitoring must continue after compliance activities are completed to detect unforeseen effects.
