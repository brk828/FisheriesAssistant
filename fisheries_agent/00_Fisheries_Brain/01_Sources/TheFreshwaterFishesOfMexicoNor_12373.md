# Freshwater Fishes of Mexico: A Descriptive Catalogue, Keys and Distribution Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This Field Columbian Museum zoological publication (Zoology Vol. V, Introduction and systematic catalogue) provides a descriptive list, identification keys and distribution analysis of the freshwater fishes of Mexico north of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. The work is based largely on two field collections: one by [[F. E. Lutz]] and the author in 1901, and one by the author in February–May 1903, supplemented by specimens loaned by the [[United States National Museum]], the Mexican collections of Dr. W. L. Tower, and collections by E. W. Nelson. The catalogue treats two classes (Marsipobranchii and Pisces), numerous orders and families, roughly 150 numbered species and varieties, and a long series of river-system checklists. The author emphasises that Mexico sits at a meeting point between northern temperate and southern tropical faunas, making the region both taxonomically complex and biogeographically significant.

## Background & Description

**Scope and sources.** The author states that the list reflects every relevant paper he could access, with taxonomic sequence following [[Jordan & Evermann]] (Bulletin 47, U.S. National Museum). Acknowledgements credit numerous railway engineers, museum curators (including [[Barton W. Evermann]], [[Barton A. Bean]], [[G. A. Boulenger]] and [[C. T. Regan]]) and officials for access to material and logistical help.

**Boundary definition.** A few salt-water genera (e.g., Pomadasys, Achirus, Gobius) are included because they occur in fresh water. The author notes that no arbitrary line separates freshwater from marine fishes.

**Taxonomic framework.** The work defines two classes:
- **Class Marsipobranchii** – lampreys, represented by a single species, *Lampetra spadicea* (Lerma Basin).
- **Class Pisces** – true fishes, the bulk of the catalogue, arranged in orders from the garpikes (Rhomboganoidea, with *Lepidosteus osseus*, *L. platystomus*, *L. tristoechus* and *L. tropicus*) through catfishes (Nematognathi), carps and suckers (Plectospondyli), killifishes and livebearers (Haplomi), to spiny-rayed fishes (Acanthopteri).

**Identification approach.** The text provides an artificial key to families, detailed morphological definitions (fin counts, scale counts, barbel and tooth characters) and notes that colour is less reliable than structural features. It explicitly warns against describing new species without sufficient material, and recommends caution with intraspecific colour variation such as that seen in [[Platypoecilus maculatus]] and [[Platypoecilus variatus]].

**Nomenclature.** Common names (e.g., *Bagre*, *Mojarra*, *Trucha*, *Roballo*) are catalogued; the author notes that several are used collectively and that spellings are uncertain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography.** Most of Mexico is a plateau at 3,000–8,000 ft. Eastern Sierra Madre ranges create a narrow coastal plain; the Isthmus of Tehuantepec is a low watershed between the Sierra Madre del Sur and the eastern range. The [[Rio Balsas]] is described as the second-largest river in Mexico, flowing to the Pacific; the [[Rio Grande de Santiago]] (with the [[Rio Lerma]]) is the longest river wholly within the country.

**Climate and hydrology.** Rainfall is highly variable: the Vera Cruz area receives very heavy rain, while northern Chihuahua has a short summer wet season. Many northern streams end in lakes that dry out, and rising alkalinity during dry-season lake contraction is reported to kill fishes in large numbers (e.g., [[Lago de Guzman]], [[Lago de Patos]]).

**Geological change.** The author proposes that northern streams such as the Rio Casas Grandes, Rio Santa Maria, Rio Carmen and Rio Nazas were once tributaries of the [[Rio Grande]]. Following [[O. C. Farrington]]'s suggestion, western rivers may have captured headwaters of eastern drainages. Recent volcanism in the central plateau is also noted. Testimony from [[A. V. Temple]] of reduced water volumes and drying lakes (e.g., La Laguna, Chihuahua) is cited as evidence of ongoing change.

**Dispersal barriers.** Fishes cannot travel overland; dispersal is restricted to streams and lakes. Cascades and falls on Pacific-slope rivers are treated as barriers, and the Falls of the Yellowstone and Shoshone Falls are offered as analogous examples.

**Reproduction and life history.** The author documents viviparity in the killifishes of the Lerma basin (*Goodea*, *Skiffia*, *Characodon*, *Gambusia*, *Poecilia* and related genera), with males of several genera showing a modified anal fin (gonopodium-like). Spawning near the close of the dry season is argued to allow young to reach size before the rains.

**Fisheries and human use.** Game fishes are limited to [[Largemouth Black Bass]] in northeastern streams and trout in Pacific-slope mountain streams. Food fishes include the whitefish *Chirostoma* (Pescados Blancos) dried for market in the Lerma region, and catfishes and garpikes sold in markets such as Tampico.

## Key Findings

**1. Species inventory.** The catalogue lists about 150 numbered taxa (species and varieties) across roughly 90 genera and 23 families. Examples include:
- Lampreys: *Lampetra spadicea* (Lerma Basin).
- Garpikes: *Lepidosteus osseus*, *L. tristoechus*.
- Catfishes: *Galeichthys aguadulce*, *Ichthyelurus furcatus*, *Amiurus lupus*, *Istlarius balsanus*, *Rhamdia oaxacae*.
- Suckers: *Carpiodes meridionalis*, *Pantosteus plebeius*, *Catostomus sonorensis*, *Xyrauchen cypho*.
- Minnows: *Algansea tincella*, *Xystrosus popoche*, *Hybognathus episcopus*, *Gila elegans*, *Leuciscus nigrescens*.
- Killifishes and livebearers (Poeciliidae): *Goodea luitpoldi*, *Skiffia lermae*, *Characodon garmani*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Xiphophorus helleri*, *Poecilia sphenops*.
- Silversides: about 16 *Chirostoma* species, the largest share of the Lerma fauna.
- Cichlids: *Cichlasoma* spp. (e.g., *C. istlanum*, *C. parma*, *C. steindachneri*).

**2. Faunal balance.** Of the 54 species known from the Valley of Mexico/Lerma-San Juan region, the author reports that 36 belong to two families, 20 Poeciliidae and 16 Atherinidae (*Chirostoma*). Only two (*Myzostoma austrinum*, *Aztecula vittata*) occur in other basins. Eight of the 21 genera in this region are endemic.

**3. River-system records.** Checklists are given for about 25 drainage systems. The Rio Soto la Marina has 18 species, twelve northern and six tropical; the Rio Panuco has 32 recorded species, fifteen northern and the rest tropical. Notably, the Rio Mezquital fauna is almost entirely Rio Grande in character, while the Rio Presidio (near Mazatlan) has no northern species.

**4. Collection effort.** Field collections are documented at more than 60 localities (about 30 in 1901 and about 60 in 1903 across Chihuahua, Durango, Nuevo Leon, Tamaulipas, San Luis Potosi, Jalisco, Michoacan, Mexico, Morelos, Puebla, Vera Cruz and Oaxaca).

**5. Biogeographic conclusions.** Southern and Central American faunas extend north to Mexico City, mostly along lowland streams. Northern genera (*Lampetra*, *Amiurus*, *Myzostoma*, *Nototropis*, *Hybopsis*) do not extend south of the Lerma. The Lerma basin is described as a centre of distribution, and the Rio Balsas, despite its size, holds comparatively few species (twelve taken from eight localities).

**6. Introduced and marine species.** About 22 shore or brackish-water fishes (e.g., *Tylosurus marinus*, *Mugil cephalus*, *Centropomus mexicanus*, *Pomadasys* spp.) have become established in fresh water. Goldfish and carp are abundant in Valley of Mexico lakes.

**7. Reproductive note.** Viviparity is confirmed in the Lerma killifishes (Gambusia infans, Poecilia occidentalis), with modified anal fins in males; the largest Lerma killifish reaches 8–10 inches.

**8. Aquaculture and recommendations.** The author records a rainbow trout hatchery at the source of the Rio Lerma (about 15 years old) producing one-half to one million eggs annually, with spring water at about 55 °F. He recommends the Lerma basin lakes as suitable for largemouth black bass or rainbow trout introduction, cautioning that introductions should avoid disrupting the native fauna.

**9. Methodological cautions.** The author stresses that numerous localities in earlier literature are vague ("Mexico" alone), that some reported records are doubtful (e.g., localities from the Columbian Exposition collection were excluded), and that the work needs further field verification.
