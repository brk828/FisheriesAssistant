# Spawning and Movements of Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Green River Basin of Colorado and Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Tyus and Karp (1990) studied the spawning behavior, movements, and habitat use of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the upper [[Green River]] and lower [[Yampa River]] from 1987 to 1989. Using electrofishing, capture-recapture, and radiotelemetry, the authors documented ripe fish in riffles from mid-April to early June, spawning migrations of 30 to 106 km, and spawning associated with rising and peak spring flows at water temperatures near 14°C. The species is described as in peril because of few spawning adults, hybridization with other suckers, minimal recruitment, and habitat loss.

## Background & Description

- The razorback sucker is a catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and the only member of its genus. It was once abundant enough to be harvested commercially but is now threatened with extinction.
- It was proposed for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) and is protected by Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming.
- Natural populations persist in some reservoirs of the lower Colorado River, near Grand Junction (Colorado), sporadically in the lower San Juan River, and in the Green and Yampa rivers. The Green River population is described as the largest extant riverine population of the species, though small.
- Stocks are composed of old individuals, and recruitment is minimal or absent throughout the basin.
- Sampling occurred in the upper Green River (km 396.8 to 552.0) and the lower Yampa River (about km 0 to 72) using [[Electrofishing]] and [[Trammel Netting]]. Seventeen fish were fitted with [[Radiotelemetry]] transmitters and tracked by the USFWS.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** Ripe fish were captured in riffles and runs over cobble, gravel, and sand substrates, usually associated with submerged bars (mean depth 0.63 m; mean velocity 0.74 m/s).
- **Temperature and flow:** Spawning occurred during rising and peak spring runoff at mean water temperatures of about 14°C (range 9 to 17°C). In a high-water year, spawning extended into June.
- **Lowland use:** Flooded lowlands (e.g., Old Charley Wash, Stewart Lake Drain) and tributary mouths (Duchesne River, Ashley Creek) were used during the breeding season, presumably as resting and feeding areas. In 1986, 47% of ripe or tuberculate fish were taken in flooded lowlands at warmer water (mean 19°C) than the main channel (15.9°C).
- **Associated species:** Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) spawned in the same riffles, and putative razorback × flannelmouth hybrids were found.
- **Threats:** Dams and dikes, loss of seasonal floodplain habitat, altered flow and temperature regimes, predation by non-native fish, and hybridization.

## Key Findings

- **Sample and capture totals:** 230 adult razorback suckers were captured from 1987 to 1989 (125 newly tagged; 70 fish recaptured on 105 occasions). Of these, 194 (84%) were ripe.
- **Sex composition:** Ripe males numbered 144 (74%) and ripe females 50 (26%); six additional fish were tuberculate without expressible sex products. Combined with earlier data (Tyus, 1987), the sex ratio was about 2.5 males : 1.0 female.
- **Size:** Total lengths ranged from 405 to 597 mm. Males averaged 507 mm TL and 1,370 g; females averaged 547 mm TL and 1,686 g.
- **Breeding period:** Ripe fish were captured from mid-April to early June. Recaptured males were ripe for 2 to 28 days and females for 2 to 15 days (about 8 days on average).
- **Spawning sites:** Jensen (km 486.4 to 504.0) yielded 177 breeding captures (91%), and the Yampa site (lower 5 km) yielded 14 (7%). Repeated captures identified specific spawning microhabitats in the Yampa River and the Green River near Green River Campground.
- **Fidelity:** Twenty-seven recaptured fish were in breeding condition in the same spawning reach in different years. One ripe male captured in 1981 was recaptured at the same location in 1988.
- **Movements:** Eight radiotagged fish and 58 recaptures indicated local movement. Seven migratory fish (12 recaptures) moved 52.8 to 190.4 km. Thirteen fish moved between the Jensen site and Ouray (mean one-way 94.4 km, range 54 to 106 km), and four moved between Jensen, Island Park, and the Yampa site (mean 49.6 km, range 30.4 to 60.8 km).
- **Hybridization:** Seventeen suspected flannelmouth × razorback hybrids in breeding condition (14 males, 3 females) were collected, the most upstream about 67.2 km above the Green River confluence in Yampa Canyon.
- **Comparative abundance (1989):** Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were each about eight times more numerous than razorback suckers at the Yampa site. Flannelmouth suckers were twice as abundant as razorbacks and four times as abundant as bluehead suckers at Jensen. Spawning of all three species overlapped from mid-April to late May.
- **Recruitment:** No razorback suckers under 405 mm TL were captured, so recruitment is presumed low or absent.
- **Conservation implications:** The authors recommend addressing habitat loss, flow and temperature alteration, non-native predation, and hybridization in recovery plans. They suggest reintroduction programs consider maintaining separate genetic stocks, since individual fish showed site fidelity. Longevity of about 10 years was noted.
