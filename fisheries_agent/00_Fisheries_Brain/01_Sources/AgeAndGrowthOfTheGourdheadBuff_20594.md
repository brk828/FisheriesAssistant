# Age and Growth of the Gourdhead Buffalo in Reelfoot Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, the sixth in a series on fish life histories at [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee, estimates the age and growth of the [[Gourdhead Buffalo]] (*Megastomatobus cyprinella*) from scales of 118 fish collected in summers 1937 to 1942. Growth in length slows after the third summer and then stays roughly constant, while growth in weight keeps increasing through the eighth summer. The average buffalo reaches the 16-inch legal length during its fourth summer, and the author recommends keeping that limit because commercial fishing removes buffalo during their fastest growth period. The document also includes a short survey of fish removed from a sinkhole bay of [[Norris Reservoir]] in 1941, plus non-research material (a teacher-recruitment appeal and a news column).

## Background & Description

The Gourdhead buffalo is the largest of three buffalo species, exceeding three feet in length and fifty pounds in weight. It belongs to the sucker family [[Catostomidae]] and gets its name from a bull-like lump on the neck of adults. It is not a game fish. Commercial fishermen take it with trotlines, set lines, hoop nets and trammel nets, and [[Reelfoot Lake]] yields about 250,000 pounds annually. The flesh is well flavored but contains many small bones.

Buffalo are bottom feeders. Their diet is about 75 percent animal (worms, larvae, eggs and microscopic organisms strained from mud) and 25 percent vegetable (algae and small plants). They feed in shallow water at night, which makes them easy to catch. They spawn in spring in inflowing streams, weedy bayous and overflows. Spawning has never been observed in Reelfoot Lake, and few young fish are found in the lake proper.

The author notes that studies of southern lakes, including [[Bennett 1937]], [[Hubbs and Cooper 1935]], [[Eschmeyer 1940]] and [[Schoffman Series]] (1938 to 1942), indicate faster fish growth in southern waters than in northern waters. The author expects rough fish in southern lakes to show the same pattern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was motivated by conservationists, sportsmen and commercial fishermen who wanted age and growth data to guide game laws at [[Reelfoot Lake]]. The author frames the work as part of restoring the "balance of nature" after human disturbance. The [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] had taken a 75-year lease on one-third of the lake and was starting a $500,000 reclamation program.

Samples came from three sites: the Spillway (south shore), Samburg (east shore) and Walnut Log (northeast end). Commercial fishermen used barrel nets, trammel nets and trotlines. Because collections were limited to June, July and August, age was counted in summers of life rather than years, so fish caught in summer were not credited with growth they had not yet completed.

The Norris Reservoir study describes a circular sinkhole bay about 870 feet in circumference and 1.4 acres in area, with a maximum depth of about 12 feet. It connected to the reservoir through a channel 135 feet wide and about 5 feet deep. The fish were poisoned in September 1941 while the reservoir was dropping. Afterward, the drained bay had little cover, only a small amount of brush and relatively few rocks.

## Key Findings

**Buffalo age structure and sampling**

- The study used scales from 118 Gourdhead buffalo, aged with the method of [[Schoffman 1939 Method]] (polarized light on unprepared scales).
- The sample spans eight age groups. Groups III to VI made up 82 percent of the fish (III: 15%, IV: 30%, V: 17%, VI: 21%).
- The oldest specimen was in its eighth summer, measuring 28.7 inches and weighing 248 ounces (15 pounds 8 ounces).

**Length growth**

- Average standard length rises from 271 mm (summer 2) to 553 mm (summer 8).
- Growth in length is fastest early. By the second summer the fish have completed 49 percent of their total length growth (relative to the eighth-summer length). The figures are 57% by summer 3, 62% by summer 4, 67% by summer 5, 77% by summer 6 and 88% by summer 7.
- Annual length increments fall after summer 2 (about 45 mm), drop to about 25 mm in summer 4, rise to about 75 mm in summer 6 and reach about 145 mm in summer 7.
- The author reports that after summer 3 the length growth rate stays roughly constant, an observation tied to the [[Law Of Growth Compensation]], which the author says applies to these fish.

**Weight growth**

- Average weight rises from 697.4 g (summer 2) to 6242.7 g (summer 8, about 13 pounds 12 ounces).
- Only 11 percent of total weight growth is completed by the second summer, rising to 53 percent by the seventh summer.
- Annual weight increments rise steadily through all eight summers, so the author concludes the average buffalo is still in a fast-growing phase in weight.
- The fish gain weight relative to length most strongly in the later summers, and the greatest weight-for-length ratio occurs in the eighth summer.

**Legal length and management**

- The average buffalo reaches the 16-inch legal length during its fourth summer.
- Because buffalo are harvested during their most rapid growth, and current law does not require commercial fishermen to weigh catches or pay royalties, the author argues the 16-inch legal length should be kept to maintain buffalo numbers. The author also notes that no yearly creel census is available, although pounds handled by fish buyers are "beyond doubt decreasing."

**Norris Reservoir bay survey (September 1941)**

- The poisoned bay yielded 2,540 fish weighing 192.4 pounds.
- [[Bluegill]] were most numerous (1,114 fish of 1 to 4 inches, plus 8 fish of 4 to 5 inches, totaling 48.4 pounds).
- Shad made up 355 fish of 8 to 10.5 inches (86.7 pounds), plus 117 of 10.5 to 12 inches (56.8 pounds) and 338 of 1.5 to 3 inches (2.0 pounds).
- Other species included [[Redhorse]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Sauger]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]] (1 fish; the footnote adds 10 Kentucky and 6 smallmouth bass as bass records) and skipjack, drum, killifish and minnows.
