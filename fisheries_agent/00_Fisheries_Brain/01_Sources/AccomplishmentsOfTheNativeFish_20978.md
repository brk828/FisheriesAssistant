# Razorback Sucker Backwater-Rearing Program: Lake Mohave Augmentation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1990 progress report and management/research plan from the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a multiagency task force formed to augment the aging [[Razorback Sucker]] population in [[Lake Mohave]]. The population of about 60,000 adults is old (25 to 45 years) and shows little or no natural recruitment, which the group attributes mainly to predation by non-native fishes. The plan tests whether young suckers can be reared on site in predator-free backwaters and then released as 25 to 30 cm fish. In 1990 the group drafted the plan and permanently isolated [[Yuma Cove]] from the reservoir. Its goal is to release at least 10,000 subadults over five years.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system. It once ranged from Wyoming to Mexico but now survives in remnant populations, including the Green River, the upper Colorado River, and Lake Mohave. The species was proposed for federal listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

- **Stocking history:** [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Department ran a 10-year reintroduction MOU. About 9.5 million larvae and juveniles were released from 1980 to 1990, with little sign of re-establishment.
- **Predation studies:** Marsh and Brooks found that stocked suckers were quickly preyed upon by catfish and other non-native species. They recommended stocking larger fish.
- **Population status:** Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population, estimated at 60,000 individuals. Except for one recent [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] collection, subadults had been absent from Lake Mohave collections for 30 years.
- **Larval loss:** Larvae disappear at 10 to 12 mm. Most scientists agree that [[Non-Native Fish Predation]] is the most important cause of recruitment failure.

Related species named in the plan as candidates for future expansion are the [[Bonytail Chub]], the [[Colorado Squawfish]], and the [[Humpback Chub]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Earlier on-site trials (1984 to 1987):** Researchers used an isolated backwater beside Yuma Cove.

- In 1984, non-native fishes were removed with piscicide, and adults were stocked. Spawning occurred in March 1985, and larvae were abundant until April 1985.
- In April 1985, storm waves washed out the sand and gravel berm, and exotic fishes reinvaded. [[Green Sunfish]] were observed preying on larvae, and stomach analysis confirmed this. Larvae reached more than 16 mm before disappearing within one month.
- A second stocking in January 1986 reproduced. Larvae of 16 to 22 mm were collected by late March 1986. Growth was comparable to hatchery conditions: 10 cm by June 1986 and 18.2 to 20.4 cm by December 1986.
- The berm was breached again in April 1987. Later collections yielded abundant exotic fishes but only one razorback sucker.

The authors conclude that razorback suckers can reproduce and recruit to the juvenile stage in predator-free habitats. They also estimate that growth to 25 to 30 cm, a size largely immune to predation, can take 12 to 18 months.

**Site design:**

- [[Yuma Cove]] is a permanent backwater, partly blocked by a natural sand berm. Its shallow, gravelly substrate makes seining easy. Adults congregate along its wave-washed berm each winter.
- [[Davis Cove]] is a larger, deeper cove. At minimum reservoir elevation it keeps about 10 feet of depth and about 1.5 surface acres, and it is a test site for a temporary flexible-curtain barrier.
- Cage or pen rearing at marinas such as Katherine's Landing, Cottonwood Cove, or Willow Beach could add capacity and provide public outreach.

## Key Findings

**1990 accomplishments**
- The NFWG drafted the [[Management Research Plan]] and permanently isolated Yuma Cove. The berm work, including [[NEPA Compliance]] and permits, took less than 8 months.
- The Bureau of Reclamation estimated the Yuma Cove berm modification at about $14,000 in construction.

**Phase structure**
1. **Phase one:** A 5-year study with four annual stockings. The goal is at least 10,000 subadults of 25 cm or more released. The expected survival to adulthood is less than 20 to 40 percent of backwater-reared fish. The plan also calls for at least 2,000 young fish annually.
2. **Phase two:** Expansion to other basin areas and species, possibly concurrent with phase one.
3. **Phase three:** A formal management agreement to maintain adult razorback suckers at 56,000 to 64,000 in Lake Mohave.

**Methods**
- **Site preparation:** Non-native fish are removed by [[Rotenone Treatment]] (August to September) and [[Antimycin Treatment]] (autumn), after electrofishing and seining for salvage. Yuma Cove is then fertilized.
- **Stocking:** Target stock is 20 females and 60 males. Fish are PIT-tagged. If spawning is not evident by mid-April, about 50,000 larvae from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] would be stocked.
- **Alternate rearing:** An alternate approach alternates the two coves between years and would require deepening Yuma Cove by 5 to 10 feet.
- **Collection:** Seining is ranked first, with a 200-foot bag seine costing about $1,000 to $2,000. Other options are [[Trammel Netting]], fyke and hoop nets, and [[Electrofishing]].
- **Berm breaching:** Fish could leave voluntarily through a channel. At Yuma Cove this would require removing about 150 cubic yards of material. A hoop or trap net would capture egressing fish.

**Tagging**
- The plan selects [[PIT Tagging]] over binary coded wire tags. In Dexter tests, PIT tags gave 100 percent verification and 83 percent survival in razorback suckers 120 mm long.
- Costs: PIT tags run about $4 to $5 each and detectors about $1,777 each. Tagging 1,000 fish with three detectors is estimated at about $10,500.
- A four-to-six person team could tag 1,000 fish in two days. A minimum of 1,000 to 2,000 fish would be tagged per stocking.

**Monitoring**
- Earlier tagging from 1988 to 1990 marked 2,152 suckers. Of these, 116 were new (tagged in the same year as release), and 62 were from previous years, a 2.8 percent return.
- Monitoring of adults is planned for November and March, using a combination of trammel netting, electrofishing, and beach seining.

**Schedule highlights**
- Spawners were scheduled for Yuma Cove in January 1991.
- Davis Cove modification was planned for July to August 1991, at about $15,000.
- Davis Cove stocking and the first reservoir releases were planned for 1991 to 1992.
- The final report was due by the end of 1995.

**Governance and outreach**
- Work is shared among agencies: [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (designs, construction, reporting), [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (tagging, monitoring), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (renovation), and the [[National Park Service]] (NEPA). Arizona State University provided scientific input.
- Participation is voluntary, and a MOU is planned. The partners also plan brochures, public signs, and a video.
