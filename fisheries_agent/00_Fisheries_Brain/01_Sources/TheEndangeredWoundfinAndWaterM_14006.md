# The endangered woundfin and water management in the Virgin River, Utah, Arizona, Nevada

## Executive Summary

This paper by James E. Deacon documents the ecological consequences of water management decisions in the Virgin River basin (Utah, Arizona, Nevada) on the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and associated native fish assemblage. The Virgin River is the last natural habitat for the woundfin, the proposed-endangered [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), and the rare [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*). The study contrasts the abandoned Warner Valley Reservoir project (halted due to jeopardy findings for woundfin and dwarf bear poppy) with the completed [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] project, which was permitted under a non-jeopardy Biological Opinion contingent on specific minimum flow and water-quality conditions. Field data (1977–1986) demonstrate that these flow conditions were violated for extended periods in 1985–1986, coinciding with a catastrophic increase in saline, hot discharge from Pah Tempe Springs after river water disappeared into sinkholes below the diversion structure. This event caused sharp declines in native fish density and reproductive failure across much of the Utah reach in 1985, partial recovery in 1986, and facilitated the invasive expansion of the exotic [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) throughout the Arizona/Nevada reach. The paper argues for continuous, integrated biological monitoring as a precondition for compatible water development and endangered species survival.

## Background & Description

Species-level morphological detail is limited in this water-management-focused paper, but quantitative size data (mean total length, mm, from seine collections) are reported longitudinally across years and river segments for six native taxa plus invasive species:

- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): mean sizes ranged ~36.9–166 mm across sites/years; notably larger mean sizes in 1985 samples above La Verkin/Ash Creek reflect **reproductive failure** (no small recruits) rather than growth.
- **[[Virgin Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*): mean sizes ~24–103.5 mm; largely absent from Beaver Dam Wash/Mesquite Diversion samples (downstream range limit).
- **[[Virgin Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta seminuda*): mean sizes ~54.5–222 mm; larger-bodied member of the assemblage.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): mean sizes ~38.9–142 mm.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Catostomus clarki*): mean sizes ~84.2–671 mm (largest reported value likely reflects small sample/outlier).
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*): mean sizes ~63.2–315 mm.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*, exotic): mean sizes ~36–49.1 mm — small-bodied, prolific invader.
- Other exotics recorded incidentally: mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), yellow bullhead (*Ictalurus natalis*).

An increased incidence of **Asian tapeworm** infection (introduced via red shiner; Heckmann et al. 1987) was documented in woundfin below Quail Creek by October 1986–February 1987, absent in the same population in summer 1985.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Mainstream Virgin River and major tributaries (La Verkin Creek, Ash Creek, Santa Clara River), a warm desert river system draining ~15,000 km² with intermittent stretches, torrential flash-flood hydrology, and saline hot-spring input (Pah Tempe Springs).
- **Water chemistry**: Specific conductance data show baseline (1981–84) ranges of 820–6,600 µmhos/cm across sites, spiking to 1,800–10,900 µmhos/cm in June–December 1985 following the Pah Tempe Springs discharge surge (from ~0.3 m³/sec to 0.85–1.2 m³/sec), then partially recovering by 1986 (1,060–6,500 µmhos/cm) after remediation (sinkholes filled with rock, river course altered).
- **Flow regime dependency**: Woundfin habitat requirements were formally defined by minimum flow thresholds — 2.58 m³/sec (86 cfs) below La Verkin Power Plant and 2.85 m³/sec at Washington Fields Diversion — used as binding conditions in the Quail Creek Reservoir Biological Opinion (USFWS 1982).
- **Community structure**: Native assemblage (woundfin, Virgin spinedace, Virgin roundtail chub, speckled dace, desert sucker, flannelmouth sucker) historically dominated multiple river segments; the exotic [[Red Shiner]] became established in the lower Arizona/Nevada reach ~30 years prior (Cross 1975) but only achieved dominance (56–100% of catch) during the prolonged 1984–86 low-flow, non-flash-flood period.
- **Disturbance ecology**: Flash flooding (defined as ≥10-fold flow increase within 24 hrs) appears to suppress red shiner dominance; its near-total absence from 1984–86 coincided with red shiner's unprecedented spread upstream to Beaver Dam Wash and above.
- **Parasite ecology**: Red shiner is implicated as the vector introducing the Asian tapeworm to the Virgin River system (Heckmann et al. 1987), adding a novel biotic stressor to native fishes already affected by degraded water quality.

## Key Findings

1. **Project comparison**: The abandoned Warner Valley Reservoir (67.8 million m³ capacity, $32M est. cost) was replaced by the smaller Quail Creek Reservoir (49.3 million m³, ~$20M est. cost), which nonetheless was projected to yield more usable water once the associated coal power plant (and its 12.3 million m³/yr demand) was cancelled.
2. **Flow violations**: Minimum flow requirements (2.58 m³/sec below La Verkin Power Plant) were likely violated for 112 days (8 June–7 October 1985) and 108 days (2 June–22 September 1986), based on USGS gage data and irrigation withdrawal rights.
3. **Sinkhole/spring crisis**: In late May 1985, the entire river flow below the Quail Creek diversion vanished into newly formed sinkholes, coinciding with Pah Tempe Springs discharge nearly quadrupling (0.3 → 0.85–1.2 m³/sec), causing basin-wide conductivity spikes and turbidity/temperature shifts.
4. **Population response**: Fish were entirely absent above La Verkin Creek in autumn 1985 (unprecedented in the time series); densities remained below historical norms even after 1986 recovery. No species successfully reproduced in 1985 near Ash/La Verkin Creeks, though Virgin spinedace and roundtail chub recovered reproduction in 1986.
5. **Woundfin decline range-wide**: Woundfin abundance declined since 1983 across more than 60% of its remaining range, with stability only in the ~10 km Quail Creek–to–Washington Diversion segment.
6. **Red shiner dominance**: The exotic red shiner rose from <10% to >90% of catch at Lower Mesquite Diversion (1984–86) and dominated (56–65%) at Beaver Dam Wash for the first time in 30 years, linked to sustained low flow and absence of flash floods.
7. **Parasitism increase**: Tapeworm infection in woundfin below Quail Creek increased markedly by late 1986/early 1987, plausibly linked to red shiner-mediated introduction of Asian tapeworm and/or habitat degradation from reservoir operations.
8. **Water quality**: Salinity hazard in the mainstream river below Pah Tempe Springs, already rated "high" in 1981–82 surveys (Sandberg and Sultz 1985), shifted to "very high" during the 1985 crisis, adversely affecting both fish and irrigated agriculture.
9. **Management implications**: The author emphasizes that (a) endangered species considerations must be integrated into all phases of water project planning, (b) continuous monitoring is essential to detect unanticipated impacts, and (c) legal/negotiated flow standards require precise definition and enforcement to prevent recurring violations.
10. **Socioeconomic framing**: The paper frames fish conservation and agricultural/municipal water needs as interdependent rather than mutually exclusive, arguing that compatible management is possible but requires sustained cooperative planning among stakeholders (farmers, water managers, conservationists).
