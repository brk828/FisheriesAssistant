# Age Determination for Imperiled Razorback Sucker (Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests the age of the remnant [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, and compares several bony structures for age estimation. Of seven structures examined, only sectioned [[Sectioned Otoliths]] (sagittae) gave reliable ages for large adults. Lake Mohave fish were 24 to 44 years old at capture in 1980-83, and most hatched before or during the filling of the reservoir. The authors conclude that the stock shows little or no recruitment and may disappear before the year 2000, following a pattern of extirpation 40 to 50 years after the completion of other mainstream dams.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historically it ranged from the Green River in Wyoming to the Colorado River Delta in Sonora and Baja California, and it was a major food fish for Indigenous peoples and early settlers. Recent records in the lower basin are infrequent and mostly involve large adults. Upstream populations were historically less well documented.

Reservoirs on the lower Colorado were initially colonized in large numbers. Razorback suckers disappeared from [[Gila River]] impoundments in the 1950s, about 40 years after the high dams were built. Mainstream Colorado reservoirs of similar age have also become increasingly rare. A large population persists only in Lake Mohave, the last major reservoir built on the lowermost river.

Earlier work by [[Minckley 1983]] reported large size and presumed great age for the Lake Mohave stock. Females averaged 50 cm and males 45 cm standard length (SL) in 1981. Minckley could not age the fish using scales, but he proposed that the stock originated before or during the construction of [[Davis Dam]] (1942-1951) and the filling of the reservoir in 1954. The present study aimed to evaluate bony structures for age determination, test the hypothesis of substantial age, and describe individual growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sample sources and specimens**

- 84 wild-caught adults were examined. They came from May 1980 and January 1981 collections (55 specimens), [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodfish that died in 1983-84 (9), [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (4), and the California Department of Fish and Game (16).
- 45 known-age progeny from Lake Mohave broodstock were provided by [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and Dexter National Fish Hatchery. They came from the 1974, 1979, 1980, and 1984 year classes and were sacrificed at various times to provide two to six potential annual marks.
- Sex could not be determined for four fish. These were aged but excluded from sex-based analyses.

**Methods overview**

Structures were prepared and read as follows:

- [[Scale Analysis]] (phase contrast light microscopy and scanning electron microscopy)
- [[Pectoral Fin Rays]], sectioned and read by transmitted light
- [[Cleithra]], [[Opercular Bones]], [[Branchiostegal Rays]], and [[Vertebral Centra]], read with direct lighting after boiling and clearing
- [[Sectioned Otoliths]], ground to a mesial section and read by light and scanning electron microscopy

Back-calculation of growth used the [[Back Calculation]] formula. Values are reported as means ± one standard error. Statistical tests were run with SAS.

**Biology and reproduction**

- Annual spawning and larval production occur. Young disappear before reaching more than 18 mm total length (TL).
- No fish under 37 cm SL were caught in annual samples from 1974 to 1981. No fish under 31.3 cm were taken by electrofishing from 1963 to 1969.
- High rates of blindness and tumors also suggested old age.
- Sexual maturity is reached at about two years in males and three years in females under hatchery conditions.
- The species has a protrusible, subterminal mouth and fine gill rakers, which suggests facultative [[Planktivory]]. Rapid growth in filling reservoirs may reflect greater availability of plankton.

## Key Findings

**Structures for aging**

- All seven structures gave annuli that matched known ages in hatchery-reared fish. Ages could be read reliably only in young fish (up to about six years).
- Wild-caught scales were small, eroded, or uniformly spaced, so annuli were obscure. They were deemed "questionable to useless."
- Unsectioned otoliths, opercles, cleithra, branchiostegal rays, and vertebrae were obscured by dense ossification and thickening in large fish. Thickening masked the focus and innermost annuli, which led to underestimated age.
- Sectioned otoliths gave clear annual growth for 83.3% of 84 fish. Unreadable sections resulted from breakage (2.4%), aberrant margins (10.7%), and sectioning off the focus (3.6%).
- Otoliths are composed of [[Vaterite]], a relatively rare crystalline form of calcium carbonate in teleosts.

**Age structure**

- Readable sectioned otoliths were available from 70 wild-caught fish. The greatest apparent age was 44 otolith years, and the text also reports annuli counts of 23 to 43 in the summary.
- Fish were 24 years old at capture in 1980 (youngest in the sample).
- Estimated hatching years ranged from 1937 to 1958. The summary reports that 88.46% hatched before or during the construction of Davis Dam and filling of Lake Mohave. The results section gives 88.6% (62 of 70 fish) for the same comparison.
- Independent evidence supports these ages. A 52.4 cm SL male from the lower Colorado was 22 years old (hatched around 1954), and a 60 cm fish had 17 annuli.

**Growth patterns**

- Otolith radius was strongly correlated with SL at capture (r² = 0.97, log10-transformed data). This allowed back-calculation of growth.
- Females reached 26.7 ± 0.8% of SL at capture in their first year. About 50% of adult SL was attained by the fifth year.
- Absolute size differed by sex, and the difference was significant (Student's t-test, p ≤ 0.05):
  - First annulus: females 140 ± 4 mm, males 103 ± 4 mm SL
  - Fifth annulus: females 276 ± 7 mm, males 214 ± 8 mm SL
- Strong [[Sexual Dimorphism]] appears early and persists for life.
- After the fifth or sixth year, growth slows to a few millimeters per year in both sexes.

**Effect of impoundment**

- Growth to the first annulus was the same before (1942-1951) and after (post-1951) the closure of Davis Dam.
- Growth to the fifth annulus differed significantly (p ≤ 0.05) for both sexes. Males hatched before impoundment reached 180 ± 7 mm, and those hatched after reached 234 ± 11 mm. Females hatched before impoundment reached 256 ± 19 mm, and those hatched after reached 298 ± 10 mm.
- The authors suggest this may reflect enhanced growth under [[Lentic Habitat]] conditions.

**Size and longevity**

- The longest Lake Mohave specimen was an unweighed female of 57.6 cm SL (73.6 cm TL).
- The heaviest Lake Mohave fish was a 4.99 kg female. A 7.26 kg record from Saguaro Lake is considered suspect.
- Maximum age is unknown.

**Recruitment and population outlook**

- The authors find no detectable recruitment since 1958 and little since 1954.
- If the 40 to 50 year extirpation pattern holds, the Lake Mohave population could disappear before the year 2000.
- Reasons for the lack of recruitment remain unknown.

**Management and ongoing research**

- Razorback suckers have been successfully propagated in hatcheries and are being reintroduced into suitable lower-basin habitats.
- Ongoing studies cover [[Predation]] by non-native catfishes, nutritional ecology, movement and habitat selection, and other life history aspects. Their goal is to identify the factors that block successful completion of the life cycle.
