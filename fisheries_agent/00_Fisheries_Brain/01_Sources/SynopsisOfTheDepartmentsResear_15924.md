# Little Colorado River Native Fishes Studies (1991 Program Description)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a cover letter from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) Research Branch to Dave Wegner of [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]], enclosing a program description and a November 4, 1991 progress report on the Native Fishes Studies of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). The program, led by Principal Investigator Robert W. Clarkson, focuses on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes, and is designed to inform management of the river in relation to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. Field work began in late March 1991 with the collection of humpback chub from the LCR for culturing and experimental rearing, and intensive monitoring of adult chub populations ran from May through July. The report states that most data were still being analyzed, so results are preliminary. The notable findings are a later-than-usual humpback chub spawning season that nevertheless produced young, evidence of a possible second sucker reproductive event, and a zonation pattern that appears to limit chub use of parts of the river.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to gather behavioral and ecological information on the humpback chub and other native species to support long-term monitoring and conservation. The program had two principal goals:

- Describing the LCR's ongoing ecological characteristics, and how the system functions as habitat for native fishes.
- Establishing a long-term monitoring program for adult chub populations, including catch rates, movements, and survival.

Specific objectives addressed:

1. Seasonal, spatial, and temporal patterns of catch, movement, and habitat use of adult and sub-adult native fishes.
2. Behaviors, habitat use, and growth rates of young-of-the-year and post-larval fish.
3. Spawning initiation and conditions, linked to hydrologic, thermal, and chemical data from the LCR.

Sampling involved two camps: one at the LCR mouth (lower 1200 m of river) and one near [[Salt Trail Canyon]], about 10.5 km upstream. Both were occupied continuously by rotating crews. Partners included the [[Navajo Nation]], the [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) team, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and AGFD, with the Native Fishes facility intended as the official repository for data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The LCR is a tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in the Grand Canyon region. The river's native fish community is shaped by its hydrology and water chemistry, and by the flow regime of the upstream [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The program measured physico-chemical variables, including temperature, alkalinity, nitrates, and carbon dioxide, along with turbidity and current velocity, to relate them to fish distribution.

Key ecological themes in the document:

- **Zonation:** The LCR shows distinct spatial zonation, which appears strong enough to restrict humpback chub use of certain reaches.
- **Turbidity:** Speckled dace were more abundant in an upstream section, which the authors suggest may reflect lower turbidity there.
- **Allochthonous inputs:** Studies were planned to examine patterns of allochthonous (externally derived) food inputs and larval contributions to the river's food web.
- **Downstream connectivity:** Physical-chemical and biological data were to be collected along the river from Blue Springs to the mouth, including algal and invertebrate communities.

## Key Findings

### Sampling Effort and Methods

- **Adult chub monitoring:** Began May 7 near the LCR mouth and continued through July 1. Monitoring near Salt Trail Canyon started in mid-May.
- **Hoop netting:** Spring monitoring used data from 6 and 13 hoop net study areas, respectively, in the two camp areas.
- **Larval and young-of-the-year sampling:** Dip nets, seines, and drift nets were used, with drift sampling providing the method for estimating egg and larval fish frequencies.
- **Longitudinal surveys:** From August, a 3-4 day kayak/canoe survey of the LCR from Blue Springs to the mouth was conducted, with two inflatable surveys (one in June and one in October).
- **Diet and age analysis:** Fish diet analyses and [[Otolith Ageing]] studies were planned, with samples collected every other day from both camps.

### Humpback Chub Reproduction

- The humpback chub spawning period appeared to occur later than usual in 1991, but spawning appeared successful in producing young up to the time of reporting.
- Post-larval humpback chub were found to use the water column for feeding and showed differences in behavior from sucker post-larvae (foraging, swimming, and agonistic percentages).
- At night, post-larval humpback chub shifted toward deeper areas with cover, but during daylight they used a broader range of available habitats.

### Other Native and Non-Native Species

- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*, OCR-garbled in source): larval and post-larval individuals were collected in October, which likely indicates a second reproductive event for the year.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): appeared in 1991 spawning efforts. The bracketed scientific name in the source is garbled by scanning and appears to refer to a sucker (possibly *Pantosteus discobolus*); this should be verified.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): no suspected individuals were observed during sampling to that date.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): larval individuals were collected from the lower LCR.
- A species garbled in the scan (likely plains killifish) reappeared in samples after an earlier absence.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*) and **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*): rare in hoop net catches. Carp were observed spawning near the lower LCR camp, and both remained susceptible to capture by sporadic angling.

### Management and Logistical Concerns

- Expected ASU staff did not take over young-of-the-year monitoring at the Salt Trail camp until after July 1, so some labor-intensive studies started late.
- ASU has since been fully funded, and the authors do not expect this problem in future years.
- Additional personnel were planned for the spring of 1992 to allow simultaneous handling of both types of studies at the lower camp.
- Start-up problems experienced in 1991 were reported as resolved, with smoother data acquisition expected in 1992.

### Status of Results

- The bulk of the data was still being analyzed at the time of writing. The author explicitly hesitated to present more than preliminary results, which remain subject to revision.
