# Larval razorback sucker response to artificial diets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Tyus and Severson (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Vernal, Utah) tested whether larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) can be reared from sac-fry to 40 days old on commercially available dry [[Artificial Diets]] instead of pond-cultured natural foods. A total of 600 fish were fed five [[Fry Diets]] in triplicate lots of 40 fry. All diets supported survival to 40 days, but weight gain and survival differed significantly among them. The authors concluded that razorback sucker larvae can be reared successfully on commercial diets as first foods, and recommended feeding the diet that gives maximum survival for the first 40 days.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endemic fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its populations are small and recruitment is absent or severely curtailed, so it is at risk of extinction. The species is protected by State statutes and is a candidate for federal listing by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], although it was not protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] at the time of the study.

Natural reproduction occurs in the upper Colorado River basin, but recruitment is poor. A small experimental hatchery was built at the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] in 1987 to help augment the population and test culture techniques under a multi-agency [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. Earlier work at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] reared larvae in ponds with natural plankton. Larvae fed on rotifers and chironomids (under 0.1 mm) as first foods, then on organisms about 0.3 mm wide once they exceeded about 18 mm total length (Papoulias 1988). Published information on rearing larvae in tanks on artificial foods was scant, and no comparison of commercial fry diets had been published. Dry diets could reduce the cost of pond construction and maintenance.

The authors' hypothesis was that the diet with the smallest particle size would give the best weight gain and survival during the first 40 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Source of eggs and milt:** Wild fish from the [[Green River]] near Jensen, Utah, collected May 31, 1988. Eggs were fertilized with sperm from five males.
- **Incubation:** Eggs were held in Heath incubators at 20 °C in flowing water. Hatching began at 112 hours, and all fry had emerged by 145 hours.
- **Pre-trial holding:** Sac-fry were held for 4 days in a 76 L aluminum tank with heated, degassed, aerated single-pass well water (flow 18.95 L/min; mean temperature 21.9 °C; dissolved oxygen 5.4 mg/L; total hardness 440 mg/L).
- **Trial conditions:** On day 5, 40 fry were placed in each of 15 breeder baskets (2.5 L, nylon-mesh with plastic frame) suspended in 29.1 L compartments. Water was supplied at 1.90 L/min with a mean temperature of 20.3 °C, dissolved oxygen of 6.1 mg/L, pH 7.4 (as printed in the source), and total hardness of 440 mg/L. Water exchanged only through the basket bottom and sides.
- **Feeding regime:** Fish were hand-fed 5% of body weight, eight times daily for five days and six times daily on weekends. The ration was not adjusted for growth during the 40-day trial.
- **Design:** Completely randomized design, with triplicate lots per diet.

### Diets Tested

The five commercial diets, all with similar protein levels, were:
- [[AP-100 Diet]] (Zeigler Bros., Gardners, PA)
- [[LIV Diet]] (Farm and Wildlife Products, Omaha, NE)
- [[4200 Diet]] (Bio-Marine, Hawthorne, CA)
- [[A-250 Diet]] and [[B-250 Diet]] (BioKyowa, St. Louis, MO)

Diets were analyzed for [[Proximate Analysis]] following AOAC (1984) methods. [[Particle Size]] was determined by sieving 1.0 g subsamples through nylon mesh of 100, 150, and 250 µm.

## Key Findings

### Survival

- Survival at 40 days ranged from 20% to 78.3% among diets (mean values).
- [[LIV Diet]] and [[B-250 Diet]] gave the highest survival (both 78.3%, with ranges of 65–90% and 75–85%).
- [[AP-100 Diet]] was intermediate (59.2%).
- [[A-250 Diet]] (31.7%) and [[4200 Diet]] (20.0%) gave the lowest survival (ANOVA; P<0.05).
- Survival was inversely related to weight gain across all diets combined, but this was not evident within diets (ANCOVA; P>0.4). Fish density at 40 days did not affect weight gain.

### Growth

- Mean weight gain ranged from 0.026 g to 0.092 g (initial mean weight 0.005 g).
- [[A-250 Diet]] produced the heaviest fish (0.092 g).
- [[4200 Diet]] (0.066 g) did not differ significantly from [[A-250 Diet]].
- [[AP-100 Diet]] produced 0.036 g.
- Among the two highest-survival diets, [[B-250 Diet]] produced 0.031 g and [[LIV Diet]] produced 0.026 g. These two did not differ significantly from [[AP-100 Diet]].
- Total length followed weight gain. The largest fry (A-250; range 18.3–26.3 mm, median 22.5 mm) and smallest fry (LIV; range 12.3–18.0 mm, median 15.2 mm) were at the extremes, with other diets in between.
- Across all treatments, total length ranged from 12.3 to 26.3 mm (median 19.3 mm).

### Diet Composition and Particle Size

- Fat varied widely (6.4–30.2%), while protein was similar (49.8–57.0%).
- Combined fat and protein was highest in [[LIV Diet]] (80.8%), followed by [[A-250 Diet]] (71.9%) and [[B-250 Diet]] (71.6%).
- Particle size differed greatly among diets:
  - [[LIV Diet]]: 100% under 100 µm.
  - [[A-250 Diet]]: 83.2% over 250 µm.
  - [[4200 Diet]]: 75.8% in the 150–250 µm range.
- Particle size had no detectable effect on survival (r=0.45; P>0.1) or growth (r=0.42; P>0.1). The authors note that particle size was not measured in water, which could matter.
- The LIV and B-250 diets had similar fine, flour-like textures, and the authors suggest palatability may explain their high survival.

### Comparison with Live Food

- Growth on dry diets was comparable to growth on live brine shrimp (*Artemia salina*) nauplii reported by Papoulias (1988). Papoulias reported largest surviving larvae of 25.0±0.6 mm and 23.8±0.6 mm TL on 500–1000 nauplii/L for 50 days, and 13.6–16.1 mm TL at 5–50 nauplii/L.
- The authors judged their ration to be more food-limited, yet it compared favorably.

### Post-Trial Performance

- Surviving fish were moved to a 76 L tank and fed [[AP-100 Diet]] at 5% of total weight via an automatic feeder.
- Survivorship over the following 100 days was 68%. Some size variation was large, with some individuals two to three times the size of others.
- Switching to Silver Cup trout starter at about 140 days produced no mortality over a further 114 days.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- All five diets supported larvae to 40 days, showing that razorback sucker can be reared on commercial diets as first foods.
- Foods giving the highest survival should be used for the first 40 days.
- Fish should then be switched to a diet offering a variety of particle sizes, because same-age larvae vary widely in length.
- The authors caution that they cannot attribute later performance to [[AP-100 Diet]] alone. Some mortality may have resulted from earlier feeding on other diets.
- Further work should test palatability, nutrition, particle size in water, and survival with these and similar diets.

### Context from Mortality Literature

Papoulias (1988) reported 10% mortality when larvae were not fed by day 11 and 86.7% when feeding was delayed until day 27. The authors assume the fish in this study were accepting feed, though performance was poor in some cases.
