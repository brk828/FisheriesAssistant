# Sampling Problems in Creel Census

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Kenneth D. Carlander, Charles J. DiCostanzo, and Raymond J. Jessen of [[Iowa State College]], summarizes discussions from a symposium on creel-census sampling held there on March 19, 1956, attended by about fifty fishery biologists. The authors argue that most [[Creel Census]] programs are poorly planned and yield data of limited value. They classify census types, critique common methods, and recommend that creel censuses use controlled statistical sampling so that reliability can be measured and biases avoided. A short closing section adds a note on [[Rainbow Trout]] nutrition and a gear-combination method for sampling [[Flathead Catfish]].

## Background & Description

Annual spending on creel censuses in the United States runs into the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Census data are used to estimate harvest, describe the type and quality of fishing, track changes in fish populations and fishing success, and evaluate management actions.

The authors classify creel censuses by who makes the report (fishermen versus biologists or census clerks) and by the nature and extent of coverage (complete, indiscriminate, special selection, or statistically selected). The resulting categories are:

- **A. Complete, fisherman-reported:** mandatory catch reports, such as those used in [[California]] for boat owners and captains.
- **B. Complete, biologist-contacted:** feasible only where access points are few and every angler checks in and out. No sampling error arises here, but the approach allows study of sampling designs.
- **C. Voluntary fisherman reports:** cards placed in mailboxes or boxes along a water body. Inexpensive, but generally unsatisfactory.
- **D. Selected fishermen keeping diaries:** respondents tend to be above-average anglers, so data are rarely representative, although they can be cheap and comparable across years.
- **E. Statistically selected mail or interview surveys:** California has mailed postcards to a randomized sample of license holders annually since 1936. A 1948 check found little or no nonresponse bias.
- **F. Indiscriminate on-site contacts:** the most common method, usually without a sampling plan, often concentrated where and when fishing is heaviest.
- **G. Fixed-site or fixed-time contacts:** useful for year-to-year comparison but representative only of that site or period.
- **H. Statistically selected on-site contacts:** the approach the authors recommend.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fishing pressure and success vary by hour of day, day of week, week in season, geography, and fisher type, so the sampling design must account for these sources of variation. Two concrete examples appear in the text:

- **Seasonal truncation:** the April catch could be 60 percent of the year's harvest in an early-spring year but only 20 percent in a late-spring year. Full-season sampling avoids this problem.
- **Weather and season effects:** results from a partial season cannot be assumed to apply to the full year.

Turbidity and cover also affected the Des Moines River survey, where fish escaped electrofishing fields and large fish sheltered in deep holes and brush piles.

## Key Findings

**Core recommendations and conclusions**

- Creel censuses should be designed for each water body, with sampling schemes matched to its fishery. Different segments of a fishery may require different methods.
- Statistical sampling is needed to measure data reliability and avoid bias. It need not cost more than indiscriminate sampling and may cost less, since unplanned samples are often larger than necessary.
- Controlled sampling requires planning expertise. The authors recommend consulting a statistician with survey-design experience.
- Stratified random sampling can improve accuracy or lower costs.
- Latin-square designs can balance time of day, day of week, and week in season. The example given is a 14-hour day, 7-week season, and 2-hour time segments, which yields 7 periods per day, 7 days per week, and 7 weeks.

**Estimating fishing pressure**

- Total fisherman-hours can be estimated by counting fishermen at randomly chosen scheduled times and multiplying by the total hours in the period.
- Pressure can also be estimated as fisherman-trips multiplied by average hours per trip. Trip counts may be derived from car counters on access roads, combined with stratified checks of fishermen per car.
- In a Lake Mendota winter study, [[Parker]] (1956) found a flat daily distribution peaking between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m. and counted fishermen only in that window. The authors warn that this approach is biased if the pattern shifts.

**Estimating catch per effort and harvest**

- Catch per unit effort should be computed as total catch divided by total fishing time, not as the mean of individual ratios, unless catch and effort are uncorrelated.
- Contacting anglers during fishing (incomplete trips) avoids the bias of sampling only completed trips at major access points. In [[Michigan]] (Schafer 1956), completed-trip data differed significantly from incomplete-trip data. At Clear Lake, Iowa, the difference was significant when all data were included but not when incomplete trips were compared with the same trips at completion.
- Anglers' time estimates can be unreliable and often include time spent on shore. In a Minnesota study (Johnson 1956), individual estimates varied widely, but the average for about 1,700 trips was close to the observed time on the lake.
- Direct harvest counts are possible at limited access points, as on Yellowstone Lake (Moore, Cope, and Beckwith 1952), and may give a better catch-per-hour estimate than interviews.
- Independent checks include boat-livery records, tagged fish, and parallel estimates of pressure or harvest.

**Gear combination for flathead catfish**

Where the electric shocker failed on the Des Moines River, R. Jess Muncy combined it with a trammel net placed around the sampled hole:

- Electric shocker: 0.75 kVA, 110-volt AC. Turbid water hid stunned fish, and many fish escaped the field. Large fish taken in hoop nets were not captured.
- Trammel net: 200 by 8 feet, 20-inch outer mesh, 3.5-inch inner mesh, held by iron stakes.
- Result: 21 flathead catfish (13.3 to 38.9 inches total length) were taken in 7 operations. Months of hoop-netting in the same area had yielded only 110 flatheads of comparable size.
- Limitations: the method did not work well in very deep holes, and currents and debris made net maintenance difficult.

**Other notes**

- Rainbow trout fed a long-term riboflavin-deficient diet at the Eastern Fish Nutrition Laboratory in Cortland, New York, developed an eye lesion typical of riboflavin deficiency in salmon and lake trout. This finding is unrelated to the creel-census topic.
