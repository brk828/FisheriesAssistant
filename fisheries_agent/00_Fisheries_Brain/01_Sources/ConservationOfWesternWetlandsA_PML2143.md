# Conservation of Western Wetlands and Their Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reviews the loss of western wetlands and the fishes that depend on them, with emphasis on the desert Southwest. It argues that wetland and fish losses since World War II have been severe, that human water demand will keep increasing, and that conservation depends on sustained effort and public recognition of wetlands' value. The author describes the history of desert fish conservation, including the founding of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], the legal protection secured by the [[Cappaert Decision]], and the development of the [[California Natural Diversity Data Base]] and the [[Interagency Natural Areas Coordinating Committee]]. The paper combines a technical review of species status with a philosophical argument drawn from [[Aldo Leopold]].

## Background & Description

The document is a symposium-style paper, probably derived from a Southwest desert-fishes meeting, though the exact source is not clear from the OCR. Its stated aim is to add philosophical background and a modest technical base to earlier contributions, and to present the relationship between western wetlands, their habitats and their fishes, then discuss the destruction of those systems and the conservation successes achieved.

The fishes discussed belong to three families:

- **Cyprinodontidae** (pupfishes and relatives)
- **Cyprinidae** (minnows and chubs)
- **Poeciliidae** (livebearers)

Salmonidae (trout) and Centrarchidae (sunfish) are also covered. Key taxa include:

- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): restricted to a single pool in Death Valley National Monument.
- [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*): nearly went extinct around 1969 (the OCR gives "L3L;9", likely 1969).
- [[Pahrump Poolfish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*): the entire remaining population was described as limited to one spring-fed site in Pahrump Valley.
- [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*): once abundant, now endangered and found only in tributaries of the Gila and Verde rivers. Its decline involved habitat change, competition from introduced species and hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]].
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): an early-described Colorado River basin fish, listed as endangered in 1973 and found in only a few springs and cienegas.
- [[Thick-Tail Chub]] (*Gila crassicauda*): extirpated across most of its native range by agricultural and urban development combined with introduced species.
- [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*): California's only native centrarchid, eliminated from much of its range.

Fish names are used with the author's wording where the OCR is clear. Where the OCR is garbled, scientific names have been reconstructed conservatively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Wetland loss:**
- California has lost more than 90 percent of its historic wetlands (the OCR reads "91 percent"), with about 96 percent lost in the Central Valley.
- The coastal zone has lost more than 70 percent of its marshlands.
- Greatest fish losses have occurred in the desert areas of the Southwest.

**Drivers of decline:** The paper identifies three main factors threatening Southwestern aquatic ecosystems:
1. Large and growing human populations.
2. Intensive resource exploitation of desert and mountain aquatic resources.
3. Very low precipitation.

Together these factors are said to have caused the extinction of several species and the endangerment of many others. Habitat disruption from agriculture and urban development is the most widespread threat. Dams, water diversion and especially the introduction of non-native species that compete with or prey on native fishes are also described as major problems. The author notes that about 50 competing species had been introduced into the Colorado River system (Pister 1981).

**Desert fish status:**
- Of 20 native fishes of the California desert (as summarized in the paper), 7 are extinct, 6 are endangered, and 7 are not presently considered endangered or threatened.
- Roughly one-third of desert fishes are restricted to springs and creeks in the Southwest and would not exist if those habitats were lost.
- Two desert fish species are as yet undescribed, which the author cites as evidence of the limits of current knowledge.

**Regional examples:**
- In the Virgin River system, Nevada desert fishes (including the Virgin River chub and Virgin River speckled dace) are endangered by habitat change, competition and predation from introduced species.
- Ash Meadows, Nevada, which holds the Devils Hole pupfish, is described as a biologically rich area with many endemic plants, invertebrates and fishes. Several of its species are listed as endangered and others are considered extinct. A recovery plan for Ash Meadows was in development.

**Conservation infrastructure:**
- The [[California Natural Diversity Data Base]] stores records for many plant and animal occurrences (about 16,000 locations, as read from the OCR text).
- The Natural Areas Program prioritizes sites using criteria such as rarity, threat level and number of associated rare taxa or communities.
- Protection tools include land acquisition, conservation easements, habitat restoration (such as planting cottonwoods for the Willow Flycatcher) and agency cooperative agreements.

## Key Findings

1. **Historic wetland loss is severe.** California has lost over 90 percent of its wetlands, and its coastal marshes have lost over 70 percent. Desert fish losses are greatest in the Southwest.
2. **Desert fish fauna is highly vulnerable.** Of 20 California desert fishes summarized, 7 are extinct and 6 are endangered. About one-third of desert fishes are spring- or creek-restricted.
3. **Introduced species are a major cause of decline.** Competition, predation and hybridization from non-native fishes (50+ introduced competitors in the Colorado system) have reduced many native populations.
4. **Legal protection has been critical.** The [[Cappaert Decision]] (1976) protected the Devils Hole pupfish and established a precedent for protecting endangered species' water sources. The author describes it as a turning point in the Southwest.
5. **The Owens pupfish came close to extinction.** Around 1969 it nearly went extinct, and it has since been managed under federal and state protection.
6. **Hatchery programs show promise.** The Mexican Fish Hatchery is described as maintaining a genetically pure stock of endangered fishes for reintroduction into rehabilitated habitat.
7. **Institutional cooperation is essential.** The Interagency Natural Areas Coordinating Committee (INACC) and the state Natural Areas Program coordinate agencies, universities and private groups. The author judges that such cooperation holds great promise.
8. **Public awareness and ethics underpin conservation.** Drawing on [[Aldo Leopold]]'s land ethic, the author argues that wetlands and their fishes must be valued both for their ecological role and for their intrinsic worth. Sustained public support is necessary for protection to survive political pressures and future water demand.
