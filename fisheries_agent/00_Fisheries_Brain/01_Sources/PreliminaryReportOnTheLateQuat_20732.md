# Late Quaternary Geology of the San Pedro Valley, Arizona

#quaternary-geology #paleolimnology #lacustrine-deposits

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report by C. Vance Haynes (University of Arizona) reconstructs the late Quaternary stratigraphy of the upper [[San Pedro River]] valley in southeastern Arizona. It focuses on the [[Murray Springs Site]] and the surrounding lacustrine and alluvial units, and tests whether a large late Pleistocene lake once occupied the valley. The document contains no fish data. Its aquatic content is limited to lake clays, marls, molluscs, ostracods and pollen-bearing deposits, which are used to infer past standing water. The main working hypothesis is that Boquillas-formation lake beds accumulated between roughly 30,000 and 12,000 radiocarbon years ago, and that the valley was tilted or faulted to allow a single lake. The author presents this as a hypothesis, not a proven conclusion.

## Background & Description

The San Pedro flows north for about 140 miles from its head near Cananea, Mexico, to its junction with the [[Gila River]] at Winkelman, Arizona. Its valley fill of interbedded alluvial and lacustrine sediments lies against bedrock and contains Pliocene–Pleistocene vertebrate fossils. Earlier work by Gidley, Gazin, Heindl, Gray, Agenbroad and Lance established mammalian ages (Hemphillian, Blancan, Irvingtonian) for the older fill. Rancholabrean faunas and Early Man sites were found mainly in the upper valley over the past 15 years.

Geomorphic surfaces were defined by Bryan as the [[Tombstone Surface]], [[Whetstone Surface]] and [[Arivaipa Surface]], from oldest (highest) to youngest (lowest). The Tombstone surface slopes about 80 feet per mile from the Huachuca Mountains toward the river. Arroyo cutting along the main river began in 1883 and reached the Murray Springs tributary soon after 1900, exposing the Early Man sites. Before this incision, the tributaries were grassy swales without distinct channels.

The [[Murray Springs Site]] stratigraphy is the most complete sequence in the valley. Its units are informally named pending formal naming. The report also compiles 43 Quaternary localities (Table 1), including Carbon-14 ages, cultural complexes and extinct mammal occurrences. Table 2 describes the Murray Springs units by colour, texture, soil structure and thickness.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lacustrine and aquatic evidence.** Boquillas-formation clays and marls record standing water. Lower clay contains disarticulated mammoth, horse and bison remains. Upper marl contains locally abundant ostracod and snail shells. The author notes that these molluscs may indicate water depth and chemistry, though no such analysis is reported. Organic black mat deposits and marl facies are interpreted as evidence of ponds between roughly 10,000 and 11,000 years ago.

**Faunal and floral context.** The [[Lehner Site]] and Murray Springs yielded mammoth, horse, camel, bison and wolf remains. Pollen from the Lehner site and [[Murray Springs Site]] sediments was used for environmental reconstruction. The fauna is terrestrial. No fish remains are described.

**Climate and erosion.** The report links erosion and deposition cycles to climatic episodes, including the [[Fairbank Drought]] (dated 2463 ± 310 B.P.) and a severe erosional period between about 8,000 and 6,000 years ago. The "Fairbank drought" is defined from an eroded buried soil containing San Pedro artifacts.

**Tectonics.** The region is still tectonically active. The 1887 [[Great Sonoran Earthquake]] produced 26-foot-high scarps about 60 miles south of the international border and was felt as far north as Phoenix.

## Key Findings

- **Stratigraphy at Murray Springs.** The sequence includes the Boquillas formation (lower clay unit D, upper marl unit E), the [[Lehner Formation]] (Graveyard, Clanton, Donnet and Wiek members), the [[Escapule Formation]] (Fry, McCool and Bakarich members) and the [[Horsethief Formation]], the last deposit before modern arroyo cutting.
- **Clovis association.** Clovis hunters killed and butchered mammoths about 11,200 radiocarbon years ago. The Clovis artifacts lie within the Graveyard channel deposit, with the Clanton black mat dated at about 10,800 years ago. The Wiek member contains Cochise artifacts.
- **Dated lacustrine phase.** Lower Boquillas clay was deposited about 30,000 radiocarbon years ago. Upper Boquillas marl accumulated between 12,000 and 22,000 years ago. Lake lowering or drainage occurred between 11,500 and 12,000 years ago.
- **Regional distribution.** Lacustrine Boquillas and Lehner units occur at the [[Lehner Site]], the [[Escapule Site]], [[Schaldack Site]], Bensen (about 3,680 ft), Cerros Negros (70 miles north, top at about 3,085 ft, deposited as late as 12,000 years ago) and [[Peck Spring]] (66 miles north). Eight further localities were found in 1967. The author states that their sediment sequence and chronology are essentially identical.
- **Paleolake hypothesis.** Geomorphic evidence includes benches and scarps on the Whetstone surface (between 4,100 and 4,200 ft), possible strand lines near the Charleston Hills and Lewis Hill, and carbonate-coated gravels resembling Wilcox playa beach gravels. The author describes these as supportive but not definitive.
- **Gemini IV imagery.** White zones on Gemini IV photograph 4-8-1 correspond to the Whetstone surface and the lacustrine outcrops. Part of the whiteness may reflect exposed carbonate beds rather than lake calcification.
- **Tilt requirement.** Outcrop elevations decline downstream. A single lake requires about 11 minutes of arc of tilt, decreasing to about 1 minute between Murray Springs and the Lehner site. The tilt would have occurred between 12,000 and 9,500 years ago. The report notes that this is later than most investigators would accept for significant tectonic activity in the Gila drainage.
- **Dam sites.** Candidate damsites include the Charleston Hills, The Narrows near Tres Alamos Wash and Redington. Lacustrine beds upstream of these sites lie up to 100 feet above any possible dam without invoking tilting or uplift.
- **Alternative explanation.** Local ponds along a valley of lower grade than the modern river could explain the deposits, though this would require too many dams and near-identical sediments in each pond.
- **Regional context.** Melton (1965) notes that valleys north and east of an arc across Arizona contain Plio-Pleistocene lake beds dissected in late Pleistocene time, while valleys to the south and west do not. The San Pedro may be anomalous in showing late Quaternary aggradation.
- **Surface ages.** The [[Arivaipa Surface]] is less than 1,000 radiocarbon years old. Sediments between about 8,000 and 30,000 years old are absent from the inner-valley fill beneath it.
- **Conclusion.** Lacustrine deposition between roughly 30,000 and 12,000 B.P. appears to have excluded significant alluvium, which suggests a former large lake. The author states that the geomorphic features support, but do not prove, this hypothesis. Further mapping is needed.
