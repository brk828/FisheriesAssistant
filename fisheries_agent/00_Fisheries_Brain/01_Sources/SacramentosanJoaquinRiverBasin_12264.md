# Sacramento-San Joaquin River Basin Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1997 report, prepared by Sue McClurg of the Water Education Foundation for the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]], examines water management in the Sacramento-San Joaquin River Basin of California as one of six Western river basins studied. It focuses on five federally influenced resource issues: environmental restoration, water supply and flood management, maintenance of federal water facilities, nonpoint source pollution, and the [[Bay-Delta]]. The report describes the basin's large-scale water infrastructure, the main federal and state agencies and stakeholder groups, and recent efforts such as the [[Central Valley Project Improvement Act]] (CVPIA), the [[CALFED Bay-Delta Program]], and watershed initiatives. Its central argument is that federal environmental laws have acted as a "hammer" driving cooperation, while gaps in scientific data, agency fragmentation, and budget rigidity constrain progress. Recommendations favor ecosystem management, flexible financing, continuation of CALFED, and voluntary, watershed-scale approaches.

## Background & Description

The Central Valley is a large interior valley bordered by the Cascade and Sierra Nevada ranges to the east and the Coast Range to the west. It is divided into the Sacramento and San Joaquin valleys, which are defined by their major rivers.

- **Sacramento River Basin:** about 59,000 square miles, with a river roughly 384 miles long. It yields about 35 percent of the state's developed water supply, with an average annual yield of 22 million acre-feet, of which about 5 million acre-feet are diverted upstream of the Delta.
- **San Joaquin River Basin:** about 32,000 square miles, with a river of about 290 miles and an average flow of only 3 million acre-feet. The basin is described as one of California's most developed and contested waterways.
- **Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta:** a 1,153-square-mile maze of channels and islands that forms the largest West Coast estuary together with San Francisco Bay. The Delta houses the export pumps of the [[State Water Project]] (SWP) and the [[Central Valley Project]] (CVP), and is described as the "heart" of California's water system.

Key infrastructure includes Shasta, Oroville, Folsom, Friant, New Melones and New Don Pedro dams. The basin contains roughly 7,000 water diverters. The report notes a Mediterranean climate with strong precipitation gradients and reliance on Sierra snowpack for summer supply.

The report identifies the main federal players, including the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Environmental Protection Agency]], and the [[U.S. Geological Survey]]. State agencies include the [[California Department of Water Resources]], the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], and the [[State Water Resources Control Board]]. About 13 stakeholder groups are profiled, spanning urban and agricultural water agencies and environmental organizations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes profound habitat loss and species decline. Over the past 100 years, 90 percent of the Central Valley's original seasonal wetlands were lost; the estimated 4 million acres of marshland in the 1850s has shrunk to about 500,000 acres. Hydraulic mining, overfishing, logging, railroad construction, dams, introduced species, high water temperatures and pollution have all been linked to the decline of native salmon and steelhead.

Key species mentioned:

- **Winter-run [[Chinook Salmon]]:** listed as federally threatened and state endangered in 1989, later reclassified as federally endangered.
- **Spring-run Chinook salmon:** Deer Creek fell from an estimated 21,000 adults in 1982 to 1,300 in 1993. Butte Creek supported 4,000 adults in the 1960s (maximum 20,000 in 1960), with 14 returning in 1987, 400 in 1993, 7,500 in 1995 and 1,400 in 1996.
- **[[Delta Smelt]]:** described as threatened.
- **Fall-run Chinook:** introduced to the San Joaquin, which now is managed for this race after the spring-run was extirpated following Friant Dam.

The Delta lies on the Pacific Flyway, providing wintering habitat for large numbers of ducks and geese, and supports roughly 25 percent of the state's warm water and sport fish. Water quality issues are central: TDS in the San Joaquin at Vernalis ranges from 110 to 900 mg/L, compared with 50 to 150 mg/L on the Sacramento at Greene's Landing, against a drinking water maximum of 500 mg/L. Pesticide, selenium and sediment contamination are key concerns. The West Stanislaus study area is estimated to contribute 1.2 million tons of sediment per year, with fish tissue showing DDT up to 2.2 mg/kg wet weight, above the 1.0 mg/kg guideline cited for wildlife protection.

## Key Findings

**Environmental restoration**
- Federal laws (ESA, CWA, CVPIA, NEPA) have been described as a "hammer" that drove cooperation, including the 1994 [[Bay-Delta Accord]].
- The CVPIA dedicates up to 800,000 acre-feet of CVP yield (600,000 in dry years) to environmental uses, but its ambiguous language has caused disputes over implementation and over whether the accord's 400,000 acre-feet counts toward this total.
- The report identifies a basic scientific gap: there is no agreed ecosystem baseline, and no study has covered the entire ecosystem. The [[Interagency Ecological Program]] (IEP) has run for about 30 years with a 1996 budget of $12 million and 60 program elements.
- Adaptive management is endorsed as a way to redirect projects based on new information, though the report raises unresolved questions about triggers and decision-makers.
- Red Bluff Diversion Dam fish passage research has suffered mechanical setbacks, including Archimedes screw pumps cracking after about 1,000 hours, and lost one to two years.
- Stakeholder-led successes on [[Deer Creek]] and [[Butte Creek]] illustrate voluntary restoration, including dam removals and fish passage projects funded jointly by CVPIA, Category III and local users.

**Water supply and flood management**
- DWR projected a shortfall of 3.7 million acre-feet in normal years to 9 million acre-feet in drought years by 2020, with population expected to reach 40 million by 2006.
- The 1987-1993 drought caused at least $1.1 billion in damages. The 1997 floods killed eight people, evacuated 120,000 and caused about $2 billion in damage.
- USBR's least-cost yield plan estimated potential yields of 1.2 million acre-feet from land fallowing, 910,000 from conjunctive use, 445,000 from conservation, 200,000 from reuse, 180,000 from local purchases, and 30,000 from enlarged Farmington storage.
- [[Sites Reservoir]] (proposed 1.2, 1.8 or 3 million acre-feet) and the [[Galloway Report]] on floodplain management are central to future planning.

**Maintenance of federal facilities**
- The CVP's main features were built between 1937 and 1951. The 1995 failure of gate No. 3 at [[Folsom Dam]] released about 400,000 acre-feet; a later forensic team blamed design failure, and USBR spent $12.5 million on repairs.
- A new maintenance model transfers operation and maintenance to users: the Tehama-Colusa Canal Authority agreed in October 1996 to a 25-year arrangement, with estimated annual savings of $12 million if similar agreements are reached.
- Backlogged maintenance was estimated at $13 million to $60 million, depending on the source.

**Nonpoint source pollution**
- EPA identifies nonpoint source pollution as the nation's leading remaining water pollution cause.
- The Sacramento River violates standards for pesticides, copper and mercury, with ambient toxicity detected in more than 50 percent of monitoring tests.
- The West Stanislaus HUA achieved 90 to 95 percent soil loss reductions in trials with polyacrylamide, and as of 1996 had treated about 24 percent (30,568 acres) of its area.

**Bay-Delta and governance**
- The December 1994 Bay-Delta Accord set standards that increase outflow by 400,000 acre-feet in normal years, adopted by the SWRCB in May 1995.
- [[CALFED]] was created in June 1994 with three goals: water quality standards, coordinated CVP/SWP operations, and a long-term Delta solution. The physical fix is estimated to cost $4 billion to $8 billion.
- [[Proposition 204]], a $995 million water bond approved 63 to 37 percent in November 1996, includes $193 million for immediate Delta improvements and $390 million reserved for CALFED ecosystem restoration.

**Institutional issues**
- At least 10 federal agencies have major roles, and budgets are set two years ahead, with one-year or two-year spending timelines that make multi-year ecosystem funding uncertain.
- The [[Federal Advisory Committee Act]] (FACA) and Executive Order 12838 (1993), which directs termination of at least one-third of FACA committees, are cited as constraints on collaborative work.
- The 1995 CALFED Bay-Delta Advisory Council (BDAC) has 32 members.

**Recommendations**
- Adopt ecosystem management with coordinated science, common terms, and a scientific advisory panel.
- Support voluntary water transfers, AB 3030 groundwater plans, and conjunctive use.
- Continue CALFED in some form, with federal control over its funds, and consider reinstating basin commissions. The report notes that all seven federal-state river basin commissions were abolished in 1981.
- Continue support for watershed programs, including the Sacramento River Watershed Program and the West Stanislaus study area.
