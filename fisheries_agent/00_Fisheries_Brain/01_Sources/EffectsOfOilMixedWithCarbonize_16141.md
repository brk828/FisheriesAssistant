# Effects of Oil Mixed with Carbonized Sand on Aquatic Animals

#ichthyology #marine-biology #toadfish #bivalve-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This 1949 Fish and Wildlife Service Special Scientific Report, by Walter A. Chipman and Paul S. Galtsoff, was prepared at the request of the US Navy Bureau of Ships. It tests whether crude oil and other oils, anchored on the bottom by [[Carbonized Sand]], remain toxic to marine organisms. Experiments on five species found that oil and oil-sand mixtures were toxic in most cases. The report notes that the [[Carbonized Sand Oil Removal Method]] is effective at removing surface slicks, but argues that sinking oil onto the bottom does not greatly lessen its toxicity. The authors conclude that sinking oil is acceptable mainly in already polluted harbors, and only in emergencies over productive shellfish beds.

## Background & Description

Oil pollution of inshore waters threatens aquatic life, recreation and fire safety around creosoted piers. The Norfolk Naval Shipyard developed carbonized sand by roasting creosote oil and sand at about 700 °F. Sprayed onto slicks, it bonds with the oil, which can then be sunk with a water hose. The Navy had cited a Life magazine caption claiming sunk sludge was "lethal to most marine life," but no corroborating evidence existed. The study therefore aimed to test this claim.

Sand requirements were measured by pouring 10 ml of oil onto 500 ml of sea water (salinity 31.65 ‰). Sinking 10 ml of oil took 29.3 to 31.3 g of sand in sea water. Fresh water needed 25.3 g. Diluted sea water needed an intermediate 27.0 g. Based on an oil density of 0.96 g/ml, the authors estimated about 36.25 lb of sand per lb of oil, about 33% above the Chemical Laboratory (1947) figure. Sea water pH shifted only slightly (8.05 to 8.08) and returned to 8.05 after 24 hours.

Test organisms were chosen for economic value, ease of handling and measurable responses:
- [[Tubularia Crocea]], a hydrozoan on pilings, the most sensitive species
- [[Balanus Balanoides]], an acorn barnacle, scored by cirri sweeping
- [[Opsanus Tau]] embryos, 8 to 10 mm larvae attached by yolk sacs
- [[Venus Mercenaria]], the hard-shell clam, the most resistant species
- [[Crassostrea Virginica]], the eastern oyster, assessed with filtration and valve-activity methods

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report cites earlier work (Nelson 1925, Galtsoff et al. 1935, Seydel 1913, Veselov 1948) showing that oil toxicity comes mainly from water-soluble fractions, such as naphthenic and volatile acids. Oil does not stay on the surface: it binds to suspended silt and sinks, and can resurface after storms or currents. Surface films disappearing does not mean pollution has ended. Slow-circulation harbors and bays may accumulate toxic effects, while open estuaries dilute them. The authors argue that heavily polluted harbors are already "almost biological deserts," so added oil there is unlikely to cause much further damage. Dilution in open bays is expected to keep sunk oil from reaching threshold concentrations for productive shellfish grounds.

The authors also framed the study in terms of national oil development. Drilling in inshore waters and expansion onto the continental shelf made toxicological research urgent to protect coastal fisheries.

## Key Findings

**Scope limitation:** The report does not extend its results to oil pollution in general or to particular field situations (Preface).

**Tubularia crocea (hydroid):**
- In standing water, oil-to-sea-water ratios from 1:20 to 1:1,000 were tested. At 1:20 and 1:40, nearly all hydranths died within 24 hours. At 1:1,000, 17 of 50 died in one day, against 5 of 60 in controls.
- Water extracts of crude oil were toxic up to 1:200 (equivalent to a 1:2,000 dilution of the original oil).
- In running water with oil placed nearby, 5 ml or more of oil-sand mixture caused a toxic effect after 48 hours. In one test, 22 hydranths died within 48 hours in the oil jar, and 35 more died by day five.
- When oil was kept away from the organisms and diluted, no ill effects were observed.

**Relative toxicity (Tubularia, toadfish, barnacles):** Crude oil was the most toxic. Lubricating oil was the least toxic and showed no effect on Tubularia or toadfish embryos.

**Balanus balanoides (barnacle):** Cirri sweeping slowed within 6 hours of exposure, followed by incomplete sweeps, shell opening, failure to respond to touch, and death. Controls showed no change over 70 hours.

**Opsanus tau (toadfish embryos):**
- Crude oil at 1:40 killed 3 of 5 embryos within 47 to 72 hours. Diesel oil showed toxicity within 52 hours at 1:20.
- At 1:200, all five embryos died within 11 days. No controls died.
- The log survival-time versus log concentration relationship approximated a straight line, indicating a time-concentration curve.

**Venus mercenaria (hard-shell clam):** Ten clams per bowl were tested over 12 days, with no deaths and no visible weakening. Siphon activity did not differ significantly between controls and oil-contaminated water. The authors suggest concentrations were too dilute to show effects.

**Crassostrea virginica (oyster):**
- Diesel oil at 1:189: 67% mortality with oil on the surface, against 25% with oil bound to carbonized sand, over 13 days. Controls had no mortality.
- Crude oil at 1:500 produced less mortality, probably because less oil was used.
- In running water, 500 ml of crude oil bound to sand over 35 days did not cause mortality or inhibit shell growth.
- Filtration (carmine cone method): oil extracts and oil-sand mixtures reduced the rate of current. Crude oil-sand mixtures reduced pumping over long periods, and the effect lasted 28 days.
- Apron method: depressed pumping and reduced open time, with signs of adductor weakness. A rise in pumping in some tests was linked to spawning, but the decline persisted.
- Copepod numbers in the oil-treated mixing chamber fell from 50–75 to 15–25, suggesting repellence.

**Discussion and conclusions:**
- Sinking oil with carbonized sand does not lessen toxicity. Oil bound near the bottom may leach toxins continuously.
- Carbonized sand is an efficient, cheap method for removing surface slicks, reduces fire hazards and localizes oil pollution.
- Use over valuable shellfish beds should be limited to emergencies and small spills.
- Waste crankcase oil was not tested and may be more toxic.
- The study did not examine the chemical nature, leaching rate or stability of toxic compounds. These require further funding and research.

**Summary (conclusions 1–6):** Crude, Diesel and Navy Grade Special fuel oils were toxic to four of five species. Toxicity occurred whether oil was on the surface or bound to sand on the bottom. Shaken extracts showed water-soluble toxins. Oysters' pumping and filtration were impaired. The method localizes pollution and is recommended for already contaminated harbors, where it is unlikely to harm nearby productive grounds.
