# Bonytail Chub Rearing Studies: Literature Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Final Report (September 2008), prepared for the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], reviews the literature and site-visit interviews on captive rearing of endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The authors, Carol A. Pacey and Paul C. Marsh of the [[Native Fish Laboratory]] at Arizona State University, sought practices linked to successful rearing and to reduced growth variation, with the goal of producing fish of at least 30 cm (about 12 in) for stocking and improved post-stocking survival. The bonytail is described as functionally extirpated in the wild, with no reproducing populations remaining, despite decades of stocking. The report concludes with 15 research and management recommendations covering diet, feeding time and rate, growth-variation management, sex determination, reproductive control and data sharing.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*), a "big river" fish once common and widely distributed across the Colorado River basin, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, and the Colorado River delta in Baja California Norte and Sonora, Mexico.
- **Status:** Listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1980). Wild populations likely numbered in the hundreds of thousands less than 50 years earlier, but remaining wild fish are too few for reliable abundance estimates.
- **Causes of decline:** Water development and introduction of non-native species, beginning early in the 20th century.
- **Stocking commitments:** The FWS 1996 Biological Opinion committed to producing 25,000 fish of 250–300 mm TL for Lake Mohave and 6,000 juveniles for Lake Havasu. The Lower Colorado Program set production goals of 620,000 fish of 300 mm TL over 50 years. Colorado committed to 24,000 fish (200+ mm TL) annually, and Utah to 16,280 fish (200+ mm TL).
- **Growth variability:** Hatchery bonytail show highly variable growth even within a single year class. Survival of hatchery-reared native fish is strongly correlated with release size, which is why uniform rearing to large size is a priority.
- **Study methods:** The review screened 1,918 potential sources. Of 501 critically reviewed, 293 were useful, and the team acquired and copied all available sources. Site visits were made to four federal and two state facilities (see below).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing facilities visited (site visits):**
- Federal: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (NM), [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (AZ), [[Uvalde National Fish Hatchery]] (TX), [[Achii Hanyo Native Fish Hatchery]] (AZ).
- State: [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] (UT) and the [[John W. Mumma Native Aquatic Species Restoration Facility]] (CO).
- Trinidad State Junior College (CO) was not visited because Mumma NASRF supplied its data.

**Holding and water conditions:**
- Wahweap SFH and Mumma NASRF used lined ponds. Willow Beach NFH used concrete raceways. Achii Hanyo used earthen ponds. Dexter NFH & TC and Uvalde NFH used mixed intensive and extensive systems.
- Mumma NASRF kept ponds near 21 °C through winter using warm well water. Willow Beach NFH had limited winter warming because its solar panels were inefficient.
- Most facilities reported high summer temperatures but no low dissolved oxygen problems.
- Water chemistry ranges reported for adults included calcium 10–105 mg/L, pH 7.5–9, and dissolved oxygen <3–15 mg/L. Most facilities used well water; Willow Beach NFH and Achii Hanyo used Colorado River water.
- Willow Beach NFH and Achii Hanyo treated disease year-round. The other facilities seldom did.

**Stocking density examples:**
- Achii Hanyo: 2005 ponds held roughly 8,333, 5,500 and 6,250 fish/ac. About 6,000 fish were harvested, a 30% survival rate.
- Dexter NFH & TC: nominal rates of 100,000 young-of-year fish/ac, 10,000 Age 1 fish/ac, and 5,000 Age 2 fish/ac.
- Mumma NASRF: 170,000 YOY fish/ac and 50,000 Age 1 fish/ac in 2005.

## Key Findings

**Growth performance:**
- Greatest adult length growth was about 10 mm TL/month at Wahweap SFH (1997 year class), compared with 6.2 mm TL/month at Dexter NFH & TC (1981 year class).
- Age 2+ fish at Wahweap SFH reached 13 mm TL/month (1996 year class), the highest length growth in that age group.
- Growth in length typically slowed after fish reached about 300 mm TL (12 in). Willow Beach NFH reported that length growth slowed at 330–375 mm TL.
- Off-station, natural pond organisms in Lake Mohave backwaters and organisms in the Middle Green River wetlands produced the greatest length growth, at 30 and 25 mm TL/month.
- Juvenile growth at Dexter NFH & TC reached 17 mm TL/month on a combined Nelson & Sons razorback and trout diet.
- Juvenile growth declined by about 50% as fish aged from 150 to 365 days post-hatch.

**Diet and feeding:**
- All six facilities fed adults a commercial razorback sucker diet (Bozeman formulation, made by Nelson & Sons). Some also used salmon or trout diets.
- Feed rates ranged from 1–5% body weight per day. Willow Beach NFH reduced feeding to 1% in summer to limit particulate load.
- Feeding times ranged from early morning to evening. Feeding frequency ranged from once to four times per day. Only one study reported feeding times, and none reported feed type (sinking or floating).
- In the one published bonytail diet study (Henne et al. 2006), a commercial high-protein shrimp diet produced the highest juvenile growth rate. The high-protein shrimp diet also gave the greatest weight gain in a separate juvenile study.

**Handling and health:**
- Only eight citations on adult growth included handling data. In one study, fish that were repeatedly recaptured and measured grew less in length and weight than fish that were not recaptured (Paukert et al. 2005).
- Willow Beach NFH and Achii Hanyo treated disease regularly. Wahweap SFH was certified disease-free.

**Growth variation and sex:**
- Hatchery year classes consistently separated into three growth groups: fast (~10%), regular (~80%), and slow (~10%).
- Dexter NFH & TC reported that the largest fish at ages 0–2 were generally female. Temperature may influence sex determination in bonytail, based on analogy to European sea bass.

**Reproduction:**
- Volunteer spawning occurred in ponds at Wahweap SFH and Dexter NFH & TC, with fish as young as 1–2 years old spawning.
- Wahweap SFH disposed of more than 100,000 bonytail from volunteer spawn under Utah state policy. The authors recommend stocking surplus fish into off-channel habitats instead.

**Data management:**
- Facilities varied in data sharing. The authors recommend standardized "chain of custody" tracking sheets and two basin-level data repositories.

**Principal recommendations (15 total):**
1. Conduct diet studies on juveniles of various sizes, including feeding time and natural pond production.
2. Develop a specific bonytail diet.
3. Classify and quantify natural pond invertebrates, and install bug lights over ponds.
4. Test feeding frequency and timing, with automatic feeders for crepuscular feeding.
5. Determine optimal feeding rates as percent body weight.
6. Quantify ad libitum feeding for larval and early juvenile culture.
7. Align field stocking within one month of hatchery spawning, when secondary sexual characteristics appear.
8. Assess sexual maturity under lab conditions as a function of temperature and age.
9. Sacrifice and necropsy slow-growing fish to determine sex.
10. Sort fish by size at least twice yearly.
11. Test whether flow in static ponds and raceways increases energy use and growth.
12. Monitor growth at Willow Beach NFH and Achii Hanyo.
13. Test constant light (24 h photoperiod) and dietary energy reduction to limit volunteer reproduction.
14. Use standardized tracking sheets for each year class.
15. Designate two permanent data repositories (upper and lower basin) and exchange data annually.

**Overall conclusion:** Current hatchery practices produce highly variable growth and limited survival of stocked fish. The authors suggest that, with better feeding, sorting and data sharing, growth variation can be reduced and fish can be reared to target size.
