# Obituary: Charles H. Lowe Jr. (1920–2002)

#herpetology #teiidae #sonoran-desert

## Executive Summary

This obituary honors [[Charles H. Lowe Jr.]], a herpetologist and natural historian who joined the [[University of Arizona]] in 1950 and retired in 1995. He died in Tucson on 13 September 2002, aged 82. Lowe built a major southwestern herpetological collection, resolved the taxonomy of parthenogenetic [[Whiptail Lizards]], and worked for decades on the physiology and ecology of the [[Sonoran Desert]]. The obituary also portrays him as a demanding, eccentric teacher whose students became leading curators, professors and conservationists. It closes by noting the [[Tucson Herpetological Society]] fund established in his name.

## Background & Description

**Early life.** Lowe was born into a Mormon family in Los Angeles, California, on 16 April 1920. He developed a love of the desert as a teenager, collecting snakes in southern California with a herpetology club. The family became poor during the [[Great Depression]] after his father lost his business. Lowe attended the University of California, Los Angeles on a basketball scholarship. He served as a U.S. Navy ensign and communications officer in the Pacific during [[World War II]], then returned to graduate study in 1946.

**Education and mentors.** His Ph.D. advisor was [[Raymond B. Cowles]], who strongly shaped Lowe's views on fieldwork and desert organisms. Other influential mentors included physiologist George A. Bartholomew, ichthyologist Boyd W. Walker, and paleobotanist Dave I. Axelrod. His 1950 dissertation covered West Coast plethodontid salamanders, studied with [[Robert C. Stebbins]].

**Career at Arizona.** Lowe arrived in Tucson in 1950 and helped transform the Zoology Department into a leading Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology over 45 years. He taught basic and advanced zoology early on and later taught advanced herpetology, speciation, and the popular "Natural History of the Southwest" course.

**Notable works.**
- *The Vertebrates of Arizona* (1964), which he edited and to which he contributed detailed descriptions of Arizona's natural environments.
- *The Venomous Reptiles of Arizona* (1986), with Cecil Schwalbe and Terry Johnson.
- A three-volume autecology of the saguaro with [[Scotty Steenbergh]] (1976, 1977, 1983).
- The map *Biotic Communities of the Southwest*, coauthored with David E. Brown.

**Personal reputation.** Lowe was physically imposing (6 feet 5 inches) and known for colorful language and an unorthodox teaching style. Many anecdotes circulated among herpetologists. He sometimes supervised as many as 17 graduate students at once. He also had friendships with [[Marlon Brando]] and [[Theodosius Dobzhansky]], and he collaborated with [[Robert H. Whittaker]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lowe's research linked individual physiology to population processes and species conservation. Key strands of his work included:

- **Thermal and water physiology:** He emphasized substratum, temperature, and water balance. His studies of supercooling and cold-temperature physiology of columnar cacti suggested cold was a key factor in a troubling decline of saguaros in the Tucson region.
- **Desert landscape and vegetation:** He worked on desert landscape ecology and classified the vegetation and biotic communities of the southwest, a project completed by Brown in a landmark 1982 publication.
- **Speciation and cytogenetics:** He and his students showed that many whiptail lizard species are parthenogenetic, some being triploid all-female hybrids.
- **Conservation:** In the 1970s he shifted toward herpetofaunas and their conservation. In the 1990s he published more than in either of the two previous decades, becoming a participant and mentor in [[Conservation Biology]].

The obituary contrasts Lowe's approach with mainstream [[Community Ecology]], which moved toward theoretical modeling of species interactions. Lowe, like [[Forrest Shreve]], was less focused on species interactions and more on physical and physiological constraints. The author argues that connecting physiological performance to population and community ecology remains an unfinished synthesis.

## Key Findings

**Collections and taxonomy**
- Lowe directed the development of a herpetological collection of more than 50,000 specimens, of which he personally collected over 8,500 as primary collector.
- He and his students helped resolve the problem of parthenogenetic whiptail lizards during the 1960s.
- He described at least 20 new species and subspecies.
- He published at least 23 papers on the Sonoran Desert herpetofauna during the 1950s.

**Physiology and ecology**
- His work on desert fishes (1967, 1969) included what was recorded as the highest temperature tolerance for a fish.
- He coauthored a classic 1963 paper on the saguaro population with Robert Whittaker and William Niering.
- Long-term studies of the [[Gila Monster]] and beaded lizards, with students including [[Dan Beck]], produced much unpublished data.
- A 1965 world tour of deserts, supported by the U.S. Army, marked a notable period of his fieldwork.

**Training and legacy**
- Lowe supervised a large number of graduate students. The obituary lists 24 masters and 23 doctorates, many of whom became curators at major museums, professors, and conservation professionals.
- He was known for demanding, sometimes harsh mentorship, yet many students credited him with their careers.

**Personal and professional challenges**
- Lowe was repeatedly fired and denied raises during his career. The obituary notes his "chaotic" paper trail and his sense of competition.
- The 1970s were a difficult decade marked by the deaths of students and colleagues and an unresolved tenure dispute.
- He was reinstated after a leave and later became more self-critical about his failings.

**Commemoration**
- The Tucson Herpetological Society established the Charles H. Lowe Jr. Herpetology Research Fund, and the Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum also accepts contributions in his honor.
