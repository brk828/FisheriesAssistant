## Executive Summary

This 1984 study by Sofield, Douglas, Hansens, and Vrijenhoek resolves a long-standing taxonomic controversy surrounding the salt-marsh horse fly [[Tabanus Nigrovittatus]], an economically important biting-fly pest of coastal New Jersey. Using protein electrophoresis, the authors confirmed that the nominal species is actually a cryptic species complex comprising two genetically distinct forms: *T. nigrovittatus* (Type I) and *T. conterminus* Walker (Type II, formerly confused with *T. simulans*). Because these species cannot be reliably separated by single morphological traits (e.g., total body length alone), the authors applied stepwise multiple discriminant analysis (SMDA) to 15 head measurements plus total length, identifying four characters that together provide 100% discrimination between the two species. This tandem electrophoretic-morphometric protocol is proposed as a general model for detecting and diagnosing cryptic species in insect pest-control programs, with direct implications for [[Biting Fly Control Programs]] in coastal recreational areas.

## Background & Description

**Study design:** 60 adult female flies ≥10.7 mm total length (the size range of overlap between species) were collected via box traps at Stone Harbor, NJ, electrophoretically sorted (33 *T. nigrovittatus*, 27 *T. conterminus*), then measured for total body length and 15 head characters using a filar ocular micrometer and digitizer system.

**Electrophoretic diagnosis:** Species-diagnostic, non-overlapping electromorphs were obtained from two enzymes:
- 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6-PGDH)
- Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (G3PDH)

**Discriminating morphological variables (in order of discriminatory power via SMDA):**
1. Width of the frons dorsally (WFRDOR) — greatest single discriminator
2. Width of the scape (WSCAP)
3. Width of the head (WHD)
4. Total body length (TOTLEN)

Together these four variables provided **complete (100%) discrimination** between the species — no single character alone (including total length) achieved this.

**Key morphometric contrasts (mean ± SD, larger flies only, ≥10.7 mm):**

| Character | *T. nigrovittatus* | *T. conterminus* |
|---|---|---|
| Total length (mm) | 11.88 ± 0.38 | 12.65 ± 0.68 |
| Width of head (mm) | 3.75 ± 0.13 | 3.96 ± 0.22 |
| Width of frons, dorsal (mm) | 0.52 ± 0.02 | 0.62 ± 0.04 |
| Width of scape (mm) | 0.25 ± 0.01 | 0.29 ± 0.02 |

**Canonical discriminant function:**

```
SCORE = 17.09 − 28.84(WFRDOR) − 22.38(WSCAP) + 5.88(WHD) − 1.45(TOTLEN)
```

- Mean canonical score, *T. nigrovittatus*: **+2.46**
- Mean canonical score, *T. conterminus*: **−2.01**
- Practical cutoff for field classification: scores > +0.30 → *T. nigrovittatus*; scores < −0.80 → *T. conterminus* (caution urged near the boundary with larger sample sizes)

**Field identification key (adult females):**
1. Total body length < 10.7 mm → *T. nigrovittatus* (unambiguous by size alone)
2. Total body length ≥ 10.7 mm → apply discriminant score model above

Small individuals (<10.7 mm) are invariably *T. nigrovittatus*; larger individuals of *T. nigrovittatus* (up to 18.7 mm) do occur, meaning body size alone is unreliable for classifying larger flies — it must be combined with the three head-character measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Coastal salt marshes of New Jersey; flies collected via box traps at [[Stone Harbor]].
- **Economic/ecological importance:** *Tabanus nigrovittatus* (the "salt-marsh greenhead") is a major biting-fly pest impacting coastal recreation and tourism economies in states like New Jersey.
- **Life history divergence:** Prior work (Freeman 1962) found that larval and pupal forms of one cryptic form ("*T.* sp. 3") were both morphologically and **ecologically disjunct** from typical *T. nigrovittatus*, suggesting habitat partitioning within the marsh.
- **Behavioral divergence:** Graham & Stoffolano (1983) documented differences in **oviposition (egg-laying) behavior** between the two sibling species.
- **Phenological/geographic divergence:** Sofield (1983) reported differences in **seasonal and geographical abundance** between the two species in New Jersey.
- **Biogeography:** The discriminant technique was independently validated on samples from Nova Scotia, Maine, Massachusetts, and Georgia, suggesting the two species may be broadly sympatric along the eastern seaboard of North America — raising open questions about potential [[Character Displacement]] in size at zones of range overlap and the possible ecological differences permitting coexistence of these syntopic sibling species.

## Key Findings

- **Cryptic species resolved:** The "*Tabanus nigrovittatus* complex," long suspected (for ~75 years) to comprise more than one species (Hine 1906; Stone 1938; Freeman 1962; Freeman & Hansens 1972), was confirmed via starch-gel electrophoresis (building on Jacobson et al. 1981) to consist of two genetically distinct, non-interbreeding forms: *T. nigrovittatus* (Type I) and *T. conterminus* (Type II).
- **Taxonomic correction:** Comparison with type specimens (BMNH) confirmed that the name ***T. conterminus*** (not *T. simulans*) properly applies to the larger Type II form; *T. nigrovittatus* is tentatively retained for the smaller species, though its validity has since been questioned (Burger 1984).
- **Morphology alone is insufficient:** No single morphological character, including total body length, could completely separate the two species due to broad overlap in the ≥10.7 mm size class — multivariate analysis was required.
- **First unequivocal female-only morphological key:** This is the first study to demonstrate that adult female morphology (without electrophoresis) can fully discriminate the species, via a four-variable discriminant function.
- **Broader methodological implication:** The authors propose a general **two-step protocol for pest control programs** managing cryptic species complexes:
  1. Electrophoretically categorize forms within a mixed sample.
  2. Use these genetically resolved groups to derive a multivariate morphometric key for rapid, inexpensive field screening (avoiding the cost/time of routine electrophoresis or cytogenetics).
- **Relevance to pest control failure:** Undetected cryptic species within target pest populations can cause control programs to appear to fail, since only one species in the complex may be effectively suppressed while the other resurges (citing the [[Anopheles Gambiae]] complex as a parallel case in medical entomology).
- **Open questions raised:** Whether *T. nigrovittatus* and *T. conterminus* are sympatric along the entire eastern seaboard of the US and Canada, where suture/contact zones occur, and whether observed body-size differences reflect character displacement or correlate with ecological differentiation enabling coexistence.
