# Water Quality of the Proposed Norden Reservoir, Nebraska, and Its Implications for Fishery Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #reservoir-fisheries #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation technical report (REC-ERC-81-8, May 1981, by James W. Yahnke) assesses the water quality of the proposed [[Norden Reservoir]] on the [[Niobrara River]] in north-central Nebraska. It was prepared after the EPA and the Nebraska Department of Environmental Control questioned possible oxygen depletion in the reservoir, and it was later expanded to a fishery analysis. Using [[Nutrient Loading Models]], a [[Temperature Simulation Model]] and transposed data from [[Glendo Reservoir]] in Wyoming, the study projects a eutrophic reservoir, a likely [[Blue-Green Algae]] bloom, a cold dimictic thermal regime, and hypolimnetic dissolved oxygen (DO) that may fall below the State standard of 7 mg/L at the river outlet. The results are described by the author as rough estimates useful for environmental impact assessment and management planning.

## Background & Description

- **Project context:** The [[O'Neill Unit]] Final Environmental Statement (September 1972) was found inadequate by the U.S. District Court in Lincoln, Nebraska, and supplemented in June 1978. The EPA then raised concern about excessive productivity and hypolimnetic oxygen depletion in Norden Reservoir.
- **Setting:** Norden lies in a transition zone between prairie grassland (south of the valley) and [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest (north), with bottomland hardwoods on the north-facing slope. Steep valley relief gives the reservoir a small surface-to-volume ratio, which favors stratification, while its depth may produce "morphometric oligotrophy" through a large hypolimnetic oxygen reserve.
- **Regulatory framework:** Congress's Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments (P.L. 92-500, 1972) funded trophic assessment through the [[National Eutrophication Survey]]. Trophic boundaries follow the [[Vollenweider Loading Model]] as modified by Vollenweider and Dillon (1974) and Rast and Lee (1978).
- **Document type:** Technical report with 12 summary conclusions, 7 tables (including the Table 7 species survival projection), 5 figures and a temperature-profile appendix (Figures A-1 to A-24). Fish and invertebrate data are secondary to the limnological analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic state:** Projected areal total phosphorus loading is 3.25 ± 0.50 g P/m²-yr, well above the "dangerous" eutrophic loading of 1.05 g P/m²-yr (permissible 0.52). Glendo Reservoir has a nearly identical loading of 3.24 g P/m²-yr.
- **Chlorophyll a:** Projected mean annual chlorophyll a is 15 ± 7 µg/L for Norden and 13 ± 5 µg/L for Glendo; Glendo's 1975 measured mean was 8.5 µg/L.
- **Stratification:** Simulated stable stratification lasted 112 to 140 days, with hypolimnetic temperatures below 10 °C. The reservoir is classed as a cold dimictic lake. Stratification breakdown is driven by epilimnetic cooling in autumn.
- **Oxygen:** Projected discharge DO before autumn overturn is 5.3 to 5.6 mg/L, below the 7 mg/L State standard. Reaeration below the stilling basin is expected to raise this to 8.2 to 9.7 mg/L.
- **Benthos:** Ekman dredge samples from the Niobrara (April 1980) averaged 2.2 organisms/m², with no organisms where velocity exceeded 0.45 m/s. Hynes (1970) is cited for sand-substrate habitat.
- **Dissolved solids:** Current TDS is 167 to 204 mg/L; post-impoundment mean is projected at 188 mg/L against a 400 mg/L standard. The increase is too small to disrupt stratification.
- **Downstream thermal regime:** Peak temperatures at the dam could be lowered 15 to 20 °C and the annual minimum raised from 0 °C to 4 °C. The annual range is estimated at 4 to 11 °C.

## Key Findings

**Trophic state and algal blooms**
- The reservoir is expected to be eutrophic. The [[Reckhow and Simpson Model]] gives a discriminant function of 0.011 (threshold 0.17), a discriminant score of 2.0, and a 98 percent probability of blue-green dominance.
- The model was checked on Glendo Reservoir (probability 0.88). NES sampling at Glendo in August 1975 found about 88 percent [[Aphanizomenon]] with chlorophyll a up to 26.3 µg/L. The North Platte system reservoirs show annual late-summer Aphanizomenon blooms (1976, 1977) that caused no evident fishery or recreation damage, though the authors note this cannot be extrapolated with certainty to Norden.

**Thermal regime**
- Duration of stratification is set mainly by annual inflow. Operational and structural modifications (multi-level outlets) had no measurable effect.
- Table 2: 1977 present-level flow gave 133 days (no spill) and 1977 depleted gave 140 days; 1978 present-level gave 112 days and 1978 depleted gave 119 days.
- Spillway use caused abrupt discharge temperature drops of up to 11.2 °C within a day or two. The report recommends avoiding spillway bypass and maximizing river outlet works releases.

**Dissolved oxygen**
- Oxygen deficit at Glendo increased linearly during stratification, with regressions of r = 0.86 to 0.98 and a significant fit for combined years.
- Projected [O2] in the release ranges from 5.3 to 5.6 mg/L, a best-estimate minimum. Mitigation options analyzed:
  - Structural: higher-level outlet (bifurcation from the canal outlet, minimum 7.6 mg/L) or hydraulic jump designs.
  - Legal: lowering the standard to 5 mg/L (considered politically uncertain) or designating a mixing zone.
- The report concludes that designating a short mixing zone, or only the stilling basin, would meet the standard. Physical reaeration would be expected to meet it.

**Fishery management**
- Of the 22 fish species currently in the river, 6 could not survive in the reservoir. Twelve are unlikely to survive in the river immediately below the dam, and 5 may survive but be stressed (Table 7).
- The reservoir would support a two-story fishery with cool-water (not true warm-water) habitat. Cold-water habitat would occupy about 55 percent of volume and warm-water about 44 percent, with the caveat that depth-based figures differ.
- Stocking list (USFWS 1980 draft): smallmouth bass, rock bass, channel catfish, walleye, yellow perch, rainbow trout (McConaughy strain), alewife and spottail shiner. The report questions whether channel catfish management is feasible.
- Key species notes:
  - [[Walleye]] should do well if spawning beds and forage (alewife, minnows) are available. Walleye diets are ~88 percent fish by volume for young-of-year (Goodson, 1966).
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] should do well, but spring-spawning stock is recommended over the fall-run McConaughy strain to avoid competition with [[Brown Trout]], which already reproduces in Fairfield Creek.
  - Yellow perch are considered detrimental, as at Glendo.
  - [[White Bass]] and [[Striped Bass]] are suggested as pelagic predators. White bass may be limited by spawning access (about 20 km upstream).
  - Gizzard shad is suggested as an alternative to alewife, which has a history of die-offs.
- **Species survival (Table 7):** 23 species were assessed. Examples: white sucker survives in both reservoir and river; channel catfish, carp, white crappie, green sunfish and gizzard shad are unlikely to persist below the dam. Minnows adapted to flowing water are likely to be eliminated from the reservoir.
- **Downstream:** The river below the dam will be cold-water. Trout growth is expected to be low, and stocking (catchable or subcatchable initially) is required because no spawning habitat exists in that reach. A 3 to 6 week extension of fall cooling is possible.

**Project conclusions**
- Benthic production below the dam may improve with lower velocities, but the whooping crane stop-over habitat requirement and projected riverbed degradation of about 11.5 m (38 ft) (ERT, 1981) may offset gains.
